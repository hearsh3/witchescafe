# For Going Out

At the top of the ladder, the merchant asked for a hand.

Chisa read the sentence twice, the second time aloud. He had reached the sill, seen how far the ground lay beneath him, and begun addressing the captain with considerable courtesy.

“Take my elbow, please. My legs have begun doing something very strange.”

Amy lowered her cake.

“Oh, poor man. He's been practising the speech and thinking about the window, and now he's up there.”

“The captain's got him,” Chisa said. “He's pulling him through.”

Lupa shifted forward on the bench.

“Put a foot on the rung below. You get more room to turn that way.”

“I'll pass that on when I see him,” Ciaccona said.

Chisa read another paragraph. The merchant came through sideways, lost a slipper in the yard, and sat on the floor while the captain brought him a glass of water. Then the aunt appeared in the doorway.

All three of the room's occupants stood up.

“She's going to ask about the tools,” Cartethyia said. “The girl still has the hammer in her skirt.”

The aunt looked from the open cupboard to the ladder. She asked the heroine why she had brought two men into her bedroom. The heroine explained that they had come to move the cupboard.

“And it ends with the aunt choosing which wall she wants it against,” Chisa said.

Lupa sat back, rubbing her thumb along the edge of its dressing.

“Let them get on with it. She'll have time to eat her plums while they're busy.”

“I think the gardener has those,” Amy said. “He's had a lovely day so far. I'd like a chapter about him.”

Chisa looked toward the next page. At its top, the heroine had begun taking her dresses out of the cupboard.

From the kitchen, Mrs Ren called that the food was ready.

Chisa put the violet sample between the pages and closed the book.

Cartethyia rose with a little length of thread still wound around her finger. She followed the others inside, unwinding it as she went.

***

After supper, Chisa laid her jacket on the bed.

The cloth seller's slip gave the width and length of the two pieces he had shown her. She put it beside the jacket, smoothed one sleeve and measured it with the cord from her bag. At the shoulder she made a knot, then another at the cuff.

When she held the cord against the numbers, the knot had shifted.

She laid it out again.

Lupa stopped at the open door with a folded towel beneath her arm. The pink soap had grown stronger in the warmth of the house.

“Mrs Ren has a measuring tape,” she said. “She was using it on the curtain this morning. I'll fetch it.”

Chisa sat on the bed while she went. Through the wall came the faint scrape of a chair, then someone walking toward the stair. She picked up the violet sample and turned its edge beneath her thumb.

Lupa returned with the tape and Amy.

“I brought paper,” Amy said. “Carte's occupied, so you get my drawing. I can do sleeves.”

She put a sheet on the bedside table and borrowed the pencil lying across Chisa's open notebook. Lupa took the jacket by its shoulders and held it up.

“Put this on first. We'll see where you want the new one to sit.”

Chisa worked her arms into the sleeves. Lupa settled the back over her shoulders and held the tape against the collar.

“Show me where you'd like the hem.”

Chisa touched the middle of her thigh.

The pencil stopped.

“That gives us a lot of coat,” Amy said. “You could have a split at the back. It would move when you walked.”

She began drawing again. Chisa heard the pencil travel farther down the sheet.

Lupa put a fingertip at the place Chisa had indicated.

“Lift your arm as if you're reaching for the blade. I want to see what happens here.”

Chisa raised her elbow. The jacket pulled across her back.

Lupa followed the tension with the edge of her hand.

“We'll want more room through the shoulder. And the sleeve should turn with you. Who are you taking it to?”

“The cloth seller gave me a name.”

“Good. Wear your harness when you go. She can fit it around the straps.”

Chisa lowered her arm.

For a few moments, Amy's pencil was the only sound in the room. Then Chisa took the seller's slip from the bed and turned it over. The seamstress's address was written across the back.

“I'd like to wear it to the tea stall,” she said. “And when we go to look at shops. I was thinking of the fan Mrs Wei had by her counter.”

Lupa's hand rested against the shoulder seam.

Chisa opened the seller's slip again.

“The fan was cream, with a dark edge. I thought it would look good with this colour.”

Lupa let the tape fall loose.

“You should take the sample back and hold them together. Her shop's dark at that end.”

She stepped around Chisa and took both cuffs lightly between her fingers.

“How wide do you want these?”

Chisa spread her hands.

Amy erased part of her drawing.

“You're going to reach across a table and collect everyone's spoons.”

“I can turn them back while I eat.”

Lupa folded the existing cuff to show her.

“Have the lining come all the way down. It'll show here when you do that. Ask her to bring out a few colours. You might like something different once it's against your hands.”

Chisa moved her wrist. Lupa held the fold while she considered it, then released the cloth.

Amy turned her paper around.

The drawing had a narrow waist, a generous skirt and sleeves which Amy had shaded more carefully than the rest. Around the throat she had added a spray of something that might have been feathers.

Chisa touched that part.

“What happens here when I sit down?”

“You look expensive from the shoulders up. It's useful in restaurants.”

“I'd like room to turn my head.”

Amy took the pencil back.

“We can move it to one side. I was enjoying that bit.”

She tried it over one shoulder, studied the result and offered the sheet again. Chisa put a finger on the plain side of the collar.

“Could you carry this around the whole neck?”

Amy rested her chin in her hand. After a moment, she drew the second version beside the first.

Lupa leaned over the table.

“Put pockets inside. Deep ones, with the opening here.”

“For the book,” Chisa said. She went to the bed and picked it up. “I'll take this with me as well.”

“Ask her to reinforce the pocket,” Lupa said. “A book's heavy once it starts swinging.”

Amy drew a little rectangle inside the coat. Beneath it she wrote *actual book* and added an arrow.

Chisa sat down to take off her jacket. Lupa watched how the hem fell against the mattress, then bent to measure it. Her hair brushed Chisa's knee.

“You have done this before,” Chisa said.

“Every time somebody wanted a new poster. They'd put me in a room with a tailor and a woman holding twenty sketches of my legs.”

Amy turned on her stool.

“Did you keep any of those?”

“There's a blue one in a box at home. The lining cost more than my first spear. I wore it once, and the woman who paid for it spent the whole evening telling people to stand farther away with their drinks.”

“I'd like to see you in that,” Chisa said.

Lupa made a thoughtful sound. She ran the tape between two fingers, flattening a twist.

“The sleeves would need letting out. I was smaller through here.” She touched her upper arm. “The back was good, though. It opened when I turned.”

She stood, held the towel against her waist and took three steps toward the door. At the last one she turned slowly, letting the loose end of the towel follow.

Amy watched from the stool.

“You did that with a room full of people looking at you.”

“They'd paid for the room.” Lupa adjusted the towel and tried the turn again. “The coat came around about here. There was a clasp on the shoulder which caught the light. I knew where they had put the lamps.”

Chisa listened to the change in her stride: the slower placement of her feet, the slight pause before she turned. She had heard Lupa arrive in crowded places with everybody moving aside. These steps invited people to keep looking.

“Would you show me once more?” she asked.

Lupa took the towel back to the doorway.

This time Amy held the lamp higher.

***

Ciaccona had cleared a place on the table by moving the terrarium onto the bed.

Cartethyia moved it back when she came in.

“The coverlet tilts,” she explained. “It sits better here.”

Ciaccona looked at the rectangle of dust where it had been. Her strings lay beside it, still wrapped.

“I'd like enough table to work on the lute.”

Cartethyia held the glass frame against her stomach. She looked from the instrument case to the terrarium, then at the little patch of table beside the lamp.

“We could move the lamp.”

“I need the light on the pegs. My hands are giving me enough trouble already.”

Cartethyia set the frame carefully on the floor. She stood with her hands on her hips for a moment, studying the room.

The table held the plant, Ciaccona's notebook, a cup, a comb, two folded shirts and the small saucer into which Cartethyia had been putting fallen leaves. Beside the notebook lay a neatly dried leaf she had apparently decided to keep.

Ciaccona picked up the shirts. One was hers. She folded it again along the middle and put both in the drawer.

Cartethyia took the cup to the basin.

They stood back and looked at the table.

“That helps a little,” Cartethyia said.

Ciaccona opened her instrument case.

Cartethyia turned the basin over and pressed her palms against its base. It rocked. She took it back to the washstand and fetched a stool from the passage. Beside the window, she tested each foot and folded a piece of paper beneath the shortest one.

The terrarium fitted with a narrow margin all around.

“I can sit on the bed to look at it,” she said. She tried that too. From there, the pale new tip stood level with her eyes.

Ciaccona put her lute across the cleared table.

“Tomorrow we can ask Mrs Ren about a shelf. Something wide enough to leave here when we go.”

“I would like to help put it up. The glazier had little blocks under his shelves to hold the corners.”

She retrieved the new glass. Ciaccona unwrapped the strings.

For several minutes, the room filled with small sounds: paper folding, a latch lifting, the soft metallic whisper of a string being drawn from its coil. Cartethyia spread a cloth beneath the damaged pane and worked at its fastenings with the tool the glazier had lent her.

Ciaccona fitted the first string through the bridge. At the peg, her fingers lost their grip. The loose end sprang against the back of her hand.

She laid it flat and pressed her palm to the table.

From the next room came Amy's voice, asking Lupa to hold something at an angle. Lupa answered at length. Then they both laughed.

Ciaccona picked up the string again. She brought it to the peg, tried to guide the end through and felt the same small cramp close beneath her knuckles.

“Carte, when your glass is in, would you wind this for me?”

Cartethyia looked up with the old pane between her hands.

“Show me how tightly to do it. I remember what happened to the peg last time.”

“I'll listen. You can turn it a little at a time.”

Cartethyia laid the cracked glass inside the folded cloth and fitted the new panel. She held it while she closed the fastenings, then sat back on her heels.

The camellia looked closer through the clear pane. Four browned edges showed plainly. So did the small, tightly folded tip.

She tilted her head to examine it from another angle.

“Come and see this before we do the string.”

Ciaccona rose and went to stand behind her. Cartethyia pointed near the end of the stem.

“You can see a line down the middle now.”

Ciaccona bent toward the glass. The new leaf had begun separating along its fold.

“I can see it. Give it some room to open.”

Cartethyia drew her finger back. She wiped a mark from the outside of the pane with her sleeve, then went to wash her hands.

Ciaccona sat with the lute across her knees. When Cartethyia returned, she guided her hand to the peg.

“Hold the loose end here while you turn. Keep the coils beside each other.”

Cartethyia bent over the head of the instrument. Ciaccona plucked the string with her thumb. A low, slack sound came from the body.

“A little farther. I'll tell you when we're close.”

The next note held longer. Cartethyia made another small turn, paused and waited for Ciaccona to strike it again.

The sound rose through the room.

They worked slowly through the remaining strings. Once Cartethyia turned the wrong peg, and Ciaccona put a hand over hers while they both listened to what she had done.

“The one beside your little finger,” she said. “You can blame that on me. I've been watching the plant.”

Cartethyia laughed and found the right one.

When they finished, Ciaccona sounded each string in turn. The instrument vibrated against her thigh. She went through them again, more slowly, letting each note last until the next was ready.

Cartethyia sat on the floor beside her chair.

“Would you like me to put it away?”

“Leave the case open for a while. I want to sit with it.”

Cartethyia folded the cloth around the broken pane. She tied it twice, put it near the door for the glazier and returned to the floor.

Ciaccona reached for her notebook. The dried leaf lay across the cover. She lifted it carefully and placed it between two clean pages.

“You'll have to remember where that is,” she said.

Cartethyia leaned against her leg.

“I look at your notebook quite often.”

“I've wondered how you keep finding the funny bits before I've read them to you.”

Ciaccona rested her hand on the lute. Beyond the wall, Lupa began another circuit of the room, explaining when to turn.

***

Amy came back to Chisa's door after the lamp had been lowered.

She held the drawing between both hands. She had gone over the pencil with ink, keeping the lines she liked, and added the measurements along the edge. The elaborate collar remained on the first figure. The second wore the simpler version Chisa had chosen.

“I thought you could show her both,” Amy said. “She'll understand where I was going with the shoulders.”

Chisa took the page to the lamp. Beneath the figures, Amy had drawn a little fan, open just far enough to show the dark edge.

“You remembered that part.”

“You spent a while talking about it. I wanted to see the whole thing together.”

Chisa looked at the coat, the fan, the lightly drawn hands. Amy had left the face as an oval, with a few lines for the hair falling beside it.

There were two small marks at the feet.

“Are these the slippers?”

“I ran out of paper. The hem took more space than I expected.”

Chisa held the drawing a little farther from the light. With the measurements beside it, the coat had acquired a shape someone could follow with scissors.

“Would you put your name at the bottom?”

Amy reached for the pen. She signed beneath the figure with the wide collar, paused, then carried a little line across to the other one.

“Both of them count,” she said. “I spent ages getting your sleeves even.”

Chisa laid the page flat on the bedside table while the ink dried.

Amy stayed beside her. From the passage came a slow footstep and the click of a door being eased into its frame. Amy turned toward it, then glanced back at the drawing.

“Show Ma tomorrow. She likes looking at things people are making.”

“I'll take it down when she has her tea.”

Amy touched the corner of the paper, checking it with a clean fingertip.

“She'll ask about pockets. You can point to the arrow.”

She gathered the pen and the unused sheet. At the doorway she put her head back in.

“I've put the cream paper under it. The violet looks better against that.”

Chisa listened to her go down the passage. She waited until the floorboards had settled, then held the sample against the drawing.

The cloth covered one of the little hands.

She moved it to the shoulder, turned it so the sheen caught the lamp and sat with it there for a while.

***

In the morning, Lupa found her on the bench behind the kitchen.

Chisa had a bun on a small plate and the book open beside it. A thin patch of sunlight reached the wall above her shoulder. She had been waiting for it to move down far enough to warm her back.

Lupa came through the door with a cup. She stopped beside the bench, leaned down to see the page number and drew a breath.

Chisa kept her finger beneath the line she was reading.

After a moment, Lupa set the cup beside her plate.

“Mrs Ren's put more in the pot. I'll leave your drawing on the table inside.”

She went back into the kitchen.

Chisa ate the last of the bun. She wiped her fingers, moved the plate to the end of the bench and brought the book onto her knees.

The heroine had taken twelve dresses out of the cupboard. While the men argued about the angle at which it would pass through the bedroom door, she sat on the bed choosing one to wear that evening.

Chisa read the description of the first dress, then the second.

She went back to the first.

By the time she had decided, the sunlight had reached her shoulder.

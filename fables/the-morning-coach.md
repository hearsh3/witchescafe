Chapter 1: The Morning Coach

The third time Suisui’s Terminal chimed on the corner of the table, she didn’t even look at it. She simply reached past the teapot, turned the small brass-rimmed slate face-down against the wood, and picked up her sewing needle again.

“That’s the fifth one since the noodles,” Amy observed from the far end of the bench. She still had on the short-sleeved summer uniform blouse and the pale blue silk tie from the morning, though she had surrendered to the mountain climate far enough to drape Lupa’s spare sheepskin vest over her shoulders like a sleeveless barrel. She had her chin propped in her palm, watching Suisui’s fingers move. “If that’s a guy, he’s desperate. If it’s a bill collector, Chisa can tell him you died in the fog.”

“I do not have bill collectors,” Suisui said serenely, drawing a double thread of grey silk through the torn hem of Yangyang’s spare under-tunic. “And if a man called me five times during luncheon on a Sunday, I would have his credit line revoked before the soup arrived.”

“Who is it, then?” Mei asked.

Mei was sitting on the floor beside the banked stove with her back against Iuno’s shins, a piece of rough charcoal wedged between her fingers and a scrap of birch bark balanced on her drawn-up knees. For the last twenty minutes she had been trying to draw a picture of an eight-year-old Amy with half-inch toothbrush bangs based entirely on the morning’s testimony. So far the bangs looked like a picket fence and the head underneath them looked suspiciously like a turtle.

“It is Little Zheng,” Suisui said, biting the thread off cleanly with her side teeth. “My deputy secretary in Mengzhou. He is twenty-two, his mother is my second aunt’s neighbour, and he panics whenever a magistrate uses yellow ink.”

Right on cue, the face-down Terminal buzzed hard enough to rattle a porcelain spoon off its saucer and skated half an inch toward the edge of the table.

Across the room, where Lupa was sharpening a skinning knife on a dry whetstone purely for the noise it made, one wolf-ear swivelled toward the table. “Answer the damn box or throw it in the water-butt. It’s putting my teeth on edge.”

“It’s in B-flat,” Ciaccona muttered from the window-seat, where she lay sprawled with her boots up on her lute case and her hat over her eyes. “Flat B-flat. Like a wasp in a beer jug.”

Suisui sighed—not annoyed, just the quiet, measured breath of a woman realizing the tide had finally come all the way up the steps—and set Yangyang’s mended tunic neatly beside the plate of osmanthus cakes. She picked up the Terminal, smoothed her skirt over her knees without using her fan at all, and stood.

“Excuse me for two minutes,” Suisui said. “Do not let Mei lift the kettle while I am outside.”

“I’m drawing!” Mei protested, holding up the birch bark. “Look, I didn’t even look at the kettle!”

Suisui stepped out onto the stone landing and pulled the heavy timber door to behind her, leaving it unlatched by an inch so the draft wouldn’t slam it.

Inside the storehouse, the talk started to pick back up—Cartethyia leaning across the table to ask Amy if the toothbrush hair had stood up in the rain, Lupa testing the edge of her knife against her thumb—and then Suisui’s voice came back through the one-inch gap by the hinge, and the room went quiet on its own.

It wasn’t loud. Suisui didn’t raise her pitch by half a tone.

“Zheng Ming,” she said outside on the step. “Slow down. Take your hand off the ledger and read me the date on the river-lock order.”

Silence for four beats while the tinny voice on the other end of the Mengzhou relay scrambled through paper. On the storehouse sill, the little wooden turbine turned with a soft, steady *whuff-creak*.

“No,” Suisui said. The pleasant, slightly musical lilt she used when she was folding napkins into swans or teasing Yangyang about pears had vanished completely, replaced by something as flat, clean, and hard as an iron scale-beam. “Do not sign the provisional waiver. If you sign the provisional waiver, the Huai syndicate holds the grain barges in the south basin until the ice closes the canal, and then the three northern townships pay winter cartage rates on barley they already bought in August. You did right to wait.”

Another pause. Inside the room, Amy slowly took her chin out of her hand and looked sideways at Chisa.

“Listen to me,” Suisui said through the door, and her voice softened by a fraction—not yielding an inch, just steadying a frightened boy three hundred miles away. “Put the magistrate’s clerk on the second line at the fourth bell and tell him the warehouse bond is under my personal seal, not the branch office’s. Move the first six barges to the salt-wharf behind Old Man Kang’s timber yard; Kang owes my father three winters of dredging and he won’t charge mooring before Tuesday. Leave the demurrage dispute on my desk. Do not let Master Zhao into the seal-room, even if he brings pastries. Especially if he brings pastries. I will be at the table by Friday noon to countersign it myself. Go eat your lunch, Ming-er. You’ve done well.”

The call clicked off.

For three seconds nothing moved on the step outside. Dumpling, the small round mechanical creature that had been dozing on the rafter above the drying rack, gave a tiny metallic chirp, dropped onto the top of the door, and peered through the crack.

Then the door swung open, and Suisui walked back in.

She had already put the voice away. The iron scale-beam was gone; she was just Yangyang’s older sister again in a slightly crumpled travel robe with flour dust from breakfast on her cuff. Only her folding fan, which she usually carried loose in her fingers, was shut tight in her left hand like a ruler.

She stopped at the head of the table, looked around at the eight of them staring at her, and smiled.

“Well,” Suisui said plainly. “My holiday is over. There is a Guild post-coach leaving from the lower gate tomorrow at the fifth bell. If I take it, I can make the Mengzhou river locks before the Huai syndicate decides I’ve died in the mountains.”

For half a beat nobody said a word.

Then Ciaccona pushed her hat off her nose with one finger and stared at her upside-down from the window-seat.

“Your *holiday*,” Ciaccona repeated.

“I came to Jinzhou for a week’s visit,” Suisui said with a completely straight face. “I had a lovely time.”

“You walked into a spatial fog that ate a transport,” Amy said, ticking her fingers off over her blue tie. “You played board games with a four-hundred-year-old fox for your own soul, your sister turned into a bird, Ma got a tree put through her stomach, and yesterday you bullied a restaurant owner out of a roof. What do you do when you’re *working*, rob banks?”

“Banking is a separate department,” Suisui said mildly.

“Tomorrow?” Cartethyia stood up so fast her knees bumped the underside of the table, making the teapot lid chatter. Her blue eyes were wide and stricken under her crystal laurel. “Wait—tomorrow morning? Before the sun’s even up? You can’t leave tomorrow, Suisui, Lupa hasn’t even split the wood yet, and we were going to go back to the fish lady on Wednesday!”

“Yeah, hold on,” Lupa barked, setting the whetstone down on the bench with a heavy thump. Her ears were up, her brows drawn together in genuine tactical alarm. “If you get on a cart at the fifth bell, who the hell’s gonna stop Mei from picking up barrels when my back’s turned? Who’s doing the rice math? I cook it, I don’t measure it!”

“I can measure rice,” Mei said from the floor, waving her piece of charcoal.

“You put three cups of dry millet in a two-quart pot on Thursday and it crawled out over the stove like a swamp monster,” Lupa snapped.

“It was enthusiastic millet!”

From the floorboards beside Mei’s hip, Iuno set her teacup down on the crate beside Barnaby the frog. She looked up at Suisui—not at Suisui’s smiling mouth, but at her left hand, where her knuckles were white around the bamboo spine of the closed fan.

“Three northern townships,” Iuno said quietly.

Suisui’s gaze dropped to Iuno’s for a second. She inclined her head half an inch. “Sixteen thousand people, give or take the winter herdsmen. If the barges don’t clear the lock before the hard freeze, the grain goes by mule over the ridge, and the price doubles at the pass. Little Zheng can hold the clerks off until Friday, and not a day past it. He’s a good boy, but he still blushes when a tax-farmer yells at him.”

Nobody argued with that. In this room, when someone gave you a number with people inside it, the joking stopped.

All eyes went to the middle of the bench.

Yangyang hadn’t moved since the door opened.

She was sitting with her back straight, her hands resting around her porcelain cup, her dark hair grading down into deep Xuanling blue and white-tipped down over her shoulders. She hadn’t looked up at her sister once. She was staring very hard at a knot in the pine tabletop three inches to the left of the osmanthus cake plate.

“Yangyang,” Suisui said, and for the first time all afternoon her voice lost a tiny fraction of its poise. “You’re coming up to Mengzhou as soon as the road here is settled. It’s four days by post-road. Maybe five if it snows.”

Yangyang took her hands off her teacup.

Slowly, deliberately, she placed one forefinger against the side of her porcelain bowl and pushed it two inches away from her toward the center of the table.

“Fine,” Yangyang said.

Her voice didn’t go up at the end. It didn’t go down either. It landed on the wood like a flat stone.

Suisui blinked. “...Fine?”

“Yes.” Yangyang turned her shoulders forty-five degrees to the right on the bench so she was facing the stove instead of the head of the table, crossed her ankles under her layered plumage skirt, and looked at the wall. “Have a safe trip.”

On the floor by the stove, Mei pressed both hands hard over her mouth, her eyes crinkling into golden crescents above her knuckles.

“Now wait a minute,” Suisui said, taking half a step around the corner of the table. “I am not going to the moon, Yangyang. I have four warehouses of salt-fish and a granary lock to clear, and the Guild Board has been sending couriers to Mingting twice a week asking if I’ve eloped with a bandit. What are you turning your back for?”

“I’m not turning my back,” Yangyang said to the stove. “The light from the window was in my eyes.”

“The window is behind you.”

“The reflection off the teapot.”

Before Suisui could answer, Yangyang reached out sideways without looking, bypassed the three small, polite osmanthus cakes on the near side of the porcelain dish, and plucked the largest, thickest, most heavily honeyed cake straight out of the middle of the stack—the exact piece they had been arguing over the jurisdiction of since Yangyang was seven years old.

She didn’t break it in half. She didn’t offer a corner across the wood. She took a large, steady bite out of the center, chewed, and kept staring at the stove bricks.

For two full seconds the storehouse was dead silent.

On the window-seat, Ciaccona had her fist jammed into her mouth to keep from howling. Cartethyia’s eyes bounced from sister to sister like she was watching a tennis match. By the wall, Chisa sat with her hands folded neatly in her lap below her blue tie, her face completely placid.

And Suisui stood at the corner of the table looking down at the top of her little sister’s head.

Yangyang had never once slammed a door. She had never once hogged the sweet-plate. She had been so careful, so neat, and so relentlessly un-troublesome for so long that Suisui had spent years wanting to shake her until her teeth rattled.

Now Yangyang had a streak of honey on her thumb, a mouthful of stolen pastry, and the posture of a twelve-year-old who had been told she couldn’t go to the lantern fair.

Suisui’s hand came up with the closed fan and pressed it flat against her own lips before the grin could ruin everything. Her dark eyes were shining so hard they looked wet.

“That was the middle piece,” Suisui said, trying very hard to sound stern and sounding mostly like she’d just won the provincial lottery. “It had the extra walnuts on the bottom.”

“Did it,” Yangyang said around the cake. “I didn’t notice.”

“You looked right at the dish before the door opened. You mapped it.”

“I’m off duty.”

Suisui let out a small breath through her nose, picked up her sewing basket from the corner of the table, and walked over to the low bench under the east window to pack her spools.

Nobody else said a word. Mei went dutifully back to shading her turtle-headed Amy; Lupa went back to *shhk-shhk*ing her knife on the stone; Iuno picked up her tea.

Ninety seconds later, Yangyang brushed the osmanthus crumbs off her fingers, stood up from the table, walked across the floorboards with her gaze fixed firmly on the ceiling rafters, and sat down on the east window-bench six inches from Suisui’s hip.

She didn’t look at Suisui. She kept her back turned at a strict thirty-degree angle, pulled her patrol log out of her pocket, and began reading a blank page with ferocious concentration. Only the small cerulean feathers at the left side of her collar lifted and leaned sideways until the soft tips brushed the sleeve of Suisui’s robe.

Suisui didn’t look up from winding her grey silk thread either. She just shifted her sewing basket to her other side so her elbow rested warm against Yangyang’s ribs.

***

Ten minutes before the fourth bell, someone rapped twice on the open doorframe with a bruised knuckle.

It was Warden Liangyu. She had her helmet tucked under her left arm and a clipboard of rough pine-pulp tally sheets in her right. The bandage around her forearm from the mill-yard had been replaced with a clean strip of unbleached linen, though there were dark hollows under her eyes that hadn’t been there on Thursday.

“Afternoon,” Liangyu said, nodding once to the room before her gaze found Yangyang on the window-bench. “Sorry to bother the off-watch. The noon riders just came down from the Cloud-Step shrine and the north watchtower.”

Yangyang closed her patrol log and stood up. The thirty-degree sulk vanished between the bench and the floor; she was simply a young woman with a sword on a peg and a job to do.

“Did they reach the upper pavilion?” Yangyang asked, crossing to the threshold.

“All the way to the bell-frame,” Liangyu said quietly. She shifted the tally board against her hip. “The incense burners are cold. Nobody’s touched the oil-lamps since the snow before the siege. And the two scouts we sent up the gorge trail after Paragon Qingxiao came back empty too. No tracks past the eastern boundary stone. Nobody on the high paths has heard her qin.”

At the stove, Lupa stopped her whetstone.

Nobody had seen Paragon Qingxiao since the first attacks on the gorge road, the day Liangyu lost her fellow Wardens. The Sword Transcendent had come down out of the mountains once, with her blade and her qin, and gone back up into the mist without a word.

“And the pagoda?” Iuno asked from beside the crate. She didn’t use the name either.

Liangyu shook her head once. “The water spires are running on their own weights, ma’am. The doors on the ninth tier are unbarred, and there’s frost three fingers thick across the threshold stone. When I asked the carters down at the market if anybody had seen a white fox by the ovens this week, two of them crossed their thumbs behind their backs and walked off without answering.”

Liangyu looked past the storehouse eaves, out toward the open gulf of sky above the valley.

High over the drifting rags of cloud, framed between the black karst needles, Sentinel Hsin’s Astrolabe Core turned inside its gigantic bronze calipers—celadon jade and polished brass, silent and unhurried, measuring the wind for an empty office.

“We’ve got six fit Wardens on the wall and four more with splints keeping the gate-books,” Liangyu said, not complaining, just laying it out. “We can hold the perimeter through the week if the fog stays flat. I just wanted you to have the count before dark.”

“Thank you, Liangyu,” Yangyang said gently. “I’ll take the first watch on the northeast parapet after supper. My ribs are fine for listening.”

“Wait a second, girl, don’t walk off empty-handed,” Lupa grunted from the stove. She reached into the covered warmer-crock, pulled out two fat pork-and-cabbage buns wrapped in a dock leaf, and shoved them into Liangyu’s clipboard hand before the young Warden could protest. “Eat those on the stair. If you fall over on the rampart because you skipped lunch, I’m not carrying you down.”

Liangyu flushed to her ears, bowed hastily over the buns and the helmet, and went down the steps chewing.

In the storehouse, nobody spoke for a moment.

Yangyang stayed in the doorway with her hand resting on the timber jamb, her head tilted back so she was looking straight up at the turning jade orb in the sky. The cold draft off the gorge caught the two cyan ribbons at her waist and lifted them an inch off the floorboards.

On the bench under the window, Suisui sat very still.

Her hands were folded in her lap over the sewing basket. From where Mei sat by the stove, Mei could see the exact line of Suisui’s jaw—the small muscle ticking once below her ear as she looked from her little sister’s back to the huge, exposed machine hanging over the clouds, and then down at her own closed fan.

Suisui had the Zhaoming Guild’s seal in her belt and a four-horse post-coach paid for at the lower gate. With one sentence—with three strokes of a brush on a Ministry requisition—she could have packed Yangyang into that cab tomorrow morning under medical transfer and had her behind thirty-foot granite walls in Mengzhou by Tuesday night.

Suisui didn’t say it. She kept her teeth shut on it, looked down at her spools of grey silk, and let her hands stay in her lap, because not so long ago she had played a game of weiqi on a timeless floor to prove that loving her sister didn’t mean locking her in a box where nothing could ever happen to her.

Mei watched her do it. Slowly, without making a sound, Mei reached over to the porcelain dish on the table, took the last small osmanthus cake, and set it down on the window-bench beside Suisui’s sewing basket before sitting back down against Iuno’s knee.

Suisui looked at the cake. She let out a long, quiet breath through her nose and stood up, brushing a stray thread from her skirt.

“Now,” Suisui said briskly, facing the room. “Since the coach boards at the fifth bell and the frost will be heavy on the stairs, I am going to pack my trunk before supper and go to bed early. I do not want anyone waking up in the dark on my account.”

She looked pointedly around the table—at Mei, at Cartethyia, at Lupa, at Ciaccona.

“A simple goodbye at the door tonight,” Suisui instructed them in her best boardroom voice, “or, if Yangyang insists on walking down with the lantern, a bow at the gatehouse and a cup of hot water from the guard-stove. Nothing more. I am a merchant going back to my desk, not an admiral sailing for the wars. Are we understood?”

Eight heads nodded with suspicious, immediate harmony.

“Totally,” Mei said.

“A simple bow,” Cartethyia agreed solemnly.

“Wouldn’t dream of getting up before dawn for a clerk,” Lupa snorted, turning back to her stove.

Suisui narrowed her eyes at them for three seconds, decided she had too many freight manifests to sort in her spare room to investigate, and went into the side pantry to fetch her lacquered travel trunk.

The instant the pantry door clicked shut, the storehouse went into motion like a thieves’ guild at a fair.

“Okay,” Mei whispered loudly, scrambling up off the floorboards with one hand pressed to her middle while Iuno automatically caught her elbow to steady her. “Five bells means four-thirty at the gate. What have we got?”

***

By dusk, Suisui knew with complete certainty that a conspiracy was occurring, primarily because nobody in the household was capable of whispering below the volume of a stage play.

At the fifth bell, while Suisui sat at the small writing desk in the alcove checking the Mengzhou river-lock figures by lamplight, she heard Lupa and Wen the cook having a ferocious argument in the scullery behind the chimney wall.

“That’s a two-gallon willow hamper, girl!” Wen hissed, accompanied by the heavy *thump* of stoneware hitting wicker. “A mail-coach has twelve inches of clearance under the bench! Where’s she supposed to put her feet, out the window?!”

“She can put her feet on the chili jar! It’s flat on top!” Lupa’s voice boomed right through the mortar before dropping into what Lupa clearly believed was a stealthy mutter. “And don’t skimp on the salt-duck! You know what they serve at the Halfway Pine relay? Boiled cabbage water and buns you could use to shingle a barn! Give me that waxed cloth—no, the big square, I’m wrapping six of the smoked ribs in case the horses throw a shoe in the pass!”

“Six ribs is half a pig!”

“She’s skinny! All these city girls eat like sparrows unless you shove it in their pockets!”

Suisui dipped her brush in the inkstone, wrote *Six barges — South Wharf* in the margin of her ledger, and pretended not to hear the unmistakable sound of Lupa sitting on the hamper lid to force the wooden toggle through the loop.

Twenty minutes later, when Suisui stepped out to the rain barrel behind the kitchen to rinse her ink-brush, she caught Cartethyia crouched behind the cordwood stack with Old Man Zhao from the lower terrace and young Qingyan, the boy with his sister’s long Warden sword strapped across his back.

Cartethyia was trying to whisper, which meant she was hissing like a steam-pipe while gesturing with both arms.

“—at the fourth bell sharp, Master Zhao!” Cartethyia breathed urgently. “A Director of the Commerce Guild is equivalent to a High Proctor of the Ragunnesi Treasury! In Egla Town, when the Proctor visited the granaries, the baker’s guild marched ahead with peeled willow wands and two geese on blue ribbons! Now, Sir Honk already knows the stairs—”

“Sir Honk bit the blacksmith’s dog this afternoon, Miss Knight,” Old Man Zhao said reasonably around his pipe. “He’s in a mood.”

“That is martial spirit! Bring a pocketful of the hemp-seed mash so he stays in formation! And Qingyan, you walk on the left flank with the lantern on the pole—not the hand-lantern, the tall one from the gatehouse, so the light falls across the frost before her boots touch it!”

Qingyan nodded with terrifying seriousness, his hand resting on the worn lacquer throat of his sister’s scabbard. “Warden Liangyu said I can sign out the long pole if I bring it back before muster. Should I wear the dress sash?”

“Definitely the dress sash!”

Suisui rinsed her brush in the cold water, tapped the ferrule dry against the rim of the barrel, and walked back inside before Cartethyia’s crystal laurel—which was sticking six inches up over the top of the woodpile—could turn around and spot her.

In the main room, Ciaccona was sitting cross-legged on the upturned flour crate by the door with her lute across her thigh and her tongue wedged in the corner of her mouth.

*Plink-plank-plunk.*

“No,” Ciaccona muttered to the ceiling, damping the strings with her palm. “Too much minor third. Sounds like we’re burying her at sea. If I move the cadence up to the fifth—”

She struck a bright, rolling chord that made Dumpling’s ears spin on the rafter, sang two bars under her breath about *the silver abacus of the eastern road*, and immediately scowled.

“Ugh. Worse. Now she sounds like a tax-collector riding a donkey. Wait—what’s that Mingting opera tune she sang in the bathhouse on Friday? The one about the magistrate’s daughter and the ferry?”

From the window-bench, without looking up from polishing her scabbard with an oiled rag, Yangyang spoke. “*The Peach-Blossom Ford.* You’re playing the fourth bar in the wrong key. It goes up on the river-turn, not down.”

“Everyone in this province is a music critic,” Ciaccona groaned, though she shifted her fingers two frets up the neck and tried it again, her brow clearing as the melody caught.

Suisui walked past her toward the trunk in the pantry. As she passed, Ciaccona clapped both hands over the strings, whistled three tuneless notes at the ceiling, and said, “Lovely weather we’re having, Director. Just checking the bridge tension.”

“It goes up on the river-turn, Ciaccona,” Suisui said pleasantly, and went into the pantry.

She didn’t even make it to her trunk.

Mei was already in there. She had both hands clamped around the brass lifting-rings of Suisui’s heavy cedar-and-lacquer travel chest, her knees bent, her tongue between her teeth as she prepared to heave forty pounds of books and winter robes off the floor.

“Mei,” said three voices at once.

Iuno didn’t even have to raise her voice; her hand simply appeared over Mei’s shoulder and closed on the back of Mei’s collar like a crane hook. At the same instant, Lupa kicked the scullery door open with a wooden ladle in her fist, and Amy leaned in from the passage and hooked Mei’s right ankle with the toe of her boot.

“I was just testing the balance!” Mei yelped, letting go of the brass rings immediately and holding both palms up in the air. “Just checking if it was heavy! For science!”

“You have stitches for three more days,” Iuno said mildly, towing her backward out of the pantry by the scruff of her shirt. “And if you tear one before Monday, Suisui will miss her coach helping me sit on you.”

“Out,” Lupa ordered, pointing the ladle at the stove corner. “Go fold something that weighs less than an ounce.”

Mei retreated to the floor beside the hearth with a long-suffering sigh, pulling a stack of stiff square linen napkins into her lap. “Fine. I’m making the road-swans. Every high-class hamper needs swans.”

She spent the next half-hour folding them by the orange light of the firebox, squinting fiercely as she tucked corners and pinched pleats. Every time she finished one and set it proudly on the flagstone beside Barnaby the frog, Chisa—who was sitting at the end of the bench quietly running the stitching-edge of her giant scissor-blade along the frayed leather shoulder-strap of Suisui’s document satchel so the rough seam wouldn’t catch the silk of Suisui’s right sleeve—tilted her head a fraction at the sound of the stiff linen creaking.

“You folded the neck under twice,” Chisa told her without looking up from the leather. “That’s a shell.”

“It’s a swan tucking its head against the wind!” Mei defended it, setting a fourth square paper-and-linen creature beside the first three. Every single one of them had four stubby corner-feet, a round humped back, and a little triangle nose poking out the front.

Then Amy clapped her hands together in the middle of the floor and announced that the light was dying.

“Up, up, everybody with feathers or a lamp, outside on the step!” Amy barked, suddenly transforming from a girl in a borrowed sheepskin vest into the director of a hundred-thousand-listener broadcast with four minutes to air-time. She had her Terminal out in one hand and Suisui’s small pocket-camera—the silver-plated Huaxu box Suisui had brought from Mingting to take pictures for their mother—in the other. “Suisui’s leaving at five, which means if Ma and Ba in Mingting are getting proof that Yangyang didn’t turn into a pigeon, we are shooting the plates *right now* while there’s still bounce-light off the snow!”

On the window-bench, Yangyang froze with her oiled rag halfway down her scabbard.

Her ears went bright pink under her hat brim. “A photograph is not required. Suisui can describe them.”

“Describe them? To your mother?” Amy marched over, grabbed Yangyang by both wrists, and hauled her to her feet before Yangyang could hook her boot around the bench leg. “Have you met moms? If Suisui goes home and says ‘Yangyang grew blue feathers on her neck,’ your mom’s gonna think she’s got mountain fungus! Out on the step! Carte, grab the two brass oil-lamps from the wall! Ma, you’re holding the tin wash-basin at a forty-five-degree angle by the boot-scraper to bounce the fill-light under her chin! Cia, I need wind!”

“I am a bard of the Toccata family, not a bellows,” Ciaccona protested, already getting up with her lute to fan the air.

For the next fifteen minutes, the stone step outside the storehouse looked like a roadside opera troupe having a nervous breakdown.

Yangyang was planted on the three-legged stool right in front of the turning wooden turbine, her hat confiscated and placed in Suisui’s lap. Cartethyia stood on the left holding two oil-lamps high enough to singe the eaves, beaming with pride; Mei sat cross-legged in the frost on the right tilting the polished tin wash-basin so a bright circle of yellow light hit Yangyang’s nose; and Ciaccona stood three paces off swinging her green cloak like a matador to make Yangyang’s twin cyan tail-ribbons flutter on cue.

Yangyang sat on the stool with her knees together, her spine as rigid as an iron ramrod, and the exact expression of a barn owl that had been dunked in a horse-trough.

“Relax your shoulders!” Amy yelled, crouching in the snow at the bottom of the steps with her blue tie swinging over her vest and one eye screwed shut behind the brass viewfinder. “Yangyang, you look like you’re being court-martialled! Smile! Think about pear cake!”

“I am thinking about pear cake,” Yangyang said through unmoving teeth.

“Then you’re thinking about dropping it in the dirt! Cia, more cloak on the left! Ma, tilt the basin up two inches, her collar’s casting a shadow—no, not in her eyes, now she’s squinting—”

“The basin’s cold!” Mei laughed, shifting her grip with her mittens on. “Wait, Yangyang, do the thing with your elbow from the woodyard! Like a martial arts poster!”

“I will not,” Yangyang said, her cerulean ruff puffing out a full inch around her chin in sheer mortification as two off-duty Wardens walking past with a bucket of coal stopped dead in the lane to stare.

*Click-whir.*

The magnesium shutter snapped once, twice, three times, catching the deep ink-to-sky gradient of her hair, the white down at the tips, and the faint blue warmth of the mark on her forehead.

“Okay, that’s three for the family archive where she looks like a very pretty hostage,” Amy announced, winding the film-spool with a brisk *ratchet-ratchet*. She didn’t lower the camera. Instead, she pointed the lens straight at Suisui, who was standing in the doorway holding Yangyang’s hat and laughing into her sleeve. “Get in there, Director. One more on the roll.”

Suisui lowered her sleeve, startled. “Me? Mother knows what I look like. And my hair hasn’t been combed since the third bell—”

“Sit on the arm, or I tell the whole lane you sang flat on the terrace on Thursday!”

Suisui opened her mouth to argue, caught Lupa grinning from the window, and gave up. She stepped out onto the flagstone, didn’t bother checking her hairpins, and leaned down behind the three-legged stool, wrapping both arms loosely around Yangyang’s shoulders and resting her chin squarely on the top of Yangyang’s blue-black crown so the two matching forehead marks were three inches apart.

Yangyang’s hands, which had been clenched on her knees for ten minutes, let go of her skirt. Her right hand came up and held onto Suisui’s forearm.

She forgot to look like an owl. Her mouth tipped up at the corner, and her voice went up at the end:

“Your chin is cold, Jie.”

“Hold still, comedian,” Suisui murmured into her hair, and laughed right as Amy tripped the shutter.

***

By the tenth bell, the magnesium smoke had cleared off the step, the massive willow hamper sat lashed and padlocked beside the pantry door like a captured boulder, and the main room had gone dim and warm around the pallets.

Suisui didn’t go to bed.

She stood out on the stone landing in her wool cloak with a cup of hot water in both hands, watching the mist creep up from the lower terraces under the stars. On the sill beside her elbow, the little wooden turbine turned with its slow, companionable creak.

The latch lifted behind her. The jagged crystal at Iuno’s left shoulder caught the faint orange line from the stove before the door closed again.

Iuno stepped out onto the flagstone, her own coat draped over her shoulders and her dented steel flask tucked under her one arm. She didn’t ask if Suisui was cold; she simply leaned her hip against the rail beside Suisui’s and looked out at the dark karsts.

For a minute neither of them said anything. Down in the lower west lane, a dog barked twice, and very faintly, four terraces away, Corporal Honk answered with a single indignant *honk* in his sleep.

“My courier says the certified copy reached the Septimont chancery in one piece,” Suisui said quietly to the mist. “He rode two horses into the lather between the coast and the relay tower to do it.”

“I know,” Iuno said. “Augusta called me before dawn on Tuesday. She graded my handwriting.”

Suisui’s mouth curved over her cup. “Did the descender hook left?”

“She noticed.”

“Anyone with eyes would notice, Iuno. You write like a magistrate who’s just discovered she has a heart.” Suisui took a small sip of her hot water and looked sideways at her. “We had an agreement. One certified copy, in exchange for a seat in the room on Tuesday.”

Iuno’s right hand slid into the deep pocket of her wool coat. Inside, next to the block of yellow soap she’d forgotten to take out after the wash-house, her fingers brushed the small square velvet box—and inside it, the ring cut and polished from a shard of her own shoulder crystal, waiting for a day that hadn’t been named yet.

“Tuesday isn’t set,” Iuno said plainly. “Tomorrow is Monday, and Mei still can’t bend over to tie her left boot without making a noise like a stepped-on frog. I am not proposing to a woman who can’t jump without tearing her abdomen open. She would jump, and then Lupa would kill us both.”

*And rightly so, darling,* the High Priestess murmured inside her head, resting her chin on a phantom hand. *When we do it, we are doing it when she can spin us around the room without bleeding on the hem.*

*Be quiet,* Iuno thought.

“And when it is set,” Suisui said, “I will be three hundred miles north arguing with the Huai syndicate over barley tariffs.” She looked genuinely put out about it—not the Guild Director losing a clause, just a friend who had helped bake the cake being told she was going to miss the party. “I wanted to see her face.”

“She already knows,” Iuno said dryly. “She asks me what day of the week it is every morning before she opens her eyes. Subtlety is not among her virtues.”

“Then I wanted to see *your* face,” Suisui said frankly. “Which is much rarer.”

Iuno looked at her. In the pale starlight off the snow, Suisui’s dark eyes were clear and tired and entirely direct.

“Augusta has first notice,” Iuno said. “She made me swear to it before she hung up. She’s bringing the pen herself.”

“The Ephor of Septimont is a formidable competitor,” Suisui allowed, tapping one fingernail against the porcelain rim of her cup. “I will accept second. On one condition.”

“Which is.”

“I do not want a two-line telegram saying *Completed as scheduled,*” Suisui said, turning to face her properly on the step. “I get forty of those a day from barge captains. I want a letter, in your own hand, posted on the Wednesday coach. I want to know what she was wearing, whether she interrupted you before you finished the third sentence, and whether you dropped the box.”

Iuno’s eyebrows rose a fraction of an inch. “I have never dropped anything in a ceremony in my life.”

“You only have one hand now, Iuno, and she makes you nervous,” Suisui said gently. “Write it all down. I’ll keep it in the back of the Mingting safe with Yangyang’s kite-strings.”

For a second the cold air on the step felt very quiet.

Iuno took her hand out of her coat pocket, unscrewed the cap of her dented thermal flask against her knee—much faster now that she’d practiced it—and tipped a splash of hot, strong bergamot tea into Suisui’s cup of plain water so the steam came up smelling of citrus and southern gardens.

“Wednesday coach,” Iuno said. “In writing. Though if she wears the frog in her pocket while I do it, I am omitting the frog.”

“If you omit the frog, I’ll know you’re lying, and I’ll ask Amy for the photographs.” Suisui lifted the cup, breathed in the Earl Grey, and smiled at her over the rim. “Thank you, Iuno. For keeping an eye on my sister while I’m at the locks.”

“Your sister,” Iuno said dryly, “is currently keeping an eye on all eight of us, and sulking at you with the structural discipline of a siege engine. Drink your tea and go to bed, Director.”

***

Suisui woke on Monday morning a quarter of an hour before the fifth bell, before the first grey had even touched the cracks in the shutters.

She woke the way she always did on road-days: all at once, her mind already running the checklist before her feet found the slippers. Boil the kettle first so the water was hot when Yangyang’s ribs woke up stiff; put two coals on the side-trivet to warm Mei’s mug; check the latch on the camellia box so the draft didn’t nip Cartethyia’s leaves; fold one clean napkin beside the porridge pot so Lupa didn’t wipe her hands on her apron; strap her own trunk last.

She pushed the quilt back without making a sound, slipped her arms into her dressing-robe, and stepped out into the main room of the storehouse.

She stopped two paces past the curtain.

Every single lamp in the room was lit.

The stove wasn’t banked; it was roaring like a blacksmith’s forge, throwing a wave of toasted-sesame and ginger heat across the floorboards. Nobody was on the pallets. Every blanket had already been rolled—some into neat quarters, some into Lupa-sized cannonballs—and stacked against the wall.

Over by the pantry, Suisui’s heavy cedar-and-lacquer travel trunk stood closed and strapped.

Lupa was kneeling on top of it with one knee, hauling a leather cinch-belt tight with her teeth while her tail thumped a satisfied beat against the floor.

“Wait—Lupa—” Suisui started, taking a step toward her trunk. “My silk ledgers go on the left side, if you put the heavy inkstones on top of the—”

“Inkstones are in the bottom corner wrapped in three pairs of your wool drawers,” Lupa said around the leather strap, not letting go. She hopped off the lid and patted the brass lock with a scarred palm. “And I put a two-pound smoked ham and a spare iron axle-pin between the shirts so the books don’t slide around on the hairpins. You can drop this thing off the cliff and the ink won’t even slosh.”

“You put a *ham* in my shirts?”

“It’s double-wrapped in bladder-skin! It won’t grease!”

Suisui opened her mouth, decided she could unpack the ham at the first relay station, and turned toward the stove to put the kettle on for tea.

Her fingers were six inches from the iron handle when a sheer sky-blue sleeve came in from her left and lifted the kettle off the trivet before she could touch it.

Yangyang didn’t look at her. She was fully dressed for the cold, her cerulean collar neat against her throat, Dumpling perched sleepily on the shoulder of her cloak. With her face completely grave, Yangyang poured boiling water into Suisui’s own floral-painted porcelain cup—already warmed with hot rinse-water, the exact way Suisui did it—and placed the cup squarely into Suisui’s empty right hand.

“The water has been boiling since the quarter-bell,” Yangyang said. “If you touch the iron handle without the cloth, you’ll blister your thumb before you have to use the Guild seal.”

“Yangyang, I have been pouring tea since you were—”

“Sit,” said Mei.

Suisui turned around with the teacup in her hand and found her knees gently, inexorably ambushed from behind by the wooden three-legged stool, which Mei was sliding into place with her right boot while keeping both hands strictly above waist-height.

“Zero lifting!” Mei beamed, patting the seat of the stool before stepping back and pulling a wooden tray across the corner of the table with two fingers. On the tray sat a steaming bowl of millet porridge dotted with red jujubes, two golden scallion pancakes already torn into bite-sized triangles so Suisui wouldn’t get oil on her cuffs, and, standing proudly beside the spoon, a stiff linen napkin folded into a squat, four-legged turtle with a triangle head. “Look, I made you a swan! Because presentation is half of nutrition!”

Suisui stared down at the linen turtle. Then she looked up at Mei’s shining golden eyes, then at Yangyang already placing the porcelain spoon into her left hand so both her palms were occupied and she couldn’t stand up.

“That,” Suisui said faintly, “is a tortoise, Mei.”

“He’s a Mingting water-swan! Very rare! Eat your pancake before it gets cold!”

Suisui tried to set the spoon down so she could at least go behind the curtain and pin her hair up before the driver saw her.

She didn’t get the spoon halfway to the tray.

Iuno materialized at her right elbow with the bone comb and the little dish of silver-and-pearl hairpins in her one hand; at the exact same second, Amy popped up on her left in her blue school tie holding Suisui’s heavy fur-lined travel cloak, which was radiating such a blast of dry heat that she had clearly been holding it flat against the warm chimney-bricks for twenty minutes.

“Chin level, Director,” Iuno said mildly, setting the pin-dish on the corner of the table and gathering the long, dark sweep of Suisui’s hair over one wrist with practiced, one-handed precision. “Do not turn your head to yell at the wolf, or I will pull.”

“Wait—Iuno, I always do the double twist on the left—” Suisui protested, trying to reach up with her free hand and finding her wrist immediately intercepted by Amy, who shoved a hot roasted sweet potato straight into Suisui’s fingers like a plug.

“Hold the potato, it’s for your pockets,” Amy ordered, peering critically at the neckline of Suisui’s robe and reaching in to straighten the inner silk collar with two brisk tugs. “And Chisa fixed the strap on your document bag with her big scissors so it won’t chew up your sleeve anymore, and she put a hot stone wrapped in flannel in the bottom of the foot-sack, so don’t kick the bag when you get in the cab or you’ll break your toe.”

From the end of the bench, Chisa nodded once over her own teacup. “It will stay warm until the third relay. After that, put it under the seat by the lantern flue.”

Suisui sat pinned to the three-legged stool with a porcelain teacup in one hand, a scalding sweet potato in the other, Iuno deftly weaving river-pearls into her chignon from behind, Yangyang silently placing a peeled boiled egg on top of her porridge so she wouldn’t have to shell it, and Mei nudging the linen turtle half an inch closer to her elbow with an encouraging grin.

Every single time Suisui twitched a muscle to do a job—to pass Cartethyia the salt, to wipe a drop of tea off the wood, to straighten the crooked ribbon on Yangyang’s cuff—four hands beat her to it and put another piece of food in her way.

In every Guild house in three provinces, Suisui was the one standing behind the chairs. She was the one who noticed the cold draft, the empty cup, the girl who hadn’t eaten her greens. Nobody fussed over the Director. People brought the Director ledgers and waited to be told where to stand.

She looked at the peeled egg sitting crookedly on her millet. She looked at Mei’s absurd four-legged linen turtle staring up at her from the tray, and at Yangyang standing two feet away with her arms crossed, pretending she hadn’t just warmed the spoon in hot water first so the metal wouldn’t shock her sister’s teeth.

Suisui’s shoulders started to shake.

“Hold still,” Iuno warned around a pearl hairpin between her teeth. “I am at the structural pin.”

Suisui couldn’t hold still.

It came up out of the middle of her chest before she could get her sleeve up to hide it—not the polite, musical chime she used at banquets with the Salt Commissioner, but the big, loud, completely unladylike guffaw that had once made her boarding-school tutor drop an inkwell, booming right out across the kitchen until her head tipped back against Iuno’s wrist and she had to set the sweet potato down on the tray to hold onto the edge of the table with both hands.

“Oh, stop!” Suisui gasped, laughing so hard tears squeezed out the corners of her eyes and ran down into her collar. “Stop, all of you, I can’t—I have two hands! I am not an infant Emperor! Lupa put a ham in my underwear and Mei made me a turtle!”

“It’s a *swan*!” Mei howled, laughing right along with her.

In the corner by the stove, Lupa’s ears went flat back against her skull, her neck flushing dull red as she glared fiercely into the soup pot. “Wasn’t in your underwear! It was between the petticoats! Eat the damn egg before I feed it to the bird!”

Even Yangyang’s mouth gave way, a quick, hopeless smile breaking across her face as she reached out with one finger and caught the teacup before Suisui’s shaking hand could tip it over the tortoise.

“You have twelve minutes, Jie,” Yangyang said softly. “Eat.”

Suisui wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist—completely forgetting to use a napkin—picked up her spoon, and ate every bite they put in front of her.

***

If Suisui had harboured any lingering hope that the household would come to its senses once they hit the freezing air outside, it died at the top of the main staircase.

It was still dark—that thick, iron-blue quarter-hour before first light when the frost on the stone steps crunches like sugar under a boot and the breath hangs white in the lantern-cones.

Waiting at the top of the second terrace landing stood Old Man Zhao, young Qingyan with a seven-foot gatehouse pole-lantern held at rigid parade rest, and, standing square in the middle of the paving stones with his chest puffed out like a feathered barrel, Sir Honk.

Someone—almost certainly Cartethyia—had tied a fresh sky-blue silk sash around the goose’s base-neck in a double bow, and his single black tail-feather had been brushed with lard so it stood straight up in the gloom like a dragoon’s plume.

“Fall in!” Cartethyia whispered at the top of her lungs, already trotting down to the landing. She looked at Qingyan’s single pole-lantern, looked down the long, shadowy flight of three hundred steps winding through the sleeping Hold to the lower gate, and shook her head. “No, no, the shadows are too deep by the dyer’s wall! A Director of the Realm cannot descend in half-light! Stand back, Master Zhao!”

“Carte, don’t you dare—” Ciaccona started, reaching for her hem.

Too late.

Golden light bloomed silently across the frosted terrace, turning the icicles under the eaves into rows of diamonds. Out of the radiance rose Fleurdelys, nine feet tall from her silver-white braids to her armored greaves, her crown of crystal thorns casting a soft, cathedral glow twenty paces in every direction.

She bent down, took Qingyan’s seven-foot pole-lantern between her thumb and forefinger as if it were a candlestick, lifted a second iron brazier-basket off its wall-hook with her other hand, and held them both out at shoulder height so the entire staircase was lit like high noon in a basilica.

“*THE ESCORT IS FORMED,*” Fleurdelys announced in her deep, echoing chapel-ceiling voice, which rolled down the stone canyon of the sleeping terraces and bounced off four separate warehouse roofs. “*ADVANCE THE VANGUARD.*”

*HONK.*

Sir Honk dropped his head, spread his wings three inches for balance on the ice, and began waddling briskly down the center of the stairs at a steady military clip, Old Man Zhao tossing three hemp-seeds ahead of him every six steps to keep him on the bearing.

Suisui stopped dead at the top of the stair with her fur cloak around her ears.

She put one gloved hand flat over her forehead, covering her eyes completely.

“I have to do business in this town again in the spring,” Suisui told her own palm. “The garrison commander’s window is forty paces to the left.”

“He sleeps like a log,” Lupa grunted cheerfully, walking past her with the cedar-and-lacquer trunk balanced effortlessly on her left shoulder and the monstrous two-gallon willow hamper wedged under her right elbow. “Move your feet, Guild lady, the ham’s heavy.”

Then Ciaccona struck the first chord on her lute.

She didn’t play it softly. She hit the G-mixolydian turnaround of *The Peach-Blossom Ford* with a bright, ringing double-strum that cut right through the frost, pitched her voice into her sun-through-clouds stage register, and began singing an entirely unauthorized second verse about *the Director of the Twelve Tarpaulins riding north with an abacus of gold and a sister made of feathers.*

Shutters started banging open before they cleared the third terrace.

First came the lutist from the tavern by the dyer’s, sticking his head out in a wool nightcap to yell, “Hey! That goes up on the fourth bar!”

“I went up on the fourth bar, you deaf turnip!” Ciaccona sang back in perfect rhythm without missing a single downbeat.

At the turn by the public wash-house, the barrel-chested washerwoman from Saturday leaned over her balcony rail in a quilted jacket and threw a paper twist of still-warm roasted chestnuts straight down into Amy’s hands, shouting, “For the sister of the bird! Don’t let the Mengzhou clerks short-weight your coal!”

Halfway down the lower west lane—right past the three houses whose roofs shone dark and tight under their fresh double-weight Ministry canvas—the Chen grandmother was waiting by her gatepost with a clay pot of hot ginger-sugar water and a ladle, while all three Chen kids ran alongside Fleurdelys’s knees cheering for Sir Honk as if he were the Emperor’s white horse.

“Drink a ladle for the road, Director!” the old woman called out, shoving a steaming wooden dipper past Mei’s elbow into Suisui’s hands while the whole ridiculous column ground to a halt to let the goose finish a pile of hemp-mash on the cobbles. “The wind comes straight down the pipe once you pass the boundary stone! We didn’t have a single leak in the back room last night!”

Suisui stood in the middle of the lane in the blazing light of a nine-foot saint’s lanterns, holding a wooden ladle of ginger water while a bard sang her audit records to the tune of a Mingting love ballad and a goose in a blue bow tried to untie her bootlace.

Her cheeks were bright pink from the frost and the spectacle. She didn’t have her fan out. She drank the ginger water down to the bottom, handed the dipper back with a bow low enough for a viceroy’s mother, and when she straightened up and caught Mei grinning at her from beside Iuno’s shoulder, Suisui didn’t even pretend to roll her eyes.

“If Ciaccona sings that verse inside the Mengzhou city walls,” Suisui told Mei as they started down the last fifty steps toward the gatehouse, “I will have her arrested for disturbing the peace, and then I will hire her for the Guild’s autumn banquet.”

*She is enjoying this,* the High Priestess observed inside Iuno’s head, watching Suisui’s chin come up as the gate-guards snapped to bewildered attention. *She has the soul of an arch-prelate, darling. Every woman who claims to want a quiet bow at the door secretly wants two torches and a brass section.*

*We did not want a brass section when we left,* Iuno thought back.

*We got a wolf and a cart with a bad wheel. It amounts to the same thing.*

***

At the bottom of the lower gate, where the paved post-road ran out under the great stone arch into the valley mist, the Zhaoming Guild coach was waiting.

Its four shaggy mountain horses blew long plumes of steam through their nose-guards. The driver—a weathered man in a greased sheepskin coat who had driven the Mengzhou mail for twenty winters—was standing by the iron footboard knocking the ashes out of his pipe when the procession rounded the corner.

He looked at the nine-foot glowing maiden lowering two braziers to the frost before shrinking back into a five-foot girl in a crooked laurel. He looked at the giant white goose coming to a halt beside his front wheel and honking once at his lead mare. He looked at Lupa marching up to the boot with a trunk on one shoulder and a wicker hamper the size of a dog-kennel under her arm.

The driver slowly put his pipe back in his mouth, unsmoked.

“The axle limit on a mountain mail-cab,” the driver told the sky, “is four hundredweight.”

“Trunk’s forty, basket’s thirty, girl’s ninety soaking wet,” Lupa said, tossing the trunk onto the rear rack and strapping it down with two jerks that made the leaf-springs groan before she yanked the cab door open and wedged the willow hamper under the forward bench with a kick of her freshly nailed heel. “You’ve got two hundredweight to spare, grandpa. Don’t hit the ruts by the third milestone or you’ll crack the chili jar.”

While Cartethyia solemnly untied Sir Honk’s sash so he wouldn’t get it caught in the spokes, and Amy shoved the warm paper twist of chestnuts into the side pocket of the cab door alongside Chisa’s mended document satchel, the crowd around the footboard thinned back half a pace.

Nobody called out to do it. Lupa simply hooked Cartethyia’s cloak-collar and steered her two yards toward the horses’ noses to feed them turnip ends, and Iuno laid two fingers on Mei’s sleeve, pausing her by the rear wheel.

That left the two sisters by the open door of the cab.

The sky over the eastern ridge was turning from iron-blue to the pale, cold colour of fish-belly. Up in the Hold behind them, the fifth bell struck once from the watchtower, the bronze note rolling out over the gorge.

Yangyang stood in front of the iron step. She wasn’t looking at the stove anymore, and her shoulders weren’t turned. Her hands hung plain at her sides below her sheer gradient sleeves, and the small cerulean feathers at her throat rose and fell with her breathing in the frost.

“The third relay station at Pine Creek changed innkeepers in the autumn,” Yangyang said in her quiet, exact voice. “Chixia told me before the masts went down. The new man waters the tea if you don’t ask for the iron pot from the back stove, and the bathhouse wall next to the stable has a gap in the boarding. Don’t sing the third act of *The Red Pearl* in the tub, or the ostlers will hear you.”

Suisui looked at her little sister. Her hands, warm inside her fur cuffs, came out and straightened the sit of Yangyang’s collar by a sixteenth of an inch—the one adjustment nobody had stopped her from making all morning.

“I do not sing in public bathhouses,” Suisui said, her voice thick and entirely steady.

“You sang at the Huaxu hostel in April. Baizhi heard you from the dispensary.”

“Baizhi has no ear for theatre.” Suisui’s fingers lingered for a beat against the soft blue feathers at Yangyang’s collarbone, right below the old scar and the healing ribs. “Write to me when the river freezes. Not a postcard about a cart. Tell me if the ribs stop aching when it rains, and tell me if the Ministry clerks give you any trouble over the Watcher stipend so I can make their lives unpleasant.”

“I’ll write on Sunday,” Yangyang promised. “And tell Ma the blue in my hair doesn’t wash out with soap. I tried twice so she wouldn’t fret.”

“She will fret for ten minutes and then brag about it to the magistrate’s wife for ten years,” Suisui said.

Then Suisui’s smile faded into something quiet and serious.

She reached into her wide right sleeve—past the cuff Chisa had mended—and drew out a heavy, four-inch square bundle wrapped in three layers of thick ivory Mingting silk. Even through the cloth, in the grey light before dawn, it held a faint, cool density, like a piece of the moon taken down out of the water and folded into a pocket.

Sentinel Hsin’s Prism.

Suisui didn’t unwrap it. She took Yangyang’s right hand, turned it palm-up between them, and pressed the silk-wrapped weight squarely into her sister’s fingers, closing Yangyang’s hand over it with both of hers.

Yangyang looked down at the silk. Her breath caught once. “Jie. You won this.”

“I won *you*,” Suisui said plainly. “The stone was just the receipt.”

She glanced up for half a second past the gatehouse battlements, where the Astrolabe Core turned high and lonely in the lightening sky, and then looked straight back into Yangyang’s eyes.

“I am going to a counting-house to argue with fat men about barley,” Suisui said. “You are staying in a fortress whose landlord left the door unlocked and walked into the fog. It doesn’t balance a ledger, Yangyang, and it’s heavy in a sleeve. Keep it for me until I come back down the road, or until we all meet in Mengzhou.”

Yangyang’s fingers tightened around the silk square. She didn’t try to hand it back, and she didn’t make a speech. She just slid the Prism deep into the inner pocket of her own tunic, right beside the folded letters she had carried across the dark on her breastbone, and nodded once.

“I’ll keep it,” Yangyang said.

“And New Year in Mingting,” Suisui added, lifting her chin half an inch as she stepped back toward the footboard. “Twenty-eighth of the twelfth month. Both of us at the gate before the dumplings go in the water. That was the condition.”

“Neither of us will be late,” Yangyang said.

Suisui turned half-round at the cab step and looked past Yangyang’s shoulder at Mei, who was standing by the rear wheel with her hands tucked dutifully into her coat pockets and her scarf wound three times around her chin.

Suisui didn’t lower her voice. She didn’t need to; everyone here already knew the shape of the house.

“My name is on the first line of the fish-lady’s book, Mei,” Suisui said frankly. “And for the next three weeks I am four days up the post-road. You’re on the second line.”

Mei didn’t grin, and she didn’t make a joke about the carp. She took her right hand out of her pocket—the blurry grey star from Amy’s pen still visible at the base of her thumb—and rested her palm lightly against the back of Yangyang’s sleeve without pulling Yangyang an inch away from her sister.

“Keep warm,” Mei quoted back, her voice plain and quiet in the frost. “The stove doesn’t go out in our room, Suisui. I’ve got the plate.”

Suisui looked at the two of them standing side by side in the grey light, nodded once, and put her foot on the iron step of the cab.

Up on the shoulder of Suisui’s fur cloak, Dumpling gave two sharp, metallic clicks.

The little round mechanism spun on its brass feet, looked inside the dark leather interior of the coach, and then looked back down at Yangyang standing on the frosted road below the open sky. With a tiny whir of internal springs, Dumpling launched itself off Suisui’s collar, cleared the three-foot gap in a neat arc, and landed squarely on Yangyang’s left shoulder—tucking its metal legs against the warm cerulean feathers of her ruff and clicking once as if locking its brakes.

Suisui paused in the doorway of the cab and looked back over her shoulder at the traitor.

“I fed you pine-oil for a week,” Suisui told the machine.

Dumpling chirped once and did not move.

“He knows who forgets to carry a lantern on the stairs,” Yangyang said, raising two fingers to steady the little creature against her neck.

Suisui shook her head, stepped inside the cab, and let Lupa slam the heavy wooden door shut with a solid *clunk* that rattled the window-glass.

“All clear behind!” the driver shouted, cracking his whip six inches above the lead mare’s ears.

The four horses leaned into their collars; the iron-shod wheels bit into the frozen ruts with a long, high groan; Sir Honk let out one final, deafening *HONK* of official dismissal that made the post-guard jump out of his boots; and the Mengzhou coach rolled out under the stone arch of the lower gate.

***

Inside the swaying cab, Suisui sat very straight by the window until the road curved around the first limestone buttress and hid the gatehouse from view.

Then, all at once, she let go.

She let her spine sink all the way back into the faded horsehair squabs. She uncrossed her ankles, kicked both slippers off inside the fur foot-sack, and planted her stockinged heels squarely on top of the flannel-wrapped river-stone Chisa had tucked into the straw—which was radiating a deep, steady, glorious heat that went straight up her calves.

The cab smelled of old leather, mountain cold, and, drifting unmistakably up from the massive willow hamper wedged under the opposite seat, Lupa’s garlic-and-chili crisp.

Suisui reached down and unbuckled the mended strap of her leather document satchel to check her Mengzhou notes before the road got too bumpy to read.

Her fingers didn’t touch ledger paper first.

Wedged right into the front gusset of the bag, where she kept her bone seal-knife, was a stiff rectangle of pasted pink cardboard: the theatre ticket stub from Thursday afternoon when she, Iuno, and Ciaccona had gone to see the travelling puppet-opera on the second terrace.

Suisui pulled it out. Across the blank back of the ticket, in Ciaccona’s sprawling, ink-blotted hand, someone had drawn a five-line musical staff with the four bars of *The Peach-Blossom Ford* where the melody went up on the river-turn, and underneath it, in Iuno’s crisp, left-hooked chancery script: *Second notice. Wednesday post. Try not to terrorize the grain syndicate before lunch.*

Suisui’s mouth twitched. She tucked the ticket stub into her inner sleeve—where she used to hide them in her shoes when she was fifteen—and reached down to unlatch the top toggle of Lupa’s willow hamper to see how badly the ham had crushed the buns.

The ham wasn’t in the hamper; it was in the trunk.

Resting on the very top of the hamper, arranged on a bed of four lopsided linen turtle-napkins that Mei had packed around the jars like protective gargoyles, lay a flat packet of thick darkroom paper still smelling faintly of Huaxu developer fluid.

Suisui lifted the packet out as the coach rolled into the first pale shaft of sunrise coming down the gorge.

There were four prints, dried by the stove overnight.

In the first three, Yangyang sat alone on the three-legged stool in front of the turning turbine, lit like a shrine statue by Mei’s wash-basin, her cerulean ruff puffed out to her ears and her twin cyan ribbons flying sideways in Ciaccona’s cloak-wind while she stared into the lens with the grim, unblinking heroism of a soldier facing a firing squad. Across the bottom white border of the first print, Amy had written in black ink: *Proof of Feathers (Exhibit A). She is eating fine. Do not let her tell you she only had half a bowl.*

Suisui slid the third print aside and looked at the fourth.

It was the one at the end of the roll. Yangyang’s shoulders had dropped two inches; her hand was holding Suisui’s wrist over her collarbone; and Suisui had her chin planted squarely on top of her little sister’s head, her eyes crinkled nearly shut and her mouth wide open in the middle of a laugh while the two blue marks on their foreheads caught the lamplight together.

Suisui looked at that picture for two full milestones while the iron wheels clattered over the bridge below the Hold.

Then she slid her hand deeper into the hamper, past the waxed cloth of smoked ribs and the stoneware jar of chili crisp, looking for the small tin of osmanthus cakes she had packed on Saturday to eat with her tea on the road.

Her fingers touched the tin at the bottom of the basket.

Tucked under the brass lid-rim—folded into a neat, one-inch square the exact way an eight-year-old girl used to fold paper slips to leave under the blue-and-white flour jar on the Mingting kitchen counter when she was going out to dawn drill before anyone else was awake—was a torn corner of a patrol log.

Suisui pulled it out and unfolded it on her knee in the morning sun.

There were only three lines, written in Yangyang’s small, level, exact hand:

*I took the big cake at lunch so you would have something to scold me about.*
*I put two more from Wen’s pantry in the bottom of your tin. The one on the left has the walnuts.*
*Eat before the third relay. — Yangyang.*

In the empty, rattling cab of the Mengzhou mail, three miles down the mountain road with nobody around for a league in any direction to hear the Director of the Zhaoming Commerce Guild lose her dignity, Suisui threw her head back against the leather cushions and laughed out loud.

***

Back at the lower gate of Xuanfang Hold, nobody moved until the last echo of the iron wheels died out behind the limestone bend and the valley mist closed back over the post-road.

Sir Honk gave his tail a shake, decided the military portion of the morning was concluded, and waddled off behind Old Man Zhao and Qingyan toward Widow Lin’s cabbage patch for second breakfast.

Yangyang stood two paces out under the stone arch with her hands in her sleeves. Her right hand rested against the cool, square weight of Hsin’s Prism; on her left shoulder, Dumpling gave a small, contented whirr and settled its chin into the feathers at her neck.

Slowly, Yangyang tilted her head back and looked up past the frost-rimed battlements of the Hold.

Four hundred feet above the roofs, where the morning sun was just striking the cloud-line gold, the celadon orb of the Astrolabe Core turned through a slow, silent degree in the open sky—watching a border whose Sentinel hadn’t come home and whose Paragon was still somewhere up in the mist.

Behind Yangyang, seven people waited in the cold without telling her to hurry.

Yangyang lowered her chin, turned back to the archway where Mei was blowing into her mittens and Lupa was already shifting from foot to foot like a horse smelling the barn, and took her hands out of her sleeves.

“The northeast parapet needs checking before the mist burns off,” Yangyang said in her plain, gentle voice. “And Wen left the porridge pot on the back of the stove. If we don’t go up now, the bottom will scorch.”

“Now she talks sense!” Lupa barked, spinning on her heel and starting up the three hundred steps two at a time, her tail sweeping the frost off the balustrade. “Last one up washes the big pot, and Mei’s disqualified from running!”

“I can walk fast!” Mei called out, hooking her left arm through Iuno’s good one and letting Amy fall in on her other side as the whole noisy pack turned back up the mountain—Ciaccona slinging her lute around to her back, Cartethyia skipping up the broad treads to keep her feet warm, Chisa walking level and quiet beside Lupa’s stride with her blue tie tucked neat inside her coat.

Yangyang walked half a pace beside Mei’s right shoulder, her boots making almost no sound on the stone.

Ten minutes later they came up over the top landing of the storehouse.

Out in the sunny woodyard behind the scullery, the four-foot butt of seasoned iron-elm still sat unsplit in the rime with Lupa’s rusted wedge buried in its crown and Cartethyia’s deep cathedral notch gleaming down its flank, and against the grey timber wall leaned the sixteen-foot cedar ladder, waiting for the next roof that needed four pairs of hands.

Inside, when Lupa kicked the door open, the stove was still throwing heat across the floorboards. Under the ceramic cover on the warm hearth-stone sat the crooked steamed bun from the fifth bell, kept hot for whoever came back late; and at the head of the long pine table, right beside Suisui’s empty chair and the cooling teapot, Mei’s lopsided linen turtle stood guard on all four corners in a shaft of morning sun.

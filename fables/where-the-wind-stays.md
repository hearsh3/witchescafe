# Where the Wind Stays

The last warmth on Yangyang’s forehead belonged to Chixia.

She carried it up the broken steps, through the dust beneath the checkpoint eaves, toward the gates opening between the cliffs. Her own breath had already cooled. Where their brows had rested together, a small patch of skin remembered another person.

Something struck the stone behind her.

She turned with her sword across her body. The blade caught beneath a joint, slipped, caught again. For the first time she saw the face above it.

An Autopuppet.

The light within its housing showed her the workmanship: fitted plates, the sheltered turning of a joint, an old crest worked into the metal. Xuanfang’s craft. Things built to stand between a village and the dark.

Blood had dried in the crest’s lower groove.

“I’m an Outrider from Jinzhou. There are injured people on the approach.”

She spoke while moving, because a command might still reach whatever was listening inside it. The head followed her. Its weapon rose.

Yangyang turned the strike down the length of her sword and let the force carry her toward the opening gates.

The passage beyond was empty. Through it she could see a watch platform, a strip of pale sky, the road continuing inward. Behind her, another metal foot came down on the stair from which Chixia had left.

She caught a loose stone with the toe of her boot and sent it skidding across the threshold.

Both heads turned.

“This way,” she said, and ran.

The gates received her into their long shadow.

For several strides there was only the sound of her own feet, small inside the passage, and the heavier steps answering them. She kept to the wall. At the far end she turned sharply beneath the watch platform, cut the support of a hanging barrier, and heard wood crash across the road behind her.

It delayed them long enough for her to look back.

The stone leaves were closing.

A narrow seam of daylight remained between them. Somewhere beyond it lay the broken landing; farther down, the watercourse, the shelter marked on the map, the way she had placed in Chixia’s hands.

She held the seam in her sight until it vanished.

Then she went deeper into the Hold.

***

At the first outpost, a bowl lay upright beneath a bench. Rain had washed it clean except for a pale crescent of food against the rim.

At the second, someone had tied a sleeve around the alarm rope. The cloth moved against the wall, too light to make the bell sound.

Yangyang pulled it as she passed.

One note travelled over the roofs. She waited for an answer while cutting through a gate fastening, her left hand braced against the wood.

The answering sound came from the road behind her: metal striking the barrier she had dropped.

She pushed through.

Beyond the empty yard the path divided. The lower branch bent toward a cluster of roofs. The upper climbed through the abandoned posts along the ridge.

She took the upper.

Her Terminal had struck the paving when the last Autopuppet caught her at the hip. She had seen its screen flare once beneath a metal heel. The map, water, dressings, blanket—all the things her hands reached for between encounters—were travelling toward Jinzhou.

She was glad of where they were. Her body kept forgetting.

At the next bend she put a hand to the absent bag and found her own ribs.

The pain there had begun to spread.

She stopped beneath a roof beam and let the first attacker come within reach. Its weight committed to the turn. She stepped inside the weapon’s arc, brought her sword through the exposed joint, and drove a narrow current into the opening.

The arm fell. She pulled clear before the rest of it struck the ground.

For a moment she could hear the loose mechanism turning inside its shoulder, searching for the limb.

The next one came over it.

Yangyang gave ground.

There was work she still knew how to do. A blade had a length; a passage had a width. She could make one answer the other. She drew the attackers between walls, around blind corners, beneath the low lintel of a storehouse whose doors had been torn away. Twice she brought one down. Once she found an opening wide enough to run without looking back.

The path curved uphill again.

At her right elbow, the old fault returned.

She waited for the touch before she knew she was waiting.

Her arm drifted outward. Empty air passed through the space beneath it.

Yangyang drew the elbow in herself.

“Stay with her,” she whispered.

The words were meant for someone far below the ridge, though she kept the sound close to her mouth. She had sent shijie away with a task. Even now, some foolish part of her feared that saying her name too loudly would bring her back.

Ahead, the last outpost stood with its shutters open. Yangyang went through the side yard, intending to reach the track behind it.

Grass grew across the stones. A water jar had split along its middle, and the two halves held the fallen leaves separately. She stepped over a spear laid beside the door with such care that she almost called to its owner.

Then the ground ended.

There had been a path once. Its first few stones remained, climbing outward into nothing. Far beneath them the gorge lay blue with evening. A root reached from the broken earth and turned downward, its fine ends moving in the air.

Yangyang stopped with one foot on the last whole slab.

Behind her, a metal heel entered the yard.

She looked along the cliff. To the left, sheer stone. To the right, a ledge narrowed beneath an overhang and disappeared. She could not see whether it widened again.

She might have reached it with both hands free.

Her sword trembled.

“Should be… far enough now,” she said. She tried to draw a fuller breath. “This way… Chixia will be safe.”

The last word hurt beneath her ribs. She bent over the pain, keeping the sword between herself and the yard.

She wanted to believe distance was something she could finish making.

***

The first voice came from the broken path.

“Liangyu, these Autopuppets… They seem…”

Yangyang turned her head.

A man stood where there was no longer ground.

She saw the turn of his shoulder before she saw his face. His arm lifted toward someone outside her sight. Sunlight lay on the stones beneath his feet—whole stones, a road, the shadow of a railing.

“Watch out! Khh—aghhh—!”

The sunlight broke.

Yangyang’s sword met the Autopuppet in front of her. The impact jarred through her teeth. She slid back half a step, and gravel fell from beneath her heel.

“What is this?! Why are the Autopuppets—Ngh—”

The cry opened red across her sight.

She had heard fear in the wind before. It came with a direction, with a distance: a child beyond a stand of trees, a wounded man below the road. She would turn toward it and the world would arrange itself into a way to reach them.

Now a voice passed through her shoulder. Another caught beneath her breastbone. She could see the shapes of sounds after the mouths that made them had vanished.

“Duzhong?! Qingzhi?!”

Someone reached into that cry. Yangyang felt the reaching in her own left hand.

She almost let go of the sword to help.

The Autopuppet struck again. She bent the air against its weapon and stumbled sideways as the force dispersed too widely, lifting every leaf in the yard.

“Go. I will hold the rear.”

The voice was quiet enough that she heard the breath beneath it.

“Qingxiao—you can’t stay here by yourself.”

“I will go back for the others. Do not worry.”

Someone began to object. The sound caught in a sob, then a different voice rose over it.

“Anyone still standing, with me! Push through! We have to get word back… to Xuanfang Hold…”

Yangyang reached toward the man on the path.

Her fingers met evening.

She understood, a little too late, that she was listening to something the wind had already carried away.

The yard returned: split jar, open shutters, the metal shape advancing between them. Her hand remained extended. She drew it back and found that her fingertips were numb.

Qingxiao’s voice lingered in the numbness.

*Do not worry.*

She had put a map into Chixia’s bag. She had kept the buckle clear of the wound. Every necessary thing had given her another moment in which she need not look at the hands reaching for her.

And somewhere on this same mountain, before she arrived, another person had made the road bearable by saying he would come afterward.

The thought entered her with the next blow.

She caught it badly. Her sword struck the stone beside her, and a bright pain went from her wrist to the place beneath her ear where the world had begun to ring.

For an instant she could hear her own pulse everywhere.

In the empty jar. Beneath the cliff. Inside the Autopuppet’s hollow chest.

Her hair lifted without a gust.

Yangyang knew the warning. She tried to lower her Resonance, to gather it back to the familiar boundary of skin. The air answered before the thought was complete. It poured toward her through the shutters and over the cliff, carrying so much sound that the mountains seemed to open their mouths.

She could no longer find the quiet between one voice and the next.

*Overclocking.*

Baizhi’s word had been plain in the examination room. Yangyang had repeated the signs carefully. There had been a cup on the table and a pale mark on the wall where a chart used to hang. She remembered being able to look away from the chart. She remembered that the room ended at its walls.

Here, the horizon had come inside her.

She tried to count her breaths. Before she reached the second, someone else breathed in.

***

“You can rest against me. I’ll wake you when we reach the next stop.”

Her own voice.

Older. Hoarse with dust.

Yangyang saw a road through rain, and her hand held above a sleeping child’s head to keep the dripping canvas clear. A scar crossed the knuckle of that hand. She had never had the scar. She knew how it pulled when the weather changed.

The rain turned dark. Water rose against a shop window. A red cap caught at a doorway, turned brim-first, and began to sink.

“Chixia, I’m here. If you can hear me, strike something. Anything that will carry.”

She knew the voice calling. She knew the force with which it was trying to remain useful.

The cap passed beneath the surface.

Yangyang’s knees bent.

The woman in the water kept calling after there could have been anyone left to answer. Her throat hurt in Yangyang’s throat. Under the taste of blood came silt, old plaster, the bitter water from a street where people had eaten lunch.

“She’s with you,” Yangyang tried to say. “Shijie, she’s with you now. Please keep going.”

Her words scattered before she could hear their end.

A bell sounded on a shore.

She was kneeling beside a stranger with seawater in her hair. Gold eyes opened, bewildered by the light. A hand closed around Yangyang’s wrist with the unthinking trust of someone waking from great depth.

“Yangyang,” the stranger repeated, learning the name. “Will you stay until I can stand?”

Mei’s voice. Mei’s face.

The hand was unbandaged, the shore unfamiliar, and the woman looking at her had never laughed into a cup of barley tea.

Yangyang felt the answer rise in a chest that belonged to her elsewhere.

“Of course I will. Take your time; we have only just found you.”

Then she was standing in a courtyard in Mingting, listening to rain on the eaves. Suisui had grey in her hair. Their mother’s flute lay warm across Yangyang’s knees, with a patch worn smooth beneath the thumb. Someone inside was asking her to play the beginning again.

Her own laughter answered. It had grown lower with age.

She had lived long enough to sound like that.

The courtyard slipped away before she could see who had asked.

Another wind entered. It carried frost and a woman’s voice worn almost beyond recognition.

“I brought the shell. I thought you might remember the sound.”

Her mother’s tide unfolded beneath the words.

For a moment Yangyang saw two moons on the same water. She felt the weight of a shell in a palm that had already begun to lose its shape. Far away, someone was waiting by a lamp. Farther away, someone was still setting out a second bowl.

She could not tell which distance belonged to home.

The voices crossed.

Baizhi told her to keep her hand where she could see it; Baizhi asked why she had left the window open; Baizhi, very gently, said there was no one beneath the sheet whom Yangyang could still help. Chixia laughed with food in her mouth. Chixia called from a stair. Chixia spoke into a darkness that had gone on so long her voice had become polite, as though the absent girl might simply have failed to hear.

“Yangyang, we’ve kept your place. You can come back whenever you’re ready.”

A younger Suisui complained that she was moving her head while her hair was being plaited. An older one asked for an address.

Between them, her own voice promised to write.

The promise had travelled farther than the letter.

Yangyang could feel that distance. It went through her like winter through an unlatched house.

These were lives. She knew it in the small things that would never have occurred to her to imagine: the pull of a scar, an unfamiliar cup held comfortably, the place a sister paused to ease a stiff knee before crossing the courtyard. Every voice came with a world around it. Every world had gone on past the moment she was allowed to hear.

She tried to hold one still.

Her hand closed on the sword hilt, and she was back at the cliff with blood beneath her glove.

An Autopuppet had fallen against the split jar. She did not remember striking it. Another was working its way around the fallen body.

The light at the edges of her hair had spread.

She lifted her free hand. Pale filaments streamed from her fingers, or behind them, or through them; she could no longer tell where the hand ended. Its outline wavered with the grass below the cliff.

The air was still full of crying.

If she opened herself farther, she thought she might reach it all.

***

The thought came gently.

There would be no need to make the ledge. No need to lift the sword with a hand that had begun to fail. She could pass through the narrow places the way shijie passed through a half-open window. She could go down the gorge, find the two she had sent away, put her strength beneath Chixia’s wounded side.

The road to Jinzhou lay somewhere under all that evening.

She had known it before she knew this pain. Willow shade by the water. Bridges warm from the sun. At the end, a window left open above a desk.

For years she had imagined someone arriving there.

Now she could almost feel herself coming in.

The inkstone would be cool. The letter beneath it would rise when she passed. Her hat would hang from its peg, and shijie could rest in the lining after Yangyang had warmed it—

She lost the rest of the thought.

Who would warm it?

The air moved against her face. Her cheeks were wet. She had not felt the tears arrive.

She remembered the crown of the hat lying open on the garden stone. She had held the fan and moved her wrist slowly so that another woman might have a breeze made for her. A flower had hovered above them. Chixia had lain back to watch.

Her wrist. Her lap. The sun on the top of her head.

She could remember the warmth with such exactness that its absence hurt more than the wound.

Mei’s hand had fitted badly into hers because of the bandage. Yangyang had adjusted her fingers and found a way to hold it. Later there had been an embrace, the seam of a collar against her cheek, the difficult pleasure of staying close to someone who could not give her every life she wanted.

She wanted that life still. The one with the difficult part in it.

The lower canal had reopened. She had been choosing which afternoon to walk there. She had wanted to go alone once, and with Mei another time. There were letters in her drawer she might never send and others she had begun to think she could.

Beyond the cliff, the voices rose again.

Yangyang could follow them. Something in her had found how. Her own outline was loosening around the knowledge, as a knot loosens after a long day in rain.

She understood why shijie had gone.

The understanding frightened her more than the open air.

She had known the story in the form that could sit beside a bowl. She had watched a page turn, heard a note continue after her own breath failed, felt the little correction beneath her elbow. She had learned to leave room for a woman who could not occupy a chair.

Here was the part before all that.

Here were the hands still capable of closing. Here was the pulse in the wrist, the warmth beneath a collar, the last full breath before the words became dear. Here was a person with someone waiting for a letter, looking toward a distance she believed she could cross if she gave enough.

Yangyang’s fingers tightened.

How much wanting could pass for courage when there was only one road left to take?

She could feel the answer moving in herself. She wanted Chixia out of pain. She wanted the crying on the mountain to stop. She wanted to reach the red cap before it sank, to give the woman calling through the flood one sound in return. If there were a way to lay her breath across all those wet faces, some part of her would already have gone.

That part was hers. She could not disown it without disowning the hand that had steadied Chixia on the steps.

But the fear was hers too.

It rose through the borrowed grief, frightened and young, wanting the solid edge of a table beneath her palms. Wanting her sister to complain about the hat. Wanting Baizhi to look up over a fish with absurd eyebrows, and Chixia’s shoulder to shake beneath her cheek.

She had been laughing so much lately.

The realization broke something the fighting had left whole.

Yangyang pressed her lips together. Her breath came through them unevenly, and she could hear it taking its place among the voices. Soon she might be unable to tell which of the women crying was herself.

She tried to say her name.

The first sound came from somewhere over the gorge.

She felt her throat move without feeling the word leave it.

Cold spread beneath her ribs. If someone called for her, which current would turn? Would she remember the face she meant to touch? Could she keep the small, useless things—the taste of green tea, the embarrassment over a ribbon, her own private opinion of a book—or would she have to spend them until only the wish to arrive remained?

She had been angry with shijie over one word.

She had needed that word too. The need stayed where it had been, aching; beside it opened the depth from which the word must have come.

“I haven’t finished listening to you,” she whispered.

Her voice wavered between her mouth and the empty yard.

“When you come back, I want to hear about the pears. I’ll sit down properly this time. You can take as long as you need.”

She tried to remember the rest of what she meant to ask. A tree. A kitchen. Somewhere the smell had made shijie want to linger. She had wanted the story in her own room, with enough time for wrong guesses and another pot of tea.

There was so much more to ask a person than whether she was cold.

The Autopuppet lifted its weapon.

Yangyang saw it through the moving light and could not remember how to bring her arm up.

For a moment its shadow lay across several worlds. She was old beneath it. She was kneeling on a shore. She was standing in water with a red cap held against her chest.

Then she was only here, with one foot too near the edge, and a voice without a body was saying, in her own familiar cadence:

“I am still trying to get home.”

She did not know which of them had spoken.

A blue wing crossed the blade.

***

It was a small bird, bright against the iron.

Fine plates opened along its wings. Blue deepened to blue at the joints, and a curved ornament above its head caught the last light. It banked so close that Yangyang saw a nick on the underside of one feather.

Then the air struck sideways.

The Autopuppet’s weapon sheared past her shoulder. Metal hit stone. The gust drove cold against her wet cheeks, sharp enough to hurt, and she gasped.

The breath entered her own chest.

Pain followed it all the way down.

The voices drew back. One remained beneath the ringing, distant, trying to finish a sentence. Yangyang caught at it instinctively, then the cold came again and she felt the sword’s weight in her hand.

The bird wheeled above the yard.

“A Rafter Kite? Why’d it fly here? Ah, that’s—Liangyu! Someone’s fighting the Autopuppets over there!”

This voice struck the outpost wall and returned smaller.

A real echo. A person on a road.

Yangyang turned toward it.

“At a time like this… How is that—” The second speaker drew breath. “Everyone, get in formation! Keep them away from the village!”

Feet sounded beyond the yard. Several people, coming uphill. A weapon scraped the doorway, and someone swore at the narrowness of it.

She could place every sound.

The knowledge was so sudden a mercy that her knees almost gave way.

A Warden came around the fallen Autopuppet with a torn bandage across one forearm. Another followed, limping, his weapon braced at an angle that kept its weight off one shoulder. Yangyang saw the empty loop where a piece of his armour should have fastened.

Their faces were grey with exhaustion.

“Hey! Are you alright?” the first called. “Keep looking this way. We’re coming to you!”

Yangyang opened her mouth.

“I…”

She had her name. She had been sent from Jinzhou; Chixia was hurt; the checkpoint had fallen. The names Duzhong and Qingzhi moved against the back of her teeth. There was someone called Qingxiao whom they needed to look for.

She tried to put the first thing first.

Her sword tip touched the ground.

The Warden’s gaze dropped to her feet. She came forward faster.

Yangyang felt herself tilt toward the gorge. Her left hand reached for the air in the old familiar way, asking it to take her weight.

Then she saw the Warden’s open hand.

She reached for that instead.

Cloth caught between her fingers. The sleeve pulled tight over an arm; the arm strained against her weight. Someone else seized the back of her coat, painfully, and the cliff moved away beneath her.

“I’ve got you,” the Warden said. Her breath was harsh beside Yangyang’s ear. “Let us bring you through. You don’t have to hold yourself up.”

She tried to help. Her legs folded.

The last thing she felt was the difficulty of being carried: her shoulder against someone’s chest, a buckle pressing into her side, two living people adjusting their grip because she was heavier than either had expected.

She went into the dark with their hands still on her.

***

The bed was cold.

Yangyang lay with her eyes closed and listened for Chixia moving in the next room.

A small metallic sound came from the window.

She opened her eyes.

The ceiling beams were wrong. One had a pale repair along its length. Herbs hung near the door, tied in bundles with scraps of different cloth. Beneath the shutter, a narrow square of moonlight lay on the floor.

The blue bird stood on the windowsill.

It turned its head when she moved. The plates at its throat shifted with a delicate click.

Yangyang tried to rise.

Pain closed around her ribs. She stopped, one hand half-lifted from the quilt, and watched her fingers until they became ordinary again.

Five fingers. A bandage over the palm. A little dried blood beside a nail.

She bent the smallest one.

It hurt.

For a while she did nothing else.

Beyond the door, someone set down a basin. Water moved against its sides. A man asked for another strip of clean cloth, explaining that the one he held had fallen on the floor.

She listened to him finish the explanation.

Footsteps crossed the room outside. They passed her door, returned, and stopped. The latch lifted.

The Warden with the bandaged forearm came in carrying a cup. Without her weapon she looked younger. Dust lay in the crease beside her nose, where washing had missed it.

“You’re awake. I thought I heard the bed move.” She put the cup within Yangyang’s reach and drew a stool closer. “I’m Liangyu. We brought you down from the outpost. You’re in a house in the village.”

Yangyang watched her mouth through the whole sentence.

“How long have I been here?”

“Only a few hours. Try the water slowly. You were having trouble swallowing when we first brought you in.”

Her hand would not close firmly around the cup. Liangyu helped her lift it without remarking on the tremor.

The water tasted of the pot in which it had been boiled.

She swallowed once, then again.

“My friend was injured outside the gates,” she said. “Chixia, from Jinzhou. She’s taking the lower watercourse. If anyone reaches the southern road, please tell them to look for her.”

Liangyu’s fingers rested beneath the cup until she had finished drinking.

“I’ll pass her name to the watch. We haven’t been able to get a message through that way.”

Yangyang looked down at the water.

She had known the distance was still there. Hearing it from someone else made her want to get out of bed.

Liangyu glanced toward the door. In the other room, a man began coughing, then tried to apologize for it.

“We were at the outer posts when the Autopuppets turned on us,” she said. “Some of us made it back here. We’ve been keeping the roads into the village covered.”

Yangyang’s hand tightened around the cup.

The sunlight on the broken path returned, and with it the reaching arm.

“I heard someone call for Duzhong and Qingzhi. There was another man who stayed behind. Qingxiao.”

Liangyu went still.

She saw Liangyu’s gaze settle on her face with a new, painful attention.

“You saw them?”

Yangyang opened her mouth, then waited until she could make the answer exact.

“I heard what happened. My Resonance sometimes catches voices left in the wind.” She looked at the bandage across Liangyu’s forearm. “I couldn’t tell how long they had been there. I’m sorry. I don’t know where they are now.”

Liangyu’s hand moved once on her knee.

“When you’re stronger, will you tell me everything you heard?”

“I will. If you can bring paper, I’d like to write the names before I lose them.”

Liangyu nodded, keeping her eyes lowered for a moment. Then she helped Yangyang settle the cup on the stool.

At the door she looked toward the bird.

“That one has stayed since we brought you in. Do you know whose it is?”

“I don’t,” Yangyang said. “It came over the cliff.”

The Rafter Kite shifted one foot along the sill.

Liangyu watched it, then went to find paper.

***

Left alone, Yangyang put her palm flat on the quilt.

The weave was rough. Beneath it, her thigh held a small, insufficient warmth. The bed had been made for someone else, in a room where she knew none of the objects, and yet she had been laid in it with her wounds bound and her boots removed.

Her sword rested against the wall within reach.

Someone had cleaned the hilt.

She looked at it for a long time.

At the cliff, the last reach had nearly taken her back into the air. She remembered that now with a clarity which made her stomach turn: the old trust of extending her hand, the terrible ease with which she had expected the wind to receive her.

Perhaps it would have. Perhaps she would have been too scattered to know.

She pressed harder against the quilt.

The voices had receded, but she could still hear the older woman laughing in the courtyard. Her own voice, after years she had not lived. It hurt to have heard so little of it. She wanted to know what had amused her, who sat inside, whether Suisui ever learned to leave that loose strand of hair alone.

There had been so many ways to go on.

At the cliff she had almost lost this one.

She turned her face into the pillow before the first sob came. The movement pulled at her ribs. She tried to keep still and found she could not cry without moving something that hurt.

Chixia had reached for her with both hands.

Yangyang had been able to feel where every finger slipped.

She had carried that feeling through the gates without looking at it. Now it was here in the bed with her, and there was nothing to strike, no road to measure, no wounded friend whose dressing she could put right.

She wanted to be angry with the stone beneath the stairs. She wanted to take back the hour at the checkpoint, to arrive early enough to find someone beside the kettle. She wanted the strength for another body in the air.

If she stood there again, with the same broken road and the same blood between Chixia’s fingers, she knew where she would put the bag.

Knowing did not quiet her.

She cried for the afternoon they had already had, complete and careless, while all its people were still there to waste it. For Baizhi saving the last piece of ginger. For Chixia encouraging a turtle. For the Wind taking her time over the painted fans, choosing yellow after blue.

Those hours had contained so little pain. Yangyang had lived inside them without understanding how fully she had been allowed to stay.

The bird clicked on the sill.

She turned toward it with her face wet.

Its blue feathers overlapped neatly along its back. When it tilted its head, she saw the small nick beneath the wing again. The sight brought the cold gust against her cheek with such force that she caught her breath.

For a moment she wanted to call it shijie.

She listened instead.

There was a fine mechanism working beneath its breast. Beyond it lay the village: water being poured, the drag of a tired foot, someone calling from one house to the next. Wind passed the eaves and found a loose place in the shutter.

She could not hear Chixia.

She had to let the silence mean she could not hear her.

Yangyang wiped her face with the unbandaged edge of her hand. She missed a tear beneath her chin and felt it run into the collar of the borrowed clothes.

“Thank you for staying,” she told the bird. “I’m afraid I don’t know how to look after you yet.”

It turned toward the sound.

She was listening for the tiny catch in its wing when Liangyu returned. She laid a few sheets of paper on a board across Yangyang’s knees and put a brush beside them. Someone had rinsed it in haste; a bead of water clung near the ferrule.

“There’s more ink if you need it,” she said. “I’ll be in the next room.”

Yangyang touched the edge of the board.

“Would you leave the door open? I’d like to hear you.”

“Of course. Call if you need help with the cup.”

Liangyu left the door wide enough for Yangyang to see her stool beyond it.

Yangyang waited until she had sat down. The wood creaked under her weight.

Then she took the brush.

She wrote the names first, as she had promised. Duzhong. Qingzhi. Qingxiao. Beside them she set down what she could remember, leaving space where she was uncertain. Once the remembered cry rose too loudly, and she put the brush down until the man in the next room turned a page.

When the account was finished, she drew a fresh sheet toward her.

For a while her hand rested above it.

There were several people she wanted to write to. She did not know which road would open first, whose hands would take the page, whether the ink would reach Jinzhou before Chixia did. She could feel herself beginning to arrange the message around all those uncertainties, making room for everything except what she wanted them to hear.

She lowered the brush.

*I am alive. The Wardens brought me to a village inside Xuanfang Hold. I am hurt, and I want to come home.*

Her hand shook. She rested it, then added:

*Please come for me when you can. There are people here who need help too.*

At the foot of the page she wrote her name.

She knew every stroke. For once she watched all of them arrive.

The bird settled its wings. Outside, the wind went on across the roofs, carrying the voices of the village toward the dark hills.

Yangyang put the brush down before the ink on her name had dried.

“Liangyu,” she called through the open door, “may I have a little more water?”

She heard Liangyu set her things aside. Her footsteps came toward the bed, and Yangyang kept her hand around the cup, waiting for her to help lift it.

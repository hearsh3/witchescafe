Chapter 1: Pillow Talk 2

The spot in the middle of the pallet was already cold when Mei’s hand flopped across it.

Amy had gone out before the first grey touched the shutter-crack, taking Lupa’s sheepskin vest and her boots with her and leaving behind a dented hollow in the mattress and a faint smell of pear-soap.

Mei didn’t open her eyes. She burrowed her chin half an inch deeper into the feather bolster, kept her abdomen flat against the ticking, and spoke to the ceiling rafters in a voice like gravel sliding off a shovel:

“What day is it?”

“Not Tuesday,” Iuno said.

Mei’s right eye cracked open a sliver. Then the left one joined it.

Iuno wasn’t under the quilt. She was sitting propped against the timber wall at the head of the pallet with her wool coat draped loose over her night-tunic and the blanket pooled across her lap. On the upturned crate beside Barnaby the frog and the two sugar badgers, her dented steel flask still had its cap screwed down tight, and the porcelain teacup sat dry and upside down on its saucer.

She had clearly tried to put her hair up an hour ago and abandoned it halfway through: the left side was twisted into its usual neat silver-blue coil above her ear, while the right side hung loose down her collarbone, and the second heavy silver pin lay idle in the crease of the blanket between her knees. In her right hand she held a folded rectangle of foolscap—not reading it, just turning the bottom corner over and back beneath her thumb—and every few seconds the jagged crystal at her bare left shoulder shifted a fraction of an inch against the pine planking behind her with a tiny, rhythmic *tink-tink* she didn’t know she was making.

Mei looked at the dry teacup, then at the half-pinned hair, then at the paper.

Slowly, keeping her hips square so the bandage around her middle wouldn’t pull, Mei scooted sidewise across the cool patch Amy had left until her thigh bumped Iuno’s knee under the wool.

“You’re clicking,” Mei said.

Iuno’s left shoulder stopped dead against the plank. “I am not clicking.”

“You’re doing your Senate-subcommittee tap on the wall. And you didn’t even make the four-minute tea.” Mei reached out from under the edge of the quilt and hooked one finger lightly over the corner of the folded foolscap. “How long have you been sitting up?”

“Since the water-cart went past the lower gate,” Iuno said, folding the paper once more with her thumb and sliding it flat under her thigh out of reach. “The axle on that man’s left wheel needs grease. You could hear it from the cistern.”

“Iuiu.” Mei turned onto her good side, propping her temple in her palm and looking straight up at her in the thin grey light. “You’ve got your tax-judge face on before the porridge is even on the stove. What’s on the paper?”

“Logistics,” Iuno said mildly. “Chisa and I are walking down to the gatehouse after muster to ask Warden Liangyu for the old sluice-gate logs. None of it requires a sword, and none of it requires you off that mattress before the sixth bell.”

“Come on, give me half of it,” Mei pressed, her thumb brushing the knee of Iuno’s night-tunic through the wool. “You and Lupa and Amy have had me parked on a rug for a week yelling at me every time I look at a bucket by myself. You don’t get to lecture me about sharing the roof and then sit here in the dark chewing on a list alone.”

Iuno looked down at her. The dry line of her mouth softened by a fraction of an inch; she reached down with two fingers and tucked a stray lock of dark hair back behind Mei’s ear.

“Your job this week,” Iuno said, pleasant and entirely immovable, “is to keep your stomach in one piece so Wen the cook doesn’t have to lend us pack-thread. The gatehouse is my department. Close your eyes for another twenty minutes.”

Mei stared up at her for three beats.

Then she rolled flat onto her back, crossed both arms high up on her chest well clear of her bandages, and huffed at the rafters loud enough to stir the paper label inside Barnaby’s stomach.

“Hmph. Fine, I’ll go talk to someone else, then. There’s plenty of cute girls in Xuanfang.”

Iuno didn’t even blink.

She looked down her nose at the pillow, and thirty years of Septimontian basilica marble slid effortlessly into her voice.

“Fine,” Iuno said smoothly. “Go, then.”

Mei’s head turned two degrees on the bolster.

“By all means,” Iuno continued, her tone as helpful and measured as a chamberlain reading out a carriage schedule. “If you hurry before the sixth bell, you can catch Ruoyun up on the west valley-gutter with her tar-bucket. I am sure she will be delighted to hold your hand in the frost while she lectures you for forty minutes on why your posture is warping the roof-slates. Or, if you prefer someone without a ladder, our new geomancer is currently out by the water-butt. She charges three silvers an hour for conversation, and she calls you Deadbeat.”

*Ask her about the boots, darling,* the High Priestess purred inside Iuno’s head, propping her chin on a phantom hand with immense satisfaction. *Do ask her about the boots.*

Iuno leaned an inch toward the edge of the pallet, glancing down at the floorboards beside the crate where Mei’s heavy leather road-boots stood side by side.

“Shall I hand you your footwear before you go?” Iuno inquired politely. “You still cannot bend past your knees without making a noise like a stepped-on frog, so I am curious which of these cute girls in Xuanfang you intend to ask to kneel in the mud and lace the left one for you.”

With a crisp flick of her wrist, Iuno turned her right shoulder toward the wall and took three-quarters of the warm wool quilt with her.

“No—wait—I take it back!” Mei lunged across the mattress, hooking both hands into the edge of the quilt and the hem of Iuno’s coat with zero dignity whatsoever. “I retract! I’m withdrawing the cute girls! There are no cute girls in Xuanfang, they’re all terrifying and smell like pine pitch, give me back the blanket!”

She didn’t stop at the blanket.

Keeping her torso rigid by pure theoretical goodwill, Mei hauled herself up off the bolster and climbed straight into Iuno’s lap like a large, determined woollen octopus.

“Mei, your—” Iuno started, her right hand flying out at once to catch Mei’s hip before she could twist the stitches.

“Not pulling, totally flat, I’m a plank!” Mei babbled straight into the hollow of Iuno’s throat, hooking both shins over Iuno’s calves to lock them in place and wrapping her arms around Iuno’s neck. Before Iuno could get a breath in to scold her, Mei was peppering quick, noisy kisses up the side of her neck, across the sharp angle of her jawbone, and right onto the warm lobe of her ear. “You’re so mean to me in the morning. Meanest clerk on the mountain. Groomer Mussolini.”

“If you speak that name in this bed again, I am rolling you under the crate,” Iuno warned, though her head tipped back against the pine wall, giving Mei’s mouth room.

“Never heard of him! Definitely a very handsome magistrate with great hair!” Mei shifted half an inch in her lap, pressing a loud kiss to the bridge of Iuno’s nose, another to her left eyebrow, and then—slower, her mouth warm and unhurried—right against the cool, faceted ridge of crystal at Iuno’s left shoulder, just above the fresh chip, so the crystal gave a soft, high chime against her cheek. “Look at me, I’m rehabilitating my reputation. Zero deadbeat behaviour. I’m right here paying my taxes on time. Tell me what’s on the paper, Iuiu, I’ll sit on your knees till muster, I weigh a ton after Lupa’s pork—”

Iuno let out a long breath through her nose.

She didn’t push Mei off her lap.

Instead, her right hand slid from Mei’s hip around to the small of her back, her palm wide and steady over the linen belly-wrap beneath Mei’s sleeping shirt, holding the whole line of her spine secure so Mei didn’t have to brace her middle at all. With a single, smooth pivot of her shoulders and her knees, Iuno turned them both over onto the pallet.

Mei hit the feather bolster flat on her back with a surprised little *oomph*.

Before Mei could open her mouth to start the next sentence about pork belly, Iuno was above her, the loose half of her silver-blue hair spilling down like a cool curtain across Mei’s cheek. Iuno braced her weight on her right knee, caught both of Mei’s wrists in her one hand—pinning them lightly to the pillow above Mei’s crown—and lowered her mouth to Mei’s.

“—wait, Iuiu, my—” Mei started, grinning against her lips.

Iuno kissed her until the grin went away.

It wasn’t quick, and it wasn’t for sport. She took her time, her mouth warm and exacting and faintly sweet with last night’s bergamot, tilting Mei’s chin up with the edge of her thumb while her fingers stayed closed around both of Mei’s wrists. Mei made a small, muffled sound at the back of her throat; her fingers uncurled against the coarse linen of the pillowcase.

On the other side of the half-inch pine wall, an iron pot-lid dropped onto the hearthstones with an almighty *CLANG* that rattled the latch, followed immediately by Lupa barking at Buling to keep her paper talisman out of the millet crock and Cartethyia earnestly explaining to someone by the door that geese required grit for their gizzards.

Iuno didn’t lift her head by a millimeter.

She let go of Mei’s wrists only to slide her hand down Mei’s throat, down the warm line of her breastbone under the quilt, and lay her palm flat and heavy directly over the bandaged stitches across Mei’s abdomen—keeping her pressed gently, inexorably down into the mattress while she kissed the corner of Mei’s mouth, the hot skin below her ear, the hollow of her collarbone, and back to her lips, slow and deep, until Mei’s knees went completely slack under the wool and both of her hands came up weak and clumsy to hold onto the back of Iuno’s neck.

When Iuno finally lifted her head two inches, Mei’s golden eyes were half-shut and hazy in the grey light, her mouth parted, her chest rising and falling fast under Iuno’s palm. She didn’t have a single joke left in her head.

Iuno looked down at her for three heartbeats, her own breathing uneven, the crystal at her left shoulder warm where Mei’s fingers lay curled against the nape of her neck.

Then Iuno lowered herself carefully onto her right side beside her, keeping her palm flat over Mei’s bandages under the blanket and resting her forehead directly against Mei’s temple on the pillow.

For a minute the room was still. Outside on the storehouse sill, the little wooden turbine turned with its slow, steady *whuff-creak, whuff-creak* in the dawn wind. Mei didn’t try to sit up; she just turned her face half an inch so her nose brushed Iuno’s cheekbone, her right hand sliding down inside the loose wool coat to stroke the bare skin between Iuno’s shoulder blades in slow, quiet passes.

Iuno reached down with two fingers, drew the folded rectangle of foolscap out from the crease of the blanket, and laid it between their chests without opening it.

“The deep barrier under the gorge has been rotting from the underside for fifty years,” Iuno murmured, her voice hushed against Mei’s temple, plain and in order. “Muyu’s cages are gone. The jade doors are bolted, and the Prism Suisui left with Yangyang is cold. The last Sentinel’s Resonator—Xuanqing—came off the garrison roll years before we ever walked up the pass.”

Mei’s hand kept moving slowly up and down Iuno’s spine. She didn’t interrupt.

“The celestials who were bound under the Fox’s silence clause are walking through the market in daylight,” Iuno went on, her eyes half-closed against Mei’s hair. “Two people in that court didn’t flinch when Muyu spoke: the man with the cat and the soot on his cuff, and the woman with the umbrella. Now Buling has walked back up from the lower ferry. And when I asked Chisa on the landing last night whether the Core up there still sounds the same, she told me it has gone too quiet for her to tell.”

Iuno paused. Out in the lane beyond the shutter, someone’s boots crunched past through the frost toward the guard-post, the sound fading down the steps.

“There are two more,” Iuno whispered. “I haven’t written them down.”

Mei’s thumb brushed the small hollow at the base of Iuno’s neck. “Tell me.”

“Mengzhou,” Iuno said. “The city hall has been nearly silent since the siege ended. Everything Liangyu sends down the post-road comes back as a stamped receipt from a receiving clerk. No relief company, no engineers, no questions. And the Mengzhou magistrate has said nothing at all.” She drew a quiet breath in through her nose. “A city that size does not ignore a fortress in its own province unless the people inside the hall have their backs against a wall of their own.”

The neat council-room order in her voice began to fray a little at the edges. She shifted an inch closer on the bolster, her knee sliding between Mei’s shins under the wool.

“You still have four days before those stitches can take a jolt,” Iuno said into the pillow between them. “Yangyang stood on the northeast parapet for three hours in the rime with three cracked ribs because she still won’t say a word when her breath catches. Amy wakes up before dawn whenever supper was too good because she’s waiting for someone to come through the door with the bill. Buling doesn’t turn up in a mountain fort unless the ground is about to give way. Cartethyia and Ciaccona are half a world from any harbor they know, and if something comes out of that gorge before the road clears, Lupa and Chisa will be twenty paces in front of the gatehouse before the bell finishes ringing.”

Her fingers tightened a fraction against the linen wrap over Mei’s stomach.

“All eight of them are sitting up on this mountain because you are here,” Iuno whispered. “And for thirty years my entire purpose in a room was to see which beam was going to crack before anyone stood under it. Now I look up at that brass thing in the sky and I might as well be looking at a painted plate.”

Mei’s hand stopped between Iuno’s shoulder blades, holding her there, warm and solid.

Iuno didn’t pull back. She stayed still for four long breaths, looking down at the folded paper between them.

“And there was a Tuesday this week,” Iuno said, much slower now, halting between the words. “I let it go past.”

Mei’s breath caught once, very softly.

“The one before that went by while I was locked in a cabin with a painted afternoon outside the porthole,” Iuno whispered. “And this one I let go myself. I told myself: not this week. Her stomach isn’t healed yet. The sky is wrong. Augusta hasn’t arrived with the pen, and Suisui is three hundred miles north.”

Iuno closed her eyes. Her forehead pressed a little harder against Mei’s temple.

“I lay here for two hours before the light came up,” Iuno said, so low it barely left her lips, “listening to you breathe, and I noticed I was waiting for the auspicious hour. Waiting until the weather cleared and the borders were tidy and every roof in the province was propped up first.” Her voice dropped into something raw and completely plain. “I spent thirty years doing that with my life, Mei. Filing the morning away until the schedule was safe. I hate it. And for the first time since the basilica, I wished I could see five minutes ahead—just to see your face when I take the box out—even though I asked you not to tell me, and I still don’t want you to tell me before I ask.”

*There,* the High Priestess murmured in the quiet of her mind, without a single trace of theatre left in her voice. *Now she has the whole coat, darling. Pocket and all.*

For two heartbeats the back bedroom was dead silent.

Mei opened her mouth. The word was right there on her tongue—Iuno could see it plain as sunrise in the gold of her eyes—and Iuno lifted two fingers from Mei’s stomach to lay them flat across Mei’s lips.

“Don’t,” Iuno whispered. “Not while you’re flat on your back in bandages and I don’t have the paper.”

Mei blinked at her over the two fingers. Slowly, deliberately, Mei kissed the pads of Iuno’s fingertips, and waited until Iuno lowered her hand back to the quilt.

“Okay,” Mei whispered. “Zero spoilers.”

She looked down at the folded sheet of foolscap resting between their ribs, then back up at Iuno’s face.

“First of all,” Mei said seriously, “I’m taking number six off your paper right now. Today after muster I’m sitting on the sunny step with my blanket and my coffee, and every time Buling walks past the woodpile, I’m checking her sleeves. If she hides two buns in her cuff, that’s normal grocery shopping. If she hides four buns and ties her shoelaces double, we tell Lupa to put her boots on.”

Iuno let out a tiny, helpless breath through her nose that was almost a laugh. “Buling always hides four buns in her cuff.”

“Then five buns! Don’t argue with the rug department, Iuiu, I’ve got the whole yard mapped.” Mei slid her hand up from Iuno’s back to cup the side of Iuno’s jaw, her thumb resting just below Iuno’s cheekbone, her voice dropping out of the nonsense into something quiet, level, and completely steady. “And second of all, I’m retiring the morning question.”

Iuno’s eyes flicked up to hers.

“I’m not asking what day it is anymore when I wake up,” Mei said softly. “The calendar in our house is always broken anyway, and spring still comes. From now on, Monday and Wednesday and Friday are whatever’s written on the gatehouse board, and Tuesday isn’t on the wall at all. Tuesday is just whichever morning or afternoon or middle of the night you take that box out of your coat. Even if it’s snowing. Even if Augusta has to sign it on the back of a barley receipt in Mengzhou and I have to jump on one foot.”

Mei’s thumb stroked once across Iuno’s cheek.

“You don’t have to hold the sky up first to ask me, sweetheart,” Mei whispered. “I live under the same sky.”

Iuno looked at her for a long beat in the grey light. She didn’t make a joke about the barley receipt. She simply turned her head half an inch and pressed her mouth into the warm palm of Mei’s hand.

“One foot,” Iuno murmured against her skin, “would be deeply undignified.”

“I’ve got great balance on one foot. Cartethyia showed me yesterday, it’s called the Heron of the Cellar.”

Up on the watchtower above the terraces, the bronze muster bell struck the first note of the sixth hour—*BONG*—and three seconds later Lupa’s fist hit the timber partition by the stove hard enough to make Barnaby hop half an inch on his crate.

“UP!” Lupa’s bellow came straight through the pine boards. “Porridge is on the trivet and the zombie’s already eaten three of the salted eggs! Iuno, Chisa’s waiting by the boot-scraper!”

Iuno sighed against Mei’s palm, gave her wrist one last light squeeze, and sat up on the edge of the pallet, bringing the cool morning draft in under the quilt before tucking the wool firmly back down around Mei’s shoulders.

“Stay flat until I bring your mug,” Iuno instructed her, standing up and picking the second silver hairpin off the blanket. “If you come out there barefoot to audit Buling’s sleeves before your bandages are checked, I am giving your bacon to the goose.”

“The goose is a vegetarian!” Mei called from the pillows, propping her chin on her stacked hands to watch.

Iuno stood before the small propped hand-mirror beside the extinguished lamp. With her right hand, she gathered the loose silver-blue coil at the nape of her neck, twisted it up in a single clean turn against her fingers, and slid the second silver pin straight home through the center of the knot without catching a single hair.

She pulled her wool coat properly onto her shoulders. Then she picked up the folded sheet of foolscap from the quilt, slid it into her deep inner pocket—right beside the small, square velvet box—and let her palm rest flat against the outside of the wool for the space of a breath.

From the pallet behind her, muffled slightly by the bolster, Mei asked:

“Hey, Iuiu. What day is it?”

Iuno looked at her in the three-inch mirror, her hand still on her coat pocket, and reached for the wooden door-latch.

“Porridge day,” Iuno said, and stepped out into the hall.

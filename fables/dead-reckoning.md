# Dead Reckoning
### *a Septimont story, in four entries and a balance*

---

*]what the dead want[*
*]is rarely what the living[*
*]have been rehearsing[*

---

## I. Debit

The medium worked the carnival strip below the Colosseum, third stall from the fountain, between a man who sold luck and a woman who sold insurance against it. Her sign read **RAFFAELLA CINQ — THE DEPARTED, LOCATED** in gold paint that had been retouched by an optimist. Her method was older than her sign: she watched hands, she watched hems, she said *he wants you to know it wasn't your fault* to widows and *she kept the letter* to sons, and everyone went home lighter, and she ate.

Twice a year, on average, it was real. Those nights she closed early and drank until the counting stopped.

Tonight she stood in the doorway of a boarding house in the upper quarter, hat in both hands, having climbed the hill at a pace her lungs regretted, and the woman who opened the door was tall, white-haired, horned, and holding a pen.

"We're closed," Zani said. The party had a policy about pilgrims now. Phoebe enforced it with a smile. Zani enforced it with the door.

"You're the Nightwalker."

"The Nightwalker's dead."

"I know." Raffaella's knuckles were white on the hat brim. "That's my whole problem, signora. The dead have started asking for you *by name*."

Behind Zani, in the room, a chair scraped. Laevatain crossed into the light with a whetstone in one hand and her sword arm loose, and looked at the medium the way she looked at all strangers: as a set of ranges.

"Séance scam," Laevatain said. "There's a grift like her in every market. She'll want gold for the introduction and more gold for the goodbye."

"Yes," Raffaella said. "Normally. Absolutely. That is exactly my business model and it's a good one." Her voice cracked down the middle. "But three nights ago I was running the widow Ossari through the usual — candle, bell, her late husband's excellent opinion of her cooking — and something *else* sat down at my table. Something that has been sitting there ever since." She swallowed. "It doesn't talk. It *counts*."

The pen stopped moving in Zani's hand.

"Counts," she said.

"Numbers. Hours of them. Percentages. Dates. I wrote some down because I couldn't sleep otherwise." She dug a fold of paper from her coat and held it out at full arm's length, the way you hand someone a live coal. "And then last night it stopped counting for one moment. One. It said: *get me the banker with the horns.*"

The paper hung in the air between them.

Zani took it. Unfolded it. Read.

Her tail went still. Wholly, professionally still — the stillness she used to bring to audits, the one that made junior clerks confess to sins they hadn't committed yet.

Phoebe appeared at her shoulder, drawn by the silence the way she was always drawn by silence, and read the column of figures upside down, and understood none of it, and understood everything from Zani's face.

"Zani?"

"It's a debt schedule." Zani's voice came out level and far away. "Principal, vig, compounding weekly. Ragunna street rates, post-Lament." Her thumb moved down the column. "Whoever's counting learned arithmetic the way I learned it. From the bottom."

"Whose debt?" Laevatain asked.

Zani read to the end of the page. At the bottom, beneath the last sum, the medium's shaking transcription recorded four words.

*Fifteen years of interest.*

"Mine," Zani said.

## II. The Table

They went that night, because Zani would have gone alone otherwise, and the party had a policy about that too.

Raffaella's stall by dark was a tent of red cloth and rented mystery — a round table, a candle, a brass bell, a crystal ball she admitted, unprompted, was a fishing float. She lit the candle with an apology in her posture. "I want it on record that I usually control this part."

"Noted," Laevatain said, and took the seat facing the entrance.

Phoebe sat slowly. Her staff rested against her shoulder; her fingers found the locket at her waist. She had been quiet the whole way down the hill, and her quiet had a texture Zani recognized: the Acolyte listening on a channel the Order had ordered her to close.

"There's something here," Phoebe said. "It's been here a while." She tilted her head, the way she did when the Echoes spoke. "It sounds like a metronome."

"It sounds like a *ledger*," said Raffaella. "Being read aloud. Forever. Do you know what that does to a person's nerves—"

The candle bent sideways.

The tent held its breath. The flame stood at a right angle to its wick, patient, wrong, and in the red cloth silence something began, softly, to count.

*…nine hundred forty. Nine hundred sixty-two and a half. Nine hundred eighty-five…*

A boy's voice. Unbroken. Reciting compound interest with the flat fluency of a child who had learned it as a survival skill, the way other children learn which dogs bite.

Zani's scars warmed under her shirt. Ten years of stored impacts, humming in their channels, recognizing something.

"Raphael," she said.

The counting stopped.

The candle straightened.

*"You remember my name."* The voice sat down across from her — that was the only way to describe it; the air at the empty chair acquired weight, and attention, and a posture. *"Good. That saves a step. I remember yours. Both of them."*

"I'm sorry." It left her before strategy could stop it, before ten years of composure could file it correctly. "Raphael. I've carried you every day since—"

*"Stop."*

The word landed like a slammed drawer.

*"I've been dead eleven years and I've had one visitor and she's a fraud—"*

"—a *working professional*—" Raffaella whispered.

*"—so I have limited time and less patience, and you're spending both on the wrong account."* The chair-shaped weight leaned forward. *"Your guilt. I've watched it. It's very thorough. You confessed to my father's knife. You confessed to your friends. You buried your other self in public with full honors."* A pause with a fifteen-year-old's cruelty in it, which is the cleanest cruelty there is. *"It's a beautiful ledger, banker. Every entry is about you."*

Phoebe's hand found Zani's wrist under the table. Zani had stopped breathing; she started again.

"Then tell me what you want."

*"Now she asks."* The candle flame shivered — a laugh, maybe. Then the voice dropped its edge, and underneath the edge was a boy, and the boy was tired. *"I ran numbers for the Bone Tithes. You know what I ran? Collections. Who owed, how much, how far behind. Every morning a courier gave me the day's book and every night I gave it back balanced. Fifteen years old and I could tell you the debt position of three districts."* A beat. *"You burned the couriers. You burned the collectors. You burned the enforcers, and one night in the Coalyard you burned the room I was standing in."*

"I know."

*"You never burned the book."*

The tent went very quiet.

*"The debts survived you, banker. Every one. The gang died and the paper lived, because paper always lives, and somebody owned that paper before the Bone Tithes ever collected on it, and somebody owns it now."* The counting rhythm crept back into his voice, deliberate now, a weapon being loaded digit by digit. *"Account 4-7-0-3-3. Averardo Vault. That's the top of my ledger. That's where the money went every week of my life and every week of my death. I never got a name. I got a number."* The chair-weight turned, unmistakably, toward her. *"You worked in that building for fifteen years. Best Employee. So here is my unfinished business, Nightwalker, and it isn't your soul: **run my numbers. Find the man at the top of the page. Bring him to the table.**"*

Laevatain spoke for the first time since the candle bent.

"Bring him to *which* table?" Her violet eyes were on the empty chair, steady. "A court's? Or this one?"

The presence considered her. When it answered, it answered slowly, and it answered honest, and the honesty was the worst part.

*"I've had eleven years to think about that,"* Raphael said. *"Ask me again when she finds him."*

The candle went out.

## III. Audit

The argument happened at breakfast, because their arguments always happened at breakfast; it was the only meal they were all awake for.

"It could be a Tacet phenomenon wearing his voice." Laevatain ate the way she did everything, mechanically, fuel in. "Grief leaves a frequency. You've been broadcasting his name for a decade. Something could have learned the shape of it."

"It knew the vig structure of the Coalyard book," Zani said.

"*You* know the vig structure of the Coalyard book. You audited half those debts before you ever burned them. If it's an echo of *you*, it knows everything you know."

Phoebe set down the teapot with a click that was, for Phoebe, a raised voice.

"It's him."

They looked at her.

"I've listened to Echoes since I was a girl. I know the difference between a recording and a *person*." Her hands folded in her lap, immaculate, and shook once anyway. "A recording repeats. He *argued*. He interrupted her apology because he'd heard it before and it bored him." She looked at Zani, and her voice softened into the register she saved for hard things. "Zani. I forgave you in that tavern. I meant it. I'd mean it again." A breath. "But I've been sitting with something since the candle went out, and it's this: my forgiveness settled the account between *you and me*. It was never mine to settle the one between you and him. I gave you a gift and we both let it stand in for a payment." She smiled — small, unfaltering, costing something. "He came back for the payment."

Silence, into which Laevatain, sharpening a knife she had no immediate plans for, said the practical thing.

"Then the question's simple. Does the account exist?" *Skrik*, went the whetstone. "The dead can be real and wrong at the same time. Ghost or echo or a boy with a grudge — 47033 either exists or it doesn't. Numbers don't grieve. Check the numbers."

Zani stood. She went to the corner where her old life lived in a locked case — the banker's coat, the brass knuckles, a clipboard with a decade of muscle memory in it — and she opened the case, and she took the clipboard.

"I approved a credit renewal for account 47033," she said, "in my third year at Averardo. And my seventh. And my eleventh." Her voice was doing the level, far-away thing again. "Charitable lending consortium. Micro-loans, debt relief, orphan funds. Clean paper. Beautiful paper. I signed off on it three times."

Phoebe went pale. "The *orphan funds*—"

"I know the name on the incorporation documents," Zani said. "Everyone in Ragunna knows the name. He endows a hospital wing. He buys distressed debt from failed lenders — for mercy, the papers say, pennies on the gold — and restructures it into *manageable installments*." Her tail moved once, a slow lash. "For fifteen years I stood at that vault window in the mornings and I hunted his collectors at night, and I never once put the day and the night on the same page, because I had built a wall down the middle of myself and I called the wall *discipline*."

She set the clipboard on the table like a drawn sword.

"Ottavio Fedele," she said. "The Coalyard's creditor. My best client. The man who owned the Bone Tithes' book before them, through them, and after them." She looked up, and her coagulated-blood eyes had gone to slits. "Both of me worked for him. Neither of me knew."

Nobody spoke for a while.

Then Laevatain reached into her coat and drew out her ledger — the object she treated with gentleness, the one whose left pages held the saved and whose right pages held the lost — and she set it beside the clipboard, and she opened it to the middle, where a page had been left blank for years for reasons she had never explained.

"You'll want one of these," she said. "The version of this where you go to Ragunna alone with a shield and come back with a body — I've run it. It ends with his lawyers, your warrant, and a fifteen-year-old still counting in a tent." She tapped the clipboard once. "The version where you go as an auditor, I haven't seen before. Show me."

Zani looked at the ledger. At the clipboard. At the two lives lying side by side on a breakfast table, touching for the first time.

"Phoebe," she said. "Write to Carlotta. Tell her the Best Employee wants her personnel file back."

## IV. Credit

Ottavio Fedele received her in a study that smelled of cedar and clean money, on the mezzanine of a building his charity owned, beneath a portrait of himself in which he was younger and had done the world fewer favors. He was seventy and silver and delighted.

"Zani." He came around the desk with both hands out. "The Vault's prodigal. When the Montellis wrote that you were consulting again I told my secretary, *cancel everything*. Fifteen years you kept my accounts and never one error. Do you know I asked for you by name? Every renewal."

"I remember, Don Fedele." She let him take her hand. Her scars lay quiet under her sleeve, warm, charged, waiting, ten years of stored violence one exhale from release, and she filed the exhale away and smiled her window smile. "It's why I'm here. The consortium's paper. The distressed-debt acquisitions." She set her case on his desk and opened it: files, schedules, her own handwriting from three past decades. "There are legacy tranches from the post-Lament years that predate modern filing. Regulatory exposure. I've come to help you restructure it before someone less friendly reads it."

"You see?" He beamed at the ceiling, at the portrait, at the world's fine sense of order. "*This* is service. Sit, sit."

She sat. She walked him through it the way she had walked a hundred clients through a hundred restructurings — page, initial, page, initial — and the pages were real, and the schedules were real, and buried in the schedules, cross-referenced in her own precise hand, was the Coalyard book: acquisition dates, transfer instruments, collection continuity, a paper spine connecting Fedele's charity to the Bone Tithes' ledger through eleven years and four shell consortiums, every vertebra of it requiring, to become legally legible, only one thing.

His signature attesting the schedules complete and true.

He signed. He signed the way rich men sign, generously, while talking about something else.

"You know," he said, at the third-to-last page, pen moving, "I followed your other career too."

The room changed temperature.

Zani's hand kept turning pages at the same speed.

"The Nightwalker." Fedele's smile had grown a second layer, and under the second layer his eyes were doing arithmetic of their own. "Oh, I put it together years ago — the schedule overlaps, the convenient audits. I never said a word. Why would I?" He signed the second-to-last page. "You were the finest collections instrument I never had to pay. Every runner you burned, I insured. Every crew you broke, I bought their book at auction the following month for a copper on the gold. Rivals, liabilities, loose ends — you *pruned* for me, my dear, for a decade, for free, out of *righteousness*." He said the word the way other men say *dessert*. "I used to raise a glass to you. Truly. So whatever this visit really is—" the pen hovered over the final page, and his smile arrived at its foundation, which was granite "—remember that everything the vigilante did fed me. If she comes out of retirement in my study, she'll feed me one last time. I have a testament with instructions and lawyers with imaginations. Sit *down*, employee."

She had risen half an inch. She hadn't noticed. Her scars were singing.

*One release,* the old voice in her offered, the alarm-clock voice, the Coalyard voice. *One. He's confessing. He's* proud.

She looked at his throat. At the pen. At the last unsigned page.

She sat down.

"You misunderstand the visit, Don Fedele," she said, in the voice of the Best Employee, which had always been her most dangerous one. "The Nightwalker's dead. I attended the funeral." She slid the final page across the desk. "I'm here as your auditor. Sign, please. Bottom line."

He studied her. Whatever he was looking for — heat, guilt, a hand drifting toward brass knuckles — he found a clipboard, and a level red gaze, and a woman waiting for a signature with the patience of furniture.

He signed the bottom line.

"Thank you." She squared the pages, tapped them twice, and filed them. "For the record: page fourteen, schedule C, line 47 — the attested acquisition chain for the Coalyard tranche — establishes criminal continuity between your consortium and the Bone Tithes syndicate under the Ragunna Receivership Statutes, and your signature converts it from my allegation into your affidavit." She closed the case. The latch clicked in the enormous silence. "Copies reached the Magistratura, the Montelli family counsel, and two newspapers as of this morning. Yours is the original. I believe in complete records."

Fedele had gone the color of his portrait's undercoat.

"You—" His voice found itself. "Whatever they pay you—"

"Nobody pays me." Zani stood, and gathered her coat, and at the door she paused, because there was one more entry to make, and she made it looking at the man rather than the portrait. "You raised a glass to the Nightwalker. Here's what you drank to. Eleven years ago, in the Coalyard, she killed a boy named Raphael. Fifteen. He ran your numbers, through four sets of hands you built precisely so you'd never have to know his name." Her tail was still. Her voice was still. Everything about her had arrived, finally, at the same place at the same time. "You'll learn it now. It'll be in the filing. It'll be in the papers. It'll be read aloud at your trial, and every gold piece is going to walk backward from your accounts to his book, in public, line by line, with interest."

She let herself out.

Behind her, faint, from somewhere in the cedar-smelling air of the study — she would never be sure, and she declined ever afterward to be sure — something young and satisfied whispered:

*…carried forward.*

## V. Balance

They burned the last candle at Raffaella's table a month later, the night the receivership order came down and the Fedele consortium's assets were frozen pending distribution to a claimant class of four hundred surviving debtors, the medium having refused payment for the session with the expression of a woman making the largest charitable donation of her career.

The candle bent.

*"Well,"* said Raphael.

"Well," said Zani.

*"You read the filings aloud at his arraignment. I heard it. The whole gallery heard it. You read* like a banker.*"*

"I am a banker."

*"You're a paladin with a filing habit."* The voice was lighter tonight. Younger. The counting under it had slowed to something like a resting pulse. *"Four hundred names in the claimant class. I knew about sixty of them. Old Marta from the fish row. The Bellucci twins. They're going to get* paid, *banker. Dead money walking back down the hill."* A pause. *"That was the business. That was all of it."*

Phoebe leaned forward. She had waited a month to ask, and she asked it gently, and she asked it anyway.

"And Zani?"

The presence went quiet. In the quiet, Zani sat with her hands flat on the red cloth, and waited for the verdict she had spent eleven years both dreading and applying for.

*"You want me to forgive her,"* Raphael said at last. *"You forgave her. Her friends forgave her. My* father *put down the knife. And she's sitting there right now with her hands flat on the table hoping I'll close the account so she can finally stop carrying it."* The candle flame tilted toward Zani, and the boy's voice came down to her level, and it was kind the way a fair price is kind. *"No."*

Zani closed her eyes.

*"Open them. Listen. Forgiveness is a gift and gifts are for the living to give each other; I checked, it's in the rules down here somewhere. What I've got is a ledger, same as you, and here's your line in mine: one death. Mine. Unrecoverable. It stays on the page."* The flame steadied. *"But there's a second column now. There's been a second column since the arraignment. Four hundred names, and the man who ate my whole life choking on his own signature, and a banker who put both halves of herself on one page to do it. The entry stands. So does the offset."* A breath that no lungs took. *"You don't get to close my page, Nightwalker. You get to carry it balanced. That's the whole deal. That's the* best *deal. Take it before I compound."*

Zani opened her eyes. They were wet, and her voice, when it came, was the drier for it.

"Terms accepted."

*"Signed and witnessed,"* said Raphael, and for a moment — one — the weight in the empty chair felt like a kid putting his feet up on the table, done with his shift, the day's book balanced, the courier paid. *"Hey. Fraud."*

Raffaella jumped. "Working professional."

*"Widow Ossari's husband hated her cooking. He wants her to buy the bakery on Coin Street with the insurance money. She already knows. Tell her anyway."* The flame began, gently, to gutter. *"There's your one real reading for the year. Don't waste it."*

"Raphael." Zani leaned in, because the candle was going, and there was one entry left. "The table. You said ask again when I found him. A court, or this one." Her hands stayed flat on the cloth. "If you'd said this one. If you'd asked for him dead."

The flame held on, low and blue, for exactly as long as the answer took.

*"I know,"* said Raphael, fifteen forever, satisfied at last. *"That's why I made you wait to ask. You'd have done it, and I spent eleven years wanting it, and it would have made us both* his *one more time."* The blue light dipped. *"He got the ledger. We got the balance. Night, banker."*

The candle went out on its own, all the way down, the way candles do when the room is finally empty.

---

They walked up the hill in the dark, the three of them, Septimont's arena glow at their backs and the boarding house lamp ahead, and nobody said anything for three streets because everything load-bearing had already been said.

At the fourth street Laevatain reached into her coat and handed Zani a book. Plain, black, blank, and its twin rode in Laevatain's own coat with a decade of names in it.

"Left pages and right pages," Laevatain said. "You know the system."

Zani turned it over in her hands. "You've carried yours for years. Does it get lighter?"

"It gets *legible*." Laevatain looked ahead, up the hill, at the lamp. "That's better."

In her room that night, Zani opened her desk drawer and took out the doll — the small plush one, the kidnapped girl's gratitude, worn soft at the seams from a decade of being touched only on the worst nights — and she sat it on the desk where it could supervise, and she opened the black book to the first spread.

On the right page she wrote: *Raphael. Fifteen. Coalyard. Mine.*

On the left page she wrote, in the smaller, denser hand of a woman with a great deal to enter and mornings enough to enter it: *Claimant class of four hundred, distributions pending. M. Ossari, bakery, Coin Street — check in spring.*

The pages faced each other across the binding, and touched nowhere, and belonged to the same book.

She left it open on the desk to dry, turned down the lamp, and — for the first time in eleven years without setting an alarm —

slept.

# Ladies' Night

The east-wind tile had a chip on its lower corner that looked like a turnip.

Buling turned it over with her thumb, set it upright in her wall with a sharp *clack*, and lifted the yellow paper talisman off her forehead so she could spit a sunflower-seed shell into the saucer at her elbow. When she let go, the paper dropped back against the bridge of her nose with a dry flutter.

Across the green felt, Ciaccona rested her chin on her fist and squinted at her own rack. Her splinted left forefinger stuck straight out over the mahogany rail like a wooden signpost.

“If you discard the three of bamboo,” Ciaccona told Mingxiao, “the dragon on your left is going to rob the table and buy more sorghum with my rent money.”

“I don't need the three of bamboo,” Longjue said peacefully. Her bare feet were propped on the lower stretcher of the table, ankle over ankle above the frosted flagstones, and her red lacquer gourd hung from the corner of her chair by its teal cord. “Throw it, Mingxiao. Don't listen to the lute player. She's been holding four winds since the kettle boiled.”

Mingxiao didn't look at either of them. She had her brass carpenter's square hooked at her belt and her sleeves pinned back with wooden clips. She pulled a tile from the end of her row, set it down dead square in the middle of the felt, and said, “Nine of characters.”

“Aiya,” Buling muttered, reaching for the dead wall instead. “Nobody in this town knows how to feed a neighbour.”

Outside the tea stall's canvas awning, the second terrace was so quiet she could hear the ice crust settling in the drainage ditch down by the cooper's arch.

The festival wires overhead were bare iron now, stripped of their red paper ribs and sagging between the eaves under a sky the colour of wet slate. Half the wine-shops down the lane had their wooden shutters hooked halfway up on the chains, neither open nor shut. Next door at the steamed-bun barrow, Old Ma sat on an upturned flour tub with both hands shoved up his padded sleeves, watching his own breath go white in the cold. Nobody had walked past the stall since the noon relief changed at the lower gate.

Buling picked up her teacup, remembered it was plain roasted buckwheat instead of coffee, made a face at the sediment, and drank it anyway.

Across the cobbles, the slatted door of the Red Ladle creaked open on its iron pintles.

Auntie Luo came out onto the step in her quilted wool vest with a bucket of warm dishwater in one hand and a rag in the other. She stood for a moment looking up the empty lane toward the plaza steps, then down toward the ferry turn, where a single stray dog was sniffing a frozen cabbage leaf.

With a heavy grunt, she hauled the tavern's tall black chalkboard off its wall-peg, propped it against the horse-trough, and scrubbed the rag across last week's Waking Moon prices until the slate ran grey and steaming in the frost.

All four women at the mahjong table stopped reaching for the wall and watched her.

Auntie Luo fished a stub of white chalk out of her apron pocket, wedged her spectacles higher on her nose, and began to write in large, square, furious characters that squeaked across the drying slate.

> **LADIES' NIGHT**
> **WOMEN ONLY — SECOND CUP FREE**
> **FREE SPICED RIVER PEANUTS AT EVERY BENCH**
> **THREE ARROWS IN THE COPPER POT WINS THE TOP-SHELF WINTERSWEET JAR**

Auntie Luo blew the chalk dust off her knuckles, planted both hands on her hips to inspect the board, and carried the bucket back inside, leaving the door propped an inch ajar so the smell of hot ginger and charcoal smoke crept out across the stones.

Buling tilted her head until the talisman slid sideways off her brow.

“Second cup free,” Buling read aloud. “And pitch-pot for the wintersweet jar. That jar has been sitting on her beam since before the south road closed. I tried to haggle for it two weeks ago and she chased me out with a broom.”

Longjue's toes uncurled on the stretcher. Her teal eyes fixed on the chalkboard across the lane with the focused, unblinking stillness she usually saved for morning forms in the orchard.

“Three arrows,” Longjue said. “From what distance?”

“In that room?” Buling snorted, fishing her Terminal out of her silk pocket with one hand. “Six paces max, unless you stand in the kitchen doorway with your heel in the pickled turnips.”

She thumbed the camera open, leaned past Ciaccona's elbow to get the whole chalkboard and the steaming horse-trough in the frame, and snapped the picture. While the tile wall waited, her thumb was already flying across the glass, opening a fresh thread on the Huangboard under **BigThunder88**:

*Second terrace dead like graveyard after festival, everybody broke already. Auntie Luo at Red Ladle just put out free second cup + pitch-pot for the ten-year wintersweet. Who says Xuanfang got no nightlife.*

“Are you telling the whole province before we get the good bench by the stove?” Ciaccona asked, peering sideways at the screen.

“Nobody on the forum lives on the second terrace except clerks,” Buling said, hitting post and dropping the Terminal face-up beside her saucer. “And clerks are men. Board says women only. Very clear.”

“I'm going,” Longjue said.

“Obviously you're going,” Buling said. “You'd go if the sign said free vinegar.”

Mingxiao reached out and squared the discarded nine of characters with the tip of her forefinger so it lined up with the weave of the felt. “The door isn't open yet. The oil lamp isn't on the hook.”

“She's lighting the brazier,” Longjue said, her nose lifting once toward the crack of the tavern door. “Charcoal's still smoking blue. Twenty minutes.”

Ciaccona didn't reach for her next tile. She tapped her three good left fingers in a slow, rolling triplet along the wooden back of her rack, looking from the chalkboard across the cobbles up toward the steep stone steps that climbed to the storehouse terrace.

“Four people is a card game,” Ciaccona said thoughtfully. “Auntie Luo's got six benches in there and a whole loft of stools.”

Buling narrowed her eyes at her. “Who else you thinking?”

“Everybody,” Ciaccona said.

Mingxiao looked up from the wall. “Everybody who?”

“The salt-house,” Ciaccona said, her grin spreading slow and wicked across her face. “The kitchen. Your lot from the Vale. The Paragon. The fox.”

Buling stared at her. She reached up, pushed her winter hat back an inch on her silver hair, and pointed a sunflower seed at Ciaccona's nose.

“You want to bring the Sword Transcendent and the Sentinel of Mengzhou to a second-cup-free night at Auntie Luo's,” Buling said. “With Ruoyun.”

“Especially with Ruoyun,” Ciaccona said. “If we leave Ruoyun behind she'll stand on the roof and shout through the chimney.”

Longjue slapped both palms flat on the table hard enough to make the sunflower saucers jump.

“The Landlady,” Longjue crowed, her whole face lighting up. “She owes me thirty-one strings and she hasn't bought me a drink since the plaza! Wait till she sees the wintersweet jar!”

“Eleven strings,” Buling corrected automatically.

“Thirty-one! With interest!” Longjue reached out to the live wall, drew her tile without looking at it, and slammed it face-up onto the felt between them.

It was the red dragon, completing a clean self-drawn hand of all-triplets down the length of her rack.

Longjue didn't even wait for Buling to pay up. She shoved her chair back across the frost, hooked the red gourd onto her sash with one hand, and stood up barefoot in the lane with her wide teal sleeves belling in the draft, leaving the winning tiles standing right where they were on the cloth.

***

At the upper terrace table, the cabbage soup had a skin of yellow chicken oil on top that kept the steam trapped underneath until you broke it with a spoon.

Ciaccona didn't sit down in her usual place by Cartethyia. She planted one booted foot on the end of the bench, rested her lute case against her thigh, and waited until Wen slid the last plate of scallion cakes out through the kitchen hatch.

“Ladies,” Ciaccona announced, pitching her voice into the bright, carrying register she used for tavern squares when the wind was off the canal. “Down on the second terrace, at the Red Ladle, Auntie Luo has just chalked the board. Women only, second cup free, free spiced peanuts at every bench, and three arrows in the copper pot wins a ten-year jar of wintersweet wine. We are going at the first bell.”

Down the length of the scarred pine board, twelve spoons stopped at twelve different heights.

“No,” Iuno said.

She didn't even look up from the tea-strainer she was tapping against the rim of her cup.

“Iuiu!” Mei protested at once, her spoon splashing back into her bowl. “It's Ladies' Night! The second cup is free!”

“The second terrace is forty-eight stone steps down and forty-eight back up in a freezing draft,” Iuno said smoothly, setting the strainer on its saucer. “And the last time you were left unsupervised near local spirits, you tried to eat a porcelain garnish.”

“It was a radish,” Mei said, already shifting her weight on the bench so her boots came unhooked from the stretcher. “I'll go slow! Look, my coat's already buttoned!”

“Your coat has been buttoned since noon because you lost the top toggle,” Iuno said. She turned her head to look at Ciaccona, her silver-blue hair catching the orange light from Wen's stove. “Does this establishment have benches with backs, or only those three-legged stools that tip when someone leans?”

“Two long settles against the south wall with sheepskin throws,” Ciaccona lied promptly, and then amended, “Wait—one settle, and I will personally steal a cushion off Auntie Luo's chair for the capo's spine.”

Iuno looked at Mei, whose golden eyes were wide and fixed on her with shameless, unblinking hope, and then down at her own small ruled pad lying blank and closed beside the soy cruet.

“One small cup of warmed yellow wine,” Iuno said, each clause laid down like mortar between bricks. “No sorghum. You sit on the settle with your back to the wall and your feet out of the door-draft. And when we come up the hill, two people walk you up the steps and you do not stop halfway to help a mason carry a beam.”

“Deal!” Mei said, lunging half off the bench toward the terrace steps before Lupa reached across Chisa's shoulder, grabbed a fistful of the back of Mei's woollen collar, and hauled her squarely back onto the plank.

“Finish your soup,” Lupa ordered. “You've got three dumplings left in the bottom.”

“I'm twenty-nine,” Amy said, pointing her chopsticks at Iuno across the pickle dish. “Before anybody starts counting birthdays on their fingers, I am legally and chronologically entitled to the free second cup.”

“You are nineteen in the liver,” Iuno told her. “You may have plum wine with hot water in it.”

“I will take the plum wine without the water and sit next to Lupa so you can't reach me,” Amy countered cheerfully. “Lupa, we're entering the arrow toss.”

Lupa snorted, tearing a scallion cake in half with her teeth. “Tavern wine's half well-water. Why would I walk down forty steps for watered wine when Wen's got the kettle on right here?”

As she said it, her left ear flicked sideways toward the black sleeve beside her.

Chisa finished chewing her mouthful of cabbage, set her chopsticks parallel across the rim of her bowl, and looked at Ciaccona.

“I have never been to a tavern,” Chisa said. “I want to go.”

Lupa's bench-end creaked as she shoved her bowl forward. “Right. I'm going. Somebody's got to watch the keeper so they don't water her cup.”

“Nobody waters the first cup on a dead Tuesday, darling,” Ciaccona said, turning toward the middle of the board. “Suisui?”

Suisui had her teacup balanced on her palm and her back straight as a reed. “I have three folders of waybills from the Mengzhou river-gate that require my seal before the morning courier leaves,” she said regretfully. “A second-terrace wine shop is hardly—Yangyang, hold still, you've got a bit of thread on your collar—hardly the place for Guild correspondence.”

With her free hand, she had already drawn the lacquered horn comb from her sleeve, unpinned the heavy coil at the nape of her neck, and was deftly re-twisting it two inches higher while angling her chin to check her reflection in the dark glass of Wen's propped shutter. On her shoulder, Dumpling gave two brisk clockwork clicks and preened a brass wing-feather.

“You're using the good comb,” Yangyang pointed out quietly.

“One doesn't go out into the lanes looking like a haystack simply because the road was dusty,” Suisui said without missing a twist. “Are you wearing your fur-lined cloak or the heavy cape?”

Yangyang looked down into her soup bowl. The new blue pin-feathers at the front of her collar stirred once in the steam. She looked past Suisui's elbow at the open kitchen hatch, where Wen was scrubbing the iron wok with a bamboo whisk, her grey hair tucked under a faded blue kerchief as it had been every evening since the frost began.

Yangyang stood up, carried her empty bowl two steps to the sill, and set it on the tray.

“Auntie Wen,” Yangyang said. “Do you want to come?”

The bamboo whisk stopped scraping the iron.

Wen looked up through the square hatch at Yangyang, then out past her at the long table, the stacked bowls, and Ciaccona twirling her lute strap round her good wrist.

“The porridge pot's soaked for the morning,” Wen said slowly. “And the garrison boys already took their supper tin down to the gatehouse.”

“Auntie Luo has spiced river peanuts,” Ciaccona offered from the bench end. “She boils them with star anise.”

Wen looked at the bamboo whisk in her hand. She tossed it into the wooden wash-basin with a splash, reached behind her waist to untie the knot of her grease-stained canvas apron, and dropped the apron onto the lid of the flour bin.

“Wait two minutes while I get my padded jacket off the peg,” Wen said, and pulled the hatch-stick out to let the shutter down.

Cartethyia leaned across the table, both hands flat on the pine and her crystal laurel tipping perilously over her left eyebrow. “Cia! Is wintersweet wine sour, or is it the sweet kind that sticks to your teeth?”

“They have dark plum wine in the stone crocks behind the counter, cara,” Ciaccona told her, reaching out with her good hand to hook the laurel back onto Cartethyia's crown. “Sour enough to make your ears ring.”

“Good,” Cartethyia said with deep satisfaction, and stood up so fast her knee knocked the vinegar bottle.

Heavy, brisk footsteps came crunching round the corner of the storehouse from the rope-walk stair.

Ruoyun marched into the lamplight carrying a stack of grey wool blankets so high she had to look round the side of them through her brass spectacles. Her ruffled teal cuffs were spotless, and her jaw was set like a gin-trap.

“If one more Artisan from the west dormitory asks me which end of the mattress goes toward the wall,” Ruoyun announced to the courtyard at large, “I am throwing the lot of them back over the boundary stones in their nightshirts.”

“Leave the blankets on the crate and come down to the Red Ladle, sweeper,” Ciaccona called, resting her chin on the neck of her lute. “Ladies' Night. Second cup's on the house.”

Ruoyun stopped dead by the gate-post. She looked Ciaccona up and down over the top blanket.

“I have forty-two Celestials on the upper terrace who have just discovered that cold floorboards make their hips ache,” Ruoyun said frigidly. “I do not frequent low drinking-dens. And if you are contemplating inviting the Xuan Paragon—” She turned her head sharply toward the far end of the bench, where Qingxiao sat wrapped to the chin in her dark storehouse oilcloth, methodically eating the last quarter of a plain steamed bun. “—you may save your breath. The Paragon has a toothache, a recovering chill, and no business sitting in a cloud of wet dog and cheap millet mash.”

Qingxiao swallowed the last bite of her bun, brushed a single crumb off her white knee, and stood up.

“I'm going,” Qingxiao said.

Ruoyun's spectacles flashed in the stove-light. “A-Qing.”

“You said the soup here is finished,” Qingxiao said, her voice altogether level. “There are chestnuts at the tavern. Hot things are good for teeth.”

For two seconds Ruoyun stood rigid behind her mountain of wool. Then she spun on her dark Mary Jane heel, spotted a young Vale mason walking past with an empty hod, and shoved the entire six-foot stack of blankets squarely into his chest until he staggered.

“Second door past the rope-walk,” Ruoyun barked at him. “Tell Gu if he puts his boots on the ticking I will skin him at dawn.” She smoothed her pleated skirt with both palms and fell in beside Qingxiao's elbow. “Someone has to see she doesn't drink out of a cracked bowl.”

“Speaking of cracked bowls!” Longjue's voice boomed up from the grain-shed alley as she came bounding up the frost-whitened steps two at a time, Buling trotting twenty paces behind her with one hand holding her round winter hat on her head. Longjue planted her bare feet below the low eave of the grain-shed roof and pointed her red gourd straight up at the ridge-tiles. “Landlady! Down you come! The Red Ladle's got a ten-year jar on the beam and you're buying my first round!”

Up on the dark ridge of the grain-shed, nine gold tails shifted against the frost.

Hsin leaned her chin over the gutter-tile in her own shape, her fox ears tipped forward and a bundle of red-ruled conduit slips held in one hand.

“I am auditing the third-terrace sluice valves,” Hsin said downward, her voice lofty and melodious. “Unlike my Celestials, I have an entire fortress to administer before the snow comes.”

“Those are last month's slips, I can see the water-stain from here lah!” Buling called up, puffing as she reached the top step and leaning both hands on her knees. “And Wen just shut the kitchen! Nobody's making you late supper!”

“I don't require—”

“Thirty-one strings, Landlady!” Longjue rattled the gourd over her head. “Come down and throw arrows for the jar, or I'm telling the whole tavern you're scared of Auntie Luo!”

Hsin's ears flattened against her hair. She looked down at the conduit slips, then at Wen coming out the kitchen door buttoning her thick padded jacket to the throat, and then at Mei already waving from the head of the stairs.

“Eleven strings,” Hsin said tartly. “And I will stay for precisely one cup to demonstrate that my aim is superior to yours.”

She did not jump from the eaves. She walked along the lower beam to the stacked grain crates by the wall and stepped down them one box at a time, gathering her nine tails over her left arm to keep the dark-tipped one out of the slush.

By the gate-post, Suoming sat on her upturned wooden bucket with Nine Shadows upright between her knees and her iron-shod boots dangling clear of the stone.

Ciaccona tipped her lute toward the bucket. “Elder?”

“No,” Suoming said.

Hsin walked past the gate-post toward the top of the second-terrace stair, her loose cuffs swinging in the cold draft.

Suoming looked at the back of Hsin's coat. She slid off the bucket, hooked the bamboo shaft of the umbrella into the crook of her elbow, and walked four paces behind the tails without looking at Ciaccona at all.

At the top of the long stone stair, the whole party bunched together between the frost-rimed balustrades while scarves were wound and mittens pulled on.

Amy stood up on the bottom plinth of the stone lion, pointing a mittened finger down the bobbing line of heads.

“One, two, three, four—move your horn, Longjue, you're blocking Chisa—seven, eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen, fifteen, sixteen, seventeen!”

“Who's seventeen?” Lupa demanded, squinting back over her shoulder. “There's fifteen of us.”

“I counted Dumpling,” Amy said.

“That's sixteen,” Suisui said, adjusting her fur collar. “Who did you count twice?”

“Nobody! Wait—one, two, three—Hsin, stop moving your tails, one of them looks like Buling's hat in the dark—fourteen, fifteen—where did the Elder go?”

“Under your elbow,” Suoming's flat voice came from directly beneath Lupa's ribs. “Walk. The heat's going out of my tea.”

***

From the doorway of the Red Ladle, Amy had a clear view of Auntie Luo's face when the latch went up.

The tavern-keeper had been leaning both elbows on her wiped counter, gloomily paring a frost-bitten turnip into a bucket with a small curved knife while a single tallow dip guttered in a saucer beside the abacus.

Then Lupa shouldered the slatted door open, and sixteen women, nine gold tails, an oil-paper umbrella, and a clockwork bird came piling in out of the freezing lane in a solid wall of wool coats, clinking weapons, and white breath.

Auntie Luo's paring knife stopped halfway through the turnip peel. Her mouth fell open, stayed open as the Sword Transcendent and the Sentinel of Mengzhou squeezed past the pickle barrels behind Mei, and then split into a grin so wide it showed every gold tooth at the back of her jaw.

“Xiao Luo!” Auntie Luo roared at the ceiling beams, flinging the turnip straight into the bucket. “Get down here! Bring the four-legged stools out of the loft! Bring the long pine board off the sawhorses! Poke up that charcoal before the ladies freeze!”

A door banged overhead, and a gangly boy of fifteen or sixteen with a Mengzhou town braid and flour on his knees came clattering down the ladder with three stools hooked over each arm.

For three minutes the small, low-beamed room was nothing but scraping pine legs, banging benches, and Lupa arguing with Longjue over who got the corner nearest the copper pitch-pot. Two square tables were shoved end to end against the south settle, a third was wedged crosswise at the foot, and Amy scrambled over the back of the settle to claim the inside corner between Mei and Yangyang, where she could see the whole board and reach two different peanut dishes at once.

The charcoal stove in the middle of the flags roared up orange as Auntie Luo dumped a scuttle of fresh nuts onto the coals. Within two minutes the windows had steamed blind white from the top pane to the sill, and the air smelled gloriously of toasted star anise, wet wool, hot rice wine, and charred pine.

The drinks came out on three wet lacquer trays, carried at a dead run and set down in no order that anybody had asked for.

“Who had the millet ale?” Auntie Luo shouted over the din, slamming a thick stoneware mug with a head of grey-white foam down in front of Lupa.

Lupa peered into the mug, took a long, suspicious swallow that left a line of foam across the bridge of her nose, and banged it back on the wood. “What did you put in the mash? Coriander?”

“Mountain barley and bitter-root, same as my mother brewed!” Auntie Luo barked back proudly. “You want city water, go drink out of the horse-trough!”

“Needs more hops,” Lupa growled, wrapping both paws tightly around the stoneware handle so Xiao Luo couldn't take it away.

Beside her, Chisa lifted a small blue-glazed cup of sweet fermented rice wine, looked at the white grains floating in the clear liquor, and took a single cautious sip. She held it in her mouth for two seconds, swallowed, and looked at Lupa.

“It's warm,” Chisa said. “I like it.”

She drank the rest of the cup down in three even swallows like plain water and set the empty porcelain neatly on the board. Lupa stared at her cup, then bellowed for the boy to bring the second one since it was free.

Down the table, Cartethyia had been given a dark earthenware cup of sour-plum wine. She took a mouthful, and both her eyes squeezed shut at once; her shoulders came up to her ears in a tremendous, happy shiver that knocked her crystal laurel sideways over her temple.

“Oh,” Cartethyia breathed when she got her mouth working again. “Cia. Try it. It tastes like an unripe lemon that's angry at you.”

“I'll take your word for it, cara,” Ciaccona said, already tipping an unglazed bowl of sixty-day sorghum against Longjue's across the peanut shells and knocking half of it back without blinking.

On Amy's left, Iuno held a small white porcelain cup of warmed yellow wine with a thin curl of ginger floating on top. Before she drank, her gaze went to Mei's cup. Auntie Luo, having caught Iuno's eye from across the counter, had poured Mei's measure into a shallow condiment cup half the size of the others.

Iuno gave a tiny, satisfied nod and sipped her wine.

Mei held the condiment cup up between her thumb and forefinger and squinted through it at the lamp. “Amymy. Look at this. A bird couldn't wash its feet in this.”

“Drink your thimble, Ma,” Amy said, grinning and sliding three peeled spiced peanuts onto Mei's saucer.

On Amy's right, Suisui held her shallow glazed cup between two fingers at chest height, the exact angle at which she held a folded fan at a chamber concert, keeping her embroidered cuff three inches clear of the damp tabletop. Beside her, Yangyang sat with both hands wrapped around a cup of warm pear-and-rice wine. She hadn't touched the peanuts yet. Every half-minute she lifted the cup to her lips, took a sip barely big enough to wet her tongue, and looked round the noisy room with her eyes bright and her blue collar-feathers fluffed out soft against her throat in the heat.

At the far table end, Qingxiao held a steaming mug of ginger-spiced wine in both palms and drank it down at the steady, unhurried pace of someone hydrating after a three-hour sword form.

Beside her, Ruoyun sat bolt upright with her hands folded over her spotless cuffs and her spectacles fogging at the lower rims.

“One,” Ruoyun said aloud to the ceiling beam.

Qingxiao set the mug down and reached for a roasted chestnut.

Xiao Luo came bustling round the foot of the table with a fresh tray balanced on his palm, his Mengzhou braid swinging over his shoulder. He stopped beside the upturned butter-firkin at the corner, where Suoming sat with Nine Shadows propped between her knees and her iron-shod boots swinging two inches clear of the floorboards.

The boy looked down at the top of Suoming's red head—which came barely higher than the soy-sauce cruet—and gave her a friendly, gap-toothed smile.

“Auntie says the plum wine's too sharp for little girls,” Xiao Luo said kindly, leaning down with his hands on his knees. “I've got a jar of sugared winter-melon water in the back pantry if you want a mug, little sister.”

Around the middle of the table, six conversations stopped at once.

Hsin's ears came straight up off her head. Ruoyun's mouth opened; Longjue clapped both hands over her own face and shook with silent laughter from her bare heels to her dragon crests.

Suoming didn't look up from the peanut she was shelling. She didn't correct him, either.

Buling leaned sideways across two benches, lifted her talisman with one finger so the boy could see her red eyes clearly, and tapped the table with her knuckle.

“Boy,” Buling said gently. “You new from Mengzhou, is it?”

“Came up with the mule-carts on Wednesday, miss,” Xiao Luo said, blinking at her hat.

“This *little sister*,” Buling said, pointing her thumb at Suoming, “has been on the Xuanfang muster roll since before your great-grandfather had teeth, and once she was the Intendant of the Ministry of Sentinel Affairs. You give her sugared melon water, she'll turn your umbrella inside out with you still holding it.”

Xiao Luo's face went the exact colour of steamed lard. The lacquer tray wobbled wildly on his palm, three porcelain cups rattling together like castanets.

“I—Intendant—Elder—” he stammered, backing into the pickle barrel.

“White tea if you have it,” Suoming said to the peanut dish. “Boiled water if you don't. Leave the peanuts.”

She reached up, lifted the stoneware saucer of spiced peanuts off the corner of his shaking tray herself, and set it beside her elbow. Xiao Luo fled for the kitchen hatch as if the hounds were behind him.

“Right!” Lupa shouted, shoving the peanut bowls and empty saucers back with a sweep of her forearm to clear a two-foot patch of wet pine in the middle of the table. She planted her right elbow on the wood and pointed her chin at Longjue. “You and me, dragon. Finger-guessing. Loser drinks the sorghum bowl.”

Longjue was up on one knee on the bench before the sentence finished, shoving her wide teal sleeves past her elbows. “Three out of five! No stalling on the call! Pipa, pour the bowls!”

Ciaccona filled two unglazed bowls to the brim from the stoneware jar while Amy hopped up onto the back rail of the settle, snatched the tavern's small tally-slate off the wall-hook, and wedged a piece of chalk between her fingers.

“I have the book!” Amy yelled over the noise. “House rules, no kicking under the table!”

Longjue and Lupa threw their right hands forward into the gap at the exact same instant.

“Two good brothers!” Lupa roared, throwing three fingers.

“Five emperors!” Longjue barked back, throwing two—and catching the five on the beat before Lupa's wrist had finished snapping down.

“Ha!” Longjue slapped the board. “Drink, wolf! You telegraphed the three with your ear!”

“My ear didn't move!” Lupa snarled, grabbing the first bowl of sorghum and draining it in one fiery gulp that made her tail bristle straight out behind the bench. “Again!”

“Three stars!”

“Six roads!”

“Eight horses!”

“All ten!”

The calls went faster than drumsticks on a kettle-head—wooden cuffs thumping the pine, fingers flashing in the orange stove-light, Wen and the Vale mason's wife from the next bench pounding their palms in rhythm while Buling heckled every call from Longjue's blind side. Longjue was roaring at the top of her lungs, her dark hair flying over her shoulders and her face red from the sixty-day spirit, yet her teal eyes never loosened by a fraction of an inch; she caught Lupa on the seven, dropped one on the four, and nailed the nine twice running before Lupa's knuckles could clear her cuff.

Up on the settle-back, Amy drew four neat chalk marks under **DRAGON**, looked down at Lupa's reddening ears, casually wiped one of Longjue's marks off with the side of her thumb while nobody was watching, and then, when Chisa quietly tapped Lupa's knee twice under the table to break Longjue's cadence and Lupa took three bouts in a row, Amy quietly added an extra chalk stroke back onto Longjue's column so the score stayed dead even at four-all.

“Tie!” Amy announced grandly, holding the slate upside down over their heads. “Sudden death for the last peanut!”

Out in the lane, Xiao Luo had been sent back outside with the tall chalkboard and a fresh stick of chalk because three women from the dyer's court had stopped at the window to peer through the steam.

Amy leaned backward over the settle rail, pressing her forehead against the warm glass where the condensation had run down in streaks, and read the boy's fresh lettering upside down as he propped the board back against the horse-trough:

> **LADIES' NIGHT — PACKED OUT**
> **THE SHE-WOLF & THE IMMORTALS ARE IN HERE**
> **NO MEN — NO MULES — BRING YOUR OWN STOOL**

***

When they spilled back out into the lane an hour later so Mei could get a breath of cold air without coal-smoke in it, the sky had come down to the rooftops.

The first proper snow of the winter was falling straight through the gorge cut—not the dry, stinging frost-powder of the last week, but big, slow, silent flakes the size of goose-feathers that drifted lazily through the orange light of the eaves and melted wet on the flagstones.

Yangyang stopped on the bottom step of the Red Ladle and tipped her face up.

She had drunk all of her warm pear-and-rice wine, the free second cup that Auntie Luo had poured before she could cover the rim with her hand, and the bottom inch of Amy's sour-plum mug when Amy wasn't looking. She had never drunk more than a thimble of sweet New Year wine in Mingting in her life.

Her boots felt remarkably light on the cobbles, as if someone had taken the iron nails out of the soles and replaced them with cork. Her ears were glowing hot under her blue-and-white hair, and the snowflakes drifting down between the dark roofs looked extraordinarily bright and funny—each one spinning round on its own little errand before landing with a tiny cold kiss on her cheekbones.

She lifted her right hand out of her wide sheer sleeve and tried to catch a particularly fat flake on the back of her wrist.

It landed three inches to the left on her thumb-knuckle instead. Yangyang looked at her thumb, realized her hand had been nowhere near where she aimed it, and gave a small, quiet laugh that went up at the end.

Across the lane, the double doors of the Brazen Carp flew open with a crash against the outer shutters.

Its keeper—a barrel-chested woman in a greased sheepskin vest with a copper soup-ladle in her fist—marched right out into the falling snow and waved the ladle at them like a battle standard.

“Don't stand there freezing on Auntie Luo's step!” she bellowed across the cobbles. “Third cup's free in the Carp! Hot mutton-and-turnip broth on the house for every table, and four iron braziers going in the main room!”

Two doors further down toward the cooper's arch, an upstairs casement at the Willow Jar banged open, and a thin woman in a green shawl leaned out with a brass hand-gong, beating it with a wooden mallet until the icicles vibrated.

“Half-price on the three-year osmanthus jars!” the Willow Jar keeper shouted down over the gong. “And free fried bean-curd skin with chili oil if the Sword Transcendent comes inside!”

“Chili oil!” Mei called back immediately, turning her head toward the Willow Jar before Iuno's fingers hooked firmly into the back of her coat belt.

“Mutton broth,” Iuno said, steering her squarely toward the Brazen Carp. “With turnips.”

All down the second terrace, the dead lane was waking up behind them. Wooden shutters rattled up into their iron hooks; paper lanterns flared gold one after another along the shop-fronts; women from the weaving sheds and the upper laundries came stepping out of the narrow side-alleys in twos and threes with wool shawls pinned over their heads, drawn out into the snow by the gong and the shouting.

Round the corner from the barracks steps came Warden Liangyu with her heavy pike left behind in the rack for once, her dark hair braided loose down her back and two off-duty women Wardens from the south gate walking at her heels.

“Warden!” Lupa roared, lunging across the slush to clamp an arm around Liangyu's shoulders. “You're late! The dragon's already two bowls up!”

“The night watch is set,” Liangyu said, a slow, startled grin breaking across her face as Lupa dragged her into the middle of the crowd. “Ge told me if I came back before midnight he'd bolt the guardroom door.”

Behind them, Wen had spotted Old Sister Zhao from the dried-mushroom stall standing by the cistern with a basket on her hip, and within ten seconds Wen had confiscated the basket, tucked her arm through Sister Zhao's, and was marching her toward the Brazen Carp while arguing loudly about the price of winter ginger.

In the middle of the lane, Mei had stopped walking to tip her head back and catch a snowflake on her tongue. She missed three in a row, shuffling two steps sideways with her mouth open until a huge wet flake landed squarely on the tip of her nose and stayed there.

Yangyang looked at the white speck sitting on Mei's nose. The pear wine was humming warm behind her breastbone, and the streetlamps made little gold rings around the wet cobbles.

“Before we came up the gorge,” Yangyang said in her plain, quiet voice, “I used to think you were always thinking about something very large when you went quiet.”

Mei lowered her chin and looked at her cross-eyed past the snowflake on her nose. “Yeah?”

“You weren't,” Yangyang said, her mouth curving. “You were just looking to see if the stall at the corner had fried cakes.”

Mei laughed, a short, warm puff of white steam in the snow, and deliberately leaned her right shoulder sideways so it bumped squarely against Yangyang's arm.

“That's a state secret, Outrider,” Mei said softly, leaving her shoulder pressed against Yangyang's sleeve as they started walking again. “And you walk crooked when you're looking at the lamps. You've drifted four feet toward the gutter since the door.”

“I like walking on this side,” Yangyang said simply. “When you look up at the roofs, you bump into me instead of the horse-post.”

Mei's golden eyes crinkled at the corners. She didn't move her shoulder away.

Three paces behind them, walking through the fresh slush beside Suisui, Iuno watched the two of them—the small cerulean feathers at Yangyang's throat lifting once against the dark wool of Mei's collar as Mei's boot-heel angled toward hers.

Iuno did not slow down, and her face did not tighten. She took three unhurried steps forward through the snow, slipped her living right hand neatly through the crook of Mei's left elbow—leaving Mei's right side warm against Yangyang's—and looked straight ahead at the open tavern doors.

“If you are going to steer by the shop-lamps like a pleasure-barge in a fog, sweetheart,” Iuno remarked dryly to the falling snow, “kindly steer toward the door with the mutton broth before my toes freeze.”

“I'm steering!” Mei protested happily, hooked between both of them as they crossed the crown of the lane.

At the kerb-stone in front of the Brazen Carp, a patch of old black ice hid under the new white drift. Yangyang's right heel slid two inches sideways off the bevelled stone.

Before her knee could even dip, Suisui's left hand came out from under her fur cloak, caught Yangyang firmly under the right elbow, and lifted her smoothly up onto the dry flagstone of the threshold without Suisui dropping a single syllable of what she was saying to Ciaccona about Mengzhou canal tolls.

Ahead of them, the keeper in the sheepskin vest braced her hip against the wide double doors of the Brazen Carp, holding them open while a great billow of orange charcoal heat, boiling mutton steam, and roasted-sesame smoke rolled out over their boots into the snow.

***

The Brazen Carp was twice the breadth of the Red Ladle, with four fat cast-iron braziers glowing cherry-red down the centre aisle and a low cedar platform at the back corner where traveling storytellers usually set their clappers.

By the second bell there wasn't an inch of bare bench left in the room.

Iuno sat at the inner angle of the long middle table with her back resting against a squared roof-pillar. The nearest brazier stood four feet from her left side, close enough that the dry radiant heat soaked into the frost-crack across her bare crystal shoulder and kept the ache out of the joint. She had drunk her one small cup of warmed wine at Auntie Luo's and a second small cup here, diluted with a splash of hot ginger broth by her own hand. Under the table, hidden by the overhang of their coats, Mei's right hand rested warm and heavy on Iuno's knee, her thumb rubbing a slow, absent circle against the wool of Iuno's skirt while Mei argued with Amy and Liangyu about whether a mule could swim across a river with two sacks of salt on its back.

Up by the cedar platform, Old Sister Zhao and three women from the second-terrace silk-looms had recognised Suisui's Mingting vowels and were banging their porcelain spoons against their broth bowls, demanding an opera aria from the capital.

Suisui raised her closed fan an inch off the table in protest. “Ladies, please, this is a mountain tavern, and my voice has been through four days of gorge wind—”

“Sing the one about the magistrate's donkey!” Wen shouted from two benches over, her cheeks flushed bright red above her unbuttoned collar and a bowl of wintersweet wine in her hand. “My mother used to sing that when she rolled the noodles!”

Suisui sighed, set her fan down beside her saucer, and stood up in her place. She didn't go up onto the platform; she stood straight-backed between the benches with one hand resting lightly on Yangyang's shoulder, waited for the clatter of bowls to drop, and came in on the opening line of *The Magistrate's Mule*.

She sang it a full quarter-tone flat on purpose, dropping her voice into a rich, chesty contralto that sat right underneath the rattle of the kettle lids.

Up on the corner of the cedar platform, Ciaccona winced once with her whole body at the flat pitch, muttered something in Ragunnese into her collar, and then grinned, sliding her three good left fingers half a fret down the neck of her lute to bend the chord right under Suisui's note so the whole cadence landed fat and square on the beat.

By the fourth bar, half the tavern was slapping the pine boards in time. When Suisui sat back down to a roar of applause and knocking mugs, a broad-shouldered quarry-woman sitting by the wood-pile jumped up with a pair of iron fire-tongs for clappers and launched into a brisk, thoroughly filthy local drinking song about a ferryman's wife who hid three coopers in three different pickle-vats.

Lupa howled with delight and joined in on the chorus at arena volume before she even knew the words to the second line. Beside her, Chisa listened with complete concentration to the second verse, and when the third chorus came round, she repeated the line about the cooper's bung-hole in her clear, calm, level voice with an entirely straight face. Lupa choked on her millet ale and went scarlet to the roots of her hair while Cartethyia clapped both hands over her own ears and giggled into Ciaccona's lute case.

Across the narrow aisle between the braziers, the Hold's old women had taken the table by the kitchen screen.

Longjue and Buling sat wedged hip to hip on the near bench with a fresh stoneware jar between them. Longjue had challenged Buling for the last piece of spiced beef shin on the platter, planting her bare elbow on the grease-spotted wood with her palm open.

“Come on, Big Thunder!” Longjue barked, waggling her fingers. “One pull! You win, you get the beef and I carry you up the hill!”

Buling looked at Longjue's forearm, which had spent the morning pruning frozen persimmon boughs with an iron hook, and instantly slammed both her palms flat on the tabletop on either side of her saucer.

“Surrender!” Buling shouted at the top of her voice. “Surrender already! My wrist is for drawing talismans, not for wrestling orchard water-buffalo! Take the beef, take the plate also!”

Longjue roared with laughter, reached across to whack Buling's winter hat so the yellow talisman swung crooked over her left eye, and picked the beef up with her chopsticks to drop it squarely into Buling's broth bowl anyway.

At the corner of their table, Ruoyun was sitting straighter than the roof-pillar behind Iuno.

For the first hour she had pointed a chopstick at Qingxiao every twenty minutes to announce her cup count. Somewhere during the ferryman's song, however, Buling had taken to quietly topping up Ruoyun's own red-glazed cup from the wintersweet jar whenever Ruoyun turned her head to glare at the quarry-woman. Two bright, hard spots of carmine had appeared high on Ruoyun's cheekbones; her round brass spectacles had slipped a quarter-inch down her nose, and she had not noticed.

Buling reached for the iron kettle to add hot water to Qingxiao's mug. As she leaned across, her yellow-banded legging showed below the hem of her blue cheongsam.

Ruoyun's bright blue eyes fixed on the legging. When she spoke, her voice had none of the blurry warmth of the room; it cut right across the clatter of the peanut dishes like a sergeant's cane on a shield-rim.

“You walked off your post for a hundred years because you couldn't hold a horse-stance through one stick of incense,” Ruoyun said clearly, “and after a century on the road you still wrap your right shin-band half an inch lower than the left.”

The barb landed flat on the wood and stayed there.

Buling's hand stopped on the iron handle of the kettle. She didn't make a joke about consulting fees, and she didn't say *aiya*. She poured the hot water into Qingxiao's mug without spilling a drop, set the kettle back on the trivet, and sat down with her mouth shut, her right hand slipping under the table a moment later to tug the yellow band at her calf straight without looking down.

Beside Ruoyun, Qingxiao sat with her storehouse oilcloth folded neatly over the bench-end and two fingers of her left hand resting lightly against her jaw where the candied haw had left her tooth aching. She had said perhaps six words since they walked through the door, and she was watching everything—the quarry-woman's fire-tongs, the steam rising off the mutton tureen, Longjue trying and failing to balance an empty porcelain saucer on the tip of her left dragon crest.

The saucer slid off the jade ornament, bounced off Longjue's nose, and landed upside down in her lap.

Qingxiao let out a single, low laugh into her mug.

All along their bench, heads turned. Ruoyun went dead still with her cup halfway to her mouth; Buling blinked under her crooked talisman. Qingxiao didn't explain herself or cover her mouth; she picked up a boiled peanut, cracked the shell between her thumb and forefinger, and ate it while Longjue grinned and immediately reached for a second saucer to do it again.

At the end of their board, Suoming sat on two stacked grain-cushions with her boots dangling clear of the slush and a single cup of warm rice-tea in front of her. She took a slow swallow, looked up at the smoke-blackened rafters above the braziers, and spoke to the table at large.

“The roof doesn't leak in here,” Suoming said, and ate a chestnut.

In the middle of the bench sat Hsin, her nine gold tails coiled tight under the trestle so the serving-girl's apron wouldn't brush them.

Everybody in the Brazen Carp knew who was sitting there. Ten feet away, at the small round table by the frosted window, a grey-haired woman from the lower dye-works—whose nephew had gone into the fog by the lime-kilns and never come out—sat with her back turned squarely toward Hsin's bench. She had turned her stool three-quarters round when Hsin walked in, and she kept her shoulder presented to the middle of the room for the entire hour, laughing with her neighbours and never once glancing toward the gold tails.

While the quarry-woman was starting the fourth verse of the song, a thick-set woman in a mason's leather vest got up from the stove-bench to go out to the latrine. As she walked down the narrow aisle behind Hsin's back, she reached into her vest pocket, set a small, unglazed cup of hot honeyed wine down on the pine board two inches from Hsin's right cuff without slowing her step or looking down, and pushed out through the back curtain into the yard.

Hsin looked at the turned grey shoulder by the window. Then she looked down at the steaming honeyed cup beside her wrist. Her fox ears twitched once back, once forward, and settled halfway between, her hands resting open and still on the wood between the two.

Under the middle table, Mei's thumb gave a warm, sleepy press against Iuno's knee as Mei laughed at Amy trying to balance three chestnuts on Lupa's head.

Iuno leaned her good right shoulder half an inch closer to Mei's, feeling the heat of the brazier on her face, the sour-sweet smell of boiled mutton in the air, and the steady thump of Lupa's boot keeping time on the floorboards. For thirty years she had known which tile would slip off the basilica roof before the cloud formed over the bay; tonight she did not even know which tavern they would walk past on the way home, and the fierce, unguarded happiness sitting behind her ribs startled her so much she nearly set her cup down off the saucer.

*Careful, darling,* the High Priestess murmured from the warm dark at the back of her mind. *Another half-cup of ginger water and you'll be clapping along on the chorus.*

*I don't know the words,* Iuno thought back.

*You know the tune.*

Iuno's living hand left the stem of her porcelain cup, slid down the front of her woollen coat, and came to rest flat on the outside of her pocket, right over the small, hard square of the unopened velvet box resting beside her dented tea flask. Her fingers stayed there on the cloth.

Across the aisle, Longjue suddenly planted both bare feet on the seat of her bench, stood up until her jade-ornamented dragon crests nearly scraped the ceiling beam, and banged her red lacquer gourd against the timber post with a crack that stopped the fire-tongs mid-clack.

“Listen up!” Longjue roared across the smoky room, pointing her free hand straight down at the top of Hsin's head. “The Landlady of Xuanfang Hold is in the house tonight, and she says the next round is on her! Top-shelf wintersweet for every bench in the room!”

Forty women turned at once and banged their mugs on the tables with a deafening cheer.

Hsin, sovereign of Xuanfang Hold, opened her mouth to cite the storehouse grain-measure, saw forty empty bowls raised toward her across four glowing braziers, and let her ears drop flat to her skull as the keeper reached for the ladder to the top beam.

***

The two ten-year wintersweet jars came down from the rafters in a cloud of grey cobwebs and red sealing-wax, and the whole Brazen Carp cheered a second time when the keeper knocked the clay plugs in with the butt of her ladle.

Hsin kept her back straight and her wide sleeves untucked on the pine board while the serving-girl went down the rows with the long-spouted copper dipper. The wine smelled of preserved winter plum and old rice, thick enough to coat the inside of the earthenware bowls.

When the keeper came round the kitchen screen wiping her hands on her sheepskin vest, Hsin reached into her left sleeve for her purse.

Her fingers closed around two worn brass cash with square holes in the middle, a twist of red string from the festival wires, and a single dried hawthorn pit from the autumn before last.

She drew her hand back to the edge of the cuff, looked down into her palm under the table lip, and felt the tips of her ears go hot.

Two seats to her left, Suisui didn't even turn her head from watching Ciaccona tune her fourth string. With one smooth motion, Suisui unhooked the heavy embroidered silk pouch from her sash, laid it on her knee, and slid three plump silver ingots and a knotted half-string of copper cash along the pine grain until they stopped with a soft *chink* against Hsin's left wrist.

Suisui tapped her closed fan once against her own teacup and kept looking toward the stage.

“I will have the treasury clerk bring the silver down to the storehouse before the first bell,” Hsin said low, keeping her chin level so Ruoyun wouldn't hear.

“Take your time, Lady Hsin,” Suisui replied pleasantly, without looking round. “The Zhaoming Guild never closes an account before the snow stops.”

Hsin laid her palm over the three ingots, pushed them across the wet wood into the keeper's waiting hand, and added the two brass cash from her own sleeve on top for the girl with the dipper.

Longjue leaned both elbows on the table, lifted her freshly filled bowl of wintersweet in a grand two-handed salute, and drank half of it down in one long draught. She wiped her mouth on the back of her forearm and beamed at Hsin across the empty beef platter.

“All right, Landlady!” Longjue announced generously. “Since you stood the whole room to the ten-year jar, I'm knocking off one string!”

Hsin stared at her. “That was three taels of silver and forty bowls of top-shelf wine!”

“One string!” Longjue slapped the board. “Thirty to go! You're getting off cheap because it's snowing!”

“Aiya, what thirty, you don't know how to count is it!” Buling squawked, shoving her bench back with a screech of wood. She bent down by the nearest brazier, snatched a thick black stub of charred pine out of the ash-pan, and marched over to the tavern's hanging slate by the counter before the keeper could stop her.

Standing on tiptoe in her cloth shoes, Buling scrubbed out the bottom line of the mutton-broth tally with her sleeve and wrote in huge, heavy charcoal strokes across the slate:

> **SENTINEL TO LONGJUE: 10 STRINGS LEFT (WITNESSED BY SENIOR CONSULTANT)**

She underlined **10** three times so hard the charcoal snapped, tossed the stub back into the brazier, and dusted her hands together while the silk-loom women by the bar hooted and Longjue pelted her winter hat with three peanut shells in rapid succession.

Behind the counter, the keeper took down her own small wooden house-board to enter the two wintersweet jars under the night's receipts. Her chalk squeaked as she wrote the date at the top.

Hsin watched the woman's hand over the heads of the benches.

“Spell it right,” Hsin called across the brazier smoke. “And if you draw a fox beside the column, put the tails in. The boy at the fish shop made me look like a turnip.”

“Nine of 'em, Sentinel!” the keeper shouted back, flourishing the chalk with a grin and drawing nine jagged spikes radiating off a round circle like a burr on a dog's coat. “Even the dark one on the end!”

Across the room, near the latched door that led out to the wood-shed, three women from the third terrace were sitting around an upturned grain-tub.

The one nearest the aisle was Butcher Sun's wife, in her brown quilted jacket with the cuffs turned back over her woollen wrist-warmers. Their nephew Sun Hu's name was in the ledger by the well.

She had a full mug of the ten-year wintersweet in her right hand, freshly poured from Hsin's jar.

Through the gap between the braziers, she looked straight across the noisy room at Hsin sitting with her cuffs untucked and Suisui's borrowed copper string still lying by her knuckles.

Butcher Sun's wife did not smile, and she did not get up from her stool. She lifted the earthenware mug two inches off the top of the grain-tub toward Hsin's bench, drank a mouthful of the dark wine, and turned her head back to say something to the cobbler's widow beside her.

Hsin sat very still while the ferryman's chorus roared up again by the platform. Her throat worked once. She did not look down at the floorboards, and she did not reach for her cuffs.

Beside her on the bench, Suoming said nothing at all.

She reached out with two small, rough-knuckled fingers, took the unglazed cup of honeyed wine the mason had left earlier, and slid it one inch closer across the pine until the warm porcelain touched Hsin's right hand.

Hsin closed her fingers around the cup, lifted it the same two inches off the wood toward the wood-shed door, and drank it down while it was still warm.

***

By the time the Brazen Carp's keeper began raking ashes over the middle braziers, the snow outside had buried the cobbles ankle-deep.

Chisa stepped out through the double doors behind Lupa and felt the cold air hit her cheeks like well-water.

The wind had dropped completely. Big, thick flakes came drifting down out of the black between the sky-rings, settling white and level on the shop awnings, the stone cistern rim, and the forty-eight hollowed steps of the long stair that climbed the cliffside to the upper terrace. Wen and Warden Liangyu called their goodnights at the barracks turn-off, their voices muffled and round in the snow, and the rest of the party turned up the wide stair in a straggling, noisy line.

Chisa walked in the middle of the climb with her left hand—the loose red string wound twice above her wristbone—tucked deep inside Lupa's big, hot right palm.

The lamps along the stair-wall were spaced twenty paces apart and furred over with wet snow, turning the figures ahead and behind into dark shapes of wool and white breath. Chisa didn't squint into the dark to sort them out. She knew where everyone was on the steps by the sound of their feet in the drift and the way their voices carried between the stone walls.

Ten paces up the stair ahead of them, Buling was trudging up the middle of the treads with Ruoyun hoisted squarely on her back in a piggyback carry.

Ruoyun's dark Mary Janes dangled on either side of Buling's blue silk hips, her spectacles were tilted sideways on her nose, and she had both arms locked stiffly around Buling's collar while lecturing the back of Buling's winter hat at top volume.

“You are bouncing on your heels,” Ruoyun said distinctly into the snow. “A first-year recruit from the south marshes walks with a flatter pelvis than you. I am only permitting this indignity because the slush will ruin the leather on my soles, and if you drop me on the landing I will have you scrubbing the third-terrace grease-traps until the spring thaw.”

“Aiya, shut up or I throw you in the cistern lah!” Buling panted, hitching Ruoyun's knees higher with both hands so the yellow talisman on her forehead flapped against her nose. “You tell everybody Celestials don't eat, how come you weigh like two sacks of wet cement!”

“That is dignity,” Ruoyun said haughtily, resting her chin on top of Buling's nuanmao hat and closing her eyes.

Just ahead of Buling, Qingxiao walked up the snow-covered risers on her own two feet in her white stockings, her step completely level and unhurried. She had the dark storehouse oilcloth wrapped round her shoulders, and every five steps she gave a small, contented sniff and wiped her nose on the back of her wrist.

Two steps behind Qingxiao came Mei, taking the hollowed treads one at a time, just as Iuno's treaty had stipulated at supper. Iuno walked on Mei's left, her living hand hooked firmly into the back of Mei's belt; Yangyang walked on Mei's right, her steady arm tucked under Mei's elbow and her cerulean collar-feathers dotted with tiny white snowflakes that didn't melt.

“Stop a sec,” Mei said cheerfully on the second landing, leaning her hip against the stone balustrade between them while her breath steamed white. “Keep going, I'm not dying, I just want to look at the roofs. Everything looks like frosted buns.”

“If you try to eat the balustrade,” Iuno told her, her shoulder crystal giving a soft, clear chime as she shifted her weight against Mei's side, “I am leaving you in the drift until Wen comes out with the shovel.”

“You wouldn't,” Mei said, leaning her head briefly against Iuno's good shoulder before patting Yangyang's mittened hand on her other arm. “All right. Next landing.”

Behind Chisa and Lupa came the quick, light double-tap of Cartethyia's boots climbing under double weight. Cartethyia had refused to transform into Fleurdelys because she said nine feet was too tall for the stair-lamps, and instead she was carrying Amy piggyback in her own small knight's body out of sheer stubborn pride, both of them laughing so hard they had to bounce off the inner wall every six steps while Ciaccona walked one tread behind them, holding Cartethyia's crooked crystal laurel in her good hand so the thorns wouldn't poke Amy's eye out.

And at the very rear of the column, coming up slow and even through the quiet snow, sounded the regular *click, pause, click* of Suoming's iron heel-plates on the stone edges of the risers.

Suoming had Nine Shadows open over her right shoulder. Hsin walked beside her in her own shape, her nine gold tails gathered up in both arms like an armful of yellow laundry to keep them out of the wet drift, her head ducked slightly so both of her fox ears stayed dry under the brown oil-paper rim of the umbrella.

Beside Chisa, Lupa's boot-heel hit a patch of frozen cabbage-water under the fresh snow and slid four inches back.

Chisa tightened her fingers around Lupa's thumb and braced her own left hip against Lupa's thigh before the wolf could stumble.

“Whoa,” Lupa grunted, catching her balance and swinging their joined hands forward between their coats. The wintersweet wine had made Lupa's ears burn hot enough that Chisa could feel the warmth radiating off them from a foot away, and Lupa's tail was wagging in a slow, wide arc that brushed the snow off the balustrade posts as they passed. “That board at the Carp was greased! If the wood hadn't been wet under my elbow I'd have taken the dragon five straight in the second set!”

“You called six horses when you only had five fingers out,” Chisa said calmly, matching her stride to Lupa's up the last ten steps.

“That's a feint! In Septimont we call that an arena feint!”

“In Xuanfang they call it drinking the sorghum bowl,” Chisa said.

Lupa barked a laugh that echoed off the storehouse wall, squeezed Chisa's hand until her knuckles popped, and bumped her shoulder hard and warm against Chisa's arm as they came out onto the flat snow of the upper terrace.

Behind the dark cookhouse, the salt-house shutter glowed with a thin orange line of light around the latch.

Lupa let go of Chisa's hand only long enough to lift the wooden bar with her free fist and shoulder the heavy door inward.

The deep, dry, baked-brick heat of the kang rolled out into the snow to meet them, smelling of warm straw matting, toasted broad beans, and coal-flue.

Before anyone had even reached for the lamp-wick or unlaced a single boot, Ciaccona walked three steps inside, handed Cartethyia's laurel back without looking, and dropped sideways onto the warm outer kerb of the brick platform with her wool coat still buttoned to the chin, her lute case hugged to her stomach, and her wet boots hanging four inches off the floor, fast asleep between one breath and the next.

***

By the sixth bell the snow had stopped, leaving the morning sky a flat, painless dove-grey above the gorges and a three-inch cushion of clean white over the flagstones, the well-head, and the far end of the long outdoor table.

Mei sat with her back propped against the storehouse wall, a hot oven-brick wrapped in grey flannel tucked inside her coat against the pull of her scar, and both hands curled round a steaming earthenware bowl of Wen's plain white rice congee.

Above her head, the kitchen shutter stood propped on its stick, and Wen—who had got up at the fifth bell with her blue kerchief tied straight and not a single hair out of place—was calmly scraping the bottom of the iron pot with her long wooden ladle as if she had gone to bed at dusk on barley water.

“Pickled mustard greens in the blue dish,” Wen called out the hatch, setting a fresh saucer on the sill. “Don't anybody ask me for fried eggs before noon, I'm not lighting the side-ring.”

Down the length of the bench, three people winced at the word *fried*.

On Mei's left, Iuno sat with her spine straight and her silver-blue hair pinned up with slightly less geometric precision than usual. Her flask of Earl Grey had been left in her pocket; she was drinking plain hot water with a slice of salted lemon in it, holding the cup in her living hand and closing her eyes for two full seconds whenever Lupa's spoon scraped the inside of a bowl.

“I am perfectly well,” Iuno told the table before anyone had asked her. “The charcoal in that second establishment was improperly kilned.”

“Sure it was, princess,” Lupa muttered from across the board. She had her chin propped in one palm, her wolf ears lying flat and sideways against her head like a wet dog's, staring darkly at her congee. “Auntie Luo put green barley in the mash. I told her at the first mug. Green barley gives you a neck-ache.”

Beside Lupa, Chisa—who was washed, combed, and sitting neatly in her black tunic without a crease on her cuffs—peeled a hard-boiled salted duck egg, split it cleanly in half with a chopstick, and laid both halves on top of Lupa's rice without a word. Lupa glared at the egg, mumbled something into her collar, and ate it.

Further down the plank, Ciaccona had dragged an entire quilted blanket off the salt-house kang and wore it wound round her head and shoulders like a penitent monk, her splinted forefinger poking out of the wool to steady her mug of ginger water while she stared bleakly at a single grain of rice stuck to the table. Right beside her ear, Cartethyia was crunching her way happily through a dish of sour pickled radish, every *snap* of the root making Ciaccona's left eyelid twitch.

Near the end of the bench, Suisui sat bolt upright with her horn comb still tucked into the slightly listing left side of her hair-coil, fanning her face slowly with her closed fan in the freezing air.

“The draft off the river-cut is exceedingly damp this morning,” Suisui observed to Yangyang in a hushed, dignified voice. “It settles behind the eyebrows.”

Yangyang didn't tease her. Two of the small cerulean feathers on the left side of Yangyang's collar were sticking straight out sideways where she had slept on her arm, and her own ears were still faintly pink. She pushed the pot of hot ginger water across the pine to her sister's wrist, picked up her own bowl of congee, and promptly burned the tip of her tongue on the first spoonful as she always did.

On the stone step of the storehouse doorway sat Ruoyun.

Her ruffled teal sleeves were pressed knife-sharp, her round brass spectacles were polished clean, and every time Dumpling gave a clockwork *whirr* on the rim of the pickle jar, Ruoyun's left hand came up to press two fingers hard against the bridge of her nose.

“I walked up those stairs on my own two feet,” Ruoyun informed the courtyard at large, looking at nobody. “Anyone who claims otherwise was intoxicated and is repeating tavern gossip.”

“My back still got your shoe-print on the silk lah,” Buling said in a hollow voice from the bench corner. She had her round winter hat pulled down so low the yellow talisman lay flat across her chin while she scrolled through her Terminal with one thumb. “And you bit my collar at the third landing.”

“I do not bite,” Ruoyun said frigidly, and turned her head to look at Qingxiao, who sat cross-legged on the sunny corner of the bench in her storehouse oilcloth, calmly finishing her second bowl of congee and reaching for the ladle to pour a third. “How is the tooth?”

Qingxiao tapped her left jaw once with a forefinger. “The wintersweet numbed it,” she said. “It's fine.”

Under the warm overhang of Wen's kitchen window, curled on a dry grain-sack out of the snow, lay a skinny nine-tailed fox. She had all four paws tucked under her brisket and the dark-tipped ninth tail pulled tight over her nose and both eyes to keep the grey morning glare out. Two feet away, Suoming sat on her upturned bucket with her boots clear of the stone, drinking plain white tea out of a chipped cup without a hair on her red head out of place.

Buling gave a sudden, strangled groan from behind her talisman and laid her forehead flat on the pine table beside her Terminal.

“Dead,” Buling said to the wood. “Finished. My reputation on the Huangboard is gone already.”

“What did you post?” Ciaccona croaked from inside her quilt.

“I didn't post! Wait—okay, maybe at the Brazen Carp I posted one blurry picture of the pitch-pot when Longjue threw three arrows in at once.” Buling lifted her head an inch, holding the screen at arm's length and squinting at it with one red eye. “Got four hundred replies since dawn. Top comment from Jinzhou says: *Why is the Xuan Paragon of Xuanfang Hold sitting behind a jiangshi eating peanuts out of a hat, is this a woodblock fake.*”

Qingxiao stopped ladling congee and looked toward the Terminal with mild interest. “Which hat?”

“My hat! Longjue put the shells in my brim when I took it off!” Buling scrolled down half an inch and winced. “Second comment rates the Sentinel's tail-fluffiness nine out of ten and asks why the ninth one is burnt at the end, did she back into the brazier.”

Under the kitchen window, the fox's ears flattened straight back under the dark-tipped tail, and a low, indignant growl vibrated out of the grain-sack.

“Wait, there's more,” Buling muttered, her thumb flicking faster. “Third comment from Septimont relay says—wah, they zoomed in on the back bench—asks if the wolf with the stoneware mug is single and whether she likes—”

Lupa's spoon clattered into her bowl. Her ears shot bolt upright and turned bright, furious crimson from base to tip.

“Give me that machine!” Lupa barked, lunging halfway across the table while Chisa calmly hooked two fingers into the back of Lupa's belt and pulled her back onto the bench.

“Cannot, somebody in Mingting just asked if that's Director Suisui's horn comb in the mirror behind the bar,” Buling said, slapping the power button until the screen went black and shoving the Terminal deep into her pocket. “Nobody looks at the thread anymore. Thread is closed.”

Down the terrace steps from the Vale path came Longjue, strolling barefoot through the three-inch snow without a shiver on her skin, her dark hair gleaming, a fresh red lacquer gourd swinging at her hip, and half a cold steamed bun in her hand.

She stopped beside the grain-sack under Wen's window and nudged the curled fox's flank lightly with her bare big toe.

“Morning, Landlady!” Longjue called out, bright and loud enough to make Iuno close both eyes and Ruoyun grip her knees. “I just checked my slate! Since you borrowed the three taels off the Guild Director and haven't paid her back yet, last night's wintersweet was on Suisui's tab, not yours! You're back up to thirty-three strings with the snow-tax!”

The fox didn't uncurl or take her tail off her eyes. From underneath the fur came a single, muffled, aristocratic voice:

“Go fall in the cistern.”

“Thirty-three!” Longjue laughed, tossing the crust of her bun to Dumpling and dropping onto the bench beside Ciaccona's quilt to steal a spoonful of mustard greens.

From the bottom of the second-terrace stair came the rapid *thump-thump-thump* of boots taking the snowy steps two at a time.

Amy came vaulting over the last riser with her cheeks glowing bright pink from the frost, her tin fox-whistle bouncing on its red string outside her coat, and a brown-paper bag of hot sesame cakes from the lower bakery clutched in her mittens.

“Ma!” Amy called out, skidding to a halt by the end of the table and dumping the warm bag between the pickle dishes. “Auntie Luo and the woman at the Brazen Carp both have their chalkboards out in the snow already! I read them on the way up!”

Iuno opened one eye over her lemon water. “I trust neither of them has used my name.”

“Better,” Amy said, grinning from ear to ear as she tore the paper bag open to let the sesame steam out. “The Carp's board says: *LADIES' NIGHT EVERY THURSDAY — SENTINEL'S TAB CLOSED UNTIL SHE BRINGS CASH — FREE BROTH IF YOU BRING THE WOLF*.”

Lupa buried her face in both hands while Chisa reached into the bag, took out the biggest sesame cake, broke the crisp corner off for herself, and handed the soft middle to Amy.

Mei laughed, felt her side give a tiny, familiar tug against the warm brick under her coat, and didn't mind it at all.

With the side of her left hand, she brushed a clump of fresh white snow off the corner of the pine table onto the flagstones. Then she picked up her porcelain spoon, slipped her right hand under the table to lace her fingers loosely through Iuno's warm ones on her knee, and blew across the top of her congee while Amy and Lupa argued over the sesame crumbs in the morning sun.

# Someone Will Remember Us
### A Story of the Last Miko of Flaming Sakura
*The Left Behind · The Theatre of the Thousand Faces · Two Bowls*

---

## I. Matters of the Heart

The leave of absence began, as most things at Startorch Academy began, with Lucilla pretending to be unserious.

"Ooh," said the President, chin propped on her knuckles, watching combat-trial footage loop across the office wall for the fourth time. A mountain's silhouette of dormant machinery filled the frame, haloed in instrument readouts. "What if we used footage of the Exostrider activating to pitch for funding?"

"And the confidentiality agreements?"

"No sympathy for my chronic funding anxiety?"

Hiyuki said nothing, which was, in their long shared dialect, an answer. She stood where she always stood — two steps inside the door, hands folded, white hair gathering the room's lamplight the way snow gathers dusk — and waited for the conversation Lucilla actually intended to have. There was always one underneath. Lucilla ran her office the way she ran her Forte: layers of borrowed brightness over something patient and very old.

The President unwrapped a sour candy and did not eat it. She set it on the desk between them like a chess piece, beside the little makeup mirror that was not a makeup mirror, and studied Hiyuki with the mild, terrible attention she usually reserved for budget shortfalls and dying stars.

"Fine," Lucilla said at last. "Go to Honami. I already approved your leave of absence."

"Why?"

"...Before I give you more thankless tasks, go deal with matters of the heart first."

There were instruments in the Spacetrek Collective's vaults that could read a Resonator's frequency down to the tremor of a held grief, and every one of them, pointed at Hiyuki, returned the same verdict: *perfectly stable*. Lucilla had never needed instruments. Lucilla had once carried a thousand borrowed lives behind her eyes and learned to smile anyway, and people like that recognized each other across any number of folded hands.

Hiyuki bowed — precisely, economically, the bow civilians pointed out to their children, *look, the Miko, we're safe* — and turned to go.

"Two days," Lucilla said to her back, light as the candy wrapper. "Bring me back something. A souvenir. A feeling. Anything that isn't paperwork."

The door closed. The office would smell of iris for an hour after she left; it always did. Lucilla never mentioned it. Lucilla mentioned everything else — the broken training swords, the docked paychecks, the structural integrity of the cafeteria's curry — and never, not once in thirty years, the door that stood between them, which Hiyuki had closed herself and which Lucilla had never knocked on.

She simply kept oiling the hinges.

## II. The City That Came Back

For thirty years, there had been nothing to return to.

That was the part outsiders never understood about Honami. Other cities the Lament had killed at least had the decency to leave bodies — coral-fossiled streets, grey statuary, ruins you could kneel in. Honami it had simply *taken*. One night of rain that would not cease, one catastrophe folding in on itself like a closing hand, and then absence: a bay with no city on it, a road that ended in air, a million names with nowhere to be mourned. For thirty years the Miko of Flaming Sakura had guarded a country whose greatest wound was a hole in the map. You cannot light incense for a hole. She had tried.

Then, last year, the city came back.

It surfaced the way drowned things surface — slowly, and then all at once — sliding out of whatever sealed fold of the world had held it, time-bleached and silent, its towers furred with salt-pale dust. The scholars had theories. A pocket reality collapsing from within; travelers, unnamed in any report Hiyuki had been cleared to read, who had walked the sealed city and broken its lock from the inside. She did not need the theories. She had stood on the new old shoreline the week it returned and felt what had come back with the architecture: the dead. Tens of thousands of them, anchored in their last unfinished moments — the city's grief, decanted whole into the world that had finally agreed to hold it again.

The city called them ghosts. The old liturgies called them Reverberations. Hiyuki called them what they were. The Left Behind: those whom death had taken and regret had kept.

She came down the coast road at first light under a thin, apologetic drizzle — the sky over Honami had never fully learned how to stop — and began.

This was the work. Not exorcism. Not battle. She found them where their moments had snagged: the cashier wiping a stain forty years gone; the child who fell at the same signpost, rose, fell again, with the patience only the dead can afford. She knelt to their level, or stood at their shoulder, and waited until the loop noticed her, and asked the only question her office had ever really consisted of: *What is your wish?*

And they told her. The dead always told her. Whatever else the Lament had broken, it had not broken that — the way a phantom turns toward a listener like a flower turning toward the one warm thing in a cold sky.

An old woman stood at a flooded tram stop holding an umbrella over an empty patch of pavement. Sixty years of weather had gone through the place she guarded, and the dry spot under the umbrella had outlived the person it was held for. "She always forgot hers," the woman said, to Hiyuki, to the rain, to no one. "I told her, I'll wait, I'll hold it. And then the sky came down, and I — I never lowered my arm. If I lower it, she'll get wet when she comes."

"What is your wish?"

"That somebody had stood under it. Just once. So the holding meant something."

"I will carry it," Hiyuki said — the oldest words of her office, possibly the only prayer she had ever fully meant — and the regret left the woman the way warmth leaves a body, and entered Hiyuki, and became a bead of ice behind her sternum, down where the frost kept everything. The woman's outline loosened. The umbrella dissolved last, still upright, still dry beneath. Hiyuki stood in the drizzle until the dry patch darkened to match the street, and then she moved on.

A calligrapher in a collapsed studio, grinding ink that was not there, had written the same letter ten thousand times and sent it none of them. *Akane — I was wrong, the shop should have been yours, come home —* "Sisters," the phantom said, as if the word explained the whole architecture of her staying. "We were sisters. You don't apologize to sisters. You assume there will be time." Hiyuki carried that one standing very still, with her eyes open, and did not let her face move at all.

A boy of perhaps ten sat on a stairwell turning an empty game cartridge case over and over. He had borrowed it from his best friend. He had meant to return it after the festival. His regret was so small that he apologized for it — *it's stupid, lady, there were people who lost whole families* — and Hiyuki knelt on the wet step and received it with the full gravity of her office, because the size of a regret has nothing to do with its weight, and the dead deserve a vessel who knows that. "It is not stupid," she told him. "He kept your save file. People keep things." She did not know if it was true. She made it true by saying it. The boy passed on grinning, and the bead he left behind her sternum was lighter than most, and ached more.

Forty-one by nightfall. She did not count the beads themselves anymore — counting implied an end, and she had stopped believing in those — but she counted the day's work, because the work was finite and the work was hers and the work, unlike everything else, could actually be finished.

Here and there, the city surprised her. Whole streets stood quiet — loops missing, moments resolved, an absence with the texture of *aftermath* rather than emptiness — as if someone had already come through with a lantern. Sixty-some pockets of completed silence, scattered along a path that ran, when she traced it on the district map in her head, from the harbor to the broadcast tower like a road someone had laid with great care. She did not know who. She filed it where she filed everything she could not afford to feel: behind the sternum, beneath the frost.

There was one street she did not walk. There had always been one street she did not walk. It climbed the eastern hill through what had been the old pilgrim district, past the stone lanterns, to the gate of the Shrine of Flaming Sakura — and in a year of pilgrimages to the returned city, in thirty years of being the last of her line, Hiyuki had never once set foot on the first stair. The dead were everywhere in Honami. The dead were her vocation. But there is a kind of mathematics even a vessel will not perform, and *who might be waiting at the top of those stairs* was an equation she had been declining, with perfect composure, for a year.

She told herself the lower city had more of them. It was even true. The best lies are.

Night came down soft and wet. She found a dry doorway, ate field rations without tasting them, and took out her knife and the half-finished figurine she had been carrying since spring — a heron, allegedly; the proportions had filed a formal protest — and worked at it by the light of a dying holo-advertisement that had been trying to sell soda to an empty street for thirty years. Carving was the one art she had never gotten better at. She found that restful. Everything else she touched, she mastered, because mastery was load-bearing. The heron asked nothing of her and forgave everything, and its wings were wrong, and she kept it anyway.

She was setting the second wing hopelessly askew when she noticed the phantom across the street.

A schoolgirl, by the uniform — one of the thousands the Lament had frozen mid-commute, satchel strap across her chest, standing at the mouth of an alley. Looping, presumably. Except.

Hiyuki had spent a lifetime among the Left Behind, and she knew their grammar the way Lucilla knew lies: phantoms repeat. Phantoms are *watched*. Their gazes run on rails, fixed to the missing beloved, the unsent letter, the spot under the umbrella.

This one was watching *her*. Had been, she realized with a slow cold that had nothing to do with her Forte, for some time. Watching her carve. Watching her badly proportioned heron with an expression of frank, delighted, entirely living amusement.

Hiyuki folded the knife. "You are not one of them."

"Oh, well *spotted*," said the girl, and stepped out of the loop she had never been in, and her shadow came with her wrong — too fluid, pooling and reforming like ink deciding what to be. "Forty-one in a day, and not one of them noticed they were being heard by the saddest woman on the continent. But *you* notice *me* inside an hour. The instruments really do undersell you, Miko."

"Name yourself."

"Mm. Which one?" The girl tilted her head, and for a moment her face was a different face — older, younger, a stranger's, a flicker of faces shuffling like a dealer warming up a deck — before settling back into the schoolgirl with the satchel and the grin that belonged to none of them. "Lately I'm Nivora. Student. Honor roll, if you can believe it. Two best friends, matching charms, the whole catalogue." She spread her hands, modest. "Before that, other things. A ferryman, once. A baker's dead daughter, once, for a *very* long time. They have so many names for me where you work, Hiyuki of Flaming Sakura, but the one I like best is the honest one."

The drizzle had stopped falling on her, Hiyuki noticed. It fell everywhere else.

"The Thousand-Faced Survivor," Hiyuki said.

"There she is." Nivora's grin widened by exactly one tooth. "The Grand Architect, at your service — or you at mine; the grammar's always negotiable. And before your hand gets any closer to that talisman, let's save us both a tedious scene: I'm not *here*, not in any way your lovely frost can bite. I'm a courtesy call." She strolled a slow half-circle, hands clasped behind her back, surveying the dead street like a landlord. "I've been watching you work. Truly — no mockery — it's beautiful. The bow, the question, the *I will carry it.* Forty-one servings of other people's despair, swallowed whole, no water. Most connoisseurs of suffering are such gluttons about it. You eat like a penitent."

"State your business or leave my city."

"*Your* city." Nivora let the words sit. "Yes. Let's talk about your city. Or better—" and here she raised one hand, fingers poised like a conductor's, like a projectionist's at the switch, "—let's not talk at all. I brought pictures."

## III. The Audience of One

The street changed.

Not violently — the Architect was no vandal; she renovated. The rain rolled itself upward and away. The dead holo-advertisement guttered out, and in its place light came up warm and amber and *wrong*, the specific gold of late summer afternoons that no longer existed, and the ruined storefront across the road was a veranda now, weathered cedar, wind chime, two cushions, a low table set for tea.

A woman knelt at the table with her back half-turned, pouring with the easy ceremony of someone who has done it ten thousand times. Tall. Broad-shouldered under the loose robe. Hair gone prematurely silver at the temple, where she'd always claimed the previous Miko's job had put it. She set the pot down, looked over her shoulder, and smiled the smile that had once been the warmest object in Hiyuki's entire world.

"My, my," said Gyokuro. "Can't a sister just want to see her favorite girl without a reason?"

Every muscle in Hiyuki's body answered before her mind could countermand it — the loosening at the shoulders, the half-step forward, thirty years of discipline simply *bypassed*, the way a wish bypasses sense. The voice was right. The voice was so unbearably right. The lazy drawl that had narrated her whole childhood, the cadence of a woman who had carried the same office Hiyuki carried and made it look like a hammock.

"...That's not what I meant," Hiyuki heard herself say, because that was the line; the conversation had happened once, in summer, on a real veranda, and her body remembered its part.

"Hehe. There's that serious little face. I just wanted to chat. Always relaxes me." Gyokuro patted the cushion beside her. The tea steamed. It would be her namesake, of course — jade dew, the leaves she was named for, brewed the way she had taught Hiyuki a lifetime ago: water cooled first, never boiling. *Patience, little one. Some things are ruined by too much heat. Steep them gently and they give you everything.* "So? How are things at the shrine?"

"The usual," said Hiyuki's mouth. "Many came to make wishes."

The veranda held its summer. Somewhere beyond it, a bell that no longer existed marked an hour that no longer existed. And Hiyuki stood at the edge of the light with her hands folded and catalogued, with the cold clinical corner of herself that had kept her alive for a century, every flawless detail: the chip in the second teacup, which was real and which she had caused, age nine, and never been scolded for. The blanket folded over the veranda rail — *that* blanket, the one she had tucked around her sister's shoulders the autumn night she'd found her drunk and weeping over the previous Miko's memorial tablet, the only time she ever saw Gyokuro grieve, and Gyokuro had never known she'd been seen, and Hiyuki had never told her, and there was no record of it anywhere in the world.

Except in Hiyuki.

That was the flaw. That was the seam in the forgery, and finding it was like pressing a wound to confirm the bone was broken: the air on the veranda smelled of *iris*.

Gyokuro had smelled of green tea and sun-warmed straw and, on festival nights, plum wine. Iris was Hiyuki's own scent — the one that followed her through Lucilla's office, the one the dead turned toward. The Architect had built this room not from Gyokuro but from Hiyuki's *remembering* of her, and grief contaminates the evidence; every memory of the beloved is soaked in the rememberer. The forgery was perfect everywhere except where it had been quarried from.

"You're not her," Hiyuki said.

The summer light did not so much as flicker. Gyokuro — Nivora — lifted her teacup, took an appreciative sip, and spoke in the dead woman's voice with the living monster's timing.

"Still regretting that you didn't try that red bean pudding with your sister, aren't you?"

The cold that went through Hiyuki then was her own, and it was the first thing in a hundred years her Forte had ever done without permission: frost feathering out across the cobbles from her boot soles, fine as breath on glass.

It had been such a small plan. The pudding stand by the east gate; the famous one, with the queue. Gyokuro had been talking about it for a month — *we'll go when the sakura turn, we'll bring the good tea, we'll eat ourselves stupid and watch the pilgrims* — and the season had come, and there had been wishes to hear, there were always wishes to hear, and they had said *next week* to each other with the absolute confidence of two women who guarded a country, and then the rain over Honami had begun, and had not ceased, and there had never been a next week again, anywhere, for anyone.

The Architect set down the cup, and was the schoolgirl again, sitting cross-legged on a veranda that was already dissolving at its edges into the wet dark street, and her voice was her own — light, fond, marrow-deep cruel.

"You know none of it is real."

"..."

"You were the one who deceived yourself." Nivora rose, dusted off a uniform that had never touched dust, and gestured — and behind her, where the veranda's painted summer was peeling away, something else stood in the rain that had no business standing anywhere: a doorway. Free-standing, frameless to the street, framed inward in marquee bulbs, a curtain of red velvet stirring in a wind from somewhere with different weather. Gold light leaked under its hem. Very faintly, from beyond it, came the sound of an orchestra tuning.

"Desperate to play pretend?" the Architect said, and swept the curtain aside with a showman's arm, and her grin shuffled through a dozen borrowed faces and settled on the one that fit her best — which was to say, no one's. "Why not commit? Please. This performance is only beginning."

Hiyuki looked at the door for a long moment.

She knew what it was. She had spent a career walking into exactly this — the anomaly, the trap, the Dark Tide's mouth — and the reasoning had never changed, was in fact carved into the bedrock of her: *threats are neutralized at the source, and the source is always somewhere no one else should have to go.* If the Grand Architect of the Fractsidus had built a theatre in her graveyard, then the Miko of Flaming Sakura would walk its aisles, find its projector, and freeze it mid-lie. That was the reason. It was a good reason. It was even true.

And underneath it, where the frost kept everything, in a chamber she did not open even alone in the dark, the other reason sat with folded hands and said nothing, because it did not need to. The trap had her sister's laugh in it. Somewhere behind that curtain was a machine that could pour cooled tea with Gyokuro's exact wrists. Hiyuki had eaten forty-one strangers' regrets that day and the day had given her, in exchange, ninety seconds of a counterfeit summer, and she was a century old, and she was so hungry, and the worst thing the Architect had said all evening was the truest:

*You were the one who deceived yourself.*

"After you," said Hiyuki, and stepped through.

## IV. The Pictures

The theatre had no walls, because the Architect did not believe in limiting an audience's imagination. There was a floor of polished black that reflected nothing, and there was a single seat — velvet, high-backed, an opera box's worth of loneliness condensed into one chair — and there was the dark, going out forever in every direction, hung at intervals with screens the size of city walls, dim and waiting, like a backlot built by someone who had heard about the night sky and decided it needed work. Far off between the screens stood the skeletons of sets: a cliff of painted plaster, a marble courtyard on casters, a precipice with its sea rolled up beside it like a carpet. An orchestra tuned eternally somewhere below the floor.

"Sit, sit." Nivora had changed for the occasion — an usherette's uniform now, pillbox hat, a tray of concessions strapped to her front that held, Hiyuki noted without expression, a single serving of red bean pudding and two spoons. "House policy: one guest per eternity. You're going to want the seat. It's a long program, and you've had such a day."

Hiyuki remained standing.

"Suit yourself." The Architect snapped her fingers, and the first screen woke with the burring purr of old film, and a title card came up in white on black, in handwriting too elegant to be machine-made:

**REEL ONE — THE GIRL THE SEA GAVE BACK**

"Now, our heroine," Nivora said, settling into the velvet chair herself, ankles crossed, voice dropping into the plush register of a narrator who has given this tour before and loved it more each time. "I want you to watch closely, Miko. I want you to watch the way a professional watches. You're a connoisseur of the fallen. So count, for me, the hands."

On the screen, a woman washed ashore with no name, no past, and a mark humming under her skin — and before she had finished her first breath of the world, a hand was already pulling her out of the water. Silver-blue hair. Grey-blue eyes. *I'm selfish*, said the stranger, by way of explanation, and taught the drowned woman to fly.

The reel ran like spring water. The nameless woman — *Mei*, the stranger named her, *the Rover* — fell, over and over, the way protagonists fall: into canals, into conspiracies, into the sea a second time, off a god's horn with her own blood sheeting down the blade. And every single time, the film found hands. A bard's. A poisoner's. A sealed girl's, reaching across a decade of solitude. When Mei died — actually died, a divine horn through her abdomen, holding on out of sheer refusal — a poisoner stood over her body and raged at a god until the god gave her back, and the screen filled with light, and three women leapt from a collapsing tower into the sea and washed up *together*, and convalesced in a castle, complaining about the soup.

When the woman on screen asked, in a cold corridor, whether she was even real — washed ashore, nameless, possibly a swapped protagonist in someone else's play — three pairs of hands held her upright, and a knight who had been a ghost for twenty years told her: *maybe whoever you were before never existed — but the woman standing here is real, and she's ours.*

The reel ended on four friends, criminal celebrities all, walking away from a grave they had tended properly, going to find somewhere warm.

The screen went dark. In the silence, the orchestra below the floor resolved its tuning into something almost like a waltz.

"Tally?" said Nivora.

Hiyuki said nothing. She had counted. Of course she had counted; counting was what she did instead of feeling. The number sat in her chest beside the day's forty-one beads of ice and did not behave like a number.

"When *you* fell," the Architect said, conversational, examining her own borrowed nails, "at Honami — when the rain came down and your knees finally met the ash — refresh my memory. How many hands?"

**REEL TWO — EVEN THE DEAD KEEP A LIGHT ON**

The second reel was about a murderer.

Mei had one, the film explained, in its lush curated voice — a woman she had wronged before her amnesia so profoundly that the wronged one had spent four centuries building a paradise out of grief, and when they finally fought, Mei's thrown sword struck true, and the woman fell smiling into dark water. So far, so ordinary; the world was full of such ledgers. What the world was not full of was what Mei did next. She handed her sword to a friend, said *wait for me*, and stepped off the platform into the water, and held her breath past breaking, and *drowned* — not to die; to follow. To apologize to her own casualty on the casualty's home ground.

And the dead woman threw her out. And opened the door again. And the screen filled with the gentlest footage in the program: potatoes peeled badly with a paring knife, stew at a long table under impossible fuchsia trees, an elegy reworked into a waltz, two women on a hill arguing softly about which kind of coward each had been. *Stay*, the dead woman offered, and Mei wept and said she couldn't, and the living world tore her back, four minutes dead, ribs cracked by the friends who refused to stop breathing for her — and far away, in her paradise, the woman she had destroyed wrote in a diary: *She held my hand until reality pulled her back. It's enough.*

"Note the economics," Nivora murmured. "She breaks a woman for four hundred years and is forgiven over dinner. Her victims keep lights on for her. Her *casualties* leave stew in the pot." The Architect rolled her head along the chair back to look at Hiyuki, and her eyes were bright and interested and entirely without warmth. "You've never even been allowed to apologize, have you. Your dead left no forwarding address."

A beat.

"Or," Nivora said, very softly, "have you simply never checked? There's a street in your city you don't walk, Miko. A hill. Some stairs. A year of pilgrimages, forty-one ghosts a day, and never once the first stair. I confess it's my favorite thing about you."

Hiyuki's hands, folded, did not move. *(She knows. She has been watching long enough to know the shape of the one thing I cannot do.)* The frost under her skin lay very still, the way prey lies still.

**REEL THREE — HER MURDERER, WHO LOVED HER**

"My personal favorite," said the Architect, and the orchestra swelled, and the third screen lit.

This one Hiyuki watched standing very straight, because it was a story about a woman who saw the future, and Hiyuki carried a bell that showed her hers.

The silver-haired stranger from the first reel — *Iuno*, High Priestess, oracle of a marble city — had not pulled Mei from the sea out of kindness. The film laid the architecture bare with loving care: a prophecy that demanded a life; thirty years of preparation; an amnesiac woken, named, fed, taught to fly, *raised* — tea ceremonies and careful silences and all of it scaffolding for an appointed hour at which the cultivated woman would walk into the dark and die in the cultivator's place. The Priestess had engineered a volunteer. And then — the reel's voice turned indulgent here, almost tender — the Priestess had fallen in love with her sacrifice, and the plan had begun to fail in the only place plans truly fail, which is inside.

Hiyuki watched the Priestess hide her prophecy for thirty years to spare the people she loved the knowledge of its price. Watched her sit alone in a tent, watching her design click perfectly into place, discovering she could no longer bear it. Watched her stand on a rooftop in the rain above the window of the woman she was raising for slaughter, fist split against stone, unable to knock and unable to leave.

*(That,)* thought some treacherous chamber of Hiyuki, with something horribly like recognition, *(is the first sensible woman these screens have shown me.)*

She crushed the thought. It did not die. It went where everything went — under the frost — and lay there, breathing.

On screen, the appointed hour came, and the two women fought at the world's edge over which of them would die, and fell together instead. And then the film did the thing Hiyuki had already learned to brace for: it refused the ending. Mei tore through a metaphysical hell with her bare hands. Found her murderer crucified at the heart of grief itself, feeding it her guilt to keep it docile. Was offered the trade every sane economy would take — leave the battery, keep the rewritten world — and chose as fools choose, plunging her hand into corruption and ripping the heart free, and the dying Priestess's eyes opened long enough to mouth *you idiot* before everything came apart. And at the end of all of it — the loops, the Shadows, the typewriter where the Priestess sat composing one last self-erasing ending — Mei slapped her hard enough to scatter crystal and said *stop giving me things I never wanted*, and four hands pushed an arrow home together, and a woman dissolved into light promising *I'll find you. Every time,* and the answer came back: *Don't be late. I'll leave the tea on.*

"She loved her murderer back to life," the Architect said into the dark, when the reel had run out. "Thirty years of premeditation, a confession at knifepoint, an actual completed *murder attempt* or two — and the story's response was a love confession and a rescue across three realities." Nivora turned in the chair, propped her chin on the velvet, and smiled at Hiyuki the way one smiles at a favorite exhibit. "Who would have done that for you, Miko? Crossed the dark for you? Pulled your heart out of the grief you've been feeding it to?"

Silence.

"Name one. ...Take your time. We have so much of it." A pause, surgical. "Oh — but there *was* one, wasn't there. Thirty years ago. A woman with sour candy and too many borrowed lives, who looked at all this—" a gesture, lazy, encompassing Hiyuki entire, the folded hands, the frost, the office of perpetual carrying, "—and said *let me hold some of them.* And you handled it. You handled it the way you handle everything." The Architect's voice dropped to a register of pure, delighted intimacy. "Tell me — does she still keep the door oiled? They do that, you know. The ones you teach to stop knocking."

*(...That's enough,)* something in Hiyuki said, and her mouth, with effort, said nothing at all, because saying it would confirm the strike had landed, and the Architect collected confirmed strikes the way other beings collected faces.

**REEL FOUR — THE VILLAIN WASHES ASHORE; IS ADOPTED BY LUNCHTIME**

"Intermission piece," Nivora announced, brightening. "A comedy."

The Priestess, the reel explained, had at one point split off her own ruthlessness, erased her memory, and washed up one-armed and nameless on a beach — the murderer, rendered down to an amnesiac with a crystal stump and a deck of borrowed dice. *And watch*, the Architect narrated, gleeful, *watch how long the world leaves her there.* An acolyte with stubborn warmth was pouring her chamomile within the day. A quartermaster wrote her a name. A living weapon she nearly killed in a ruin handed back the ring keeping her enemy alive — *you're not done yet; neither am I* — and was handed it back in turn when their positions reversed, and the four of them, suspect, believer, employee, deserter, were a family with warrants by the end of the week. Strangers bled for her. A village threw a feast for her. A vampire owed her a favor.

"Two women washed up on two beaches," Nivora said, ticking them off on her fingers. "The fool and the murderer. Both nameless, both broken, both caught before the tide could even take the smell of the sea off them. Do you begin to see it? It is not a pattern of *worlds*, Miko. Worlds are indifferent; I've worn several. It is a pattern of *her*. Things organize around Mei the way iron filings organize around — well." The grin. "Around something I'd very much like to understand. Whereas the pattern around you—" she let the sentence stand empty, a frame with no picture, which was crueler than any picture.

And then, lightly, the first lacquered edge of the actual pitch: "Of course, patterns can be *changed*. With power enough, any beach can be taught new manners. But I'm getting ahead of the program."

**REEL FIVE — A BIRTHDAY. ATTENDANCE: EVERYONE**

The fifth reel was short, and it was the worst one yet, because nothing in it suffered.

A courtyard under a full moon. A fire built deliberately too large, so everyone would have to sit close. Crescent moons cut from a knight's own cloak, strung between rebar posts a queen bent without being asked. A name spelled in stones — IUNO — amended to IUNO + 8, and a wolf quietly adding a ninth stone. Wine of unexplained provenance. A woman with thirty restored years and one arm being taught to dance by the queen who had forgotten her and remembered; a bard weaving someone's structurally unsound humming into a melody as if it had always belonged; the murderer-priestess-amnesiac, whole at last, looking up from cobblestones into golden eyes and saying, lighter than gravity, *hello.*

"They threw her Shadow a birthday party," Nivora said. "Her *Shadow*, Miko. The compartment where she kept the murder. They gave it cake." She rose from the chair at last, strolled to stand beside Hiyuki, and together — horribly companionable — they watched nine people fail to leave a fire. "The stones spell a name, plus the number of people who would notice if it stopped being spelled. The arithmetic of being loved. Run yours for me." The Architect's voice was kind now, which was the worst register she owned. "Go on. I'll wait. HIYUKI, plus..."

*(Plus the dead,)* Hiyuki did not say. *(Plus a shrine I cannot climb to. Plus a door I taught to stop knocking.)*

The fire on the screen burned without her, too large on purpose, warm enough for nine.

**REEL SIX — AND EVEN GRIEF WAS CAUGHT**

"Now the prestige picture," said the Architect. "Pay attention. This one concerns your field."

The sixth reel was about the thing under everything — the oldest grief in the world, an entity born from humanity's longing to be one and its terror of the sea, a being that fed on isolated sorrow and grew vast in the dark, swallowing cities' worth of heartbeats and stringing them from its cathedral ceiling, out of sync, alone together forever. The Leviathan. The deep that had asked, in its own voice like water below the light line, the only question it had: *I am what comes after the surviving. I am what happens when the reaching fails.*

Hiyuki watched a company of mortals walk into that cathedral and refuse to fight it correctly. Watched a bard play individual griefs back to themselves, one at a time, until being witnessed became more interesting than being ordered. Watched thirty thousand stolen heartbeats laugh at a joke about a flat bridge and remember they were individuals and leave, each in its own time, with somewhere else to be. Watched Mei sheathe her blade and concede everything except the conclusion — *you're right, you are what comes after the surviving; so we keep surviving; we survive you; but we don't want to defeat you* — and watched, at the end, a god in shorts kneel to the oldest grief in creation the way you kneel for someone you love who has had a hard time, and offer his palm, and help her up. The grief chose its own shape at last: a tired woman who had carried something heavy for a very long time and had finally been given permission to put it down. And it left. Not slain. *Seen.* Driven off into the morning by gentleness, leaving the dawn and the paperwork to the humans.

The reel ended. Nivora let the silence ripen.

"A being of swallowed grief," she said at last, idly, to the dark. "Carrying ten thousand sorrows that were never hers. Growing heavier and lonelier and more certain that the carrying *was* her, until she had no shape left of her own at all — only cargo." A pause of exquisite length. "Strange. I keep feeling I've described someone. It'll come to me."

She did not look at Hiyuki when she said it. She did not need to.

"And do note the lesson, Miko, since the picture made it so generously: even *that* — even the eater of cities, the author of half your nightmares, the cousin of the rain that took your everything — even that, in *her* story, was caught. Helped up. Walked home." The Architect turned, and her smile had shed every borrowed face and was only itself, which was a door with nothing behind it. "Whereas the things that fall in *your* story... ah. But here we are at the finale. And this one—" she snapped her fingers, and the seventh screen, the largest, woke alone in the dark, "—this one I didn't have to name. The title came with the footage."

The card rose in white on black, and it was addressed.

**REEL SEVEN — FOR YOU,**

— a held beat, the orchestra hushed —

**WHO WALK IN SNOW**

Hiyuki, the woman of snow, hair like first frost, born in it, named for it, stood in the Architect's endless dark and felt the title land on her like a hand on the shoulder, and could not tell — this was the artistry of it — whether the film was addressed to its subject or to its audience of one.

"Both," said Nivora, who had not been asked. "Roll it."

It opened in Honami.

Hiyuki's breath stopped working correctly somewhere in the first thirty seconds, because there on the screen was her city — not the bleached ruin she had walked all day but the sealed city, the inside of the wound, Honami as it had been for the thirty years it was a hole in the map: cherry blossoms falling upward and dissolving, holograms playing to no one, her dead locked in the loops she knew them by. And walking into it, off a ship, through a bleeding fracture in the sky, came Mei and her pack — and they did in days, with music, what Hiyuki had been doing for a year with her sternum.

A bard played the final chords that let looping ghosts dissolve. Mei accepted an undeliverable package from a phantom courier with the courtesy of a recipient. The one-armed priestess told a spectral grandmother that her granddaughter had passed her exams and had wanted, more than anything, to come — and the grandmother *passed*, lighter, resolved, witnessed, and Hiyuki stood in the dark theatre with sixty-three quiet streets suddenly explained inside her, the lantern-path from harbor to tower, the aftermath-textured silences she had walked through all day without understanding, and her own voice came out of her before she could freeze it:

*(So it was them. They were the lantern.)*

The reel poured on. A girl alone in the dead city — alive, the only one, face-blind, abandoned-by-choice — and the pack folded her in without a vote. A dead researcher's road of crystallized memories, each amber a piece of herself burned as fuel, climbing a frozen tower junction by junction, her last entries shaking — *all I ever wanted was to lay down a clear road, so those who come after can walk across my bones and reach the light; that's enough for me; I feel content* — and Hiyuki read the words off the screen and felt them in her own handwriting, because she had been laying that road for a hundred years and calling the laying a life.

The barrier before the tower did not shatter for them. It *parted* — sixty-three resolved griefs translated into one sustained note, amplified through a crystal arm — parted like a crowd making way for something it recognized, and Hiyuki's frost, unbidden, whispered across the black floor in a thin bright ring around her boots, because she knew that grammar, she had a bell that spoke it, and no door of hers had ever once parted. Hers she had always had to break, or become.

"Ah... if only Ashinohara could have..." The Architect's voice drifted out of the dark beside her, wistful as incense. "You had so many companions fighting at your side. And yet, the rain over Honami simply would not cease."

"...That's enough."

*(Every single time,)* the thought went through her anyway, through the discipline, through the frost, in her own voice, in the dark, *(Mei succeeded in saving them.)*

*(If only I could have... Ashinohara...)*

The reel, merciless, kept giving. The pack hauled the broken girl bodily across the threshold of the collapsing city — *YOU'RE GONNA CARRY THAT WEIGHT* — not *put it down*; carry it; *walk anyway*; and the girl walked, and was given back a mother who had kept her room unchanged for twenty years, because the door had always been unlocked. Then a city under the earth, a school, a love that finally stopped being unfinished — *I love you* landing like the next thing before breakfast — an ex-mercenary's forged life answered with an entire pack registering for a first-year entrance exam so she wouldn't sit it alone; a professor stripped of every borrowed miracle, crawling across a relay floor, discovering her students arriving with her proof distributed across dozens of merely human minds; a second sunrise over an underground city, the first dawn its people ever built with their own hands.

And then the ghost.

A voice in the walls signing itself with a singer's old stage name. A luminous girl who had died at nineteen saving the city and hadn't entirely; ten years a ghost in a reactor's heart, and the first thing she did with reach was find Mei — because the ghost had been an orphan once, drowning in a frozen lake, and a stranger had pulled her out and not put her down again, raised her in a cabin with a blue door, planted wisterias, taught her everything. In private, away from every audience she ever had, the ghost had called the stranger *Ma.*

Hiyuki watched a daughter assemble for her mother a single perfect day. A joyride across the city. A parade of ridiculous seal mascots. A final fan-meeting where the ghost sang, at last, the unfinished song from a decade ago — completed — and a rooftop at night under smokeless fireworks, where the girl said, plainly, *I want to be with you, I mean it,* and Mei said *Me too,* and both of them meant it.

Fireworks. Two faces upturned in their light. The orchestra below the floor found a major key and held it like a promise.

**— AND THE LIGHTS OF LAHAI-ROI BURNED ON, EVER AFTER —**

Cut to black. House lights, golden, gentle. The end card dissolved.

"There," said the Grand Architect of the Fractsidus, into the warm theatrical hush, with the satisfaction of a chef setting down the final plate. "The whole program. Seven reels. One woman who falls, and a universe that has never once let her land."

She came around to stand before Hiyuki — small, uniformed, borrowing a schoolgirl's face to deliver a verdict older than nations — and her voice, for the first time all night, dropped every ornament.

"Now let us speak plainly, you and I, vessel to vessel. I have watched you for years. I watched you eat this city's despair one spoonful at a time and call it a vocation. I watched you teach the one friend you had left to stop offering. And I have read the only oracle that matters." A pause. "Only you, Hiyuki. Only you could peer into the Suzu and see a future so utterly... pitch-black."

The bell at Hiyuki's hip — bronze, old, worn smooth by a lineage of hands — seemed to grow heavier as it was named. It was true. That was the unspeakable center of it: it was *true*. The Suzu showed the futures of its bearer, possibility by possibility, each ring a coin of lifespan spent — and once, the ringing had shown her bright things, branching things, selves worth becoming. She had spent them. One by one, crisis by crisis, century by century, she had rung the bell and drawn out her own tomorrows and burned them in other people's todays, and the last time she had dared to look — years ago now — the bell had shown her what remained, and what remained was a darkness so total it had texture. She had told no one. There had been no one to tell. She had seen to that.

"Terrified," Nivora went on, gently, the way one speaks at a bedside, "of ever again feeling what you felt when your sister died, when your family burned, you closed every door, declined every hand, made yourself the last of your line so no one would ever have to grieve a Miko again — and called it mercy. And maybe it was. But you are so *tired*, Hiyuki. You have been too small for your cargo for a hundred years, and every year the cargo grows, and every year you grow only older." The Architect spread her hands, and the dark theatre seemed to lean in. "So. My offer.

"The power that ended your world is going to be born again — that is not a threat; it is a *weather report* — and when it is, it will need a shape. A vessel. I have watched candidates for centuries, and every one of them broke because they carried for *themselves* — for glory, for godhood, for survival, for the plain animal terror of the dark. The carrying ate them because they had a self left for it to eat. But you—" and the borrowed eyes went over Hiyuki slowly, appraising, almost reverent, "—you spent a century sanding the self away and called it a priesthood. You carry for everyone and permit nothing back. You have already amputated every appetite a vessel cannot afford. You are, walking around in the world on two legs, the finished article. So understand me: I am not offering you corruption. Corruption is for people with something left to corrupt. I am offering you *capacity*. The one thing you have ever lacked. Be the vessel that does not overflow. Be, at last, the size of the cargo. No more choosing which griefs to carry — carry all of them, forever, at once. No more being too small. Be the size of the sky." Her voice fell to its softest setting. "The rain that would not cease, Hiyuki — be the one who decides, next time, where it falls."

And then she stepped back, and gestured at the floor, at the dark, at the ruined city sleeping somewhere beyond the theatre's skin, and delivered the close.

"You stand on Honami's soil. You stand on a graveyard of millions. Go on. Ask them if your 'dignity as a human' matters."

The words were wearing borrowed clothes. Hiyuki knew the original; she had been hearing it across a desk for thirty years, wrapped in sour candy and funding complaints — *the dignity of walking one more mile* — Lucilla's creed, looted, gutted, aimed. The Architect quoted the way she did everything: perfectly, with the inside missing.

And the terrible thing — the thing Hiyuki would have to live with afterward, in whatever she had instead of confession — was that it nearly worked.

Because the dark between the screens was leaning toward her now, and it was cold in a way her own cold recognized: a vast, patient, kindred cold, the cousin of the rain. It did not whisper of thrones. It whispered *capacity.* It said: never again too small. It said: the bell can go dark, the arithmetic can finally balance — every bead behind your sternum, and every bead to come, carried at once, forever, by something the size of weather. And the part of her that had knelt on wet steps all day receiving the regrets of children, the part that had been insufficient at Honami and insufficient every morning since — that part did not hear a temptation. It heard a *job description.* The first one in a hundred years she might actually be equal to.

Her hand had risen. She had not asked it to. At the edge of her fingers, where the offered cold met her own, the frost on her skin had gone faintly, beautifully dark, like ice over deep water.

She had exactly one refusal left in her, and standing in the Architect's theatre with seven curated reels burning behind her eyes, she could not, for the life of her, remember what it was made of.

That was when she heard the song.

## V. Da-Da-Da

It was small. It was far away. It was off-key in one voice and sure in the other, and the two were braided so closely that it was impossible to say which was carrying which. And it was coming from the wrong direction — from behind the seventh screen, from underneath the golden afterglow of the end card — which, in a world with exactly one direction, meant it was coming from the truth.

*...sharing the same time and space...*

A thin line of light lay along the bottom edge of the great dark screen. She saw it now. Leaking. The way light leaks under a door when, in the room beyond, something has been left running.

"—and so the only remaining question," the Architect was saying, and stopped.

It was the first unscripted silence of the entire night, and it lasted less than a breath, and it told Hiyuki more than seven reels had. Nivora raised one hand; the orchestra below the floor swelled obligingly, strings piling on strings — and the song came through anyway, the way water comes through a ceiling: patiently, in more places.

Hiyuki was a listener. It was the whole of her office; it was the one thing the century had only deepened. She turned her head a fraction, and gave the small far sound the same attention she gave the dead, and understood three things in the space of three notes.

The first was that it was a duet. Two voices — one grown, one young; one steady, one wandering happily off the pitch and back — woven so that neither was the melody alone. The braid itself was the music.

The second was why the Architect could not silence it. The Thousand-Faced Survivor could wear any voice in the world; Hiyuki had heard her do it, tonight, with her own sister's. But a face is a single thread, and a voice is a single thread, and this was two threads *crossing* — and the Architect's loom, Hiyuki saw with sudden total clarity, had never once taken two. She could counterfeit anyone. She could not counterfeit *between.* And once you saw it, you saw it everywhere: all night, reel after reel, every scene had ended one beat early — releasing its grip at the exact instant the catching began to cost, cutting away the moment two people's weight actually met. Hiyuki had taken it for pacing. It was not pacing. Every splice in the program had been made at a point of contact. Not because contact disproved the Architect's thesis.

Because contact was the one frequency she could not wear.

The third thing Hiyuki understood was the simplest. "There is more footage," she said. "Behind that screen. You cut it, and you kept it. Roll it."

"The program," said Nivora, with the bright finality of a curtain, "is concluded—"

Hiyuki had spent a century being assured — by instruments, by colleagues, by her own disciplined reflection — that she was perfectly stable, and the secret of that stability had always been this: stillness is not the absence of motion. It is motion, mastered. She went still now the way an avalanche is still in the second before it isn't.

Then she breathed out, and froze the theatre.

Not the Architect — there was no Architect there to freeze; a courtesy call, a broadcast, she had said so herself. Hiyuki froze the broadcast. The soundstage fog; the projector beams, turned to solid gold bars of light and dust; the manufactured air with its manufactured warmth; the velvet, the marquee bulbs mid-glow, the orchestra's swelling chord caught and held like an insect in amber — frost took all of it, fast as a gasp, and the endless dark bloomed white, and the screens iced over like ponds in one hard night. And here was the thing about ice that the Architect, a creature of edits, had never had to learn:

Frost is honest. It keeps what it is given — *all* of what it is given. In a hundred years of her art, Hiyuki had never once asked it to keep anything but pain. She asked now. The frozen light held every frame that had ever passed through it, and crystal does not curate, and across ten thousand facets — refracted, multiplied, undeniable — the cut footage began to play.

She saw the rest of the perfect day. She saw what the fireworks had been for.

She saw a barrier that could not be cut, could not be broken, could not be walked through, and a nineteen-year-old's palm flat against the glass from the other side, and the smile that had been rehearsed for exactly this. *You asked if I was happy. Yes. Every single second... So don't cry for me, Ma. You raised a star.* She saw the girl turn, and walk into the dark of a sealed god, singing.

She saw Mei hit the glass.

She heard the sound that came out of her — the reels had been silent about this sound, and now the ice gave it whole — and saw the wolf at the door go grey under her fur, because the wolf had heard that sound exactly once before in her life, from a mother in the arena stands, and had prayed to nothing in particular never to hear it again.

She saw the sealed pod that read OCCUPIED. The shell inside it — warm, breathing, heart beating, materially perfect, and empty: a candle-shaped object with no flame. She saw Mei take it *home*, to the cabin with the blue door, and begin, with absolute conviction, to live with her daughter — fires laid for a body that could not feel them, meals spooned into a mouth that did not swallow, *Iuiu, say hi to Amy* — and she saw the priestess, terrified that the alternative to the dollhouse was Mei following the girl into the dark, silently erase every record, close every inquiry, and step *inside* the delusion to co-author it: keeping her alive by keeping her company in the madness. She saw the wolf outside in the snow, raging at what she refused to anesthetize — *watching the woman I followed into hell try to feed a corpse* — and saw that neither of them was wrong, and that love had no protocol for this, and did it anyway, badly, in shifts.

She saw the piece of paper that ended it: a child's crayon drawing, a lone hero against an empire, the cape left uncolored where the right crayon was never found — and a mother's voice speaking a child's logic to a breathing shell: *You won... you're the hero... so why won't you come home?* — and the question had no answer, and the dollhouse came down all at once into real grief, and in that exact moment the shell moved. The hand rose — the last residual instruction left in the chassis, the final thing a dying girl had thought to record there — and wiped the tear from her mother's cheek. *Don't... be sad...* The hand fell. Outside, the blizzard stopped. Spring light came through the window.

And from the cabin's shelf, an impossible record played — a recording that should not exist, of a young ghost and a mother who no longer remembered making it, two voices braided across dust and years — and the frozen theatre, ten thousand facets strong, played it with them, entire, uncut, the song the Architect had spent all night editing around:

*Isn't it so incredible —
We're here sharing the same time and space.
In your eyes, the sparks look like
Thousands of bright stars I have gazed at for thousands of time.
May all your sweet dreams take flight,
With the truth to be your guiding light.
So let's sing together —
Every light I'm giving all comes back to you.
Comes back to you.*

And then the words gave out — not because the song was over, but because the singers had run out of need for them — *da-da-da*, *da-da-da* — two people too happy to need words, going on past the lyric the way light goes on after the lamp.

The ice was not finished.

It showed the morning after the embrace: the strongest carrier in two worlds curled into the smallest possible shape on a dormitory bed, not asleep, not resting — *absent* — a body that had stopped deciding what to do next. It showed the priestess recognize the posture from the inside, and cancel a prosthetic calibration three weeks in the scheduling, and lie down with her crystal stump pressed to the curve of Mei's spine — not comfort; *coordinates.* It showed boots pausing outside the door and returning at noon only long enough to slide a thermal flask of hot tea across the floor and withdraw without demanding acknowledgment; soup later, two portions, the second taped shut. It showed *you should go* answered with a flat, un-negotiable *no.* It showed a single syllable — *hungry* — crack something open, and a spoon, and a bath, administered with the unromantic competence of someone keeping clean the body of the woman who had always caught everyone else. *Iuiu... I'm sorry.* — *Don't apologize for grieving your daughter. You're apologizing for being inconvenient. I know that logic.* It showed five people filling a room built for two, nobody performing, nobody asking for anything, a pack breathing around its broken center of gravity. And it showed a forest bench, and the conversation the seventh reel had been cut to never reach:

*Why am I like this? Why don't I just value myself? ...I've hurt so many people.*

*You have hurt people. I've hurt more. ...You're like this because someone put you in a box and told you the box was the world. The tree doesn't need a reason to grow. But someone kept pruning yours and calling it a mission. ...You also taught her to kneel in snow for frightened children. She chose to come back for you — because you taught her some people are worth the trip. You just never included yourself in the math.*

*It still feels wrong sometimes. I stay anyway. And the wrong gets smaller. You don't have to believe you're worth it yet. I'll believe it for both of us until yours catches up.*

*Don't let me go, Iuiu.*

*No.*

And behind these, in deeper facets, the rest of the suppressed program — the whole negative of the print she had been shown. A conductor falling into dark water with the heroine's thrown sword in her chest, four hundred years of waiting ended by the woman who had caused them. A gladiator coming apart into ash, thanking her killer *for remembering.* A man pulling a battlefield's whole corruption into his own frame and asking his friends for the spear, so the ending would be his. A half-crystallized girl on a tower's ninth floor writing *I feel content* in a failing hand — and not coming back, ever, no matter how many people walked her road to its end. A god's last servant pouring himself into a calculation and remaining there, merged, a gift the city would never know it received. Sumika. Mya. Avidius. Phrolova. The Shaper. Aemeath. The reels had been edited to show a woman who saved everyone.

The ice showed the graves.

And the ice showed the heroine herself, uncurated. Crying herself to sleep arguing over which version of her beloved she was grieving. Filing a city's worth of revelations behind a smile and going to pick up her coffee order. Walking an infinite school corridor where a faceless girl made her promise, loop after loop, *you'll come back to me, right?* — and promising, every single time, because she could not help herself. A wolf's flat diagnosis over morning drinks — *she lives to meet others' expectations* — and, in a sparring yard, the sentence that had been waiting for Hiyuki all night, spoken to someone else and true of them both: *you fight like you're apologizing.* The ice showed three in the morning at the bottom of Mei's own mind, when the oldest grief in the world came to her not with monsters but with an offer — the dissolution of every wall, the end of the exhausting work of being a separate self — and showed how close she came. Bone-tired of being the one who always carries, the woman who saved everyone *very nearly stepped into the warm dark.* What pulled her out was not strength, and it was not virtue. It was the waking room. A name said aloud. A song sung at her, badly, on purpose. People.

Hiyuki stood in her theatre of ice with the uncut story burning on every facet, and the realizations came the way thaw comes — not gently; all at once; with the sound of things cracking.

*The world does not catch her.* That was the first, and it took the entire curated night apart at a stroke. There was no property of the universe called being-caught — no luck, no birthright, nothing for a vessel to envy. There were only people: people who had been caught once and learned the grammar of it, knotting a net out of their own falls, hand over hand over hand. And at the center of the net, the one knot that let all the others hold — a woman who had finally, after two lifetimes, on a forest bench, in a fed-by-spoon morning, consented to be one of the people. *I can't anymore*, said out loud. That was the keystone. That was the whole architecture. The catching only works on someone who stops bricking up the door, and Mei had needed two lifetimes to stop, and it had cost her a daughter to learn how.

*She did not save everyone.* That was the second, and Hiyuki felt thirty years of meticulously tended envy simply — dissolve. The way a phantom dissolves: having finally been heard. *(Every single time, Mei succeeded in saving them)* — she had bled the thought in the dark like a wound, and it was false; it had always been false; the reels had been cut to make it true. Mei's dead would fill a district. What Mei had never lost was not people. It was the willingness to remain one.

The third realization arrived wearing her own face.

She had watched the ghost-girl all night from the wrong side of the glass — of course she had; Hiyuki was *the one who goes in.* The orphan pulled from the ice. The prodigy of self-erasure, *don't worry about me* engraved on every step. The perfect day built as an anesthetic; the walk into the dark, alone, singing; *content* — content, the word the crystallizing girl had written on the ninth floor, the word Hiyuki had been drafting as her own epitaph for a hundred years. Aemeath was not a stranger in someone else's tragedy. Aemeath was the manual Hiyuki had written her life by, executed flawlessly by a nineteen-year-old inside a single decade. And the ice had just spent an eternity showing her, in surgical detail, what *content* does to the one left at the glass.

And she knew the glass. That was the unbearable symmetry of it. The mantle of Flaming Sakura was a lineage of women who walk into the dark singing — each one vowing the next will not have to, each one ensuring the next does — and when Hiyuki's own turn at the glass had come, when the rain took her sister and her family and her city in a single unceasing night, she had made the only vow that seemed to follow: *there will never be another Miko of Flaming Sakura.* No successor. No one trained for the next walk. No one left standing at the glass when her own walk came. She had called it mercy, and it was mercy — and underneath the mercy, in the chamber where the frost kept everything, it had always also been armor. No successor meant no mourner. No mourner meant no glass. No glass meant the clean exit, the road of bones, *walk across me and reach the light, that's enough for me.*

Except the ones at the glass are not appointed, and cannot be decreed away. That was what the ice was for. You do not get to choose — by vow, by silence, by a hundred years of perfect composure — who stands at the glass when you go into the dark. You only get to choose whether you ever looked at them. And Lucilla — Lucilla of the sour candy and the oiled hinges, Lucilla who had offered once, thirty years ago, *let me hold some of them,* and been handled — Lucilla had been standing at the glass the entire time. Every leave quietly pre-approved. Every pilgrimage into a graveyard tracked at a tactful distance through a communicator disguised as a makeup mirror. *Go deal with matters of the heart first.* Hiyuki had never spared her the glass.

She had only forbidden her to knock on it.

(And the priestess in the ice, the woman fate had crystallized as the price of her foresight, glass climbing her arm year by year — Hiyuki had needed no fate for that. She had been administering her own frost by hand, dose by careful dose, for a century, and entering it in the records as discipline.)

The fourth realization came from inside her own chest.

Because she was part of the frozen theatre now, and crystal does not curate, and the beads behind her sternum — the day's forty-one, and beneath them the years', the uncounted reliquary of everything she had ever swallowed — were refracting too. For the first time in a hundred years of carrying, she *heard* them. Whole. In chorus. Every regret she had ever taken, playing back at once through the facets — and they were not what she had filed them as.

*I never lowered my arm. If I lower it, she'll get wet when she comes* — not pain. A reach.

*You don't apologize to sisters. You assume there will be time* — not pain. A reach.

*It's stupid, lady, there were people who lost whole families* — a reach, a reach, every one of them a reach: every regret a severed connection, every wish the same wish conjugated ten thousand ways — *I should have crossed the distance while the distance could still be crossed.* The dead of Honami had never been asking her to suffer for them. They had been telling her — one by one, spoonful by spoonful, in the only tense the dead possess — the single thing the dead know that the living refuse to: *don't do what we did.* A hundred years of final instructions. She had filed every one of them as freight. She had built her isolation out of bricks that were, each and every brick, a plea against isolation. She had been carrying a library, and she had never once read it.

And under all of them, beneath the umbrellas and letters and unreturned cartridges, the wish below the wishes, the one every phantom carried whether or not it ever found words: *let someone remember us. Someone. Anyone. That is all it ever was.*

The fifth realization she did not have to reach for, because the Architect had hand-delivered it herself, smiling, between the sixth reel and the seventh. *A being of swallowed grief, carrying ten thousand sorrows that were never hers, growing heavier and lonelier until she had no shape left of her own — only cargo.* *I am what comes after the surviving. I am what happens when the reaching fails.* It had never been the description of a monster. It had been a forecast. Keep swallowing alone — year upon year, century upon century — and you do not become a saint. You become weather. You become the thing the rain was. The Architect's offer was not a rescue from that future.

It was that future, with better staging.

Somewhere in the ice, on a loop, a recorded hand rose from a dead girl's shell and wiped a tear from her mother's cheek — *don't... be sad...* — the last kindness left in the chassis, performed, and performed, and performed.

Hiyuki put her hand to her own cheek and found it wet.

She had not noticed. That was the truth of it; that was one hundred years compressed into a single fact: she had not noticed she was crying, because noticing had no function, because there had never been anyone to notice *for.* The tear ran the line of her jaw and fell. It did not freeze. In all the long cold of her, this one warm thing fell the whole way down and struck the frost at her feet and made a small dark circle there, like the first drop of rain on a dry street, and she looked at it for a long time.

No hand had risen to wipe it. There was no hand. That — and she understood it now completely, the way you understand a blade only once it is all the way in — that had been the catastrophe all along. It was never the rain.

It was the absence of hands.

The song wound on through all of it, da-da-da, da-da-da, patient as water through a ceiling. *Every light I'm giving all comes back to you.* She had run the other economy her whole life — light out, nothing permitted back, a one-way ledger she had mistaken for virtue — and here were two voices, one belonging to a dead girl who had run the same ledger all the way to its end, singing the correction across dust and years to anyone with frost enough to keep it: the giving was never the point. The *circuit* is the point. A light that does not come back was never shared. It was only spent.

Mei was not her opposite. Mei was her, with witnesses. Hiyuki had been the Priestess without a precipice, the Fool without a pack, the ghost-girl without a Ma at the glass — she had been, for one hundred years, a faithful rendering of what every one of them would have become if no one had ever been allowed to stay.

And no one had ever been allowed to stay because *she was the one doing the allowing.* There was no sealed homeland refusing her transmissions. No voice behind a cold door pronouncing *finish the mission.* The Miko's box had a warden once; the warden was thirty years dead; the only person still pruning Hiyuki's tree and calling it a mission was Hiyuki. Which meant — and this arrived not as despair but as something with light in it, terrible, usable light — the door had never been locked. The prison had no staff.

She could simply stop. Not stop fighting. Not stop carrying.

Stop doing it alone.

And in one facet — a single frame, far down in the ice, smaller than all the others, hidden the way one hides a thing from oneself — there was a picture that did not belong to the heroine's story at all: a ferryman at a dark river, between fares, no audience anywhere in any world, holding a lantern up over the water to study a face that would not stay the same. Practicing, in the privacy of centuries, the expression of someone glad to see someone.

Hiyuki looked at that frame for exactly as long as it deserved, which was longer than its owner would have liked.

And said nothing. Yet.

## VI. Pitch-Black, and Then

The Architect let the ice have its say. Credit where it was due: she did not bluster, did not rage, did not pretend the theatre was still hers. She walked out into the middle of the frozen backlot — a schoolgirl in a wonderland of frost, her breath making no mist, because she had no breath — and surveyed the ten thousand burning facets of her own suppressed archive with the expression of a curator assessing flood damage.

"Well," she said. "This is why one doesn't keep prints."

"You kept everything," Hiyuki said.

It was the first thing she had said in a long while, and it came out level, almost gentle, and it landed harder than any blade she owned. "Every frame. The glass. The spoon. The bench. The song. You cut nothing, Architect — cutting would have meant destroying it, and you could not. You only looked away. You built a trap for me out of her story, and salted away in the same archive, in the dark, every part of her story that you cannot stop watching." She turned from the ice to the small uniformed figure, and asked the question with no edge on it at all, which is the kind that goes in deepest. "A thousand faces. Has even one of them ever been caught?"

For one frame — one — something moved behind the borrowed face that was borrowed from no one.

Then the Survivor did what survivors do, and survived it. The grin reassembled itself tooth by tooth. "Sentiment noted, filed, and — may I say — *adorable.* But you've mistaken the exhibit for the argument, Miko. Nothing in your pretty ice refutes my offer. If anything—" she gestured at the keening, the dollhouse, the spoon, all of it burning steadily on across the facets, "—it closes the sale. *Look* at what caring at human scale did to the strongest carrier in two worlds. Broke her like a wishbone over one ghost. You aspire to that? To grieve like that — to be fed like that? Or—" and the voice went soft as a bedside lamp, the cruelest register she owned, "—to be done with scale entirely. I told you no lies tonight; audit me, you'll find I never needed any. The rain is coming back, Hiyuki. When it comes, it will need a shape — and the shape can be some frightened stranger who carries it for all the wrong reasons. Or it can be the one vessel alive with the discipline to hold a Lament and the kindness to *aim* one. Your dead are listening, Miko. Last chance to give them a better ending."

"I asked them," Hiyuki said.

The Architect's head tilted, a degree of genuine curiosity. "...I beg your pardon?"

"The graveyard. You told me to ask my dead whether the dignity of a human matters." She touched her sternum — the reliquary, where forty-one and ten thousand wishes had spent the night becoming legible. "I have been asking them, one at a time, for a hundred years, and tonight I finally heard the answers. Not one of them — not one, Architect, in a graveyard of millions — wished for power. Not one wished the rain had been theirs to aim. They wished they had lowered the umbrella. Sent the letter. Returned the cartridge. Eaten the pudding while the pudding was warm and the sister was alive." Her voice did not rise; the ice carried it everywhere. "Every wish in this city is a reach across a distance that could have been crossed, and wasn't. That is the verdict of the dead of Honami, entered into evidence, ten thousand to nothing. You stand in their graveyard offering me the power that dug it, and you call my refusal pride."

She regarded the small figure almost kindly.

"And the offer itself. You watched me for years; then you know precisely what I am. A woman who swallows grief alone, and grows heavier. So hear what you are actually proposing. You are not offering me power, Architect. You are offering me a larger silence. The same disease, at scale — my one mistake, made infinite and weatherproof. You showed me a woman spooning meals into her daughter's empty shell and called it the saddest thing in seven reels. You are inviting me to spoon-feed a dead world until the stars go out, and calling it apotheosis." A breath. "I decline. Not because I am strong. Because I have seen the uncut reel."

"The Suzu, then." The Architect's voice had cooled by degrees; the showmanship was thinning, and what lay under it was older and stiller. "Peer into your bell one last time, Miko, before you spend your only offer on principle. Look at the future you are choosing, and tell me what you see in it that is worth—"

"No."

Hiyuki took the Suzu from her hip. It lay in her palm, worn bronze, smoothed by a lineage of hands — every Miko who had ever rung tomorrow out of herself and paid for it in years.

"You quoted a colleague of mine tonight," she said. "Badly. *Dignity as a human* — you wore the words the way you wear faces, with everything underneath missing. So let me return them with the underneath included. She believes — she has believed it loudly, at me, across a desk, for thirty years, while I filed it as noise — that the point was never the far end of the road. There is no far end. The fight does not finish. The answers stay out of reach. The campfire is dying and we carry it anyway, one more mile into the dark — not for glory, not even for survival. For the dignity of walking it *beside someone.*" The bell sat in her hand like a small bronze verdict. "You ask what future I see. I am done asking the bell to show me. The last time I looked, it was pitch-black — and I let a pitch-black room convince me it was empty. But I have it on recent authority—" and here, despite the night, despite everything, something at the corner of her mouth moved that on any other face would have been a smile, "—that a dark room only means you have not yet decided what it contains."

And she rang it.

Not as an oracle. She drew no tomorrow out of herself to spend; she paid the coin of lifespan freely, eyes open, and rang the Suzu the way her office had always meant it to be rung — the way ten thousand pilgrims had rung it at the shrine gate with wishes cupped in their hands: to call what is sacred near, and send what is false away.

One ring. One clean bronze note into a theatre of frequency-bearing crystal — and ten thousand facets took it up at once, the way sixty-three resolved griefs had once been taken up into a single sustained note before a frozen tower, and the note went through the Architect's architecture the way a sung truth goes through a city: everywhere, at once, impossible to confiscate.

The theatre did not shatter. It *parted* — screens, sets, marquee bulbs, the velvet seat for one, the rolled-up sea, the plaster cliff, all of it folding away from the sound like a crowd making way for something it recognized — and through the parting came the ordinary dark of Honami an hour before dawn, and the thin rain, and the wet living smell of a real ruined street.

The Architect stood in the middle of her collapsing wonder, hands in her uniform pockets, watching it go with what might have been professional appreciation. Around her, one small hidden frame burned a half-second longer than everything else before it went. They both saw it. Neither said so.

"Well," Nivora said at last, brightly, to the rain. "I came for a vessel. I appear to be leaving with a critic." The borrowed face turned to Hiyuki, and the grin on it now was new — and the newness was the unsettling part: there was a thing in it the whole evening's grins had lacked, which was interest, unperformed. "Do you know, in several centuries of recruitment, you are the first to refuse me by *diagnosis.* I'm going to think about that. I detest thinking about things. Consider it your damage." She had begun to come apart at the edges, ink remembering it was ink. "Keep the memories, Miko — all of them, the cut and the kept. Consider them a gift between collectors." A pause, at the very threshold of dissolution; and the voice, when it came again, was almost — almost — its own. "She does collect people like us, you know. Eventually. It is the single most appalling thing about her."

"Nivora."

The ink paused.

"The face under all of them," Hiyuki said. "When you find an evening with nothing left to wear — the dead of this city take walk-ins."

For a moment the rain fell through the space where a schoolgirl had been, and the dark carried something that was either a laugh or the beginning of one, withdrawn unfinished. Then the last of the theatre folded into nothing, and Hiyuki of Flaming Sakura stood alone in a wet street in Honami at the hour before dawn—

—and the rain over Honami ceased.

Just like that. No ceremony. The drizzle thinned, faltered, stopped — the way, in another country's frostlands, a blizzard had once stopped outside a cabin window — and through the salt-bleached towers came the first grey light already leaning toward gold, and the puddles in the street lay still, and began, one by one, to hold the sky.

## VII. Two Bowls

She had one day of leave remaining, and she spent it among the Left Behind, and the work was the same, and it was entirely different.

In the printers' quarter she found an old man rehearsing an apology to a son forty years gone — the same eleven words, polished featureless by repetition, never sent. The old way was simple: take the regret, swallow the stone, let him pass. Instead, Hiyuki sat down on the step beside the loop and said, "Tell me. I will hear it as he would have."

The phantom faltered. Turned. Told her — the eleven words first, and then the eleven hundred underneath them that the eleven had been carrying all this time. She did not absorb them. She *received* them: witnessed, answered, asked the one question a son would have asked. And when the old man passed, he passed the way the grandmother in the footage had passed — lighter than any phantom she had ever carried — and left nothing behind her sternum at all. No bead. No stone. For the first time in one hundred years, the work weighed nothing, and Hiyuki stood in the brightening street with a kind of vertigo, understanding that it always could have.

Not all of them could go that way. Some griefs have no door but through; some stones still had to be swallowed, and she swallowed them, because the office was the office. But she had a second grammar now, and she spent the morning using it everywhere it would hold, and by noon she had stopped flinching at how light it left her. Lighter is not less serious. The wolf in the footage had said it best, roaring a girl across a threshold: you carry the weight. You just stop carrying it *instead of* walking.

At the edge of the ruin, where survey crews and the first stubborn pilgrims had raised a camp of tarps and cookfires, a single food cart stood under a salvaged shop banner, selling tea and sweet things to people who worked among the dead. Hiyuki bought two servings of red bean pudding. The vendor, a young woman with flour on her wrists, looked at the white-haired stranger with the bearing of a winter shrine, and visibly decided not to ask.

Then Hiyuki climbed the eastern hill.

The stairs were exactly as long as a year of not climbing them. The stone lanterns of the pilgrim road stood at their old intervals, capped with moss, patient as only stone is patient, and she went up through them at the processional pace her office prescribed, because if she stopped prescribing herself things she was going to run.

The Shrine of Flaming Sakura stood at the top the way a burned thing stands: in outline, in memory, in the stubbornness of stone. The torii had survived. The great sakura had not — it was a black calligraphy of itself against the brightening sky — and yet the courtyard had been swept. That was the first impossible thing: thirty years of ash and salt-dust, and a clean path from gate to offering step, the broom-strokes still visible, renewed and renewed and renewed by someone with all the time in the world.

The second impossible thing stood at the gate itself, looking down the pilgrim road. Looking — and the understanding arrived the way a blade arrives, all the way in before the pain — down the road Hiyuki had just climbed. The way you look when the person you are waiting for is someone particular.

There was no iris in the air. That was how she knew. The air smelled of char and rain and, underneath, faint as a held note, of steamed green leaves — sweet grass — jade dew. Her name; the joke of her name; the namesake they used to brew on slow afternoons at a cool, patient temperature, because *patience, little one. Some things are ruined by too much heat.*

"My, my," said Gyokuro, without turning around. "Can't a sister just want to see her favorite girl without a reason?"

Hiyuki's knees took her the last three steps the way they had taken her through battles: without consulting her. She set the two bowls of pudding on the offering step with hands that did not shake because she did not permit them to, and then her sister turned around, and the not-shaking stopped mattering.

"Hehe." The smile was the one from the veranda, and from everywhere, and from nowhere ever again. "There's that serious little face."

"You were—" Her voice had carried verdicts across battlefields. It managed four words. "You were waiting. Here. All—"

"Mm. Sweeping, mostly." Gyokuro looked down the road, easy, unhurried, the loop and the woman indistinguishable now, both of them made of waiting. "You always did take the long way home."

There was a protocol. There had been a protocol for a hundred years; it was the spine that had carried her up ten thousand mornings; and Hiyuki heard her own voice perform it now and break, for the first time in her career, on the second word.

"What... is your wish?"

Her sister considered the question with the unbearable lightness of the dead, who have had time to think.

"I made it look easy," Gyokuro said. "That's the one I'd take back, if taking back were a thing the office offered. I carried it where you could see and set it down where you couldn't — late, drunk, on verandas, on the nights you thought I didn't know you'd tucked the blanket — and so you learned half the office, little one, and called it the whole thing. The carrying, you mastered. The setting down where someone can see you—" She glanced at the two bowls, and her smile did the thing it had always done, the thing no forgery had managed: it arrived in the eyes first. "I never taught it. So that's the wish, if I'm owed one. Not a regret. I refuse to leave you a regret; you're *full.*" She crouched over the offering step, over the pudding she could not taste, and breathed the steam with her eyes closed, the way the sealed girl in the footage had held honey cakes she could no longer taste, because nearness is its own whole language. "An errand, instead. The second bowl. Eat it with someone whose hands are warm. That's the wish, Hiyuki. That's the whole wish."

"...The vow stands," Hiyuki said. It came out smaller than any voice she owned. "There will never be another Miko of Flaming Sakura."

"Good." No hesitation at all. "Keep the vow. Lose the reason. Let it end with you because the *aloneness* ends with you — not because you do."

Then her sister rose, and reached out, and ruffled her hair — and Hiyuki's whole body went still under it, because she knew that gesture; she had spent a century pressing it into the heads of frightened children with a charm folded into it, and had never once wondered where her hand had learned the motion. From this hand. From exactly this hand, which passed now through the crown of her head with the weight of a memory of weight, twice, three times, unhurried.

"Look at you," Gyokuro said softly. "Last and best."

She was already going — not dissolving the way the carried ones went, not thinning into the ice behind a vessel's sternum, but *passing*, lit from somewhere ahead of her, the way the witnessed go. At the last edge of her, she paused, and the grin came back around like sunrise.

"Hiyuki. The pudding stand by the east gate, when it reopens." A wink, across thirty years and the whole of death. "Order for two anyway. I'll manage."

And she was gone, and the swept courtyard held its morning, and the great black tree stood over one cooling bowl of red bean pudding set out for the dead — and the woman kneeling beside it, who stayed a long time, and did not carry anything away from that hilltop that weighed what stones weigh.

She did not leave a regret behind to be carried.

She left an errand.

On the long walk down, Hiyuki made one further decision, and it had golden eyes. Somewhere under the mountain, in the lit city, was a woman who had no idea that her uncut story had spent a night in a frozen theatre keeping a stranger from saying yes to the rain. Someday — not soon; Hiyuki was new at this, and the woman had grief of her own still cooling — she would find the Arbiter and tell her so. *Every light I'm giving all comes back to you.* Somebody ought to bring her hers.

---

She was back at Startorch by evening, a day early, smelling of rain and iris and, faintly, of red beans.

Lucilla's office was lamplit and loud with the silent traffic of a hundred open documents. The President looked up from the funding-committee mirror, took in her colleague in a single pass — bearing unchanged, frost unchanged, and underneath both of them something moved that thirty years of memory-tuning could read like large print — and set the mirror face-down on the desk. Which was, from Lucilla, the equivalent of clearing a battlefield.

"You're back early," she said. And then, carefully, lightly, the way one tests ice: "You're back *different.*"

Hiyuki crossed the office and set down a lacquered box. Inside: two servings of red bean pudding, and a small travel flask. She poured. The tea came out pale gold-green, and the steam off it rose gently, because it had been brewed the way some things must be brewed — with the water cooled first.

"Her name was Gyokuro," Hiyuki said.

It was the first time in thirty years the name had been spoken aloud to a living person, and Hiyuki discovered, in the speaking, the true mechanics of the thing she had feared all this time — that her dead existed only in her; that when she ended, they ended; that a whole world of them balanced on one tired rememberer. The mechanics turned out to be simple. You say the name to someone.

Now there were two.

"She was the Miko before me. My sister. She liked her tea cool — it's her name; it was our joke; we were going to—" The sentence did what sentences do at that depth, and stopped. Hiyuki looked at the two bowls and began it differently. "There was a pudding stand by the east gate. We never went. I have carried that for thirty years, and yesterday I was informed, by a reliable authority, that I have been carrying it wrong." She slid one bowl across the desk. "She left me an errand instead of a regret. The terms specify someone whose hands are warm."

Lucilla looked at the bowl for a long moment. When she looked up, her composure had a crack in it the exact shape of thirty years.

"You are *going*," she said, "to make me cry in front of the funding-committee mirror, and I will dock your pay for it."

"Understood."

They ate. The Academy hummed beneath them like a great patient creature, carrying its campfire one more mile into the dark, and the pudding was sweet, and the tea was cool, and neither of them hurried.

Somewhere in the second half of the bowl, without looking up, Hiyuki said: "You offered, once. To carry some of them."

The spoon in Lucilla's hand went very still.

"I remember," said the woman who remembered everything — in the voice of someone who had kept one particular memory dusted for three decades, in case it was ever sent for.

"Ask me again," Hiyuki said. "Sometime. Not tonight. I am told the wrong gets smaller."

"Then I'll ask every so often," Lucilla said, picking the spoon back up, light as anything, eyes too bright, "until your *yes* catches up."

"...There was also this." Hiyuki set it beside the teapot: a small carved bird, lately finished, its proportions in open rebellion, a familiar ornament suggested at its brow in three knife-strokes. "You asked for a souvenir."

Lucilla picked it up and turned it in the lamplight. "...Is this me?"

"It's a heron."

"It has my hairpin."

"It is a very dignified heron."

And Lucilla laughed — the real one, the one from before the door, the one Hiyuki had not heard in thirty years and had, the ice had shown her, missed the way you miss a season — and the sour candy came out of the drawer and was set beside the heron with ceremonial gravity, an exchange of confections ratified between heads of state.

Later — the pudding gone, the lamps low — Lucilla hummed something while she stacked the bowls. Tuneless. Off-key. Three falling notes, *da-da-da*, the way people hum when an evening has gone somewhere words don't go.

It was not the same song.

It did not need to be. That was the secret the impossible record had been keeping all along, the one a dead girl and her mother had braided into the dust for whoever had frost enough to find it: the words were never the cargo. The cargo was two voices in the same room in the same hour, glad of it — and any three notes will carry that, hummed badly, by anyone, anywhere there are two.

Hiyuki took up the second voice, almost inaudibly. *Da-da-da.*

Far to the north, on a swept hilltop, a bowl of pudding cooled under a black tree that would have to decide, some spring or other, all over again what it contained. Behind her sternum, ten thousand wishes lay legible at last — a library now, not a load. The office lamp burned. The hour went by.

She was not alone.

*Someone Will Remember Us — fin.*

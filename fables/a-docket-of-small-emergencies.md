## I. 鵝 — The Goose
### *Jinzhou, the tax office, harbor ward*

The goose has held the tax office door for six years.

It came off the lake in a hard spring and declined to go back, and the assessors fed it out of pity for one week and out of policy thereafter, and by the second year it had a name that the office used in correspondence and by the fourth it had a place in the census.

Column twelve. *Non-human residents, permanent.* Occupation: *deterrent.* Housing status: *doorway.* Income: *exempt.*

The clerk who made that entry was having a week. The entry stands.

Patroller Captain Chixia is 0 for 14.

"Fifteen," Baizhi said, at lunch, writing.

"It was a draw."

"You went in through the window."

"I got the form."

"You got a form. You went for the levy exemption and came out with an application for a fishing licence, and you were bleeding from the ear." Baizhi turned the ledger so the table could see the column. "I have started a second chart."

"Why."

"The first one is about you." She capped her ink. "The second one is about the goose. They are diverging."

Yangyang ate her rice and stayed out of it, which was her whole diplomatic position on the goose and had been since the incident, and the incident was this: the goose likes her. It comes to the rail when she passes. It keeps its head up the entire time. She earned this by walking past it, once, on a Thursday, and she has stopped trying to account for it, and Chixia has kept it warm for two years like a grudge with a pension.

"It's because you're quiet," Chixia said.

"Mm."

"You don't make eye contact. It reads that as submission."

"It sits on the sun-warm flagstone at eleven and I walk past at eleven."

"So it's *scheduling.*"

"It's a goose."

"It's a goose that has *scheduling.*"

---

The formal complaint went in at the end of the month, and Chixia wrote it herself, and it was four hundred characters long and used the phrase *obstruction of a lawful officer* twice.

The tax office's reply was the fourth posting on the notice board.

She escalated. This is a thing you may do. The escalation went to the ward magistrate's office, and the ward magistrate's office established that the goose was a registered resident of the premises under column twelve, and that a registered resident may be removed from a premises by eviction, and that eviction requires a warrant, and that a warrant for a residential eviction in the harbor ward is issued by the Magistrate.

Chixia read that sentence three times in the muster yard.

"You're going to do it," Baizhi said.

"I'm going to do it."

"Her Excellency dreams the fall of cities."

"Then she'll enjoy a change of pace."

---

The application went up through channels with the correct chops in the correct order, because Chixia is sloppy about many things and thorough about exactly two, and one of them is paperwork that is going to be read by someone she respects.

It came back in nine days. It came back fast, which is its own kind of comment, and it came back with one line under the disposition in the flat clean hand that the whole city can identify at forty paces.

> *Denied. The goose holds the tax office door better than two of my assessors. Approach from the east; it favors the left eye. — J.*

Chixia stood in the yard with the paper.

"She gave me tactics," she said.

Baizhi looked over her shoulder and read it and wrote something in the second chart.

---

The east approach worked.

It worked on a Tuesday, in front of nine witnesses, and Chixia walked up the tax office steps unopposed and through the door and got her levy exemption stamped by a clerk who applauded, and came out again into a courtyard where the goose was standing in the middle of the path, facing her, at rest, with the whole of the east side of its body available.

She stopped. It looked at her with the left eye.

"Oh," she said.

They regarded each other for some seconds. Somebody's shutter closed upstairs. Then Chixia broke off half her sesame bun and put it down on the flagstone, and stepped around, and went back to the barracks, and told nobody anything for two days.

Baizhi wrote it down anyway, because the second chart is very good now, and she has begun considering a title.

## II. 雲 — Cloud
### *Jinzhou, the drill yard, forenoon*

At the eleventh stroke a cloud came over the north shoulder shaped exactly like Sergeant Mo.

The likeness was total. The jaw. The set of the shoulders. The specific angle at which the head is held by a man who has spent twenty years about to say something disappointing. Third Squad saw it first and went absolutely still, and Fourth Squad saw Third Squad go still and looked up, and within a minute the whole yard was standing at ease with its face at the sky and its formation dissolving from the back.

"Do we tell him," somebody said.

"We do not tell him."

"He's going to see it."

"He is going to see it and we are going to be *elsewhere.*"

"Wei's telling him."

Wei was telling him. Wei had crossed half the yard in the confident stride of a man who has misjudged a room and does not yet know it, and reached the sergeant, and pointed.

Sergeant Mo turned around and put his hands behind his back and looked up.

The yard waited.

He looked at it for a long time. The cloud came on over the wall, unhurried, four hundred paces of him against a scrubbed autumn sky, and the light moved across the drill yard the way it does.

"That's my father," Sergeant Mo said.

Nobody had anything for that.

He looked another moment. Then he faced the yard, and the yard reassembled itself out of pure reflex, and he ran them the wall circuit twice for standing about, and they ran it, and the cloud went over the lake and came apart the way clouds do, from the jaw first.

At the trough afterward Wei said, to nobody, "Does he mean the resemblance or does he mean—"

"Drink your water," said Third Squad.

## III. 字距 — Kerning
### *Mengzhou, Bookbinders' Row, the compositing floor*

Master Liao has been setting type for forty years and drinks, and both facts arrive on the Row in the same sentence, and he has stopped fighting the sentence and started using it as a signature.

He is also the best compositor in three cities, and this is the part of the sentence that people leave off, and it is the part that matters at four in the morning when a broadsheet has to be on the street by six.

The trouble began with the ballad sheet.

He set it on a Tuesday, tight, the way he sets everything, because the Row pays by the sheet and a tight set is a cheap set and Master Liao has been feeding a house on the difference since he was nineteen. He locked the forme, pulled a proof, hung it to dry, and went home.

In the morning the forme had been opened and eleven quads had been inserted through the third stanza and it was, the whole Row agreed, better.

Master Liao stood in front of it for six minutes.

"It's *airy,*" he said.

The night porter, who had swept the stone and seen the paw-print and said nothing on the grounds that thirty years of night work teaches you which mornings to be somewhere else, was somewhere else.

"It's a ballad," Master Liao said, to the room. "It's about a drowned bridegroom. It's meant to be *close.*"

He took the quads out. He locked the forme. He went home.

In the morning the quads were back and a comma had moved.

---

This ran thirteen days.

By the fourth day he had stopped taking the quads out and started leaving notes, chalked on the stone, in the compositor's shorthand that only about nine people in the world can read:

*THIS IS A LAMENT NOT A MENU*

By the seventh day the notes had answers, and the answers were set in type, laid out on the stone in a hand of ten-point that the Row has never owned:

> *A lament read at a funeral by a man who is crying. Give him somewhere to breathe.*

By the ninth day the argument had moved on to the em-dash and had become, by the private judgment of the apprentices, the finest thing any of them had witnessed in the trade.

On the thirteenth day Master Liao came in at dawn with his coat still on and looked at the stone and read the night's line and put his bag down slowly.

"…Fine," he said.

He set the third stanza airy. He set the fourth stanza airy. He pulled the sheet and it went out on the Row at six and it sold out by noon, which a ballad about a drowned bridegroom has done exactly twice in the Row's recorded history, and the Row talked about nothing else for a week.

That night he left a cup on the stone.

The apprentices asked about the hawthorn and he was firm with them: the hawthorn is the porter's business and the porter has his own arrangement. What Master Liao left was the yellow wine, poured properly, at drinking temperature, on the corner of the stone where a person sets a cup down when the person intends to be there a while.

It was empty in the morning and the stone was clean.

The Row's ballad sheets have been airy since. The apprentices call it the wide set. Master Liao calls it the Tuesday set and declines all further questions, and the night porter sweeps, and the partnership is forty years old and has, this quarter, taken on an argument.

## IV. 卡 — The Card
### *Mengzhou, the terrace hawthorn stand, morning*

The old vendor's niece took the stand for the autumn while her aunt's hip mended, and she was nineteen and thorough and had been told exactly once, at speed, on the way out the door, that the accounts wanted watching.

So she watched the accounts. She checked the ration coupons against the ward list, every customer, in order, which the aunt had stopped doing in the reign before last on grounds of arthritis and knowing everybody.

The queue on the fourth morning ran nine deep. At the front of it, at the correct distance behind the previous customer, with her hands in her sleeves, stood a woman with fox ears and nine tails and an expression of perfect civic patience.

"Two sticks."

"Ward list, please."

The queue behind her achieved a stillness that the terrace has produced maybe four times in six hundred years.

The Sentinel of Mengzhou put her hand in her sleeve and produced a ration card.

It was soft with handling. It had the census chop, the ward stamp, and renewal marks in every box the card provides, and the niece took it and read it against her list, running her finger down the column the way she had been shown, at the pace she had been shown.

Occupation: *judicial.*
Housing status: *ambulatory.*
Income: *hawthorn.*

"You're on the list," the niece said.

"I am."

"Two sticks." She handed the card back and counted the change into the waiting palm, coin by coin, out loud, the way you do. "Next."

Hsin took her hawthorn and stepped aside and the queue moved, on legs, with difficulty.

---

The niece found out at closing, from a boy who had been fourth in line and had been waiting all day to be the one to tell her.

She went home and lay on her bed with her hat still on.

In the morning the queue ran the length of the terrace and at the front of it, at the correct distance, with her hands in her sleeves, stood the same woman.

"Two sticks."

The niece looked at her.

"Ward list, please," she said.

"Mm." Hsin produced the card. "Good."

She has queued at that stand every third morning since, and she has produced the card every time, and the niece has checked it every time, and the aunt, when she came back on her mended hip in the winter and heard the whole business, sat down on the stool behind the brazier and laughed until she had to hold the counter.

"Eleven years," the aunt said, wiping her eyes. "Eleven years I've been handing her hawthorn like a neighbour. She's been *carrying a card.*"

The niece said the card was in order.

"Of course it's in order." The aunt got up and went back to the coals. "That's the joke, child. That's the whole joke."

## V. 呃 — The Hiccups
### *Mingting, the Censorate, the corridor*

The corridor has no doors, and lamps replaced on the hour, and attendants in soft shoes, and an acoustic property that four centuries of masons have refined toward a single purpose, which is that a whisper at the filing window arrives at the far end intact.

At the second hour of the afternoon, a junior clerk of the third division got the hiccups.

They were audible in nine rooms.

They were audible in the Chief Commander's antechamber, where a delegation from the Ministry of Rites was waiting on a matter of some delicacy, and the head of the delegation turned toward the sound, and away, and then, four seconds later, toward it again, because the interval was regular and the regularity was doing something to the back of everybody's neck.

The clerk held his breath at his desk with his ears going red.

*Hic.*

Down the corridor, at the midpoint, in front of the mirror, Agent Qiu Han stopped walking.

She listened for four intervals. Then she went and found him.

"Name."

"Ah— Song, ma'am— third division, filing—" *Hic.*

"How long."

"Twenty minutes." *Hic.* "I'm fine."

Iron, immediate and total, filling her whole mouth like a coin under the tongue.

Qiu Han looked at the ceiling for a moment with the expression of a woman who has spent eleven years being told the truth by her own body at the least convenient times.

"You are not fine, and the building can hear you, and the delegation from Rites is currently constructing a theory," she said. "Come with me."

"Am I in troub—" *Hic.*

"You are in the corridor. Everyone is in trouble. Walk."

---

She took him to the filing window and made him drink a cup of water from the far side of the rim, which is a thing her grandmother taught her and which the third division's medical annex has no position on.

It stopped for nine seconds.

*Hic.*

She made him hold his breath and count to thirty. She made him swallow with his fingers in his ears. She stood in front of him at the exact midpoint of the corridor of the Imperial Security Affairs Bureau, in a boned collar, in the middle of a working afternoon, and said, "Do you have a debt."

"What?"

"A debt. Money. To anyone."

"…Eleven silver, to my brother-in-law, for a—"

"He forgives it."

*Hic.*

"That is a lie," Qiu Han said, "and you have no idea whether it is a lie, and neither do I, and the not-knowing is the point." She checked the corridor. The lamps burned on. "Say it back to me."

"He forgives it."

They waited.

The corridor did its work. Somewhere behind them the attendants came through with the hour's lamps, in step, in silence, and passed, and receded.

"…That's it," the clerk said. "That's them gone."

"Go back to your desk."

"Ma'am—" He was pink to the collar. "Thank you."

"Go."

She walked back down the corridor past the mirror, and she stopped, as she has begun to, and looked at a competent face at the exact center of a passage that has been exactly as long as it needs to be for four hundred years, and she thought about the fact that the finest interrogation instrument in the service of the Emperor had just been used to cure a boy's hiccups with a lie about eleven silver.

She adjusted her collar.

The taste in her mouth was copper, and warm, and it stayed all afternoon, and she got a great deal of work done.

## VI. 兩杯 — Two Cups
### *Xuanfang, the wall, section eleven*

The unit at section eleven boils a kettle at dusk. It has done this for twenty-two years, for a garrison cook who took his tea with it every evening and died at home, retired, in spring, and the frontier court has ruled the behavior harmless and left it alone.

The Sentinel came down at the ruling and drank the cup the machine had poured and told it: *tomorrow, set out two.*

The unit set out two.

The second cup sat on the parapet stone for eleven days at the correct temperature, cooling on schedule, while the garrison walked past it with the specific brisk casualness of soldiers avoiding a thing.

On the twelfth day Sergeant Tan drank it, because he had come off a wet patrol and was cold and had run out of opinions.

It was, he reported, the best cup of tea he had ever had in his life.

---

By the end of the month the unit was setting out nine.

By the end of the second month the quartermaster of section eleven filed a requisition for leaf at four times the post's establishment and attached a two-page justification, and the justification was returned by the frontier court with a note asking why the post required forty portions of tea at dusk, and the quartermaster replied, in writing, over his own chop:

*Because the doll makes it correctly and the men have found out.*

The court approved the requisition. The line reads *sundry, shrine.*

---

The unit's standard is rigorous.

Water is drawn at the third hour and rested. Temperature is held at eighty-five for the green and ninety-six for the dark, and it will tell you, in a garrison cook's voice out of the year the wall was rebuilt, that the difference matters more than the leaf does, and that most men ruin tea by being in a hurry, and that a hurry is a thing you can taste.

Sergeant Tan once poured his own from the unit's kettle at the wrong moment and was corrected in front of nine men.

> *"That's ninety-nine. Sit down, sit down. Two breaths."*

He sat down. He took two breaths. The tea was better. He has been telling this story for a year and it improves each time and the core of it is true, which is the rare kind.

---

The first cup goes on the parapet stone at the western end, where the light lasts longest, at the place the cook used to stand with one boot up on the coping and complain about the rice ration.

The first cup stands where it is put.

This is now a standing order at section eleven. It has a number. It reads, in full:

> *Regulation 9. The western cup is not for issue.*

New postings ask about it in their first week. The answer they get is the answer the wall gives: a shrug, a chin at the stone, and *that's Wu's.* And they say *who's Wu,* and somebody tells them, and the telling takes about a minute, and after that they walk past the western cup the way everyone else does, which is to say they leave it a little room.

The unit pours forty-one cups at dusk.

It counts them out loud, in the cook's voice, from one.

## VII. 貓 — The Cat
### *Huaxu Academy, Jinzhou branch*

The Academy cat is grey, elderly, and administratively invisible, and it has been sleeping in the fourth-floor seminar room for two years.

In the spring it moved to the second floor.

Xiangli Yao noticed on a Tuesday and mentioned it to no one, and on Wednesday he brought a thermometer.

---

The hypothesis was thermal. It is the obvious hypothesis and it is a good one: the building has poor insulation on the north face, the fourth floor loses heat through the roof, and a cat of that age has a documented preference for ambient temperature at the upper end of its comfort band. He mapped the building over nine days. Two hundred and six readings. Morning, noon, and the third watch, because a graduate student who complains about the third watch is a graduate student who has learned something.

The second-floor seminar room came out fourth-warmest.

He ran it again.

---

"Sir."

"Mm."

"It's the biscuits."

Xiangli Yao removed his glasses, cleaned them, and replaced them. "Elaborate."

"Second floor is where the historiography seminar meets. They bring biscuits. The cat gets biscuits." The student had rehearsed this on the stairs and it was coming out faster than she had planned. "Every Tuesday and Thursday. It moved in the spring because that's when the seminar changed rooms."

"That is a social hypothesis."

"Yes, sir."

"It is untestable without an intervention I would have to explain to the ethics board."

"…Yes, sir."

He looked at the thermal map for some seconds. It was a beautiful map. It had cost nine days and a graduate stipend and it was, as a description of the thermal behavior of a badly insulated building, the finest document of its kind in the province.

"Withdraw the biscuits," he said.

"Sir—"

"For one week. Announce it as a crumb policy. The historiographers will comply because historiographers comply with anything that has a policy name." He was already writing. "Log the cat's position daily. If it stays, I am right. If it moves, you are right, and you will present the finding at the term seminar, with the map, under your own name."

The student stood there.

"Is there a difficulty?"

"You'd let me present against you."

"I would let you present a *result.*" He cracked a sunflower seed. "The map is publishable regardless. Buildings are stupid and it is useful to know how. Go on."

---

The cat moved on the second day.

The finding was presented at the term seminar by one student, in front of forty, with the thermal map on the board behind her and the principal investigator in the back row eating sunflower seeds.

The paper is called *Ambient Temperature and Social Provisioning as Competing Predictors of Feline Habitat Selection in a Multi-Storey Academic Building,* it has been cited nine times, and every one of the nine is a joke.

Xiangli Yao has a copy in his office.

The crumb policy stands, which the cat has adjusted to by attending the historiography seminar itself and sitting on the table, and the historiographers, who compose in the passive voice, have entered it in the minutes as *the chair was occupied.*

## VIII. 凳 — The Bench
### *Xuanfang, the village at the base of the fifth pillar*

The new bench arrived on a Thursday in the back of a Ministry of Development cart with two men and a certificate.

It was very good. Everyone agreed about this from the beginning and the agreement made the whole affair much longer. Cut stone, dressed, regulation height, with a drainage lip and a small plaque explaining the initiative, and it had been allocated to the village under a rural amenity programme that had spent four years reaching them and had reached them, at last, correctly, in the person of two tired men who had driven it up the gorge road on a cart with a bad wheel.

Old Ge came out and looked at it and thanked them and gave them tea and asked after their families and their route and their wheel.

Then he said, "Put it in the shrine."

---

The delivery men explained the amenity programme.

Old Ge listened to all of it. He is seventy-nine and has been listening to explanations from the capital since he was six, and he has developed a listening face that has been described by three separate census takers as unnerving in its quality.

"Crossroads," said the senior man, tapping his sheet. "It's specified. Crossroads bench, one, village of the fifth pillar."

"There's a bench at the crossroads."

"There's a bench in poor condition at the crossroads."

"It's in the condition it's in." Old Ge poured. "Nine generations. It's got a shape in it."

"Sir—"

"Look at the top of it. Go and look." He waved his cup. "Four hollows. My grandfather's on the north side, my father's beside it, mine, and there's a fourth one starting on the west where the toll collector's girl sits when she's got a grievance, and she's twenty-two, and she'll wear it in by fifty." He drank. "You put a flat stone at that crossroads and every man in this village sits at a slightly wrong angle for the rest of his life."

The junior delivery man wrote something on the back of his hand.

---

The matter went to the frontier court, because everything here goes to the frontier court, and the frontier court has a whole shelf for Xuanfang and a clerk who has learned to recognize the handwriting.

The village's submission ran to two pages. It was drafted by Old Ge and copied out by his son the retired clerk, who improved the grammar and worsened the argument, and the court read both versions because the son had helpfully attached the original.

The court's disposition, in full:

> *The amenity is delivered and the amenity is accepted; the village's receipt is on file and the programme's obligation is discharged.*
>
> *The village has sited the amenity in the village shrine. Siting is a local matter.*
>
> *The court declines to inquire further and notes, for the Ministry's comfort, that the bench is receiving more attention in the shrine than it would have received at the crossroads.*

The bench is in the shrine. It faces east. People leave persimmons on it in the autumn, and a child's drawing appeared on the plaque within the month, and the shrine-keeper dusts it daily and has filed one complaint about the drawing which the court denied in nine days.

The old bench is at the crossroads.

Old Ge is on it.

## IX. 零 — Zero
### *Mingting, the Bureau of Rites, a teahouse on Lantern Street*

The eighth reader is a difficult person to eat lunch with.

The office has arrived at a protocol. You order before she sits down. This is because Ruan Shu picks up whatever is on the table, and her hand goes flat on it, and she says a number, and the number is enormous, and the table then spends four minutes contemplating the nineteen thousand people who have read this menu, and the noodles arrive cold.

She knows about the protocol. She thinks it is reasonable.

The junior copyist tests her about once a month, because he is twenty-three and cannot help it. He brings things. A bill. A wanted poster peeled off a post. His own examination results, which he claims were read once and which came up four, and he has been quiet about his family ever since in a way the office finds touching.

On the ninth day of the month he brought a folded paper and put it face down by her cup.

She put her hand on it.

"Zero."

He picked it up and put it in his coat.

"Ordering," said the copyist, and turned around to the counter and ordered, at length, with corrections, for a party of six.

The table let him have it. Ruan Shu picked up her chopsticks. Across the road a shop was putting up its winter awning and getting it wrong, and the whole teahouse watched through the window in perfect silence for a while, and then the noodles came.

He carries it still. She has watched him take out his wallet twice since and seen the fold of it sitting against the coin, and she has let it alone, and she will go on letting it alone, and on the day he puts it down on the table again she will put her hand on it and read whatever number is there and say the number, because that is the arrangement, and he set the terms himself, at twenty-three, by bringing it in the first place.

## X. 投書 — Post
### *Jinzhou, the lake, dusk*

The fisherwoman's daughter is eight and posts letters to the lake.

She started in the summer. The method is her own: paper, folded small, wrapped in a leaf and tied with grass, dropped over the gunwale at the deep place where the water is warmest and the fish go. She writes them at the kitchen table with her tongue out. Her mother has read exactly one, by accident, and has declined every one since, and has developed a way of looking past the table that the whole household has learned to do with her.

The first one said: *Do you get cold.*

The lake turned over on the ninth of the month, which it does three or four times a season, one slow displacement crossing the whole surface, and the lamps along the crest wall guttered once together and steadied, and the night desk logged: *weather: calm; lake: attentive.*

The girl came out at dawn and the whole bay was warm to the ankle.

She took this as correspondence.

---

Her mother's objection is postage.

"That's my good paper."

"It's *one* sheet."

"It's one sheet a week for four months and I've counted." The fisherwoman coiled her line. "You know what paper costs at the ward shop."

"He's very old."

"He's very old and he's got the whole lake, he can afford his own paper—"

"He can't *write.*"

The fisherwoman stopped coiling.

"That's why it has to be paper," her daughter said, with the terrible patience of eight. "He can only do weather. So I do the words and he does the weather and that's how it works."

The mother went back to the line.

At the ward shop that week she bought the good paper, and a second bundle of the cheap, and put the cheap on the shelf by the door where a child can reach it without asking, and the good back in the box, and left both where they were, and the arrangement has held since.

The Almanac Bureau has an entry for the lake's turning that runs to four hundred years and reads, in every reign, *tectonic, normal.*

The Bureau's Jinzhou station has begun, this year, appending a second column. The column has the date of each turning, and beside it, where the station keeper found a correlation he cannot publish and has stopped trying to explain to the capital, a note on the previous evening's weather at the deep place.

Nine entries so far. Eight of them fall within a day of a small boat going out at dusk from the second landing.

The station keeper has filed the column under *courtesy.*

The girl is on to a new question. She has been working on it for a week and it is the longest letter yet, and it has a diagram, and her mother has seen the diagram from across the kitchen and has decided, on the whole, to let the diagram be a surprise.

> **CLERK'S CLOSE.**
>
> *These are the disposals of the season. A goose holds a door. A cloud resembles a sergeant. Eleven quads go into a third stanza. A god shows her card. A clerk stops hiccupping. Forty-one cups are poured and forty are drunk. A cat follows the biscuits. A bench receives persimmons. A folded paper reads zero. A lake answers in weather because it has no hands.*
>
> *Past the north face the ground keeps its own appointment, eight days apart and closing. The beacons are at double watch. The Bulwark is walking. At the southern port the harbormaster has been told to expect five guests and a portable tea service.*
>
> *The clerk observes that every item on this docket was raised, argued, escalated, and disposed of by people who could hear the drums through their boot soles at supper, and that the drums appear on this docket in one line, in the weather column, where the office puts what it intends to go on shopping in front of.*
>
> *The country has been saying this for four hundred years in the only grammar it has, which is the grammar of opening the tax office at the usual hour with the goose in the doorway.*
>
> *Filed under: ordinary. Duration: as long as it holds.*

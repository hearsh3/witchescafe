## I. STAGECRAFT
*grave* · `c = 26`

She had made arrangements for nothing. Nothing kept her waiting.

That was the first joke and she was too irritated to laugh at it. She had done the math on a bench under ceiling tiles named after desserts; she had audited it twice; the arithmetic completed at the center of the lake exactly as promised, the collar went home to its lock, the seam took what it was owed and shut, and then the dark, which had been advertising clean subtraction to her nightly since the age of frost, handed her a floor.

She stood on it. It held her weight. It had the give of a stage deck.

Above her, a very long way up, the surface of the water hung like a ceiling with weather on it.

"Right," said Denia, to the floor. "Complaint filed."

The dark had furniture. That was the part nobody had mentioned in fifteen years of nightly advertising. It had a slope to it, and a temperature, and a faint smell she recognized before she could place it: cold glass, ward antiseptic, and under both, sweet and wrong, honey.

She followed the slope up, because a slope with a direction implies a room, and a room implies a door, and she had been raised by a woman who wrote excellent befores and had therefore learned to check every wall for hinges.

The room was Auditorium C.

Four hundred seats banked in the dark. The board at the front carried Halvard's handwriting, mid-derivation, entropy going the way entropy goes. The house lights were off and the work light was on, one bare bulb over stage left, which is what a theater keeps burning when everyone has gone home and nobody wants the ghosts to trip.

Every seat was empty except the fourth row, aisle.

Her seat. Somebody was sitting in it, and the somebody was applauding — slow, unhurried, a sound like a tap dripping into a full sink — and it kept applauding for eleven seconds after she came through the door, which is exactly long enough to become a statement.

*"You were extraordinary,"* it said.

"Thanks. Who's asking."

*"The house."*

She came down the aisle at the speed of a girl with a whole afterlife to spend, and stopped at row four, and looked at what was in her seat. It had the shape of a girl with dark hair and it had her tilt and it had, tucked behind its ear, a strand of hair that went there the way hers went there. It had gotten the tempo right. Fifteen years of study will do that.

"Bold," Denia said. "Sitting in the aisle seat."

*"You always took the aisle."*

"So I could leave."

*"So you could be seen leaving."* It smiled her smile at her. *"Sit down, little tenant. The house is yours. I've held it fifteen years and never once billed you."*

She sat, one seat over, because she had never in her life given anybody the satisfaction of a refusal when a lateral move was available.

---

Here is what she had been promised, nightly, since the crib third from the wall:

A game with no pieces. Nothing falls. Nothing clears. Nothing keeps score, nothing loses, nothing is asked of anything.

Here is what it looked like, delivered.

The handheld was on the armrest. Grey, scuffed, LAHAI-ROI BLOCKS in raised plastic on the cartridge, the exact one, down to the crack in the shell where a six-year-old had thrown it and then lied about it. The screen was on. The well was empty. The counter read a number she had personally set, once, in a corridor, with her tongue between her teeth.

Nothing fell.

She pressed rotate. Nothing rotated, because there was nothing in the air to rotate, and the machine registered the input politely and did what it was designed to do, which was hold still.

She pressed it again. Then eleven more times, fast, the way you do when you already know.

*"You've won,"* said the Erlking, beside her, warm as a coat handed over. *"There's nothing left to clear."*

"That's a screensaver."

*"That's peace."*

"That's a screensaver with a philosophy degree." She set the handheld down on the armrest between them, screen up, well empty, and folded her hands. "You spent fifteen years selling me this. I want you to know I'm looking at it."

*"And?"*

"And I've seen better lighting."

The Erlking laughed. It was a good laugh. It was, she noted with the flat professional interest of a girl who had been graded on affect since before she could read, *her* laugh, from the third floor, from flashcards, from a war story about an unrelated equation — and it had learned the laugh so well that it had also learned the little upward hook at the end that she only did when she meant it.

Which meant it had heard her mean it. Which meant it had been in the room.

She filed that. She filed it very carefully, in the drawer where the load-bearing things went, and gave no sign at all.

The floor of the house sloped away past the front row and kept sloping, and out there, where an orchestra pit would go if the theater had ever been finished, were the voices.

She had expected a chorus. What she got was a waiting room. Thousands of them, drifting at ankle height like the smoke off dry ice, each one a fragment of somebody's last useful thought, worn down to the phrase that mattered.

*—sixteen minutes from home—*

*—tell them the readings were good, the readings were good all the way down—*

*—Ad astra—*

*—I left the stove—*

Denia stood at the lip of the stage with her hands in her pockets and listened to them go past, and did the only thing she had ever known how to do in a room that was going badly.

She started setting the table.

There was no table. She set it anyway. She had done this in a ward with forty-eight tiles and a countdown for a dinner guest; she had done it as a child at a table built for twenty and laid for two; and she had learned from the best that a place setting is a claim about the future made with cutlery.

"You," she said, to a voice that had been circling her ankle for a while saying something about a stove. "You're by the window. Bring the stove, it's fine, we'll put it under the table."

*—I left the stove—*

"You did. It's off. I checked." She said it with total authority, having checked nothing, having no idea what a stove was for. "Sit."

The voice sat. It had been going in circles for a hundred years and it sat.

She named it Pavlova, because it was structurally unsound and she was sentimental. She named the next one Kasutera. She got through eleven before her mouth started to go, and she kept going anyway, out of spite and habit and hostessing, until a voice arrived that declined a name.

*—I have one,—* it said. *—Mya.—*

Denia stopped.

The shape of it was a girl about her size, worn thin as a coat of paint. Everything else down here had been sanded to a phrase. This one had held onto four letters.

"How."

*—Somebody upstairs keeps saying it.—* A pause; the shape thinned and gathered. *—Loudly. In front of people. She won a thing and made them write it on the record.—*

"Wolf-shaped, by any chance."

*—Yes.—*

"I've met her." Denia sat down on the edge of the stage, feet in the pit, among the drifting. "She told me eleven days was worth having."

*—She would.—* The shape considered her with attention it could barely afford. *—You're the one after me.—*

"I'm the one who worked."

*—Mm.—* Something in the worn-thin thing found the energy for dryness. *—Congratulations.—*

Denia laughed, once, and the laugh went up off the stage and into the empty house and came back with reverb, because the house had good acoustics and no audience, which is the worst combination there is.

"They'll make another," she said, when it came back. "After me."

*—Yes.—*

"I hate that." She turned the word over. It came out of her the way it always did, sideways, in the only grammar she had ever been issued. "That's the wrong word. I don't have the right one. There's a key for it somewhere, I gave it to a woman with gold eyes."

*—Then it's on file,—* said Mya, and went out like a match, having spent what she had.

Denia sat with her feet in the pit for a long time after that, in the work light, in the empty house, and the Erlking sat four rows back in her aisle seat and let her, because it had all the time there was and it knew, the way a good landlord knows, that the tenant always comes back to the room she pays for.

## II. BREAKDOWN

It began the way it had always begun, with the format she had been raised inside.

```
CALIBRATION — SESSION ∞.
Format: call-and-response. Purpose: the examiner declines to state.

EXAMINER: A room with a light in it. Say it back.
SUBJECT: A room with a light in it.
EXAMINER: The light goes out. What remains in the room?
```

She was nine the first time. She had answered *a room* and the pen had nodded along the flatline and satisfaction had bought her the courtyard hour, and the trick, the whole trick, the trick she had built a life on, was to answer with the noun and keep the verb for yourself.

She was dead now. The courtyard hour was no longer on the table.

"The lamp," said Denia.

The dark took a moment with that.

*"The lamp is out."*

"The lamp's still there. Out is a thing lamps do on their way to being lit again." She examined her fingernails, which she still appeared to have. "Next question."

```
EXAMINER: A door closes behind you. Name the feeling.
```

At nine she had said *a door*. It had been the best work of her early career.

She opened her mouth to say it again and found the machinery of it standing there, familiar, competent, and entirely willing — and then she looked up at the ceiling of the water, forty meters and one whole world away, and thought about how much longer she was going to be down here, and how there was nobody left to buy a courtyard hour from.

"Warm on the right side," she said.

The dark went very still.

"That's the feeling. The door shuts and the heat comes at you from the right, because the door that shuts behind you is the same door that opened, and for about one second you're standing in the warm with the cold still in your boots, and you don't know yet which one you're going to get." She sat down on the stage lip. Her hands had begun to do something she declined to look at. "That's the feeling. I've had it twice. Once at four and once at a doorway on a Thursday, at a volume calibrated for machines and gods and nobody."

Then it started taking.

It worked from paperwork, which she respected. The Fractsidus had built her from a schematic and the schematic listed her parts to the last coefficient, and the thing in her seat had read that document the way other people read scripture, and it went down the manifest with the unhurried courtesy of a bailiff.

It took the Resonance first. That went easily; it had never been hers.

It took the frequency, the chemistry, the eleven engineered reflexes, the tolerance thresholds, the affect suite, the null face — she felt the null face go and was surprised how heavy it had been — and it took the smile, gentle, omnipresent, four hundred hours of installation, and she stood in the work light without it and felt the air on the front of her head like a person whose hat has blown off in a street.

"Everything moves me. I'm extremely cheap." She started up the aisle. "It's past midnight anyway. Nothing fits me now. Ask the pumpkin."

It waited until she had nothing left to hold and then it gave her the only thing she had ever wanted, and it gave it to her perfectly, which was its mistake, though the mistake took a while to surface and cost her everything on the way.

There was snow first.

Snow before anything — the fat unhurried kind, each flake taking its full salary of air. A smell of honey and burnt sugar riding on wool. She was small enough to be carried and was being carried, and the wall came up out of the white, and the door in the wall, and the light through the frosted glass, curved by the curve of the pane into a low warm crescent, the one warm-colored thing in the world, and she reached for it because she was four and that is what four does.

The wool set her down on the step. Cold climbed into her boots and she let it, interested.

The shape against the snow-light was already three steps gone. It turned. A hand came up.

And then it came back.

It came back up the steps, and it knelt in the snow in a good coat, and it took her under the arms and lifted her, and Denia — who was thirty centimeters tall and had a bootlace charm and zero documents — put her face into the wool at the neck, where the honey was, and stopped.

She stopped for a long time.

It said her name. It said the rest of the sentence, the one that had been cut off at the door for fifteen years, and the rest of the sentence was good, and warm, and had her name in it twice.

It said: *I came back for you.*

It said: *I have been looking for fifteen years and I never once stopped, and it was never your fault, not for one second, and you were wanted from the very beginning and you have always, always been wanted.*

Snow went on landing. Politely. Asking first.

And Denia, with her face in the wool, thirty centimeters tall and fifteen years old and dead, at the bottom of a lake, in the arms of the only thing she had ever asked the universe for, noticed the coat.

The coat was new.

The nap of the wool stood up in every direction like a lawn nobody had walked on. The elbow held its own shape. The cuff had the shine of a bolt of cloth in a shop window, the way cloth shines before a sleeve has gone into a pocket ten thousand times.

She had carried this memory her whole life the way other children carry a blanket: by one corner, everywhere, until the color wore through to thread. She knew the exact place on that corner where her thumb went, because it had gone there every night for fifteen years and taken the pile off.

This one had come straight off the bolt.

She turned her face and looked up at it, and it was perfect. Every eyelash. The pore structure. The small chapped place at the left of the mouth that she had never once, in fifteen years of handling, been able to see.

"You remember more of this than I do," Denia said.

*"I remember all of it, милая."*

"Yeah," said Denia. "That's the problem."

She got herself down out of the arms. It took a while, because the arms did not want to open and she had to do it in stages, and the whole time the snow kept coming down at the right rate and the honey stayed exactly where honey stays.

She stood on the step in the cold, in her boots, at four, at fifteen, and looked up at the finest work anyone had ever done on her behalf.

"Here's the thing," she said. "I left a protocol with somebody. It's short. It goes: something wearing this face gives you a grand answer — a beautiful answer, an answer that *explains* — you walk. The right answer is always small. It's owed." She wiped her nose on her sleeve, four years old about it, entirely without dignity. "You gave me a reason. Fifteen years and a reason and *never your fault*. That's an ending. That's what an ending sounds like."

*"It is the truth."*

"It's a *rewrite*." Her voice went, and she let it. "A real one would have got the coat wrong. A real one would have forgotten the cake. A real one would have said something stupid about my boots and I'd have spent fifteen years wondering what she meant. You handed me a clean one." She laughed and it came out ruined. "Nobody gets a clean one. I know that. I've watched forty people not get one."

The shape in the good new coat stood on the steps in the falling snow.

*"Don't go,"* it said.

Which was the sentence. Which was, of every sentence in every language, the one she had been standing at the bottom of a lake to hear.

Denia looked at it for four full seconds, which is a very long time, and then she raised one hand — lazy, precise, a girl waving off a lecture —

"До свидания," she said, and walked into the dark, and got about nine steps before her legs went, and finished the walk on her hands and knees, and did not once turn around, because she had spent her whole life leaving ropes where people's hands would be and she knew exactly what a rope looks like when it's being paid out behind you.

## III. FINAL ACT
*adagio cantabile* · `c = ½`

After that there was a long stretch with nothing in it, and she spent it lying on the stage deck in the work light counting her independent components the way you count fingers in a dream, which is to say slowly, twice, and with a growing suspicion about the arithmetic.

She got to one and stopped.

She had no proof of it. That was the annoying part. There had been proof once — a single spike on a doctored trace, session sixty-one, question six, pinned under the calm print of official truth like a flower pressed by somebody who could have been executed for gardening — and there had been a recording of it, sealed provenance, the sound of her own heart caught by a hand that held still and lied in print, and she had put that recording in a toy and left the toy on a pillow beside a sleeping girl's ear.

So the only evidence that Denia had ever had a heart was currently at Startorch Academy, in a dormitory, in a fist.

"That was funny," she told the ceiling of the water. "In retrospect."

The Erlking came and lay down beside her on the deck, which was undignified of it and therefore effective.

*"Do you know what I'm called?"*

"I've heard it a few ways."

*"Ничего."*

"Mm." Denia considered the dark above her. "Where I'm from, that means 'never mind.'"

*"You are from nowhere in the catalog."*

"Sure." She tucked the strand behind her ear. "It still means never mind."

They lay there. Somewhere out in the pit, a hundred years of somebody's last useful thought went round and round about a stove.

*"I want to show you the whole thing,"* it said. *"You should see it once. Everybody's is seven parts. Yours is short."*

And it ran her.

The infant — a room of cribs like a room of parentheses, mewling on schedule, third from the wall. The schoolgirl — creeping like a snail, and she had crept, she had been *magnificent* at creeping, four years of face-down on desks. Then it skipped. Lover. Soldier. Justice, with the good belly and the wise saws. The lean slippered pantaloon. It skipped all of them because it had no footage, because there was none, and it let the gap hang there in the work light with the honesty of an accountant.

And then the last one, which everybody gets: the end of the strange eventful history. Sans everything.

*"Deformed,"* the Erlking said, quite gently. *"Unfinished. Sent before your time, scarce half made up. I didn't write that. Somebody's brother did, four hundred years ago, about a man who decided to be a villain because the arithmetic left him nothing else. I only thought you'd like knowing there was a precedent."*

"Half," said Denia.

*"Half."*

She thought about that for a while, lying on her back on a stage at the bottom of the world, at the smallest nonzero charge a system is allowed to carry — one half, the value of a thing that can only point up or point down, the cheapest arrangement in physics that still has an opinion.

"Do you know what happens to a star," she said, "when it's finished."

*"It ends."*

"It stops burning. Different verb." She laced her fingers on her stomach. "The fuel's gone, the show's over, everything it was for has dissolved off it like foam. And what's left is this small dense idiotic thing about the size of a planet with the mass of a sun, and it isn't doing anything, and it isn't going anywhere, and it is *incandescent.*" She turned her head. "It takes longer to cool than the universe has been alive. Every white dwarf ever made is still up there glowing. All of them. All the way back to the first one."

*"And?"*

"And you're on the clock, sweetheart. I've been down here what, an hour? A century?" She smiled at the dark and it was a new smile; nobody had installed it. "You can have me when I've cooled. Bring a chair."

---

The Erlking sat up.

It was the first time she had seen it move without a plan, and there was something in the movement that she recognized from three floors of study rooms at four in the morning, from a girl reading formulas out loud to stay honest, and it took her a second to name it, and then she named it and the whole architecture of the last fifteen years quietly changed address.

*"Say it once,"* it said. *"That's all I want. Say it and mean it and I'll stop."*

"Say what."

*"That none of it counted."*

The house was very quiet. Out in the pit the drifting went on.

"...You need me to say it."

*"I would like you to say it."*

"No." Denia sat up too. "No, you *need* it. Fifteen years, you've needed it. You did the voices. You learned my vocabulary at the exact rate I learned it, which means you sat down and did homework every single night in a building with a curfew. You sang. You wrote *verses.*" She was starting to laugh and she hadn't decided to. "You followed a nine-year-old to a courtyard hour."

*"You are stalling."*

"I'm *marvelling*. There's a difference and I've had a lot of time." She got her feet under her. "You have every consciousness that ever walked into the dark. You have the whole archive of everything that ever ended. And you spent fifteen years lobbying one lazy schoolgirl in a Fractsidus crib." The laugh got out of her hands. "You tried *so hard.*"

*"I—"*

"I barely showed up!" She was on her feet now and the work light had her. "I slept through everything! I did the minimum for fifteen years, I ran away from every single thing that was ever asked of me, it was my entire *personality* — and you were up all night with the golden verses like a boy under a window!"

*"You loved them,"* the Erlking said, and it came out fast, and it came out with the whole archive behind it, and it played her the file. *"Third floor. 'She always complained about her grades, her tiny little problems.' Your words. 'I absolutely hated all of you.' Your words. 'And to be honest, I hated Sigrika too.' You wrote it down. You transmitted it. It is on a terminal in a kitchen. I have every syllable and every syllable is* hate.*"*

Denia stood in the light and looked at it with enormous tenderness.

"Keep up," she said. "That's the code."

*"...What."*

"Hates become likes. That's the whole verification protocol. I gave it to the Rover in writing so that nobody could ever wear my face and get it wrong." She spread her hands. "You've got the transcript. You've never had the *translation*. Nobody ever gave you the key, because nobody ever thought you'd need one, because who in their right mind hands a dictionary to the weather?"

The thing in the work light had her face, and her tilt, and her strand behind her ear, and it was doing something with its mouth that it had definitely learned from somebody.

*"All of it was staged,"* it said. *"Every kindness. You are a machine for manufacturing exits. You performed a girl for four years."*

"Name the audience."

*"...."*

"Two in the morning. Third pot of tea. Somebody's reading declensions out loud to stay awake and I'm face-down on the desk *pretending to sleep so she won't feel watched*, four years, every exam season, and I never told her, and I made sure the whole campus thought I was a professional slacker so nobody would go looking." Denia took a step forward. "There is no version of that with a house in it. Who was watching?"

The Erlking did not answer.

"Who," said Denia, "was watching."

*"...I was."*

"Yeah," she said.

And there it was: the only witness. Fifteen years in the room, ears open, taking minutes on every unlit hour of her life — the sole surviving testimony to the one thing she had never once done for an audience — and it had brought the transcript into court itself.

"You're my witness," Denia said. "You're the only witness there is. Congratulations, you saw the real thing, you saw all of it, you're the expert." She had both hands over her mouth now and it was doing nothing to help. "You've been standing there for fifteen years telling me it was empty and you were *in the room.*"

Something in the house let go then — a position held so long the holding had become furniture.

And Denia laughed.

It came out waterlogged and catastrophic and enormous, both hands, shoulders going, the laugh from the doorway of a study room, the one with her name in it — and it went up off the empty stage into four hundred empty seats and came back with reverb, and then went up further, up past the pit and the slope and the drifting hundred-year stove, up the whole long water, and broke on the underside of the surface as foam.

Bang.

The endless dark, it turned out, had an end. She had been standing on it the entire time. It was about four centimeters below her feet and it had the give of a stage deck.

---

*"The cage is open, little tenant,"* the Erlking said, from somewhere. *"That was the entire promise. You are out of the flesh. Nobody can shut you in anything ever again."*

Denia wiped her face with the heel of her palm.

"Mm," she said. "I liked the cage."

*"You were taught to hate it. It was written on the wall of the room where you learned to read."*

"I know what was written on the wall. I read it before I knew what half of it meant, and what I decided it meant was that a cage is a shape that holds you." She looked at her own hands. "Like a crib does. Like a shell does. I was five and I got it wrong and it kept me warm for a decade, and then somebody explained it to me properly, and I have been cold ever since." She flexed the fingers. "I've had eleven days out of anybody's hands but my own and one week in a ward and one Thursday, and I want the shape back. I want to be *held in.* That's the entire wish. Put it on the form."

*"It's a cage."*

"It's a *cage*," Denia agreed, delighted, "and I want it, and if you'd like to know what fifteen years of your best work bought — that. You got me all the way down here and I want my cage back and a coat and possibly cake."

The Erlking, for a moment, wore no face at all.

*"I only wanted you to rest,"* it said.

And Denia — who had heard the whole catalogue, the golden verses and the nightly rentals and the daughters and the dance, who had been offered oblivion in six voices by an entity that had never once, in fifteen years, been thanked — reached out and put two fingers on where a wrist would go, the way she had done to her maker in a courtyard on a Thursday.

"Ничего," she said.

Never mind. It's alright. Don't worry about it.

*"...That is not what it means."*

"It is now. I've had the name re-cut." She let go. "It's a family trade."

---

She went up.

The water was a long way and she was carrying nothing, which helped. Halfway there she found she had been wrong about the ceiling: something had arrived on the far side of it, red on black, seven of them, riding the surface on their own small tides.

Flowers. Deep wet red, stamped darker at the centers, the shape of a bloom that keeps one day's hours and signs its work at dusk.

She had been drowned three minutes or three months. There were flowers on the roof of the world and they were floating over her like a ceiling somebody had bothered to name.

Denia looked up at them from underneath and thought, with the last of her professional laziness: *someone's laid a table.*

Then a hand came through.

It came from the wrong side — down, into the water, from the world — pale and unhurried and absolutely certain, the way you reach into your own house in the dark. And Denia, who had spent her entire manufactured life leaving ropes where other people's hands would be, and who had watched a broadcast in a ward on a loop and called it the best thing she had ever seen —

— eyes shut, arms out —

reached back.

"That's a new one," said Herta, who had heard everything.

"That's a *rude* one," said Black Swan, gathering the seven from the water, red flower down.

In the shallows Mina put her hands into the exact place with the certainty of a harbor, and lifted, and what came up out of the black glass was a girl the size of a held breath: guttering, stubborn, four centimeters of stage deck still under her somewhere.

"Denia drowned," Mina said, to the water, to the coven, to the standing rule of her household. "Who came up?"

The girl's eyes came open about a third of the way.

"Is it cold out," she said.

The wind moved the reeds. Viviane, who had kept a light on for four hundred years and knew precisely what she was standing at the end of, answered before anybody else could get there.

"Bitterly."

"Good," said Denia, and went under again — into sleep this time, which is a different water, and had a household on the far side of it, and a saucer, and a season of ordinary sentences in the future tense to build a floor out of.

They carried the answer home.

Spring, after.

Auditorium C, mid-morning, Thermodynamic Ethics, attendance credits for the desperate. Halvard had aged one year and forgiven nothing. Denia took the old seat, fourth row, aisle, dozed off inside six minutes, and stayed under all the way to the end, which was a personal record and would be reported as such.

She surfaced at the sound of four hundred people standing up.

There was a coat on her.

It was a bad coat. Somebody's spare, too big in the shoulder, smelling of the dorm's regulation soap and, faintly, of vending-machine coffee. It had been laid over her at some point in the last fifty minutes by a person who had then gone back to taking notes and said nothing about it and was, by now, out the door and halfway to lunch.

Denia sat in the emptying hall with a stranger's coat over her and ran, out of fifteen years of habit, the audit.

Motive: none available.
Audience: none present.
Debt incurred: unclear.
Ledger entry: —

She got that far and then she put the pen down, which is a thing that can be done, apparently, and which nobody had ever mentioned.

She pulled the coat up to her chin, and put her head back on the desk, and let it stay.

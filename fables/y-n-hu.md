Chapter 2: Yān Huǒ

By the stove, in the grey first light, Chisa slid the blunt nose of her bandage scissors under the linen wrap at Yangyang’s left side and cut straight up through the four turns of cloth.

The stiff bandage parted and fell open across Yangyang’s lap.

Lupa leaned over from the kettle with the porridge ladle in her fist, squinting at the bare skin below Yangyang’s ribs where the old pale seam and the purple fading of the three cracks sat dented from a week of tight strapping.

“Didn’t swell,” Lupa said, and turned back to the pot.

Amy reached down from the corner of the table and scooped up the cut lengths of unbleached linen before Yangyang could fold them. “Don’t throw these in the firebox, Watcher. That’s tight-weave Huaxu linen. If I boil the resin out, I can use it to strain the grit out of the turbine oil.”

“Button your coat before you go out,” Chisa said, setting the scissors back in the tin box.

Yangyang drew her shirt together, fastened the frogs to her throat, and walked to the open front door.

Out on the stone landing, the frost lay white and crisp on the coping wall. Nobody else had come out into the lane yet. Yangyang stopped by the wooden turbine post, let her shoulders drop, and drew the cold morning air in through her nose—all the way down to the bottom of her lungs, where the bandage had caught her all week.

Her ribs lifted clean and wide under the cloth. Nothing pinched, and nothing clicked against the breastbone. For a moment she stood still in the frost with her eyes half-closed, feeling the cold air reach the bottom of her chest and come back warm.

As she let the breath out in a long white cloud, the dawn drum sounded up at the inner gatehouse: four sharp beats on the hide, a pause, four beats, and the dry tap of the stick against the wooden rim.

The roll echoed off the cliff behind the pagoda and died out.

Two heartbeats later, from far down in the gorge mist beyond the second bend, a second drum answered it.

*Boom-boom-boom-boom. Boom-boom-boom-boom. Clack.*

The exact same roll, beat for beat, coming up through the canyon from the river cut where there was no drum and no tower.

Inside the storehouse doorway, Lupa stopped with two bowls in her hand. Next door, the cooper’s upper shutter banged open and the cooper leaned out over the sill in his undershirt; across the lane, Old Man Zhao stood still beside his woodpile with a split birch log halfway to his arm.

Down in the white gulf, the distant drum did not sound again.

Yangyang looked down at the flat stone cap of the low wall beside her hip, where the ninth bowl from the festival table had sat since yesterday morning.

The bowl was empty.

The cold broth, the yellow film of fat, and the red berry were gone. The inside of the earthenware had been wiped clean down to the glaze, and the two bamboo chopsticks no longer lay crossed over the rim—they sat side by side on the stone to the right of the bowl, tips even, the way a person lays them down when a meal is finished.

Mei came to the threshold buttoning her dark jacket, with Iuno and Amy behind her shoulder.

On the back of Mei’s wrist, the pale lines flared gold, and Abby popped out into the cold draft above the lintel. He took one look at the wall-cap and planted both paws on his hips.

“See!” Abby shouted, his long white ears perking straight up. “I told you! Somebody was running late! Cleaned the whole bowl and didn’t even leave the berry!” With a satisfied nod, he popped straight back into the warm light on Mei’s wrist out of the frost.

“It was the grey cat from the granary roof,” Lupa said from the doorway, looking down at the clean glaze.

“A cat doesn’t put its chopsticks down side by side on the stone, Lupa,” Amy said, picking up the empty bowl by the foot-ring.

Inside the storehouse, small boots clattered down the loft ladder. The cooper’s brother’s two boys dropped onto the floorboards, followed by Master Lin rubbing the back of his neck.

“Who was marching up and down the lane all night?” the older boy asked, looking round at Lupa’s spear in the rack. “We heard them right through the roof-tiles—*clack, pause, clack-clack*.”

Master Lin paused by the flour barrels, frowning toward the open door. “I thought that was your night-watch, Watcher. Heavy boots in step on the cobbles from the third watch till the sky went grey.”

“They turned right outside this shutter,” Ciaccona said from the window-seat, where she was tying off a fresh strip of linen round her splinted left forefinger with her teeth. “Four-beat garrison step. I assumed Liangyu had doubled the guard.”

Lupa’s ears flattened once against her hair.

“There wasn’t any guard in this lane,” Lupa said flatly. “I went out at the fourth watch with my spear because the tramping was rattling the latch. The lane was empty from here to the well, and the frost on the cobbles didn’t have a single print in it.”

She turned on her heel and went back to the stove.

Quick footsteps came up the alley from the plaza. Buling walked up to the landing with her hands tucked in her royal-blue sleeves, her breath steaming beneath the yellow paper talisman on her forehead.

She looked once at the two yellow slips she had pasted on the storehouse doorposts the night before; both were dry and bright. Then she stepped across the gutter to the cooper’s door and tilted her head back at the oak lintel.

Along the top and bottom edges of the slip over the cooper’s door, a greasy grey-black stain had crept half an inch into the paper.

Buling raised her right hand to peel it loose.

Before her fingertips touched the wood, the small brass bell hanging at her sash rang by itself.

*Ting.*

Buling froze with her arm up. The air in the lane was dead still, and the silk cord at her waist had not moved.

She dropped her hand and closed her fist hard around the brass bell. Inside her closed fingers, the clapper struck the metal a second time, buzzing sharp against her palm.

Buling pulled a scrap of waste paper from her pocket, twisted it into a tight wad with her thumb, and jammed it deep into the mouth of the bell until the brass tongue was wedged solid against the rim. Then she took two fresh yellow slips from her sleeve, slapped them with thick paste right over the grey one on the cooper’s lintel, and smoothed them flat with the heel of her hand.

From the storehouse step, Mei watched her. “Buling?”

“Frost got into the brass,” Buling said shortly, wiping her thumb on her skirt without looking round. “Cold makes the pin slip. Very common.”

She turned and walked back down the lane toward the plaza at a fast clip.

After breakfast, the household went out through the doorway in three directions: Amy to the yard turbine with her wrench and the boiled linen; Yangyang and Cartethyia toward the coal-bunker with a pair of wicker hods and a bundle of grey blankets; and Mei to the head of the twelve wide bluestone steps above Huizhen Plaza, her boots laced to the top hooks, with Iuno half a pace to her left and Lupa behind her with a market basket on her arm.

***

Mei took the twelve wide steps down to the plaza one riser at a time.

She kept her hands out of her jacket pockets for balance, setting her left heel down, bringing her right boot beside it, and pausing a beat on the flat stone before taking the next drop. Six inches to her left, Iuno matched her step with her right hand tucked in her coat pocket; behind them, Lupa swung the empty willow basket against her knee. At the twelfth riser, Mei stepped off onto the plaza paving, blew out her breath, and grinned back up the stair.

Huizhen Plaza was bright with cold late-morning sun. By the stone well-head, four village girls were turning a long hemp cart-rope across the dry flagstones while two smaller boys hopped in and out of the swing, chanting to the slap of the hemp on the stone:

*“North gate iron, south gate stone,*
*Who keeps the third watch all alone?*
*Black rain falling on the ditch,*
*Throw your pine-knot in the pitch—*
*Call the Yuan wall, one, two, three,*
*No one comes for you and me!”*

Old Mother Pu came round the corner of the cabbage stall with a stoneware vinegar jar in the crook of her arm. At the fifth line, her shoes stopped dead on the cobbles.

“Where did you get that song?” Mother Pu demanded, turning on the tanner’s little girl at the end of the rope.

The girl let the rope drop slack against her shin, blinking up at her. “Nobody gave it to us, Mother Pu. Everybody knows that one.”

Mother Pu stared down at the rope for a breath, then flapped her free hand at the lot of them. “Off the well-step with that cord before you trip the water-carriers! Go on, take it down the alley!”

The children scooped up the rope and trotted off toward the cooper’s shed. Mother Pu pulled her shawl up over her ears and walked straight up the terrace steps with both arms locked around her jar.

At the vegetable trestle beside the steamer shed, Mei leaned over the boards and picked up a round, purple-shouldered winter turnip the size of a soup-pot, with frozen garden dirt still crusted in its whiskers.

“Three coppers for the big one,” the stall-man said.

“Two coppers, and I won’t tell the plaza the little ones in the back basket are pithy,” Mei said, hefting the turnip in both palms.

“The little ones are sweeter than river-pears, miss. Four coppers if you want the greens left on the top.”

“Sold!” Mei beamed, digging four coppers out of her pouch and dropping them into his mitten before Lupa could open her mouth.

Lupa reached over Mei’s shoulder, lifted the big turnip out of her hands by the leafy crown, and dropped it into the willow basket with a solid thump. “Over a pound. Mine.”

Mei immediately snatched a thumb-sized bundle of green scallions tied with a blade of straw off the corner of the plank. “I’m carrying the onions! Nobody touch the onions!”

Out of the narrow cobble alley behind the cooper’s shed came Master Shao.

He had no coat over his ink-stained wool vest, his grey hair stood up at the crown where he had dragged his fingers through it, and his face was the colour of tallow. In his right hand he held a single damp sheet of coarse bamboo paper by the top corner, holding it out from his apron as if it were hot from the stove.

He walked straight across the cobbles to Iuno and Mei.

“Lady,” Shao said, his voice scraped thin. “Read the top column on this for me. My eyes aren’t taking it in right.”

“Where did this come from, Master Shao?” Iuno asked, taking the damp sheet in her right hand.

“My own bed-plate,” Shao said, wiping his empty palm hard down the side of his trousers. “I took the holiday padlock off the door at dawn with the key from my own neck-string. The iron chase was wedged tight on the stone and inked fresh. Set with eighteen-point square chancery lead from the back-wall cases my grandfather brought up from the valley—cases I haven’t pulled a drawer on since the old Commander’s time. Nobody broke the shutter, and the boy’s been at his mother’s since Friday. I pulled one proof to see what was in the frame, and then I took the iron mallet and knocked the whole forme into the box.”

Iuno looked down at the damp black impression on the bamboo sheet.

*Look at the reign-mark in the margin, darling,* the High Priestess murmured in the back of her mind, all the drawl gone out of her voice. *That type was not set for this century.*

*Be still,* Iuno answered silently, and read the heavy square characters aloud in her clear, even voice:

“‘In the twelfth month of the third year of Jingping, from the Commander of the Pass to the wards below the rock: At the beating of the second evening drum, all households of the lower terraces shall bring their children and their grain within the inner gatehouse wall. At dusk the outer iron shall be dropped and the bridge-pins drawn. None shall open to any voice from the ditch until the morning horn.’”

Beside Mei, Lupa’s fingers tightened on the willow handle of the basket.

Iuno folded the damp proof sheet cleanly in four against her hip with her thumb and fingers. “May I keep this, Master Shao?”

“Take it and put it in your stove,” the old printer said, already backing toward his alley with both hands tucked into his armpits. “I’m putting the bar back on the door.”

Across the flagstones, at the end of the Fairmaiden’s counter where the steam from the dumpling cauldron drifted past the pillar, Suoming sat on the tall three-legged stool with her brown umbrella upright between her knees.

Before her sat an earthenware bowl of carp dumplings with a dull film of cold grease set around the rim, the porcelain spoon dry beside it. She was not looking at the cauldron; her pale eyes had been fixed on the mouth of the printer’s alley since before Shao walked out of it.

As Iuno slid the folded sheet into her inner coat pocket, Suoming slipped down off the stool, laid two coppers beside the untouched bowl, and walked off toward the lower stair, her iron heel-plates clicking steady on the stone.

At the foot of the twelve wide steps leading back up to the third storehouse, Mei shifted her bunch of scallions into her left hand and started up.

She climbed to the fourth riser, the sixth, the seventh. On the eighth step, the breath went out of her nose in a short huff and her right palm pressed flat against the side of her jacket.

Mei sat down squarely on the cold bluestone of the eighth step, her boots planted on the seventh and the green scallions laid across her knee.

Two steps below her, Lupa stopped with the turnip basket. Beside her, Iuno stopped on the eighth riser, six inches from Mei’s hip, her right hand resting inside her coat pocket.

Mei took three slow breaths, watching the white steam leave her lips in the noon sun. Then she braced her palm on her own right knee, pushed herself up onto her boots, and climbed the last four steps onto the landing.

***

In the first hour after noon, Yangyang and Cartethyia carried the coal and the blankets down to the lower barracks terrace.

Yangyang had the heavier wicker coal-hod braced against her left hip—where she could not have borne the weight of an empty bucket the day before—walking down the frosted risers with a light, even step and a quiet pleasure in the set of her shoulders. Beside her, Cartethyia carried a tied bundle of grey wool blankets tall enough to brush the crystal thorns on her brow.

Inside the long stone granary shed beside the barred lower gatehouse, the four valley families—millers and lime-burners from the lower bend, eleven people in all with three children under ten—were sitting on stacked hemp grain-bags around a small iron brazier. The fathers stood up at once to take the coal-hod, their faces pinched gray with cold and dark hollows under their eyes.

“Did the children get any sleep after the drum, Mistress He?” Yangyang asked, helping the miller’s wife spread a thick wool blanket over a corner of dry straw.

“Not after midnight, Watcher,” the miller’s wife said quietly, glancing toward the brazier where her little boy was warming his mittens. “We heard the camp down in the gorge.”

Cartethyia paused with the second blanket in her hands. “A camp?”

“Right down below the south wall where the fog sits,” the lime-burner said from the grain-sack, holding his rough palms over the coals. “Plain as if the draft was blowing straight up a chimney-flue. Brass mule-bells jingling on a picket-rope, men splitting green pine with axes, iron cook-pots banging on the stones, and a cook yelling out that the millet was boiled. Went on for two hours in the dark.”

“And then they called the roll,” his wife said beside him, pulling her wool shawl tighter across her chest, her voice flat and hurried like someone telling a bad dream by daylight so it won’t come back. “A man with a big voice standing high up on the rock, reading out names one after another and waiting for the answer. Hundreds of them. My grandfather’s people have worked the lime-kilns in this valley since the road was cut, Watcher, and there wasn’t a single family name in that call I ever heard in the pass.”

Outside in the barbican yard, heavy iron-shod boots crunched down the steep stone steps from the upper cliff-track.

Yangyang and Cartethyia stepped to the open granary door as Warden Ge and his four spearmen came down off the eastern goat-path. Their wool cloaks were stiff with grey rime, their breath blowing white in the cold, and every man still carried his pike whole.

Warden Liangyu met them by the dry stone water-trough.

“Well?” Liangyu asked.

“No way down, Warden,” Ge said straight out, grounding his pike-butt on the cobbles while the two valley fathers stepped out onto the threshold behind Yangyang. “We climbed the goat-track to the limestone comb two hundred feet above the cut. You can see down behind the black wall from the ridge.”

Ge looked once toward the white gulf beyond the barred gate, then back into Liangyu’s face.

“It isn’t just a wall across the road today,” Ge said evenly. “The whole fortress is standing behind it in the gorge. Square watch-towers, slate roofs on the barracks, timber galleries over the river, and cook-smoke going straight up out of the chimneys through the fog. There are men in the old high-collared Warden plate standing post at every brazier along the battlements with their pikes grounded.”

Behind his shoulder, the youngest spearman swallowed hard. “Warden Ge hailed them three times through the brass horn. Not one of them turned his head or looked up at the cliff.”

“We tried the south chimney to scramble down past the third milestone,” Ge went on. “Ten yards into the mist below the ridge, the goat-track turned us right back out onto the top of the east cliff facing the Hold. And the black stone has grown eastward since yesterday afternoon. It runs clear across the foot of the goat-gulley now, keyed straight into the rock.”

For two breaths the barbican yard was quiet except for the post-mule shifting its hooves by the empty hay-rack.

Liangyu turned toward Corporal Meng by the guardroom door.

“At the second evening drum, bring the lower gate-watch and everyone on this terrace up inside the inner gatehouse wall,” Liangyu ordered plainly. “Drop the inner iron portcullis at dark and leave the lower barbican empty till sunrise. Nobody stays down on this tier tonight.”

“Yes, Warden,” Meng said.

On the granary step, the miller’s wife took her small boy by the shoulder and looked at Yangyang.

“Watcher,” the woman asked simply. “Are we going to get home to the valley?”

Yangyang looked at her tired eyes and the child’s mittened hand holding the edge of her apron.

“I don’t know, Mistress He,” Yangyang said in her clear, gentle voice. “The south road is shut, and none of us knows why the stone is there or when it will open. What I do know is that our stove up at the third storehouse is hot, Lupa has her big brass pot on the fire, and the cooper’s brother’s boys are up in the loft. Come up the steps with your blankets before the drum goes. Nobody should sit in a granary tonight.”

Along her collar, the small cerulean feathers lifted once against her neck and settled warm.

Cartethyia stepped right up beside her elbow, her blue eyes bright and earnest under her crystal laurel as she turned toward Corporal Meng, Warden Ge, and the two stranded peddlers by the trough.

“And you must all come too!” Cartethyia declared warmly. “We have Mother Pu’s winter squash and dried jujubes, and I can bake three more braided loaves before the lamps are lit if somebody helps me knead the millet!”

***

At the seventh bell of the afternoon, the sky between the karst peaks turned the colour of bruised iron.

A line of moon-white light slanted down through the cold air to the inner gatehouse watch gallery, and Qingxiao stepped off the flat of her flying sword onto the cedar planks. The blade dissolved into quiet light at her cuff as she drew the indigo storehouse oilcloth closer over her shoulders.

Ruoyun stood beside the corner embrasure with an unlit brass watch-lamp in her left hand and a tin oil-cruet in her right. On the limestone cap beside her elbow, the dark kaya-wood weiqi board sat untouched since yesterday: nine black handicap stones on the star-points, the tenth black stone on the third-line shoulder, and Qingxiao’s second white stone sitting unanswered on the fourth line.

Qingxiao stopped beside the corner pillar, her breath steaming white in the canyon draft.

“The wall reaches the eastern spur,” Qingxiao said in her level voice. “There is a sentry in old plate at every brazier from the cliff to the river.”

Ruoyun did not look up from the brass reservoir she was filling with oil.

“Only the stretch above the sally-port arch is empty,” Qingxiao said. “No one stands there. And the mist turned my blade back at the first milestone today.”

Ruoyun set the tin oil-cruet down on the pine bench without spilling a drop. She turned the brass thumb-wheel to raise the cotton wick, struck a flint-steel once, and touched the spark to the oil. A flat yellow flame caught behind the glass slide.

“I’m taking the third watch up here tonight myself,” Ruoyun said in her fast, hard voice, snapping the brass shutter down over the glass. “Tell Liangyu’s boys they can stay down by the guardroom fire.”

Qingxiao looked at the rigid line of Ruoyun’s jaw below her small golden horns, and then toward the stairwell, where the smell of birch-smoke and boiling mutton broth was already drifting up from the third terrace.

“The girl from Mingting and the small knight have asked half the Hold to the third storehouse for supper,” Qingxiao said. “Mei told me at the well to bring you down.”

Ruoyun’s head came up, her blue eyes sharp and cold behind her round brass spectacles.

“And do what, sit on a flour-barrel listening to those idiots bang spoons while the ditch crawls up the stairs?” Ruoyun said, every word clipped and biting. “I have a wall to keep, A-Qing. Go eat their charity stew if your stomach’s growling so loud you can’t stand a watch. Leave me alone.”

She turned her back squarely on Qingxiao and set the lit watch-lamp down on the limestone cap beside the unanswered weiqi board.

Qingxiao stood on the cedar planks for the space of a breath. The wind off the pass stirred the loose blue hair across her cheek, and her shoulders gave a small, distinct shiver inside the indigo oilcloth.

Then she turned and walked down the timber stairwell toward the warm lane below, leaving Ruoyun standing alone under the dark eaves with her hand resting on the cold stone beside the lamp and the board.

***

By the first watch of the night, the stone landing outside the third storehouse and the broad bluestone steps down to the cistern were packed shoulder to shoulder under the crimson eaves-lanterns.

Hardly a house on the mountain had kept its shutters barred. Up from the lower barracks came the four valley families carrying their quilts and their children; out of the lanes along the second and third terraces came the neighbours who had heard the second drum in the mist, the boots on the cobbles, or the mule-bells in the gorge, bringing three-legged stools, stoneware jars of pickled mustard greens, and iron skillets of fried turnip-cake to set along the coping wall. Only the Ministry lodging down on the second terrace sat dark, its pine shutters wedged shut from the inside.

Lupa and Wen had two cauldrons and the three-foot brass hotpot roaring over three charcoal braziers in the middle of the yard, sending clouds of white steam and peppered mutton broth rolling across the cold stone. Widow Tan sat on the pine bench with one arm hooked firmly around Little Tan’s waist while he propped his bandaged ankle on his broomstick crutch and ate a hunk of Cartethyia’s hot anise-bread; beside the water-butt, Old Mother Pu argued with Ciaccona over the proper way to salt a winter duck while Ciaccona tuned her lute against her knee with her splinted finger sticking out; and down by the split-cedar fence, Old Man Zhao struck a flint, lit his brass pipe at last, and blew a real cloud of blue tobacco smoke into the red lantern-glow.

By the wooden turbine post, Qingxiao stood wrapped in her camphor-scented indigo oilcloth, holding a deep earthenware bowl of Lupa’s mutton-and-squash stew in both bare hands and eating steadily standing up after Chisa handed her a clean spoon. Across the gutter on the cooper’s step, Buling sat with her knees drawn up under her royal-blue silk and a roasted sweet potato in her palm, the paper-stuffed brass bell hanging silent at her sash.

And on the end of the pine bench nearest the coping wall sat Suoming. She had come up the steps at dusk when Cartethyia waved to her from the landing; she sat with her furled brown umbrella upright between her knees, her iron-heeled boots dangling clear of the flagstones, and ate a bowl of hot broth Lupa had ladled out for her.

In the press of padded coats and wool shawls along the lower steps, a few faces stood quiet at the back of the crowd that nobody quite placed: a tall man by the dyer’s pillar in a heavy, high-collared wool coat of an old valley cut, his hands tucked deep inside his sleeves; two steps lower, a grey-haired woman holding a flat willow market-basket woven in a herringbone plait nobody in the Hold made any more. Nobody jostled their elbows and nobody looked at them twice as the bread-basket went round.

Over the black teeth of the eastern pass, a heavy bank of carmine-red cloud began to glow.

Along the landing and the steps, the talk around the braziers quieted down.

Up through the red vapour over the ridge rose the tarnished copper rim of the moon—and half a hand’s breadth to its left, rising at the exact same slant behind the glowing cloud, came a second moon, twin to the first, pale and blurred like a lamp seen through oiled horn.

On the bench, the miller’s youngest boy pointed his wooden spoon straight at the pass, delighted. “Look! There’s two of them tonight!”

Beside the stone cistern trough, Yangyang stood with a warm mug of ginger-water in her hands. She looked down into the black, unfrozen water of the trough at her feet, where the twin moons sat side by side on the same still surface.

Slowly, Yangyang’s right hand left her mug and touched her left side beneath her cloak, pressing once against her whole, unstrapped ribs.

Before the quiet on the steps could turn cold, a deep, hollow *thump—thump—thump* rolled up from the southern gorge, far down beyond the second bend.

Three streaks of pale gold whistled straight up out of the black canyon mist into the sky above Xuanfang Hold.

High overhead, right between the karst spires and the dark, motionless rings of the Astrolabe Core, they broke open at once.

A huge, trailing willow of burnished gold spilled across the clouds, followed in the next heartbeat by a monstrous crimson peony and a double ring of bright emerald-green stars that opened wide enough to turn every upturned face on the mountain terraces green and gold.

*Thump-thump-thump-thump.*

More mortars coughed from the dark gorge below the wall, and the whole narrow sky above the Hold went up in a roar of light. Great silver chrysanthemums bloomed and hung slow in the freezing air; showers of violet and white-gold rain drifted down toward the cliff-pines, lighting the black celadon shell of the stalled Core for a breath at a time before another burst of ruby and copper-gold washed the slate roofs brighter than noon.

Down by the cistern, the cooper’s boys and the valley children jumped up and down on the flagstones, cheering at the top of their lungs. On the storehouse landing, Cartethyia clapped her mittens together; Mei leaned her head back against Iuno’s shoulder with her face tipped up to the silver rain; and after a heartbeat, the valley lime-burner and Corporal Meng and the quarrymen joined in, shouting and banging their tin mugs against the stone balustrade as the gold willow-trails poured down the dark.

“Hey, Liangyu!” Lupa yelled across the landing through the crackle of a huge brocade crown, squinting up at the glare. “Where’d the garrison hide the powder-chests? I thought the magazine was down to signal rockets!”

By the doorpost, Liangyu lowered her mug, her face lit red and green by the bursts overhead. “Those aren’t ours. We haven’t had festival mortars in the vault since before the siege.”

“Did the Guild barges send a crate up with the coal, Watcher?” Warden Ge asked, turning his head.

“No,” Yangyang said, looking toward the southern cut where the bright shells kept rising out of the white fog. “And the Fairmaiden didn’t buy them.”

“Wait a second,” Amy said from the top of her tool-crate, tilting her chin back as three giant silver peonies broke right above the gatehouse tower and vanished into the dark. “Look at the roof-line. There’s no smoke.”

Beside her, Lupa raised her nose into the canyon draft, her wolf ears swiveling forward.

Overhead, a dozen red-and-gold comets burst with a deafening *crack-crack-crack* that bounced off every cliff wall—and the instant the sparks winked out, the air between the spires was completely clear. Not a shred of grey powder-smoke drifted across the twin moons; not a whiff of burnt saltpetre or sulfur came up on the gorge wind; not a single charred scrap of pasted cartridge-paper fluttered down onto the roofs.

“Maybe the wind’s blowing the smoke back down the river—” Little Tan started from the bench.

“The wind’s coming straight up the cut into our faces, boy,” Old Man Zhao said around his pipe-stem, and then banged his boot-heel hard on the flagstone as another enormous gold willow filled the pass: “Never mind the smoke, look at the size of that one! Go on, boys, yell for it!”

Nobody ran for a doorway. Across the landing and down the steps, the children cheered harder, and the adults shouted right along with them into the cold, odorless blaze of light.

At the end of the pine bench, Suoming had not looked up at the sky once.

Her pale eyes were fixed on the low stone coping wall ten feet away, where the cooper’s brother’s two small boys were standing up on the bench with their mittens cupped round their mouths, cheering at the rockets.

Right beside the older boy’s elbow—standing up on the narrow, frosted stone cap of the coping wall itself with her heels over the three-hundred-foot drop and her hands holding onto nothing at all—was a thin girl of twelve or thirteen.

She wore a short, patched hemp tunic far too light for the mountain winter, her bare wrists chapped red in the glare. Her long dark hair was put up at the crown in the exact same twist as Suoming’s, skewered through with a plain wooden hairpin with a small round ball carved on the end.

She was cheering with the two boys, wholly happy, bouncing on her toes on the edge of the drop as a huge cascade of gold and crimson rain washed over her face.

At the height of the gold burst, the thin girl turned her head sharply to her left—turning toward the empty foot of coping stone beside her elbow, the way a child turns to tug the sleeve of the person who brought her to the fair—and said something bright and quick up at the empty air. Over the *crack-boom* of the mortars in the gorge, no sound of her voice carried across the landing.

The cooper’s brother reached up to steady his younger son’s coat without glancing an inch to the right.

Suoming set her half-full earthenware bowl down on the pine bench beside her hip.

She slid her boots down to the flagstones and stood up with the furled bamboo shaft of her umbrella in her left hand.

“Ninth,” Suoming said, flat and quiet under the roar of the sky. Nobody heard it.

Overhead, a white chrysanthemum flashed and went dark.

When the next green peony broke open half a breath later, the space on the coping wall beside the cooper’s older boy was empty. The girl had not climbed down or walked away; there was only the frosted grey limestone under the falling green light, and the two boys went right on cheering beside the bare stone.

Suoming turned on her heel and walked out of the storehouse yard into the red-lit lane.

By the brazier, Mei glanced down at the end of the bench, where a wisp of steam was still rising from the unfinished bowl of broth. “Hey—where’d Suoming go? She didn’t even eat the squash.”

Beside the cistern trough, Yangyang’s head turned toward the upper steps.

Under the cheering of the children and the steady *thump-crack* of the fireworks above the gorge, she heard the steel ferrule of the long umbrella striking the bluestone risers up the fourth terrace—not the slow, unhurried tap of every other night, but fast and hard, climbing two steps at a beat toward the cliff.

Halfway up the long, solitary flight of the Fogveil Pagoda stair, where the rows of red paper globes swung beneath the bursts of gold and violet light in the sky, Suoming did not slow her feet.

Under the glare of a wide crimson shell over the river, her thumb hit the wooden spring-catch on the shaft.

The brown oil-paper umbrella shot open overhead with a report like one of the gorge mortars, and for a single heartbeat, a jagged thread of crimson-black lightning ran silent and hot along the steel-tipped ribs under the paper.

Holding the open umbrella over her shoulder, Suoming climbed fast up the red-lit stone steps toward the frosted, bolted jade doors in the mist, while behind her back the silent, smokeless fires kept breaking across the dark sky above the Hold.

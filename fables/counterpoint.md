# Counterpoint
### *a Lyre, Speak to Me cycle — five stories in which two grounds run at once*

---

## Preface: The Pairings

A chaconne runs one line in the bass and everything else on top of it. Two chaconnes at once is either a disaster or a form. These five are the pairs where the disagreement is load-bearing.

**Iuno and Lupa — Abraham against the public realm.** Kierkegaard's Abraham cannot speak; the moment he explains himself the act becomes murder, so he goes up the mountain in silence and the silence is the whole anguish. Arendt's entire position is that a deed done where nobody can see it has no reality, that the meaning of an act is supplied by the person who tells it afterward, and that the two remedies for having done something irreversible are forgiveness, which only somebody else can give, and the promise, which is what you use when forgiveness is unavailable. Put them in one room with a file that has sixty-three pages in it and a stamp on the front that says *withdrawn.*

**Ciaccona and Chisa — redemptive memory against the un-representable other.** Benjamin's storyteller redeems the past by choosing it and singing it, because the archive that keeps everything gives back nothing. Levinas answers that the Other exceeds every account you could give of them, and that a good likeness is a kind of confiscation. One of them wants to write a dead girl a song. The other one is keeping a hole in exactly that girl's shape and has been keeping it for twenty years.

**Mei and Cartethyia — the leap against the turning arms.** Both women have organised their lives around being present at the edge. Mei's method is the single decisive accompaniment: she goes over with you. Cartethyia's is attrition: she is there at the sixth bell every day for as long as it takes and lets you swing at her. Cadanto told her *stories have endings; fight like weather instead, weather continues.* Mei is shaped like a story. The two methods are incompatible in the hands, and both are correct, and somebody has to be the case they argue over.

**Amy and Iuno — the clay that volunteers against Abraham on the mountain.** Both would do it again. Amy read the projections and put her own name in; Iuno ran the arithmetic and put somebody else's. The gap between those two is the size of a house, and they live in one.

**Lupa and Amy — the *who* and the *what*.** Arendt's distinction, arrived at from opposite ends. Lupa's likeness was owned outright by a House and sold as instant noodles under clause four. Amy built a persona at fourteen so that a girl could be liked without any waiting attached. There is a statue of Amy in a memorial garden in a cape the sculptor had to pick the colour of alone, and the subject is alive, and Lupa is the world's foremost authority on what to do about that.

## I. The Eleventh Delivery
### *Iuno and Lupa*

---

> **GUILD ARCHIVE, THIRD TERRACE — STANDING ORDER 4**
> *The archive amends nothing. Material touching a closed bracket is shelved as received and cross-referenced.*
> *Where the depositor was party to the events deposited, the material is admitted under countersignature by a person of standing in the matter. The countersignatory is entered in the catalogue beside the depositor and may be applied to.*

---

### i.

The board bundle came off the nine-week post on a Thursday with the usual tape on it and a slip on top that Lupa read four times standing in the doorway of the passage office.

*ARCHIVE, THIRD TERRACE — IRREGULARITY, FILE 'M. OF RAGUNNA'*

*The archive has received, out of sequence, a deposit from a second party. The material is substantial and touches a closed bracket. Under Standing Order 4 it cannot be shelved without countersignature.*

*The depositor has supplied a name. The archive holds one other supplier of this file, who has never supplied one, and whose fees continue to arrive four times a year in loose shells, and whose signature the catalogue records as 'device, unclear.'*

*The board is asked to advise. The archivist notes that she has now been keeping one book for two people for some time, and that this is a first in her career, and that she has formed opinions.*

---

She went up the service stair three at a time and it rang the whole way and eight people heard it and one of them put a cup down.

Iuno was at the kitchen table with the pot and the correspondence and the good cup, and she looked up, and the crystal stump caught the light, and her face did the thing it does, which is arrive at the whole conversation about four seconds before anybody else.

"You've had the slip," she said.

"Two years."

"Yes."

"Two *years* I've been paying that woman in shells through a third party so she'd never have a name to put in a catalogue, and you—" Lupa put the slip on the table with a flatness that was worse than throwing it. "You walked up to the counter and *signed.*"

"I posted it. There's no counter involved."

"Iuno."

"I signed," Iuno agreed.

---

### ii.

The pack cleared the room the way weather clears a market. Cartethyia took Amy under one arm and a plate of something under the other and had the door shut inside eleven seconds, and Chisa put a second pot on and left it where a hand could reach it, and Ciaccona went as far as the corridor and sat down on the floor outside with her back to the wall and her lute across her knees and made no attempt whatsoever to disguise what she was doing.

Mei stayed in the doorway.

"Out," said Lupa.

"No."

"Mei—"

"Two chairs, one table," said Mei. "I'll be on the roof. If either of you comes up before this is finished I'm sending you back down." She left. The door stayed open about a hand's width, which was a message, and neither of them closed it.

---

### iii.

"What's in it," said Lupa.

"Eleven pages. Written out and sworn in front of a Guild notary on the second terrace nine weeks ago by post, which took some arranging, since I am legally a foreign national with one arm and an address inside a god."

"What's *in* it."

Iuno turned the cup a quarter-turn on the table.

"The petition," she said. "The Temple keeps a petitions book. Every citizen has right of audience with the Priestess once in a life, and the book records the day and the name and one line for the matter raised, and it is public, and I have thought about the word *public* a great deal since I stopped being entitled to it."

"Mya petitioned."

"Mya petitioned. Twentieth of Frostbreak, twenty years ago. The line in the book reads *matter of health.*" Iuno's voice stayed exactly level. "I wrote that line. I wrote it in front of two acolytes and a clerk and I chose those three words in about a second and a half and they have been sitting in a public book in my handwriting ever since."

Lupa sat down. The chair took it.

"And the eleven pages say what actually happened."

"The eleven pages say what actually happened."

---

### iv.

"She came up the Hill at the sixth bell in a coat with the collar up, which was the closest thing to a disguise available to a woman that recognisable, and it worked, because people looking for Contender Mya were looking in the fighters' quarter."

Iuno had her hand flat on the table. She kept it there.

"She had been going strange for a season. Everyone in that city knew the word for it and no one used it. She had stopped eating at the arcade table. She was talking to somebody in the training tunnels who wasn't in the training tunnels. And she had worked out, by herself, with no instruction from any quarter, that the thing in her head was coming from outside and had a purpose."

"She was clever," said Lupa.

"She was extremely clever, and I would like that noted somewhere, because the record has her down as *heart* and the word *heart* has done her a great deal of damage." Iuno breathed out. "She sat down in the audience chamber and asked me a technical question. She asked what she was being made into."

"And you told her."

"I told her." The cup went another quarter-turn. "Lupa, I want you to understand what I was. I was twenty-two years old, three years invested, the most gifted of my line, born under an eclipse, and I had already begun to think of people as terms. I sat opposite a woman of thirty who had come to the Temple as a last resort and I read her the way I read everything, and I saw the whole of it — the Harbinger, the door, the funnel, the fifty thousand voices, what she would be used for and what would be left after — and I laid it out for her in order, clearly, accurately, at conversational volume, because I was proud of the reading."

The vents cycled.

"And I told her the truth about the options, which was that there was one. Because a Harbinger's power runs through a living body, and the door opens through a willing one, and there is exactly one act that closes it, and I said so, and she asked me to repeat it, and I repeated it."

Lupa's hands had gone around the edge of the table.

"You told her to kill herself."

"I told her the mechanism," said Iuno. "I did not tell her to. I have run that distinction for twenty years and it has never once held up under any weather, and I put it in the affidavit anyway because the affidavit is a record and a record takes what happened." Her jaw moved. "She sat there for a long time. Then she said thank you, and she asked whether the Temple would keep her name on the bracket, and I said the Temple has no authority over the Guild's brackets, which was true, and useless, and I said it because I wanted the conversation to end."

"And?"

"And she stood up." Iuno looked at the table. "And on her way to the door she said something to herself, half under her breath, the way people do when they are moving from one room of their life to the next. I have it exactly. I have everything exactly; it is the whole of my affliction."

"Say it."

"*Just this once more.*"

---

The kitchen was very quiet.

Lupa sat with her hands on the table and something behind her sternum coming apart in a slow, structural way, because she was sixteen years old in a dark archive room with a tape running and a woman's voice coming through a boom left live by accident before a bout, low and fast, talking to itself, *you love this, you love this, they came to see you, don't — please — let me not disappoint them, please, just this once more —*

"I've had that sentence since I was sixteen," she said.

"I know. You told the whole of Fulvia's tavern about the tape and I was on the roof of the building opposite, because I was always on the roof of the building opposite." Iuno's mouth did something. "It's the same sentence. She used it going into the light and she used it going out of my chamber. Twenty years apart. I have had both halves of it for two decades and you have had one, and I have known that, and I have said nothing."

---

### v.

"Six days later she went into the water off the Old District mole," Iuno said. "At night. By herself. There is a harbour watch log and I have a copy and it is in the eleven pages. The bracket sheet was stamped four days after that because the Guild waits a week before it stamps anything."

"*Withdrawn.*"

"*Withdrawn.*" Iuno's hand came off the table at last and went around the cup. "Twenty years of a public record that says a woman with a reputation for crying got frightened and quit. Twenty years of a betting desk that priced it at sixty to one. And what actually happened is that a resonator of moderate strength and no institutional protection was told, by the state, in a room, what she was being made into, and worked out on her own that the only lever she still owned was the last one, and pulled it, on purpose, to shut a door."

"And then Julia Silva pulled her out of the sea."

"And then Julia Silva pulled her out of the sea and I let a playwright have the body, and undid the one thing that woman had kept for herself, and I have already said that in front of her and she gave me two words instead of a pardon." Iuno set the cup down. "*Don't waste it.* Which is the hardest sentence anybody has ever handed me, because it has homework in it."

---

### vi.

"Why now," said Lupa.

"Because the file is going to outlive both of us and I am the last living witness to the only part of it that matters."

"That's the reason you'd put on a form."

"Yes." Iuno looked up. "Here's the other one. There's a girl of eight in Caldara who wrote you a letter, and you wrote her back, and you gave her a name to say at a table, and the name you gave her was Mya's, and the only thing you had to hand that child was the tape and the shoulder-drop and *never once ran.* And the reason that is the only thing you had is that the rest of it is in my head." She flattened her hand on the table again. "You have been building that girl a person out of a betting slip and a boom left live by accident. I have the six days and the coat and the question she asked. I have been sitting on the load-bearing part for twenty years and calling it discretion."

"Discretion."

"It was never discretion." Iuno's voice went somewhere flat and cold that Lupa recognised, because it was the voice of a woman describing a specimen. "For thirty years I had one talent for the truth, and the talent was to hold it until holding it stopped being possible, and then to hold it a little longer. My throat sealed for a decade and I let the sealing take the blame. The throat has been open since the precipice. I have had eight months of a working throat and I have used it to be funny about the vents."

---

### vii.

Lupa got up and went to the window and stood there with her back to the room, which was a thing her mother used to do, and which she had noticed herself doing at twenty-six and had chosen to keep.

"There's a countersignature," she said.

"There is."

"You could have used anybody with standing. Augusta has standing. The Ephor of Septimont would sign anything with your name on it and dare the archive to argue."

"Augusta would sign it out of thirty years of loyalty and the catalogue would show two names that have always been in the same column." Iuno's chair moved. "I want yours."

"Because it's harder."

"Because it's the only one that means anything." A pause. "And because Standing Order 4 says the countersignatory may be applied to. Anybody who reads that file can write to whoever signed it. Forever. It is a door with your name on it and it stays open after you're dead, and I know exactly what I'm asking, and I'm asking."

Lupa looked out at the terraces.

"If I sign," she said, "does that mean I've forgiven you?"

"No."

"You answered that fast."

"I've had eleven pages and nine weeks of post to answer it." Iuno's voice stayed level. "You cannot forgive me on paper. You cannot forgive me by holding a pen. If it ever happens it will happen in a corridor over something stupid, three years from now, and neither of us will be able to date it afterward. That's how it works. I've read about it."

"Read about it."

"There is a great deal of literature on the subject and I have been through most of it in the last eight months, because it turns out that when you stop calculating you have a considerable amount of free time and no idea what to do with it."

Lupa laughed. It got half a breath out of her and stopped in the middle.

---

### viii.

She came back to the table and sat down and picked up the slip and put it down again.

"I can't," she said.

"I know."

"I'm going to say it properly because you'll do the thing where you assume the worst version." Lupa put both hands flat. "I can't forgive you. Not today and probably not this year. When I close my eyes I've got a woman on a table in a room under my own city and a priestess in blue silk letting a playwright praise her cleverness, and I put a spear through the machine and the machine wasn't the part that needed it. That's what's in me. It might change. I'm not promising it will."

"That's a real answer."

"It's the only one I've got." Lupa's tail lay flat on the concrete floor. "Now here's what I can do, and I want you to hear all of it before you say anything, because you're going to want to be gracious and I'd rather you shut up."

Iuno inclined her head about a quarter of an inch.

"I'll sign," said Lupa. "I'll sign my full name and my Guild number and my hometown, because column nine goes on everything I sign now, and it'll sit in the catalogue next to yours, and any kid in that city with a copying fee can read the two of us together on a spine card for the next four hundred years." She breathed. "And what it'll mean is this. It'll mean I was there. It'll mean somebody with standing looked at your account of the worst thing you ever did and put her name under it and said *this is what happened.* That's what a witness is. That's the entire job. It has nothing to do with liking you."

"Lupa—"

"I said shut up." Lupa's ears went flat and came up again. "You spent thirty years doing everything where nobody could see it. That's the actual crime; the rest is symptoms. A thing that happens where nobody's looking doesn't count as having happened, it just sits there being true in one person's head and rotting them from the inside, and I've watched you do it and I've watched what it made. So you're going to appear. In a book. On a shelf. In public, with a name, next to somebody who can be written to."

"That's a sentence."

"That's a *record*." Lupa picked the slip up. "And here's the part I actually want, and it's the only thing I'm asking for. The file's got a stamp on the front and the archive amends nothing, on principle, and I've hated that principle for two years. *Withdrawn.* It stays. Fine. But underneath it there's going to be eleven pages in your hand that say she wasn't taken and she didn't break and she didn't run out. She looked at what they were making her into and she said no, and she paid for the no herself, and the door stayed shut for twenty years because of it."

Iuno had gone very still.

"That's an end," Lupa said. "That's a woman reaching an end. I've been terrified of that stamp since I was sixteen years old and it turns out the woman under it *finished.*"

---

### ix.

They did the paperwork at the table with two pens because Iuno's writing hand is the only hand and the notary's copy needed a witness signature in a different ink.

*DEPOSITED BY:* Iuno Caecilia Severa, formerly High Priestess of Septimont. *Hometown:* Septimont, fourth quarter, Temple precinct.

*COUNTERSIGNED:* Lupa. *Guild no.* 0114. *Hometown:* Caldara.

"You've never put your number on anything."

"I've never put my name on anything I couldn't leave," said Lupa, and blotted it.

---

### x.

Later, at the door, with the envelope sealed and Ciaccona still audibly in the corridor pretending to tune:

"Mya said something to me," Lupa said, "at the end, after you'd said your piece and gone."

Iuno stopped.

"She asked me whether you were different now."

The vents cycled.

"And I said yes," said Lupa. "I said it fast, before I'd thought about it, and I've been annoyed about that for months, and I'm telling you because you're going to find out one day from somebody else and you'll build a whole cathedral on it, so here it is, plain, from me, with the qualifiers attached." She put her hand on the door frame. "I said yes because it's true and because she'd earned an accurate answer. It's still true. It doesn't get you anything."

Iuno stood in the kitchen with an envelope in her one hand.

"You're ours," Lupa said, from the doorway, in the voice she uses in rooms where saying it is binding. "That's the rule and I wrote it. It doesn't come with a pardon attached, and I want that in the record too."

"Noted," said Iuno.

"Say it back."

"...Noted, and entered, and I will not go looking for the pardon." A breath. "Thank you for the number."

"Don't thank me. Thanks is an ambush." Lupa went out. "There's soup. Two portions. The second one's taped shut so a certain someone remembers meals exist."

## II. The Verse Where the Joke Goes
### *Ciaccona and Chisa*

---

### i.

Ciaccona came up the service stair on her heels.

Chisa had the kettle on before the third landing.

"You've been talking to Lupa."

"I have been *observing* Lupa." Ciaccona arrived on the drying terrace with her lute on her back and a folio under one arm. "She's got a whole grammar. Heels for *I'm bringing something.* Toes for *I've eaten your food.* Two at a time for *someone's about to be counted.*"

"And the service stair at the fourth hour."

"That one I invented myself." She sat down on the reinforced crate. "It means I've come with a proposal and I'd like a run-up."

Chisa poured. The mug went across. She kept the other and put the third away, and neither of them mentioned the third.

"Go on, then," she said.

---

### ii.

The folio held forty pages of frequency captures and a sheaf of transcription in Ciaccona's fast hand.

"The Archive has forty hours of Honami survey audio," she said. "Iuno has the readings. I've had the frequencies since we came out, and I've been in them since the spring, an hour a night, and I want to tell you what's in there before I tell you what I want."

"Tell me."

"Ambient. Buildings. Weather that repeated for twenty years and got very good at it." Ciaccona turned a page. "The Sphere kept everything. That's the horror of the place and it's also the physics. Every sound made inside that boundary between the seal and the break is in that recording somewhere, layered, all at once, twenty years thick. Somewhere in there is a girl at a counter with a physics magazine turning pages. Somewhere in there is every single morning."

Chisa's hands went flat on her knees.

"You can't separate them."

"I can separate about four percent of it and the four percent is doors." Ciaccona set the folio down. "The instrument's honest. You get texture. You get weather. You get, on one afternoon in what I think is the eleventh year, ninety seconds of somebody dragging a chair. A voice in a twenty-year stack is one thread in a rope, and I have spent four months learning that at length."

"So it's the sea."

"It's the sea." Ciaccona picked her cup up. "Total, loveless, keeps everything, gives nothing back. I met that god at slack tide when I was twenty-five and I have now met it again in a data format."

---

### iii.

"Then here's the proposal, and I'd like you to let me get all the way to the end of it before you say no, because you're going to say no in about four seconds and I've written this out."

"Go."

"I want to write Sumika a song."

Chisa let it stand. The vents changed registers. Down the line a shirt cracked once in the warm draft.

"That was four seconds," said Ciaccona.

"It was three."

"Let me finish."

"Finish."

Ciaccona put her cup down and leaned forward with her elbows on her knees.

"You've got a hundred and fifty-one entries in her handwriting. You've got her arithmetic. You've got the shelves of stopped clocks and the winding and the setting and the delight when they were wrong by evening. You've got a girl who walked up a frozen tower alone and spent herself entry by entry laying a road out of her own bones so that somebody could be found, and who wrote *that's enough for me, I feel content* on the way down." Her voice had gone level, which was her voice being careful. "That is a whole person. That is more of a person than most of us leave. And the only place any of it lives is inside you, and you are one woman with a job that involves gods, and when you go, she goes."

"And your solution is a song."

"My solution is a song. Not because a song is nice. Because a song is the only container anyone has ever built that survives its owner." Ciaccona held up one hand. "Two hundred students in that Academy know three notes they learned at a memorial transmission and they have kept them for thirteen years. Nobody told them to. Nobody paid for it. That's the mechanism. It's the only one I've ever found that works past a lifetime and I've looked."

"And I sing it to you and you make her."

"You tell me what you've got and I set it and I leave the holes in and I put it in circulation and in forty years there's a version in a town neither of us has been to, and somebody's grandmother knows the second verse wrong, and *that's* the outcome I'm offering." Ciaccona sat back. "That's the whole proposal. That's the best thing I have and I'm putting it on the table."

---

### iv.

Chisa picked up her mug with both hands and looked out at the reservoir.

"No," she said.

"Right." Ciaccona breathed out. "Tell me the shape of it."

"You'll be good at it."

"I'll be very good at it."

"That's the problem." Chisa turned her head. "In three years there'll be a Sumika in two hundred people. She'll have a laugh you've decided on and a way of standing you've inferred from an entry about clocks and one strong image of a girl on a stair. And every single bit of it will be defensible and most of it will probably be close, and all of it will be *yours.*"

"That's what a song is."

"Yes."

The vents cycled twice.

"You gave your father to every drunk kitchen on two coasts," said Chisa.

"I did."

"So say the difference out loud, because you know it, and you've known it since you walked up my stairs on your heels, and you came anyway."

Ciaccona sat with that for a while.

"My father was one line," she said finally. "*One aboard. Wind fresh from the west. Singing.* That's the total surviving record. Everything anyone ever added to that man was addition. There was nothing there to overwrite. You cannot spend a dead man down when there's no principal left to touch."

"And her."

"And you've got a hundred and fifty-one entries and one hole in the middle of them."

"Two seconds," said Chisa. "Every morning for two months. A noise a machine made in the back of my teeth, and a girl waking up and saying something about it. The same thing, probably. A joke, probably. And the city took the sound of her saying it out of my head a night at a time while I slept, in even amounts, and left me everything around it."

She set the mug down on the concrete.

"You fill that," she said, "and it's a patch. And a patch on a hole that size holds. It'll hold beautifully. And in a year I'll reach for the shape of what's missing and my hand will land on something you wrote, and I'll know it's yours, and I'll go on reaching, and after a while I'll stop being able to tell where the seam is." Her voice stayed exactly where it always is. "The hole is the last accurate thing I have about her. It's got her shape in it. It's the only object in my possession that has never been touched by anybody's guess."

---

### v.

Ciaccona rubbed her thumb along the lute's old repaired seam, back and forth, once.

"There's a counter," she said.

"I know there's a counter. Make it."

"The hole dies with you too."

"Yes."

"So in fifty years there's a version of her that's mine and wrong and alive in six grandmothers, or there's nothing at all, and you're picking nothing at all, and you're picking it because it's *accurate.*" Ciaccona's eyes had gone bright. "Chisa, I have stood on the rocks under a fire tower and asked the sea for one voice and been given ten thousand at fortissimo with blood coming out of my ear, and the lesson I took off those rocks is that accuracy without a singer is a grave. Graves keep everything and give back nothing. That's what makes them graves. A man told me that while feeding me fish soup and he was right, and you are currently building one on a laundry roof."

"That's a good argument."

"It's the best one I've got."

"It's still no," said Chisa.

"I know." Ciaccona let out a breath. "You were going to say no when I came up the stairs. I came up the stairs anyway, because there's a third thing, and I couldn't get to the third thing without doing the first two properly."

---

### vi.

"There's a version where I write about the *noise*," she said.

Chisa turned her head.

"Not her. The hum. Two seconds at six in a city where every other clock had stopped, going off every morning for twenty years and reporting to nobody." Ciaccona had her hand flat on the soundboard. "That's a whole song and it's a good one and I've been hearing it since the night you told Lupa about it on this roof, which she reported to me in about four sentences, which is Lupa's version of an aria."

"She had no business—"

"She had every business, she's Lupa, she filed a fire report." Ciaccona waved it off. "The song's about a machine that kept an appointment for twenty years with nobody on the other end of the wire. That's the whole shape. It's the best thing I've had in a year and it belongs to you and I would like your permission."

Chisa was quiet a long time.

"And Sumika?"

"Sumika's in the second verse," said Ciaccona. "Because somebody woke up to it. That's a fact and it's yours and I'll set it plain. *And a girl woke up to it, and every morning she said —*"

She stopped.

"And then a rest," she said. "A written one. Notated. Two beats of measured silence in the middle of a verse, marked, so that everybody who plays it knows there's a thing missing and knows that they don't know it. Not a hole for mending. A hole with a fence round it."

---

The false stars ticked through their wrong arrangements.

"You'd have to teach people to leave it alone," said Chisa.

"I'd have to teach people to leave it alone, and about a third of them will fill it anyway, and I can't stop that, and I'd be lying to you if I said I could." Ciaccona met her eyes. "Here's what I can do. I can put the fence in the notation. I can teach it to two hundred students with the rest in it and the reason for the rest attached. And after that it's out of my hands, which is the entire point of my trade and the price of it, and I've never once pretended the price was small."

Chisa picked up her mug, found it empty, and got up and put the kettle back on.

"That," she said, with her back turned, "is the first proposal anyone has made me in twenty-five years that had the cost written on the front of it."

"I've had practice."

"Do it."

---

### vii.

She taught it in the spring, in the small auditorium, on channel 9's old equipment, to a hundred and ninety people who had come because the broadcast club had put a notice up and forty more who had come because Lynae had painted the notice.

It went the way songs go. It got out of the building inside a month.

The rest held for about eleven weeks.

---

### viii.

Chisa was crossing the east concourse in the fourth week of summer when she heard it coming out of a stairwell — two first-years, badly, in unison, laughing at each other in the places where the harmony went wrong.

*and a girl woke up to it, and every morning she said —*

And one of them sang, into the fence:

*— "there's the mountain, right on time."*

Chisa stopped in the middle of the concourse.

The two of them went past her without looking up, arguing about the third line, and got about nine meters down the corridor before one of them said *that's not the words, there's a gap there, the gap's on purpose,* and the other one said *well it's better with words,* and the first one said *it's a rest, it's marked, my sister's in the club, they're really strict about it,* and their voices went round the corner.

Chisa stood in the concourse with her hands at her sides.

*There's the mountain, right on time.*

It was wrong. That was the first thing and it arrived complete and from nowhere, with the force of a hand on a shoulder: it was wrong, it was the wrong shape, it had the wrong number of things in it, the rhythm of it went up where the real one came down, and it had been made by a seventeen-year-old in a stairwell who had never stood in a kitchen in a stopped city at six in the morning.

And it was wrong *against something.*

She stood there for a long time working out what had just happened to her, which was this: for twenty-five years she had reached into a hole and found a hole. And a stranger had put a bad guess in it, and her whole body had said *no, that isn't it* — instantly, without consultation, from below the level where anybody gets a vote.

Which meant the right one was still in there.

Under everything. Under twenty years of a city taking her a night at a time. Too deep to fetch and near enough to argue with.

### ix.

She found Ciaccona on the terrace at the fourth hour and sat down on the second crate without being asked, which between them was a whole negotiated treaty.

"Somebody filled it."

Ciaccona's hands stopped on the strings.

"...Ah," she said. "Right. How bad."

"'There's the mountain, right on time.'"

"That's terrible."

"It's *appalling.*"

"There isn't a mountain, that's — is there a mountain? Was there a mountain?"

"There was a hill and it was a municipal embarrassment." Chisa took the cup that was already being poured. "The metre's wrong. It goes up. She never went up at six in the morning. Nobody goes up at six in the morning."

Ciaccona looked at her for a moment. Then she set the lute down very carefully, which she almost never does, and put both hands round her cup.

"Say that again," she said.

"Which part."

"*She never went up at six in the morning.*"

Chisa opened her mouth.

Closed it.

"I've had that for twenty-five years," Chisa said slowly, "and I could not have told you it was there ten minutes ago."

"You couldn't have told me. You could recognise it." Ciaccona's voice had gone quiet. "That's a different faculty. That's the one that survives. You know how I know? Because a woman in a tavern in a grain town heard me play a lullaby badly and stood up and said *wrong* before she could have told you a single word of it."

"Fiora."

"Fiora." Ciaccona picked her cup back up. "Nobody in that room could have recited the verse. Six of them could tell me it was wrong inside a bar and a half. Wrongness is stored somewhere else. It's cheaper to keep and it lasts longer and it's the last thing to go."

Chisa held her tea.

"So the fence," she said.

"The fence held for eleven weeks and then a seventeen-year-old put a mountain in it, and I'm going to have to write about six letters about that." Ciaccona shrugged. "And now you've got something you can argue with, which you have not had since the seal broke. So I'd call that a bad week for the fence and a good one for you, and I'd like it on the record that I take no credit for any of it, because I planned exactly none of this and the alternative was a grave."

"You'll take the credit in the notebook."

"I'll take *some* of the credit in the notebook," said Ciaccona, "and I'll draw a star next to it, and the star's my father's, so it's already a fraud."

They sat.

"Cia."

"Mm."

"Leave the fence in," said Chisa. "Write your six letters. Leave it in anyway."

"Forever?"

"Forever." She turned her mug a quarter-turn. "Somebody has to keep saying there's a thing missing. Otherwise in forty years there's just a mountain."

## III. Weather Continues
### *Mei and Cartethyia*

---

### i.

"She's fine," said Mei, on the tram.

"Mm."

"That's the thing I don't like."

"Mm," said Cartethyia, who was upside down on the seat with her boots on the window and her hair on the floor, because the eleven-forty runs empty and she had been raised badly.

"Her marks went up."

"Mm."

"Her *marks went up,* Cartethyia."

"I heard you the first time. I was letting it sit there so you could hear it too." Cartethyia rotated herself the right way round in one motion that gave a tram inspector at the far end something to think about. "You want to go in."

"I want to go in."

"And I want to turn up."

"Those are the same thing."

"They are so wildly the same thing," said Cartethyia, "that we've been arguing about it since the Dimmr Plains and neither of us has ever once won."

---

### ii.

The facts, as the pack had them.

Sigrika Vantus, third year, Voidmatter Reverse Engineering, moderator of a student megathread with eleven thousand subscribers and a reputation for being three steps ahead of the room. In the winter she had watched a miracle come home through a cracked phone screen. In the same week she had learned that one of her two closest friends had been manufactured, hung on strings, and cut free of them at the bottom of the world, and that the other one had walked away into a crowd wearing a volunteer's vest.

Three months on she was attending everything. She had returned to the thread in the second week and had run it, by common report, better than before. Her tutors described her as steady. Lucilla's word, delivered flatly in a corridor to two people who had asked, was *impressive.*

"Nobody's impressive at nineteen three months after that," said Mei.

"Somebody might be."

"Nobody is."

"Somebody might be, and we'd look extremely stupid, and I'd enjoy it," said Cartethyia, "and I agree with you."

---

### iii.

Mei went in on a Tuesday.

She did it well. That has to be said, because it matters to what came after: she did it the way she does everything, which is early, gently, and as though she had been standing in that spot since yesterday. She found Sigrika in the west reading room at the hour when it empties. She sat down across the table. She said the girl's name once and waited. She kept the dead out of the first minute.

"I'm going to say a thing and you can throw me out afterward."

"Okay."

"I've been where you are."

"I know," said Sigrika.

"I woke up on a beach with nothing and it took me two years and about nine people to work out I was allowed to be angry about it, and every single one of those two years I was *fine.*" Mei put her hands on the table. "I ran the whole thing. Marks, jokes, showing up early. I'd have told you I was coping, and I'd have believed it, and about eleven months in I nearly walked onto a boat by myself at midnight because somebody offered me answers."

Sigrika looked at her steadily.

"Did you go?"

"I got as far as the doorknob."

"What stopped you?"

"A wolf bent a fork ninety degrees at lunch the next day and told me she'd tie me to the chair."

"Mm," said Sigrika. And then, after a moment: "Thank you. That's a good story. I mean that."

"But."

"But I've read the interviews, and I've read the thread, and I've read the thing Ciaccona wrote for the memorial, and about four hundred people have now come to me with a very good story about their own thing." Sigrika's voice was perfectly pleasant. "You're the fourteenth this month who has been where I am. Two of them were faculty. One of them was a man whose cat died."

Mei sat with that.

"Fair."

"I'm not being cruel."

"You're not being cruel, you're being *accurate*, which is worse and I'd do the same." Mei rubbed her face. "Okay. Different question. What do you need?"

"That's the second one."

"Second what?"

"Second most common question." Sigrika started stacking her notes. "Everybody wants a list. It's the kindest thing people know how to do and it's an *invoice*, and I have to fill it in, and then I have to receive whatever's on it and be visibly better afterward or the person who brought it feels bad." She squared the stack. "So I say *time,* and everyone nods, and it works out for both of us."

"Sigrika—"

"You jumped into the Nihility after your daughter," said Sigrika, and it came out level and it landed like a dropped plate. "In front of two hundred people. On a livestream. Everybody at this Academy has watched a woman go into the dark after somebody she loved and come out with them, and I have watched it *forty times,* and I want you to understand that it is the single most useful and single most unbearable thing that has ever happened to me."

The reading room hummed.

"Because you can't do it for me," she said. "There's nowhere to jump. There's a girl who never existed and a girl who left, and both of them went in a direction you can't follow, and I have run the whole search and I was extremely thorough about it."

Mei sat very still.

"Yeah," she said. "Okay."

"I've upset you."

"You've been right at me, which I've earned." Mei stood. She got as far as the door and stopped. "One thing and then I'll go. That thing you just did — where you named the fourteen people and the invoice and the two hundred and the forty times."

"What about it?"

"It's *load-bearing,*" said Mei. "You're holding the whole building up with it and you've been doing it for three months and you're brilliant at it. I did the same trick for two years." She put a hand on the frame. "It gets very tiring around month five."

Sigrika's pen stopped.

Then it started again.

"Noted," she said.

---

### iv.

"How'd it go," said Cartethyia, on the steps, with a paper bag.

"She had me on the floor in about nine minutes."

"Good."

"*Good?*"

"She's a moderator of a megathread with eleven thousand people on it," said Cartethyia. "She has spent three years watching strangers try to manage each other in public. She can read a rescue at four hundred meters. Of course she had you on the floor, you came in *shaped like a rescue.*"

"I came in shaped like a person who's been there."

"You came in shaped like a *leap,*" said Cartethyia. "You always do. It's your whole engine and it's a magnificent one and about a third of the time it works instantly and forever, and this is not that third."

Mei sat down on the steps.

"So what's yours."

"Sixth bell," said Cartethyia. "Every day."

"For how long?"

"Until it stops being necessary or I die."

"That's not a plan, that's *erosion.*"

"Yes," said Cartethyia, brightly. "Have a bun."

---

### v.

The way Cartethyia did it, as reconstructed afterward from a stand-keeper, two porters, and a bad-tempered tram inspector:

**Week one.** She turned up at the arcade on the Ward Three concourse at the hour when the third-years go, and she was terrible at every single machine, and she was loudly terrible, and on the fourth evening a girl with a coffee said, from two machines down, "your left hand is doing nothing," and Cartethyia said "my left hand has never once been consulted," and that was the whole of the interaction.

**Week two.** Same hour. Cartethyia lost forty-one shells to a claw machine in a manner that drew a crowd.

**Week three.** Sigrika beat the machine on her second attempt without appearing to look at it and handed Cartethyia the plush and walked off.

**Week four.** Sigrika arrived at the same hour on her own initiative, established the fact by sitting down at the machine next to Cartethyia's, and let it stand, and so did Cartethyia.

**Week six.** They went to noodles. Sigrika talked about the thread for forty minutes and the moderation queue and a dispute about a user who kept reposting the livestream, and Cartethyia asked eleven questions about the dispute and none about anything else.

**Week nine.** Sigrika said, halfway through a bowl, without preamble, "she liked this place," and then went on with the sentence she had been in the middle of, and Cartethyia let her.

**Week eleven.** Sigrika said, "You know what you're doing, right?"

"Yep."

"Just checking."

"Do you want me to stop?"

"...No."

"Sixth bell," said Cartethyia, and went to lose to the claw machine.

---

### vi.

The argument happened on the roof at the end of the third month and it was the worst either of them had ever had, which was survivable, because between the two of them they had approximately one temper.

"She's *lonelier,*" said Mei.

"She's tireder. That's different and it's the direction we want."

"You've been feeding her a routine for eleven weeks—"

"I have been *keeping an appointment* for eleven weeks—"

"—and she's got somebody to lose to at a claw machine and nobody to say Denia's name to."

"She said it," said Cartethyia.

Mei stopped.

"Week nine. Noodles. Halfway through a sentence about something else." Cartethyia had her arms folded and her chin up. "She said *she liked this place* and then went straight on, and I let her go straight on, and that is the single most important thing that has happened to that girl in three months and it happened *because* there was somewhere for it to fall out."

"That's an accident."

"That's *weather,*" said Cartethyia. "It's the whole method. You stand in the same spot at the same hour for long enough and eventually a person is standing next to you on a day when the thing comes up, and it comes up sideways, in the middle of a sentence about noodles, because that is how it actually comes up in the lives of real people, and it will not come up in a reading room in front of a woman who has arranged her face."

"So my way is worthless."

"Your way pulled a girl out of the bottom of the void."

"That was—"

"That was *the third of the time,*" said Cartethyia. "I've watched you do it for two years. You go over the edge with people. It's the finest thing about you and I have personally been the beneficiary twice, and here is the whole of my objection, and I've been saving it."

"Go on."

"Somebody has to be there when they land."

Mei went quiet.

"You jump," Cartethyia said. "You get to the bottom with them, which is the part nobody else on this continent can do, and it saves their life, and then the next morning they wake up in a body in a city and there's a Tuesday to get through, and there are about four thousand Tuesdays after it." She spread her hands. "You cannot jump four thousand times. Nobody can. That's the thing the ballads leave out, and it's the whole of my trade, and I learned it in a tower where absolutely nothing happened for twenty years."

---

### vii.

The vents cycled. Below them the reservoir did what it does.

"Cadanto used to correct me," Cartethyia said. "Two degrees at a time. I asked him once what he was actually teaching, because I was twelve and insufferable, and he said, *you fight like a story.*"

"That sounds like a compliment."

"That's what I said." She snorted. "And he said, *stories have endings. Fight like weather instead. Weather continues.*"

"And you've spent thirty years being weather."

"I've spent thirty years being *told* to be weather and being a story anyway and paying for it, and then I spent twenty in a folded tower doing nothing at a hundred names and finding out he'd been right about everything since I was ten." Cartethyia leaned on the rail. "I broke furniture in a god's house last year. I'm not the patient one. I've got a *technique* for patience and I built it out of desperation in a room with no exits and I use it like a crutch, and it works, and I'd throw it in the reservoir tomorrow for one afternoon of being able to fix something in an hour the way you do."

Mei looked at her.

"You want to jump."

"I want to jump *constantly.*" Cartethyia's laurel caught the false light. "Every single sixth bell. Every time she says something sideways and I have to let it go past. There is a version of me that goes into that arcade and takes hold of that girl and says *your friend was real, I was grown in a pod for a purpose by a thing that meant me harm and I am standing here with honey cake in my hair, made things are real, made things are the realest things there are* — and it would be true, and it would be the single most self-indulgent thing I have ever done, and it would be *mine.*"

"Why is that self-indulgent."

"Because she'd have to receive it." Cartethyia's voice dropped. "That's the whole of it. It's a magnificent gift and she'd be holding it, and the next thing after holding a gift is thanking somebody for it, and she's got fourteen people a month to thank already."

---

### viii.

"So how does it end," said Mei.

"It doesn't. That's the definition."

"Cartethyia."

"Fine." She thought about it properly, which she does, and which people miss because of the volume. "It ends when she brings something to the arcade instead of finding it there. When there's a Tuesday she turns up to say a thing and I'm the fixture she says it at. That's the whole of the outcome and it might be two years away and it might have happened in week nine and I'll find out in about a decade."

"And if she goes under before then?"

"Then I've got it wrong and you'll have been right and I will hear about it forever." Cartethyia turned around and put her back against the rail. "Which is why I want yours in the arrangement."

Mei looked up.

"Say that again."

"You're the fastest reader of a falling person alive, and I am a windmill, and windmills are famously bad at emergencies." Cartethyia folded her arms. "You take the edges. You watch for the doorknob at midnight — you'd see it a week out, you saw it in yourself and you were *inside* it. And I take the four thousand Tuesdays, because I've got the temperament and the tower training and nowhere else to be."

"That's two protocols on one person."

"That's a roof and a floor," said Cartethyia. "The girl's not a case. She's got a life going. She'll take what she takes."

---

### ix.

Sigrika worked it out in the second week of autumn, which was later than she would have liked, and she said so.

"You two are running two different protocols on me," she said, over noodles, to the knight who had just lost eleven shells to a claw machine on purpose. "And I don't think you told each other for about three months."

Cartethyia put her chopsticks down.

"We told each other," she said. "Eventually. On a roof. There was shouting."

Mei looked at her.

"Because you were fine," she said, "and because somebody smarter than me told me that turning up is the whole job and that going in is a thing you get to do about once."

Sigrika considered this. Then she went back to her bowl.

"Okay," she said. "Sixth bell's fine. Keep doing it. I'm going to keep pretending I don't notice, because it's easier for me and I think it's easier for her." ## IV. Would You Do It Again
### *Amy and Iuno*

---

### i.

Three in the morning is a shift, in a city where the sun is a committee.

Iuno takes it because thirty years of an office got into her spine and never came out. Amy takes it because she spent thirteen years on a clock that ran at a different speed and has never entirely renegotiated.

They overlap at the kitchen table two or three nights a week, and the arrangement has rules, all of them unwritten, all of them observed. Iuno makes the pot. Amy does the toast. Conversation is permitted and never required. Whoever goes up first turns the lamp down rather than off, because Mei comes through at some point most nights on her way to check a door that is already locked.

The first six weeks of it were silent.

The seventh week, Amy said, "You do the thing where you look at the door before you look at people," and Iuno said, "I do," and Amy said, "Ma does it too now, she caught it off you, it's like a *cold*," and that was the beginning of the whole business.

---

### ii.

"Can I ask you a rude one," said Amy.

Iuno turned the cup a quarter-turn.

"You've been holding it for a fortnight."

"Eleven days."

"You've been coming down at three for eleven days and eating toast you don't want."

"That's slander, I love toast," said Amy. "Fine. Eleven days."

The kettle ticked as it cooled.

"Go on, then."

Amy put both hands round her mug.

"Would you do it again?"

---

### iii.

Iuno sat with it.

She has an answer for most things inside a second and a half; the pause was the answer arriving, and Amy, who is her mother's daughter and reads a room like a schematic, sat still through all of it.

"That question has three versions," Iuno said. "Ask me the one you want."

"There's three?"

"There's *the plan* — would I again build a person for the purpose of spending her. There's *the precipice* — would I again go over the edge to keep her from going alone. And there's the one underneath both, which is whether the arithmetic that produced the first one is still in me." Iuno's hand went flat on the table. "The first two are easy and I'll answer them for free. It's the third one you came down eleven nights for."

"...Yeah."

"Ask it properly."

Amy took a breath.

"If it came round again," she said. "Tomorrow. Somebody has to be spent, and the numbers say who, and the numbers say her — would the woman doing the sums still be you?"

"Yes," said Iuno.

---

The vents cycled. Down the corridor somebody's door settled.

"Okay," said Amy.

"You wanted a flinch."

"I wanted a *hesitation.*"

"I've had thirty years of hesitation and I turned every single one of them into a plan, so I've stopped." Iuno picked her cup back up. "Here's the whole of it and then you can throw the pot. The arithmetic is a faculty. It's the same faculty that reads a room in eleven seconds and gets everybody out of a building, and it does not have an off switch, and anyone who tells you they have removed theirs is lying or has never had one. It runs. It ran this morning at the tram junction on a hundred and forty people and I could tell you the four who would go first if the shaft failed."

"That's horrifying."

"It's *useful*, and it's horrifying, and both of those are load-bearing." Iuno set the cup down. "What changed is what I do with the output. For thirty years the sum came out and I treated it as an instruction. Now it comes out and I treat it as *information*, and I take it to a table with eight people at it, and one of them bends a fork."

"That's a procedure."

"That is *entirely* a procedure. I am a woman made of procedure. I tried being made of feeling for about four months after the precipice and I was completely useless and everybody was very kind about it."

---

### iv.

Amy ate a corner of the toast she loved.

"Now do me," she said.

"You've already done yourself. You've been doing yourself for eleven days at three in the morning, which is the worst possible hour to audit anybody." Iuno tilted her head. "Say it out loud anyway. That's the deal."

"Fine." Amy set the mug down. "I'd do it again."

"Which part."

"All of it." She said it steadily. "The chair. The sync. Ninety-nine point six. Mornye's hand on the override and me making her promise not to pull it. If a city with thirty thousand people in it was leaning on a door and I was the only one whose numbers worked — yes. Tomorrow. Without the pause you wanted from you."

"Good."

Amy's head came up.

"*Good?*"

"Yes."

"That's the worst possible response—"

"It's an accurate one." Iuno turned the cup. "You are describing a person volunteering herself, with full information, for a thing she is uniquely able to do, in a place she grew up in. There is a word for that and the word is not pathology. If you'd like me to be worried about you I will be, and I am, but the worry isn't about the answer."

"Then what's it about?"

"It's about the sentence that comes after it," said Iuno.

Amy looked at her.

"Say the sentence after it," Iuno said.

"...I don't have one."

"You have one. You've had one since you were seven and you've never once said it in a room with the lights on."

Amy sat very still.

The lamp was down, the way it is at that hour. Two floors up, a woman who catches things was asleep with one hand shut around a fistful of somebody's sleeve.

"*Don't worry about me,*" Amy said.

---

### v.

"There it is."

"That's not the—"

"That's the whole thing." Iuno's voice stayed exactly level, which was the kindness in it. "*I'd do it again* is a position. It's arguable. People have held it for good reasons since there have been people. *I'd do it again, don't worry about me* is a two-part instrument, and the second half is where the damage lives, and I know the model because I built one myself out of different parts."

"It's not the same thing."

"It's not remotely the same thing. Say the difference; you're the one who's been chewing it."

Amy pushed her mug an inch away from herself.

"You spent somebody else," she said.

"I did."

"I spent me."

"You did."

"So it's *mine.* It's the one thing that's actually mine. Nobody asked the clay in the story and in my version the clay read the projections and put its own hand up, and I've been very proud of that, and I've said it in about four different rooms like it settles the matter." Amy's edges had gone soft; she noticed and put her hand on the table until they came back. "And it settles *nothing,* because Ma walked out a blue door with her own hand up, on her own, for her own reasons, and it was hers, and it was mine too, and I was seven and I stood at a window and watched the snow take her coat."

The kettle ticked.

"And I did it to her," Amy said. "Exactly the same. Down to the sentence. I sat in a dorm room with a professor who loved me and I made her *promise* not to save me, and I did it with my mother's smile on my face, the one that's engineered to make the person left behind feel like the leaving is strength, and I've had thirteen years to look at that from the inside of a sun and it is the cruellest thing I have ever done to anyone."

Iuno let it sit for exactly as long as it needed.

"Yes," she said.

"You could argue with me."

"You'd win." Iuno turned the cup. "And you'd be arguing about the wrong thing, which is whether you were right, and you *were* right; the gate held; there are thirty thousand people in this city who got a Tuesday out of it. Being right is available to almost anybody. What you are actually sitting here at three in the morning about is that you were right and you did it in private."

---

### vi.

"That's your whole diagnosis," said Amy. "Everything's a filing problem."

"Everything I have ever done badly has been a filing problem."

"That's a *bit.*"

"It's a bit and it's true and I'm too tired to separate them at this hour." Iuno's mouth did something. "Consider the two of us. I did the arithmetic and I did not say it out loud, for thirty years, to anybody, and it produced a woman who pulled a girl out of the sea in order to spend her. You did the arithmetic and you did not say it out loud, and you signed the forms alone, and you left a fire laid and a match beside it and a save file at a door, and you told everyone at the lunch table that the special research posting was going great. Neither of us was stopped. Do you know why?"

"Because nobody could."

"Because nobody was *given the chance.*" Iuno set the cup down. "That's the whole of it. Mornye filed six objections and had every one of them come back stamped, and then a girl of nineteen sat on a bed and made her promise, and she kept the promise, and she has been running the numbers looking for the decimal place ever since. Augusta was told the day before it was too late and she said *I won't allow this* and it was already running. Both of them would have stopped us. Both of them were handed a fact at the point where the only remaining option was grief."

Amy had her hands flat on the table.

"So what," she said. "A rule?"

"A rule," said Iuno.

---

### vii.

She wrote it on the back of a Guild circular, because that is where she writes things, and it took her four attempts, and Amy read each one and said *no* three times, which Iuno received with visible enjoyment.

The fourth one read:

> *HOUSEHOLD STANDING ORDER, THE FIRST*
>
> *Any member of this house who is about to spend herself says so at the table, out loud, before the fact.*
>
> *Permission is unavailable here and would be an insult. This is a notice. It is entered, it stands, and it binds the speaker in one respect only, which is that she has to say it.*
>
> *The table's obligations are two. To hear it. And to be given the chance to argue, which it may lose.*
>
> *Drafted by I. Countersigned by A.*
>
> *The house is invited to amend. The house is expected to amend loudly.*

"'Which it may lose,'" said Amy.

"That clause cost me the whole fourth draft."

"You have to have it."

"I have to have it. Without it it's a veto, and a veto is a leash, and I have worn one and issued about nine hundred and I have opinions." Iuno blotted it. "With it, it's a record. It says: this happened in front of people who were allowed to disagree. That is the entire difference between what you did to Mornye and what a person is permitted to do to the people who love her."

Amy took the pen and signed under it. Her handwriting is round and young and trying hard and has been since she was seven.

"It won't stop anybody," she said.

"It won't stop anybody. It'll cost them about four minutes and a very bad conversation." Iuno capped the pen. "Four minutes is what Mornye didn't get. Four minutes is what Augusta didn't get. It is the smallest possible intervention and it is the only one I have ever seen work, and I have looked at this problem for thirty years with professional equipment."

---

### viii.

The stair creaked at the eleventh step, which it always does, which is why nobody in that house has ever successfully arrived anywhere unannounced.

Mei came into the kitchen in a shirt that was Lupa's, with her hair doing what it does, and looked at the pot and the two mugs and the circular on the table with two signatures on it.

"You're both awake."

"We are," said Iuno.

"It's three."

"It's ten past."

Mei got a glass and filled it and drank about half of it standing at the sink, and then turned around and leaned there with her arms folded, and waited, because she has learned some things.

Iuno looked at Amy.

Amy looked at Iuno.

"Right," said Amy, and pushed the circular across the table. "So. Household standing order. Read it."

Mei read it.

She read it twice, actually, and put the glass down, and read it a third time, and by then her face had done the thing it does when something arrives sideways and lands somewhere old.

"Who drafted it?"

"Iuno."

"Who signed it?"

"Both of us."

"When?"

"About ninety seconds ago," said Iuno.

Mei looked from one of them to the other, at three in the morning, in a kitchen inside a god, at the two people in her life who had each independently decided to be the sum.

"Okay," she said. "I've got an amendment."

"The house is expected to amend loudly," said Amy.

"It's not loud. It's one word." Mei took the pen. "It says *any member of this house.* Take the *house* out and put *pack.*"

"That's more people."

"That's *nine* people and a knight who'll amend it further at breakfast, and a wolf who'll want a numbering system, and a bard who's going to set it to music out of pure spite." Mei crossed a word out and wrote a word in. "And a girl who'll say the whole thing's a filing problem."

"That's what I said," said Iuno.

"I know what you said. I heard you through the floor." Mei blotted it and put the pen down and picked her glass back up. "It's a good rule. Go to bed. Both of you."

She went.

At the door she stopped, with her back to the room, in the way she does when the thing has caught up with her.

"For the record," she said, "since apparently we do that now."

"Go on."

"I'd have argued." Her voice came out very even. "Both times. I'd have lost both times, probably. I'd have argued anyway."

"Noted," said Iuno.

"Entered," said Amy.

## V. Appeal Pending
### *Lupa and Amy*

---

### i.

The memorial garden sits on the third tier under a stand of engineered plum, and it is small, and it is very well kept, and about four hundred people a year come and stand in it.

The statue is nine feet of pale composite with a cape.

"It's the cape," said Amy.

"Mm."

"He had to pick the colour on his own. There's an interview. He said the family — " she made a face — "the *family and associates* had no record of a preferred palette, so he went with the memorial standard and then took it two shades warmer on his own authority, and he says in the interview he still thinks about it."

Lupa stood with her arms folded and looked up at it the way you look at a thing you have professional views on.

"It's a good cape."

"It's a *great* cape."

"It's better than anything I ever got put in." Lupa's ears rotated. "I had a forty-foot face on the eastern granary drinking a soda I'd never tasted. There was a noodle brand with my tail as the mascot. There were bathhouses."

"Bathhouses."

"A chain."

"Did you get a cut?"

"Clause four," said Lupa. "Likeness and name, all public and commercial uses, at the pleasure of the Domina. I got boots."

---

### ii.

The broadcast club had asked on a Tuesday, formally, in writing, with three signatures, because they had done their homework.

*Ward 734 / Fleet Snowfluff — proposal for relaunch of ch. 9.*

*Requesting: (a) permission to reactivate the equipment-test band as a licensed student channel; (b) the use of the name; (c) if she wants it, and only if she wants it, a slot.*

*We are aware that the name has been in public use for thirteen years without her knowledge or consent, including on merchandise sold by parties unaffiliated with this club, and that a legal position on this exists which everyone has been careful to leave untested. We are asking anyway. Please tell us to go away if you'd like.*

*The club would also like to state, since the record is silent on it, that channel 9 was hers and we have all been squatting.*

Amy had read it four times and then gone and sat in the memorial garden, which was a thing she had done twice before and had never told anyone about, and had been found there inside twenty minutes by a woman with a nose.

---

### iii.

"I want it taken down," said Amy.

"Right."

"I'm *here.* I'm standing next to it. There's a nineteen-year-old with a hole in her arm from a cannula and a house key round her neck and there's a nine-foot martyr looking heroically into the middle distance, and it's got a plaque with a *quotation* on it that I said to a professor in a stairwell."

"What's the quotation?"

"*I can hold it.*"

Lupa breathed out through her nose.

"Yeah," she said. "That'll do it."

"They put my worst sentence on a rock."

"They put your worst sentence on a rock and four hundred people a year come and read it and feel improved." Lupa looked up at the cape. "I know exactly what you're feeling and I'm going to tell you a thing you won't like."

"Go."

"I took mine down," said Lupa. "The wolf came off the Silva gate with a crane and two chains and I stood in the street and watched it and it was the best afternoon of my life, and I got it wrong."

---

### iv.

They sat on the bench, which is provided, and which is bolted down, and which has a small plaque of its own.

"Sixteen years I was a *what,*" said Lupa. "That's the word for it and I only found the word last winter off a book Iuno left in the kitchen deliberately, in the manner of a woman feeding a stray. A *what*. An asset with a designation. Wolf, Howl, The Silva Wolf, tail device. Every one of those was on a schedule and the schedule was filed at the Temple and I could have read it for a copying fee any day of sixteen years and I never did."

"But you were also—"

"I was also a person, and the person was in the *deeds.*" Lupa put her elbows on her knees. "That's the whole trick and it took me twenty years. Nobody can own what you actually did in front of witnesses. They owned the face. They could not own round three of the Ashen Pair, because fifty thousand people were in the building and every one of them saw a wolf refuse an opening she should have taken and get hit for it. That's a *who.* You can't licence it."

"So the statue's a what."

"The statue's a what and it's an *enormous* one and it's doing a job." Lupa's tail moved once on the flagstones. "There are people who come here. There's a woman who comes on the anniversary with lilies and she was in the third barrier crew that year and she has personally told two hundred first-years that the gate held. That's real. Her grief is real and it's load-bearing and it built half the safety culture in this city."

"I know."

"So if the crane comes, that goes."

Amy pulled her knees up onto the bench, which the bench has opinions about.

"So I'm stuck with it."

"You're not stuck with it. You've got the same problem I had and I want you to get further than I got." Lupa turned to face her. "Here's what I got wrong, and it took a girl with scissors on a laundry roof to make me see it. I took the wolf off the gate. I got the *what* out of somebody else's hands. And then I stood in the street with nothing on my paperwork at all, and the hometown field on my Guild form was blank for two years, and I told myself the blank was freedom."

"And it wasn't."

"It was a *vacancy,*" said Lupa, "and a vacancy gets filled by whoever's nearest, and what filled mine was the crowd. Removal's half a job. You've got to put something in."

---

### v.

"They want the name," said Amy.

"They asked nicely."

"They asked *beautifully,* they've got a paragraph about squatting, I nearly cried at a moderation form." Amy put her chin on her knees. "That's the other half of it and it's worse. She's a *what* too. Fleet Snowfluff. Except I built her."

"Go on."

"I was fourteen and I was talking into static every night saying goodnight to a woman who'd gone, and a boy on a homemade receiver said *who is this,* and I panicked, and out of the panic came a name." She said it flatly. "And she was perfect. She had my voice and none of my homework. Her story started after all the doors. She was born at midnight, age zero, free of forms, free of wards. People loved her on sight with zero waiting required."

"That's a good invention."

"It's a *great* invention. My best friend spent three years presenting evidence about Snowfluff's identity at a lunch table and the evidence was never me and it was the purest joy available on the market." Amy's mouth went sideways. "She got to be the version where Ma stayed. I let her have it. Somebody should."

Lupa was quiet for a moment.

"You built a *what* so you could have a break from being a *who,*" she said.

"...Yeah."

"That's the opposite of my thing."

"It's the exact opposite of your thing. Yours got taken and mine got *made,* on purpose, by me, at fourteen, at midnight, out of self-defence." Amy turned her head. "And it worked, and it's the only structure I ever built that never once let me down, and now three students have written me a letter asking permission to keep using it, and I have discovered that I would rather die than say yes and I would also rather die than say no, and I have been sitting in a memorial garden about it like an absolute clown."

"Yeah," said Lupa. "Welcome."

---

### vi.

She went and got two of the bad coffees from the stand at the tier gate and came back and put one in Amy's hand without asking whether she wanted it.

"Right," said Lupa. "Here's my professional opinion and I charge for this."

"You've never charged for anything in your life."

"I charge in kind and you're doing the washing-up for a fortnight." She sat back down. "The statue stays. The name goes to the club with terms. And you do the actual work, which is the boring one, and it's the only part that lasts."

"Which is."

"Paperwork."

Amy stared at her.

"Column nine," said Lupa.

---

### vii.

"There is a form," said Lupa, "and everybody in the world hates it, and the form is where the *who* lives, and I fought the Ephor's own drafters for a season over nine words on one and I would do it again with a broken hand."

"You're talking about the hometown thing."

"I'm talking about the hometown thing. Every fighter's terms on a public board at Olymdos and a column on the right-hand side that says where they came from, filled in by the athlete, in her own hand, blanks disallowed." Lupa's ears went flat and came up. "Caldara is six letters of light on a concourse in Septimont because I stood at a counter and spelled it for a clerk. It doesn't get me a thing. It's on the record and it's mine and the record's public and I put it there. That's the whole mechanism."

"And what's mine?"

"Yours has been sitting in an Academy file for eleven years," said Lupa, "and I have read it, because I'm on a board and boards can request things and nobody has ever once told me no."

She took a folded page out of her jacket.

Amy took it.

> [STARTORCH ACADEMY — FORM 7-C: TRANSFER OF GUARDIANSHIP (MINOR)]
> [WARD: **Aemeath**, __________]
> [FAMILY NAME: *field left blank. Annotation in the ward's handwriting:* "the cabin with the blue door, Roya Frostlands. ask the seals."]
> [SPONSOR OF RECORD: withheld under Privacy Provision 9(2)]
> [REMARKS: Ward insisted on retaining one (1) house key. Approved. Ward insisted the wisterias be listed as dependents. Denied. Ward appealed. **Appeal pending.**]

Amy looked at it for a long time.

"I was eight," she said.

"You were eight and you filed an appeal."

"I was *furious.* They'd taken everything into a folder and I wanted something of mine in there and the wisterias were the only thing I could think of that was still alive." Her voice had gone thin. "There was a woman with city boots and the frostlands ate her ankles the whole walk from the shuttle pad and she was very brave about it, and she kept calling me *the ward,* and I kept looking behind me."

"Appeal pending," said Lupa. "Eleven years."

"They never answered it."

"They never answered it because the officer who denied it retired in the spring of the year after, and the file went to storage, and there is currently no living person in that building who knows the appeal is open." Lupa took her coffee back off the arm of the bench. "Which means it's *open.*"

---

### viii.

It took four weeks, which Lupa described afterward as a scandal and which the registrar's office described afterward as the fastest anything had moved through that department since the sealing.

She did it the way she does things, which is logistics: she found the officer of record, who was seventy-one and living on the second tier and delighted to be consulted; she established that the appeal had been filed correctly, in crayon, with a supporting drawing; she obtained a statement from a professor of engineering to the effect that the specimens in question were extant, healthy, and had budded out of season for eleven consecutive years in defiance of the Lament's terms; and she stood in a registrar's office in a corridor with a tail and held her voice at conversational volume for four straight weeks.

The determination came back on a Thursday.

> [STARTORCH ACADEMY — FORM 7-C, APPEAL DETERMINATION (RETROSPECTIVE)]
> [RE: WARD 734, *Aemeath.*]
> [The appeal is **upheld.** The dependants schedule is amended to include: two (2) wisteria, Roya Frostlands, at the cabin with the blue door.]
> [The registrar notes for the record that this determination is eleven years and four months late, that the ward is now an adult and requires no guardian, and that the office is issuing it anyway because the appeal was properly filed and has never been withdrawn.]
> [The registrar further notes, in a personal capacity and outside the determination, that she has read the original appeal, and that the drawing was good.]

Amy read it at the kitchen table and put it down and put both hands flat on either side of it and stayed like that for about a minute and a half, and Cartethyia started to move and Lupa's hand came out sideways and stopped her without looking.

Then Amy said, "the drawing was *good,*" in a voice that came apart in the middle, and Mei got there first.

---

### ix.

The plaque took longer, because plaques are municipal and municipalities are municipal.

What went on it, in the end, under the nine feet of composite and the two-shades-warmer cape, was four lines.

> **AEMEATH.**
> *Of the cabin with the blue door, Roya Frostlands.*
> *Ward 734, Startorch Academy. Broadcaster, ch. 9.*
> *Dependants: two wisteria, extant.*

The quotation came off. That part was Lupa's, and she fought for it at a hearing, and her argument ran to one page and she read it out loud because the charter said *audibly.*

"It's a sentence a nineteen-year-old said to a woman who was trying to talk her out of it," she said, to a subcommittee of five. "It's the worst thing she ever said and it's the reason this city exists and both of those are true. Put it in the archive. Put it in the textbooks. Put it in the safety curriculum where it'll do some good." She put the page down. "Take it off the rock. Rocks are for what somebody was. That one's for what somebody did to herself in a room where nobody could argue."

The vote was four to one. The one abstained afterward and asked her a question in the corridor and she answered it and bought him a coffee.

---

### x.

Channel 9 relaunched in the autumn, licensed, with a slot on Thursdays at the twentieth hour, run by three students who had written a letter about squatting.

The first broadcast was theirs. That had been the condition, and Amy had been immovable about it, and the club had argued and lost.

The second one was hers.

She keyed the mic at midnight out of pure superstition, at the old hour, on the old band, with the club's new equipment and her own handheld rig sitting on the desk beside it, unplugged, present.

Down in the Third Ward a stand-keeper had the radio on because the manager preferred it. On the fourth tier a crew that reaps barley had a receiver in the shed. In a kitchen four floors up, a tape machine that a professor swears she leaves running by accident was, by accident, running.

"Evening," said Amy. "This is Aemeath. Some of you know me as Fleet Snowfluff — she's still on the roster, she's got a Thursday, she'll be in and out."

A pause, exactly long enough.

"Full name for the record, because somebody I live with has very strong opinions about records, and I've come round to her way of thinking."

Another one.

"Aemeath. Of the cabin with the blue door, Roya Frostlands. Ward 734."

Somewhere in a memorial garden on the third tier, four hundred people a year read four lines under a statue and one of them was about plants.

"Keep the radios on," she said. "Let's go."

# The Ninth Step
*A "Lyre, Speak to Me" side story.*

---

## 19:40

Iuno had booked it three weeks in advance, and it was, by every measure available, correct.

White cloths. Nine kinds of fork. A man at the door who took coats and gave you a small wooden number for them. Somebody playing something restrained in a corner on an instrument with too many strings.

They lasted an hour and ten minutes.

"There's no prices," said Cartethyia.

"That's the style."

"How does anyone *decide*?"

"You order what you want and it costs what it costs."

"That's a terrible system." Cartethyia turned the card over, found the back blank, and turned it again. "That's how you get people ordering the fish."

Lupa had taken the chair with its back to the wall and a sightline to both doors, and had refused the offer of a different one twice with escalating politeness. Her cutlery was in the order she wanted it, which took a waiter two attempts to stop correcting.

Amy had been reading the same line of the menu for eleven minutes.

"What's a *reduction*."

"It's a sauce."

"Why's it called that."

"Because they boiled it down."

"So it's less sauce." Amy set the card flat. "They boiled it and it got smaller and they put the smaller amount on a plate and charged you for the labor of removing it."

Ciaccona, who knew the sommelier, and had said so, and had made everything worse by saying so, put her chin on her hand.

"You're going to be insufferable all night."

"I'm going to be *right* all night."

Chisa ate four courses without comment and enjoyed all of them. Mei drank two glasses of something pale and expensive and told a story about a customs officer that was, at three separate points, physically impossible.

At 20:50, Iuno put her napkin down.

"This was a mistake."

"It's *lovely*—"

"Lupa has been on duty since we walked in and Amy has been conducting a war against a sauce." Iuno signalled for the bill without turning her head, which is a skill. "I picked it because it looked like the correct thing to do. That was the error."

She paid. Nobody saw the number.

Out on the street, in the wet neon of the upper concourse, six people stood around with the specific unmoored energy of an evening that had ended two hours early.

Lupa was doing up her coat.

"I know a place."

The whole group turned.

"*You* do," said Ciaccona.

"It's near the lower market."

"Eleven months in this city and you've got a *local*—"

"I know four," said Lupa, and started walking.

## 21:15

The Ninth Step was under the ninth step of a public stairwell in the lower market, which is how it got the name, and it had eleven seats and a low ceiling and a smell of hot oil and vinegar that hit the back of the throat like a fact.

Three things on the menu, chalked. Fried things. Stew. Pickles.

The woman behind the counter was about sixty and built like a door, and when Lupa came down the steps she looked up and said, "Same?"

"Same. Times seven."

"Seven."

"They're all mine."

Bo looked past her at the six people crowding into a room that fit eleven, and made a small noise of a woman recalculating her evening, and started putting things in oil.

Nobody sat down for about four seconds.

"*Same*," said Ciaccona.

"Don't."

"She said *same*. That's a *same*. That's years of same—"

"It's Thursdays."

"You come here on *Thursdays*—"

"Every week," said Lupa, sitting, "and I have a stew and I read the shipping notices and then I go home, and it's the best hour of my week, and now all of you know about it, so that's finished."

"That is *not* finished—"

"Sit down."

They sat down.

Amy leaned into her mother's shoulder.

"Ma. Lupa has a *hobby*."

"I heard."

"Lupa has a hobby and it's *reading shipping notices in a hole*."

"I heard, sweetheart."

---

Bo's stew arrived in seven bowls of four different sizes and was, without argument from anybody including Ciaccona, the best thing any of them had eaten in a month.

The drink came in a jug. Nobody asked what it was.

"So what *is* this," said Amy, four minutes later, holding her cup at arm's length.

"Rice, mostly," said Bo, from the counter.

"Mostly."

Bo went on cutting things.

Chisa drank hers, refilled it, and drank that one too, and folded her hands, and said, "That's the good stuff," in a voice of quiet professional recognition that made Ciaccona put her cup down and stare.

"Chisa."

"Mm."

"How much of that can you drink?"

"I don't know. Nobody's ever asked."

## 22:00

The gossip started at the third round, the way it does, sideways, through a complaint.

"Somebody's moving my mugs," said Mei.

"They're being washed," said Iuno.

"They're not finished."

"They're four days old."

"There's a *system*—"

"Name the system."

"...There's a system," said Mei, with dignity, "and the system is that I know where they are."

Chisa raised her cup two inches.

"I move them."

"*Chisa.*"

"They're on the counter edge. Somebody's going to knock one off and it'll go on the floor and I'll be the one who steps in it." She drank. "I move them two inches in. That's all I do."

"Two inches is a *lot*—"

"You've never noticed once."

Mei opened her mouth. Closed it.

"...Two inches?"

"Eleven months," said Chisa.

Lupa laughed into her bowl, which set Cartethyia off, which set Amy off, and by the time it went round the table Mei had put her forehead on the counter.

"The lute," said Iuno, into the wreckage, "goes in the hallway."

"The hallway's got the acoustic—"

"The hallway has a *width*, Ciaccona, and the lute takes forty percent of it, and Cartethyia has walked into it eleven times—"

"Nine," said Cartethyia.

"—and she has a bad hip and she's too polite to mention it, so I'm mentioning it."

Ciaccona looked at Cartethyia.

Cartethyia looked at her stew.

"...Nine?"

"It's fine. It's a nice lute."

"*Nine times*—"

"I said it's fine!"

"It's going in my room," said Ciaccona. "Tomorrow. It's going in my room, and you're going to tell me the next time I do something like that, and don't say it's fine, because you say it's fine about *everything*, you said it was fine when Lupa broke your—"

"It *was* fine—"

"You had a *splinter in your hand for a week*."

"It came out!"

Lupa raised one finger.

"I'd like it on record that I said sorry at the time."

"You said 'huh,'" said Amy.

"That's how I say it."

## 22:40

"Right," said Amy, banging her cup down. "Augusta."

Iuno went very still.

"What about her."

"You've had something since the towers came back. You've had a *face* about it." Amy pointed. "For a month. I've been watching."

"I have a duty of—"

"You're a private citizen with a household budget—"

"There's a wire," said Iuno.

The table stopped.

"...A what."

"A loop of copper wire. On her desk. She's been bending it and rebending it since the sunrise at the camp, which was six months ago, and the metal's gone soft at the curve." Iuno drank. "It's a ring gauge. She doesn't know Galbrena's size and she can't ask, because if she asks, Galbrena will know why she's asking."

The Ninth Step erupted.

"SIX MONTHS—"

"She fought a *Sovereign*—"

"She has led fifty thousand people into a tide," said Iuno, over the noise, "and she is being defeated by a *finger*."

"What did you tell her—"

"I told her to say it's for a gauntlet fitting."

"That's *lying*—"

"It's *staging*," said Iuno, and drained her cup, and put it down, and the table went up again.

Cartethyia had both hands over her mouth.

"Is she going to do it?"

"She'll do it badly and Galbrena will say yes before she's finished the sentence." Iuno pushed her cup toward the jug. "The woman writes forty percent weather in her reports. She's been in love since Ragunna and everyone in two cities knows except the one person the reports are addressed to."

---

The talk went sideways from there, as it does.

Zani and Phoebe took nine minutes, mostly Amy, mostly delighted. Chisa's mother's plan to move the table and the cabinet to fit seven people took six. Marit's four-year-old took four, and produced from Ciaccona a story about a child in Ragunna that Iuno tried twice to stop.

Then Cartethyia, four cups in and entirely lucid, said:

"Can I ask something and nobody make it a thing?"

"No."

"Cia—"

"Sorry. Go."

"How do you know if you *like* somebody." Cartethyia turned her cup on the counter. "Like that. I've read about it and it's all — the books are all fainting, and I don't faint, so either the books are wrong or I've got something missing, and I'd like to know which."

Nobody laughed, which Cartethyia noticed, and which made her sit up straighter.

"The books are wrong," said Chisa.

"How wrong."

"Completely. It's mostly wanting to tell them things." Chisa refilled. "You find something out during the day and there's a person you want to tell. Same person, every time. That's the whole of it."

"That's it?"

"That's the entire mechanism. Everything else is decoration."

Cartethyia thought about it for a while with total seriousness.

"...Huh," she said.

Amy leaned toward Ciaccona and said, at a volume that had aimed at confidential and missed, "Who's yours?"

Ciaccona laughed, and poured, and told a joke about a luthier in Egla, and it was a very good joke.

Chisa watched her tell it and drank and said nothing at all, and put a very small mark in a very large ledger.

## 23:30

"So," said Amy.

Her mother, next to her, said, "Mm," in the voice of a woman who had seen this coming for two hours.

"So who *was* that."

The Ninth Step got quieter. Bo, at the counter, went on cutting things.

Iuno set her cup down.

"Say the question properly."

"On Wednesday. Halfway through the fight. You took your glasses off and you turned into somebody else and she was *hilarious* and she called me Nyx Ravenhollow in a voice like a *knife* and I have not stopped thinking about it for five days." Amy put both elbows on the counter. "Who was that."

"That's the High Priestess."

"That's a *job title*."

"It's a job I did for thirty years, and somewhere in about the eleventh I stopped being able to tell where the job stopped." Iuno turned her cup a quarter-turn. "So she got a separate voice. Inside. She's the part of me that reads a room in four seconds and knows exactly which thing to say to get what I want, and she's *fast*, and she enjoys herself."

Lupa was watching her with her chin on her fist.

"She's the good one," Lupa said.

"She did the harm."

"And she's good at things." Lupa shrugged. "Both can be true. That's most people."

Iuno looked at her for a second longer than she meant to.

"She went into the water with me," she said. "At the precipice. All of it, the whole apparatus. And when I came back up I had her again, and I have spent a year keeping her occupied with a household budget, because the last time I let her run something without supervision, a great many people died."

"And Wednesday?"

"Wednesday she asked."

"She *asked*—"

"On Tuesday. While I was doing the initiative cards." Iuno drank. "She said, *let me do the fight, darling, you're going to bore them to death.*" A pause. "She was correct. I'd written the first encounter as a negotiation and this table would have adopted it inside four minutes."

The place went up. Ciaccona was hitting the counter.

"So you gave her the *animals*—"

"I gave her six rabid animals and a doorway and she was *very happy*," said Iuno.

"She rolled everything in the open," said Chisa.

"She did."

"Every single one. Same as you." Chisa turned her cup. "That's the same person. That's the tell."

Iuno stopped with her cup halfway.

"...Yes," she said. "That's the tell."

---

Then Ciaccona said, "And Mei was smiling like a *lunatic* the entire time."

"I was not."

"Mei." Ciaccona pointed with her whole hand. "You had your chin in your hand and you were grinning like somebody had given you a *horse*. For two hours. Amy asked you what was happening and you said 'that's her other one' like you were describing the weather."

Mei drank.

"Go on," said Amy.

"It's not a big—"

"Ma."

Mei put the cup down.

"She's been carrying that around like it's a loaded thing in a cupboard," she said. "For a year. Every time she's sharp with someone she checks my face afterwards. Every time she reads a room too fast she apologizes for it. She's been keeping half of herself in a drawer because the last time it was out, it cost her everything."

Nobody said anything.

"And on Wednesday she took it out," Mei said, "and used it to make six people laugh for two hours, and then put it away, and nothing bad happened."

The oil hissed at the counter.

"So yeah," said Mei. "I was smiling."

Iuno was looking very fixedly at the chalkboard menu.

"It was frightening for about four seconds," said Cartethyia, brightly, into the gap, which was the kindest available thing and everyone knew it.

"That few."

"And then you did a *voice* and I forgot to be frightened." Cartethyia beamed. "You did the steward. Three weeks ago you did him for eleven words and then Wednesday you did him again and it was the *same voice*. You didn't write it down. You just had him."

"I had him."

"That's the thing I like best," said Cartethyia. "Out of everything. That you just had him."

Lupa lifted her cup two inches off the counter.

"To the animals," she said.

"To the *animals*—"

"To Kettle," said Amy.

"TO KETTLE—"

## 00:45

Cartethyia went at ten to one, sitting up, mid-sentence, with her cheek against the wall and her hand still around her cup.

"That's four," said Lupa, checking her.

"Of what?"

"Four drinks. She's never had one before this year. That's a *good* four." Lupa took the cup out of her hand and set it on the counter and put her own coat over her. "She'll be fine. She's warm and she's breathing and she's got a bad hip so leave her sitting."

Amy went at one, in a different way: loudly, affectionately, and at length.

"I love all of you," she announced, to the room. "I love every single one of you. Bo, I love you."

"Mm," said Bo.

"Ma, I love you the *most*, which is a *fact*, and it's not a competition, and if it was a competition you'd win it—"

"Drink some water."

"—and *Iuno*." Amy rounded on her. "Iuno. You are objectively the most terrifying person I have ever met and you buy the tea I like and you have never *once* mentioned it—"

"Water."

"—and I'm going to say something now that I'll be embarrassed about—"

"You are going to drink *water*," said Iuno, and put a cup in her hand, and Amy drank all of it, and then said the embarrassing thing anyway, at volume, and Iuno went the color of the neon outside and told her to drink another one.

Mei was cycling. She'd been cycling since eleven. Nine minutes of being extremely drunk, then a shudder somewhere behind the sternum, then eleven minutes of being annoyingly sober, then back.

"It's the regeneration," she said, in a sober window, mournfully. "It reads it as damage."

"That's an accurate reading," said Chisa.

"It's *recreation*."

"It's a solvent."

"You've had nine."

"I've had eleven," said Chisa, "and I've been counting, and that's the difference between us."

Ciaccona went at half one. She went the way professionals go: gracefully, in a chair, having correctly identified the exact point at which she would still be able to walk. She put her head on Cartethyia's shoulder and was asleep in under a minute, and Cartethyia, without waking, made room.

## 02:10

Three of them left upright.

Iuno, entirely composed, with a technique built over thirty years of ceremonial functions where the wine was compulsory and the alertness was mandatory, and who was, by Mei's estimate — and Mei was the only one who could tell — comprehensively drunk and would deny it under torture.

Chisa, on her thirteenth, holding her cup in both hands, doing fine.

And Lupa, who had matched everybody and lost nothing, and who had been quietly counting other people's drinks all night with the same pen she used for the casualty log.

Bo brought out a plate of the fried things nobody had ordered and put it down and went back to the counter.

"How old are you," said Chisa.

Iuno considered whether to answer, and then answered, which was the drink.

"Forty-six."

"Huh."

"Say it."

"You look forty-six," said Chisa. "That's a compliment where I'm from. We had a whole thing about it."

"Where you're from is twenty years ago."

"That's the other thing." Chisa drank. "I went in at eighteen. I came out and the world had done twenty and I hadn't done any of them, so I'm eighteen and I'm thirty-eight and both of those are on the form." She turned the cup. "I've got a mother who wants to move a cabinet for me and I've got two decades of nothing, and the actual number is thirty-eight, and when I say it out loud it sounds like somebody else's."

"Thirty-eight," said Lupa.

"Mm."

Lupa put down a fried thing she had been about to eat.

"You're older than me."

Chisa turned her head.

"By how much?"

"Ten years."

The Ninth Step went quiet in a specific way.

"...Say that again," said Iuno.

"I'm twenty-eight."

Chisa put her cup down. Iuno set hers on the counter with great care, the way you set down something you have discovered is full.

"When."

"In the spring."

"You are —" Iuno stopped, and started again. "You are the youngest person at this table."

"Cartethyia's twenty-four."

"Cartethyia is twenty-four *on paper* and she spent twenty of it in a sealed tower talking to a hundred dolls, so the arithmetic is a courtesy." Iuno pushed her cup away. "Amy's twenty-nine and she'll tell you she's nineteen and she's right. You are twenty-eight, and you carry the shopping, and you run the door, and you write a *casualty log*, and I have been treating you like the oldest woman in that house since the day I met you."

Lupa shrugged, which is what she did with things she had no place to put.

"I went into the gates at seven," she said.

"I know."

"So there's no — " She stopped, and thought about how to say it, and took the shortest route. "There's no *before*. People say 'when you were a kid' like it's a place. I don't have one. I've got the pens and then I've got the sand and then I've got a spear."

Nobody moved.

Then Chisa said, very seriously, "Lupa."

"Mm."

"You're the baby."

Lupa's ear went flat.

"Don't."

"You're the *baby* of this house."

"I will put you in the canal."

"Iuno," said Chisa, "she's the baby."

"She is *demonstrably* the baby," said Iuno.

"I have carried every single one of you at some point—"

"That's what the baby does," said Chisa, "when nobody tells her she's allowed to stop."

Lupa opened her mouth.

She shut it.

Her ears went all the way back and she looked at the counter and her tail, which had never once in its life kept a secret, gave her away completely.

"Ninety seconds," she said, to the counter.

"What?"

"You can do it for ninety seconds." Lupa picked up the fried thing again. "And then I'm never talking about it again and one of you is going in the canal."

So they did it for ninety seconds.

Iuno topped up her cup before her own and told her to eat something. Chisa said her name in a tone that had a hand in it. Bo, who missed nothing and had been running that counter for twenty years, put a second bowl of stew down in front of Lupa without being asked, which was either coincidence or the funniest thing that happened all night.

At the end of ninety seconds Lupa said, "Right, that's it," and stood up, and started collecting coats.

Her ears stayed pink for the rest of the night.

## 03:00

It took forty minutes to get six people up a stairwell.

Lupa carried Cartethyia, who weighed nothing and whose hip would have finished her on the steps, and who woke up halfway and said, "Are we doing the lamps," and went back to sleep.

Ciaccona walked under her own power and sang, quietly, most of the way, something with a moving bass line in it.

Amy walked with an arm around her mother and told her four more times that it was not a competition.

Chisa took the front, because Chisa had a map of the whole quarter in her feet and could do it in the dark.

Iuno took the back and counted heads, twice, at every corner, all the way home.

At the door Mei got the key in on the third attempt, cycled sober halfway through, got it on the first, and said nothing about it.

The house took them in. Cartethyia went into the alcove with two quilts. Ciaccona got the couch and her lute got, for the first time in eleven months, a place in her own room. Amy went up the stairs on her hands and knees, singing, and was heard to arrive.

Mei found Iuno on the balcony with a glass of water at four minutes past three.

"You're drunk."

"I am composed."

"That too."

Iuno drank her water.

"She's twenty-eight," she said.

"I know."

"Twenty-eight, Mei."

"I know."

Iuno set the glass down on the rail.

"She sat down on a step for forty minutes because a woman in a chair told her that standing over her was the part that hurt," she said. "She keeps a log of who went down and how long they were down for. She has a *place*, on Thursdays, in a hole, where she reads shipping notices." A pause. "And she's the youngest one of us and nobody has told her that in her entire life."

Mei leaned on the rail beside her.

"So tell her again next week."

"She'll put me in the canal."

"She'll let you finish first."

Down in the yard, in the cold, Lupa was doing something to the woodpile that at three in the morning could not possibly have needed doing, ears still faintly pink, tail going in the dark where she was absolutely certain nobody could see it.

— fin.

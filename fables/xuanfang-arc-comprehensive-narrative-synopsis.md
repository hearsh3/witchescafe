# Xuanfang Arc 

## The Way Back

Before the road to Xuanfang broke open, Yangyang’s life was divided between departures and the ordinary things she rarely admitted she wanted. She was an Outrider of Jinzhou, trained to hear messages in the wind and to answer danger before other people knew it had arrived. At eight years old she had chosen a sword beneath the eyes of her family in Mingting, leaving her mother’s flute on its shelf; at nineteen she had passed through the family gate toward the registry office and the frontier, teaching herself that competence meant becoming the person no one needed to worry about. Her elder sister Suisui wrote, called, sent cakes and complaints, and waited for replies that came late. Yangyang loved her, but treated love as one more duty best discharged without making herself heavy in anyone’s hands.

In Jinzhou she had acquired, almost despite herself, a second household. Chixia’s irrepressible certainty, Baizhi’s cool and exact care, Mei’s golden-eyed openness, Iuno’s hard-won attentiveness, Amy’s fierce watchfulness, Lupa’s blunt provisions, Chisa’s unornamented perception, Ciaccona’s music, and Cartethyia’s extravagant gentleness had turned rooms, tables, and patrol routes into places to return to. An afternoon in the garden had shown the life at stake more clearly than any oath. Baizhi ate sweet tofu with extra ginger beneath a willow while pretending to read. Chixia painted a fish whose thick, skeptical eyebrows accidentally resembled Baizhi’s, and Baizhi loved the ridiculous fan enough to trade away her elegant iris. Mei painted green turtles, Cartethyia worked flowers into everything, Wind and Amy made leaf stencils, and Iuno and Mei wore one blue drop earring each. They ate noodles, threw rings, won a glazed dish and a duck egg cup, and wasted time around a pond as though time could always be wasted again.

Wind moved among them without a body. She was another Yangyang from a ruined worldline: an older sister-self who had watched her Jinzhou drown, found Chixia’s red cap in black water, and reached every catastrophe after it had already begun. At the rim of her world she had unbound flesh, blood, and most of her name, becoming weather so she could cross the dark between possible worlds and deliver a sleeping golden-eyed traveler to the Gorges of Spirits. She had arrived too late even then; Iuno, a priestess in silver-blue, had already claimed that shore. Contact with the Arbiter had torn Wind thinner, until she became a guest current haunting the living Yangyang’s world. For a year she corrected Yangyang’s sword elbow at dawn, carried incense toward the unmourned, entered half-open windows, listened at tables, and learned the agony of remaining near a life she could not inhabit.

Xuanfang Hold stood beyond the Gorges as an almost impossible frontier refuge. Its Sentinel, Hsin, had built a city that rearranged itself through jade, bronze, resonance, and faith. Autopuppets guarded its roads. Lift-kites held freight lanes above the chasms. Deep mechanisms listened to the frontier and moved walls, sluices, bridges, and weapons in response. Hsin trusted purpose so completely that the Hold’s greatest powers were not hidden from those they served. Yet travelers had begun vanishing along its approach, and no proper message explained why.

Yangyang and Chixia went to investigate. They reached a deserted checkpoint where a kettle had boiled dry and found the road already under hostile control. Autopuppets that should have protected travelers attacked them instead. The fighting shattered the service stair and drove them toward a gorge. Chixia was badly wounded; Yangyang’s Terminal was destroyed; Wind was too exhausted to support both women down the broken descent.

Yangyang calculated the available lives and made the old choice. She strapped her own field bag and map to Chixia, aligned Chixia’s sword arm, and ordered Wind to take her. Chixia resisted, knowing exactly what it meant to leave Yangyang above while machines closed in, but Yangyang forced the separation. She said one of them had to make it home. The great stone gates of Xuanfang opened beyond her as Wind bore the bleeding Chixia over the edge, and Yangyang turned alone to draw every machine away from the gorge.

For two days Wind guided Chixia down the drainage channel. She cushioned falls, carried sound, and held the wounded woman inside the world whenever her breathing weakened. Every glimpse of the red cap summoned the Chixia from Wind’s own drowned Jinzhou, already dead in the water. Wind could not rescue that girl; she could only choose this one, again and again, until patrol scouts found them. At Huaxu Academy, Baizhi stabilized Chixia while the household gathered around the knowledge that Yangyang had remained behind.

Suisui arrived from Mingting expecting an ordinary visit. Instead she entered Yangyang’s empty apartment carrying a traveling bag and found grief, armed strangers, and cold air that behaved like a person. Ciaccona’s lute stirred the carved sound-tide shells in Yangyang’s desk. Through them Suisui saw Wind’s lost Mingting: another Suisui older at the temples, baking bread in their mother’s kitchen, leaving room on the board for a second batch, asking a sister who had already become weather for an address she could not give. She saw Wind leave that kitchen secretly and cross the edge of the world.

The knowledge broke Suisui’s neat expectation of one younger sister without making her retreat from the second. She thanked Wind for bringing Chixia back. When Wind came apart at the gratitude, Suisui lifted her scarf and kept turning so the cold could find another warm fold. She told the air to come closer and admitted she was still trying to find Wind’s shoulders. Wind had abandoned those shoulders at the rim of her world, but Suisui continued adjusting as though they existed. By the time Jinhsi gave the rescue party Jué’s ancient key, Suisui had placed Yangyang’s coat over her arm, tucked Wind inside the scarf, and made room in the family arithmetic for both sisters.

Hsin had foreseen enough to expect their arrival. That foreknowledge did not reassure them. The key opened the way toward a Hold whose Sentinel had sent no plain request, whose machines had turned on travelers, and whose gates had swallowed Yangyang. Mei, Iuno, Amy, Lupa, Chisa, Ciaccona, Cartethyia, Suisui, and Wind went after her.

## Where the Wind Stays

Above the gorge, Yangyang led the Autopuppets away from Chixia’s path. She baited them through gates and narrow turns until exhaustion, blood loss, and a crushed Terminal left her at a sheer drop. Her Resonance overclocked. The wind ceased to be merely air and became every cry the mountains had kept: Wardens calling for home, travelers dying between settlements, alternate lives ending in water, and the older Yangyang kneeling with a red cap in a drowned street.

Her outline began to soften. Dissolution offered relief and reach. Without bones, pain, or a single body to defend, she could pour down the gorge, find Chixia, and be useful everywhere at once. She understood why Wind had surrendered her body. She also understood that the impulse was not pure. It contained despair, pride, and the wish to make death look like perfect service.

Against that vast invitation, Yangyang remembered painfully specific things: the texture of a quilt, the tea she preferred, Suisui’s complaints, Mei’s hand, the Jinzhou canal she had not yet walked beside Mei, and the private fact that she wanted more time. She did not want to become a beautiful absence. She gripped the rock and named herself alive, hurt, frightened, and still desirous of home.

A blue mechanical Rafter Kite struck the next Autopuppet off its line. Liangyu and the Xuan Wardens reached her, carried her away from the cliff, and brought her to Xianling Village. There, in a borrowed bed, Yangyang insisted the door remain open so she could hear ordinary living sounds. She recorded the names the mountain had forced into her—Duzhong, Qingzhi, Qingxiao, and others who had died without a proper listener—and wrote a message stripped of professional concealment: she was alive, she was hurt, she wanted to come home, and other people needed help. She asked her family to come for her.

Xianling itself was evacuating. The villagers had trusted the Autopuppets until those machines killed Duzhong and Qingzhi. Qingyan, Qingzhi’s young brother, wanted to answer the loss with a sword. Yangyang did not tell him his anger was wrong; she told him not to let the sword decide what the anger meant. Liangyu, carrying her own dead, revealed the old account of the Xuanling birds. Once they had flown the gorges as messengers, carrying military reports, family words, and the last longings of people cut off by war. None had survived the ancient catastrophe. Liangyu knew only the first half of their homecoming song because the second half had vanished with an ancestor at Yuan Fortress.

Yangyang and Liangyu found companionship in the admission that neither could make the losses acceptable. Yangyang told her about Chixia reaching for her while the gorge opened between them. Liangyu spoke of comrades left where the road failed. They held one another without pretending the embrace repaired anything.

When scouts disappeared in the fog, Yangyang worked with Yanlang to map a route for the villagers. The machines struck again at a mill yard. The evacuation passed through, but Mingyu and Yanlang died holding the road. Their deaths tore open a Sonoro impression of the Fortress and Gorges War two centuries earlier. The ancient defenders flooded the present with unfinished rage, pleas for family, and the need to know whether their sacrifices had preserved anything.

Yangyang nearly opened too far again. Instead she used Liangyu’s incomplete song. She could not sing the dead back to life or make the mill yard less bloody. She could only remain bounded enough to hear them as people. One by one, voices that had been reduced to noise finished human sentences. The old pressure released. Yangyang returned to the present and wept because Mingyu and Yanlang were still dead. Liangyu found her there, and they sang the partial song together rather than letting either woman complete grief alone.

The rescue party entered Xuanfang through a fog that folded roads and senses. Its traps were intimate. It offered each traveler an apparently obvious rescue that would separate them from the others. A spar impaled Mei when she shielded Amy; Amy could have retreated into Nyx, but stayed in her own body and helped remove it. Mei, who had once made decisions for others through sheer willingness to suffer, asked before acting and accepted help while bleeding.

Iuno and Wind navigated another fold. Iuno did not demand that Wind forgive the old contest at Mei’s shore. She asked permission before fastening a wound and refused to interpret proximity as absolution. Lupa, Ciaccona, and Chisa distrusted rooms that arrived too neatly. They anchored themselves with rope, repeated locations, and one another’s inconvenient habits. Cartethyia held a collapsing roof off the driver Shen Rong and saved a camellia root while admitting aloud that she was afraid and calling for Ciaccona. Suisui followed the Rafter Kite she named Dumpling, leaving thread markers rather than trusting any path that could not be checked.

The fog was defeated by declarations it could not counterfeit: names, injuries, locations, explicit permissions, and promises with enough detail to be tested. The scattered groups rejoined. Lupa carried Mei when Mei could no longer convert pain into forward motion. Wind returned to Iuno after every chance to abandon her. Suisui kept the coat ready. Together they reached the Hold Yangyang had entered alone.

## The Price Hsin Named

Before Suisui could reach Yangyang, Hsin drew her into a pagoda and placed incense, weiqi stones, and a wager between them. Hsin offered certainty. Yangyang could be guaranteed a return, but only if Wind were annihilated completely and Suisui bound the rest of her life to Hsin’s service. If Suisui refused the certain road and won the game, she would receive a Prism and the instructions for a more dangerous possibility.

Hsin made certainty seductive. She showed Suisui carefully selected disasters in which hopeful alternatives failed. She preserved an evening where Yangyang could remain at the family table forever, safe and happy because nothing beyond that room could reach her. Suisui recognized the cruelty inside the comfort. A Yangyang who could never leave, choose, fail, or answer a later call was not rescued; she was curated.

Wind arrived prepared to pay. Her willingness was immediate because self-erasure had become the language in which she apologized for surviving her own world. Suisui rejected the idea that consent made the price clean. Wind’s readiness to vanish did not prove she had no value; it proved how long she had lived without anyone protecting her from her own arithmetic.

The Prism could distinguish Resonance frequencies. Used properly, it might carry a call through the Snare, separate captives from their copied cries, and protect Wind from being consumed. It offered no guarantee. It required Yangyang to answer and everyone else to risk uncertainty. Suisui chose it.

Then she forced Hsin to name her own acts. Hsin had opened the gates. She had known Yangyang would be endangered. She had arranged a crisis severe enough that the entire household could not postpone coming to Xuanfang, because the Hold was failing and ordinary appeals could be deferred. Years earlier, Hsin had trusted an uncertain rescue during a winter emergency and lost both the settlement and the relief column. Since then she had treated certainty as mercy, even when certainty required turning people into pieces on a board.

Suisui did not forgive her. She also refused to spend Hsin. She recognized that the coercion depended on a hidden hope: Hsin believed this particular household might create a third course she herself could no longer imagine. Suisui could not promise no one would die. She could not borrow the others’ courage in advance. She could only refuse the stone that declared one sister’s life payable by the other’s disappearance. She won the Prism and carried Hsin’s compromised hope back into the Hold.

Yangyang, meanwhile, faced Muyu’s Myriad Snare. Muyu had been one of Hsin’s trusted artisans, commissioned to help make Xuanfang’s Skyworks a civic wonder. He had instead built a machine that imprisoned workers, copied their voices, and drew power from pain. The Snare used a second Prism, mirror paths, and recorded distress to turn every rescue attempt against itself. It offered familiar voices in wrong places and multiplied attackers from reflected movements.

The household reached Yangyang inside it by refusing to fight as isolated heroes. Lupa broke routes and guarded exits. Chisa cut invisible seams. Ciaccona listened for the real cadence under copies. Fleurdelys and Cartethyia used scale and wind to hold paths open. Amy and Nyx controlled the air. Mei and Iuno coordinated without hiding information. Wind brought Suisui’s Prism.

Hsin asked Yangyang to swear a vow with a missing word. Yangyang refused to fill a blank whose terms she could not know. When she learned that Suisui had rejected the price of Wind’s death, she also had to confront her own contradiction. At the checkpoint she had told Chixia that one person had to make it home, then quietly decided which person could be left behind. In the Snare she had written that she wanted rescue, yet tried to finish Muyu alone before her family arrived.

She did not repair that contradiction with another promise to survive at any cost. She told the others what she actually needed. The trapped workers could not be abandoned, and the Snare’s Prism had to be turned into itself. The plan was uncertain. She asked them to take the risk with her instead of volunteering their faith after the fact.

The Snare shouted with copied suffering. Yangyang listened beneath it for breath. She answered each living captive individually, giving a name, direction, and human reply that the machine could not manufacture. One prisoner was already dead; the others carried the body out and spoke the name rather than letting a failed rescue become an omitted number. With both Prisms aligned, Yangyang made the Snare catch its own paths. Mirrors multiplied the machine’s bindings back upon itself while the pack closed every exit.

The final path seized Yangyang’s hand and tried to make her the last anchor. Instead of cutting herself free alone, she asked Chisa to sever it. Mei caught her when she fell despite the timber wound still open in her abdomen. The Snare bound itself and collapsed. Muyu escaped, leaving the deeper purpose of the diversion unresolved.

Outside, Yangyang did not walk away pretending the rescue proved she could bear everything. She let Cartethyia help a staggering worker. She accepted support herself. She radioed Suisui and asked for the coat. Suisui reached her, wrapped both Yangyang and Wind inside it, and became extravagant about how many sisters were allowed to answer.

The welcome at the gate unfolded in several directions at once. Mei reached Iuno and Amy still carrying the puncture through her middle. Iuno’s relief came with jealousy she did not disguise and restraint she did not turn into martyrdom. Amy immediately reported every charged glance between Mei and Yangyang with the prosecutorial enthusiasm of a daughter who had spent too long watching adults conceal dangerous information. Lupa counted wounds, exits, and meals. Ciaccona and Cartethyia returned to one another with the awkward knowledge that both had called out in fear. Chisa let Lupa inspect the repaired rope instead of declaring it adequate from a distance.

Suisui ran at Yangyang, then adjusted the collision to include Wind inside the coat. The embrace had none of Hsin’s clean arithmetic. There were too many limbs, no shoulders where one sister ought to have them, cracked ribs, bad footing, and a bowl still waiting somewhere. It was therefore the first solution Suisui trusted completely.

In the recovered quarters, care became specific enough to refuse. Food went around the table, and Yangyang was asked what she wanted rather than fed the most medically sensible portion without consultation. Mei asked for bread because she wanted bread. Lupa gave people seconds while pretending the pot had made the decision. The rescued workers received blankets and intake entries. The name of the dead captive was carried into the record beside the living rather than placed in a separate, quieter account.

Suisui reconstructed Hsin’s moves for the household. Her anger did not make her careless. Hsin had not merely predicted Yangyang’s arrival; she had built pressure around it. The Sentinel expected the household to resent her and had still chosen coercion because she could no longer trust an appeal whose refusal remained possible. Suisui insisted that any confrontation include the fact that Hsin had also offered herself for future service and had let the uncertain path leave the pagoda. A person could be guilty, desperate, useful, beloved by a city, and wrong in the same act. None of those facts cancelled another.

Yangyang listened to how readily Wind had stepped forward as payment. She recognized her own checkpoint decision in it. Each sister had treated the other as the life that should continue and herself as the acceptable remainder. Suisui refused to congratulate either of them for the symmetry.

Chixia’s recovery remained the first evidence that the decision had saved someone. When Yangyang finally heard that Baizhi had stabilized her, relief did not retroactively make the abandonment at the gorge inevitable. Chixia was alive because Yangyang chose, because Wind carried, because scouts found them, and because Baizhi worked. Yangyang was alive because the Rafter Kite arrived, Liangyu’s Wardens reached the cliff, and Yangyang herself held the rock. No outcome belonged to one heroic gesture.

The household also spoke of Muyu as more than the silhouette who escaped. The Snare’s craftsmanship required knowledge of Hsin’s systems, the Skyworks, and the Hold’s civic trust. He had turned commissioned beauty into an apparatus that made pleas indistinguishable from bait. His flight meant the Hold could not call the crisis complete merely because its most visible machine had collapsed. Watch rotations, water access, the Core, and the rescued workers’ testimony all became part of the next search.

That evening, Suisui combed Yangyang’s hair. Yangyang told her what dissolution at the cliff had promised: reach without limit, usefulness without need, and an end to the humiliation of having only two hands. Suisui did not answer with a prohibition against dying, because prohibitions were another way to make impossible promises in advance. She asked Yangyang to state when the temptation returned and allowed herself to say that she would be frightened. Yangyang accepted the fear as information rather than a debt.

Mei and Iuno conducted their own inventory. Iuno admitted that watching Mei catch Yangyang had hurt. Mei admitted the attachment did not vanish because it was inconvenient. They agreed that Iuno would not pre-read or manage Mei’s answer, Mei would not use suffering to force permission, and Yangyang would not be treated as an event occurring between them. Iuno asked that marital intimacy keep its boundaries while trust grew. Mei agreed without calling the limit rejection. Amy, who had seen timber pass through her mother, then made the simplest request of the night: she wanted to sleep between them. They arranged their wounds around her.

Across the hall, Suisui left a lamp where Yangyang could see it without being watched. The next day’s rice soaked in a pot. Xuanfang remained damaged, Muyu remained free, and Hsin remained absent, but the household went to sleep with the questions spoken in the same rooms as the people they concerned.

## The Heart in the Open Sky

For a brief interval, Xuanfang became inhabited again rather than merely defended. Mei returned to Iuno and Amy. Lupa checked every door and then claimed she had only happened to pass it. Ciaccona and Cartethyia admitted how afraid they had been for one another. Chisa repaired ropes instead of cutting them. Suisui combed Yangyang’s hair and heard the full account of the cliff, including the moment Yangyang had wanted to dissolve. The family ate together, which meant the dangerous decisions could no longer be hidden inside emergencies.

At that table Suisui described Hsin’s wager without cleaning it for easier consumption. She admitted she had nearly accepted the certainty stone. She disclosed that Hsin had arranged the gate crisis and risked Yangyang to force the household’s arrival. Yangyang did not defend the Sentinel because the Hold needed her; nor did she convert anger into a sentence of permanent exile. They would confront Hsin with enough chairs for everyone affected. Jinhsi would receive a dispatch. Muyu would be tracked. The rescued workers would be counted by name.

Yangyang made a new operational rule for herself. She was one of the people who had to come out. If she needed a cut, a catch, a hand, or time, she would say which. The others were not required to infer it from blood on the floor.

The same honesty spread through the household. Mei and Iuno discussed Yangyang without converting jealousy into command. Iuno admitted that uncertainty frightened her and that she wanted kisses to remain inside the marriage while the relationships found their shape. She also supported the promised canal walk because Mei’s affection was not a theft if everyone could speak. Amy, shaken by the timber and the fog, asked to sleep between her parents. Mei and Iuno made room. Suisui placed a hallway lamp where both rooms could see it. Rice was set to soak for morning.

Yet Xuanfang’s quiet was not safety. Chisa and Lupa took advantage of Ciaccona and Cartethyia’s loud musical distraction to enter the restricted machinery beneath the streets. Chisa read the city by vibration. Its pendulums were physical logic gates; its chimes absorbed shock and translated it into signal; its foundries assembled and launched the Autopuppets; its bronze shafts moved the visible city above. Every road, bastion, bridge, and sluice was part of one enormous instrument.

The central frequency led not to a locked crypt but to the sky. Hsin’s Jade Heart hung above the cloud line in an armillary frame, exposed to wind, wings, and any Resonator capable of matching its note. It monitored Tacet Discords, held the Hold’s spatial folds together, and coordinated the defenses. Chisa found no lock, wall, or ward because Hsin had made openness an article of faith. A power built to protect could not, in Hsin’s logic, need protection from the people beneath it.

Chisa memorized the harmonic that could break it.

At dinner she reported the vulnerability. Cartethyia understood Hsin’s intent immediately: hiding the Heart would concede that love and purpose were insufficient safeguards. Lupa answered that purpose did not stop a resonance strike. Suisui, remembering the weiqi board, knew Hsin had not overlooked the risk; she had calculated it and chosen exposure anyway.

Then they realized no one had actually seen Hsin since arriving. The presence in the pagoda had been a fragment. The Hold’s subdued voices sounded less like ordinary battle shock than a vigil carried on without announcement. Muyu had been trusted with Hsin’s design. If the Snare had been his true objective, he would have remained with it; because he had escaped, the Snare was probably cover for an attack on the Heart or something still deeper.

Every fighter at the table prepared to move. Mei told them to sleep.

The demand startled them more than an alarm would have. Mei had once run toward any crisis as though speed could erase the cost to her body. Now she counted what was present: cracked ribs, a punctured abdomen, a damaged shoulder, rope burns, sleeplessness, and people functioning on soup and spite. If Muyu attacked immediately, they would answer broken; if he waited, they could meet him whole. Lupa accepted dawn and threatened to wake the Hold by howling if necessity arrived earlier.

After the bowls were cleared, Yangyang asked Mei to walk with her. Iuno understood the request and promised only to remain in the room, not to possess the path. On Xuanfang’s wet upper promenade, Yangyang and Mei moved at the pace their wounds permitted. Yangyang tried to declare the military bench, iron railing, cloud sea, and drainage grate an adequate fulfillment of the promised Jinzhou canal walk. She had nearly dissolved at the cliff furious that she would die before hearing the vinegar seller’s opinions; accepting the lesser substitute would settle that dangerous desire into something already completed.

Mei refused. The bench could be a good evening, but it could not become a goodbye. They would walk the real bridges, see laundry hung over the lower quarter, choose the wrong vinegar, endure the owner’s lecture, and buy it anyway. Mei put the occasion on an itinerary beyond the present emergency. Yangyang’s ears reddened; then she settled against Mei’s shoulder and let Mei’s loose hand remain over hers.

The next crisis arrived through the past. A temporal resonance drew them into Xuanfang two hundred years earlier, when the Fortress and Gorges War was still approaching. Artisans, Wardens, families, and ordinary laborers prepared Yuan Fortress and Mengzhou for a Tacet Discord assault they did not expect to survive. Xuanling birds crossed the sky above farewell after farewell.

Among the soldiers was Lingfang, Liangyu’s ancestor. She had once wandered with a liuqin and married a humble painter who loved Xuanling birds. After Tacet Discords killed him, she put down the instrument and took up a blade. The bamboo xiao he had carved for her had never produced a proper note, but she kept it at her belt. Her infant daughter remained behind while she tried to determine whether she was defending a homeland, avenging a husband, or simply making grief useful.

Yangyang told her that land was not separate from the people loved upon it. The child would inherit whatever tomorrow the defenders preserved. Lingfang left for Yuan Fortress steadier than before and sang the half of the Xuanling song that later generations had lost: the verses of distance, the watched road, and the table kept for someone the road delayed.

When Yangyang and Mei returned to the present, Muyu’s second attack had begun. Rogue Autopuppets and Tacet Discords swarmed the Hold in a deliberate echo of the ancient war. Liangyu restored communications; Commander Qiuhong rallied the Wardens; Qingxiao opened corridors with sword formations; Fleurdelys shattered chassis at the gate. Behind the army, a layered Sonoro Sphere spread over the lower terraces like a bruise.

Lupa divided the household by function rather than attachment. Mei and Yangyang would enter because Muyu and the old memory had fixed on them. Chisa would cut seams. Ciaccona would read the composition. Lupa, Iuno, Amy in Nyx, Suisui, Fleurdelys, Qingxiao, and the defenders would hold the gate and the exposed Heart.

Amy wanted to go with Mei. Mei did not dismiss the fear. She explained that Nyx alone could cover both wall and sky if Muyu struck the Core. Amy was therefore not the child being left behind; she was the most necessary person on the wall. Amy ordered her mother to return. Mei answered with the vinegar itinerary. Iuno promised to keep Amy alive and ordered Mei to keep Yangyang alive. Lupa squeezed Chisa’s shoulder once. The four entered the Sphere.

The Sonoro swallowed the battle noise and opened as an imperial garden of cedar walks, amber pools, suspended leaves, and several eras stitched into the same ground. Wind grew denser against Yangyang’s throat. Then time stopped for everyone but her.

An older Yangyang crossed the moon bridge. This was not Wind restored, but a possible ending worn into a human figure: early field armor, a scar nearly taking the throat, the same face aged by years torn rather than lived. Behind her voice lay a chorus of Yangyangs from endings where nothing had been saved. She could see Wind directly. She rested a hand against the otherwise untouchable air, and Wind leaned into it, trembling. Three notes of their mother’s fever lullaby passed through the frozen garden.

The older woman offered withdrawal. Yangyang could turn back, leave what followed to a name the Sonoro redacted, and live. Every known version of this choice had ended in failure. One stone thrown into the ocean could alter an entire world; once this wind rose, it would not cease. The warning did not come from an enemy. It came from women with Yangyang’s instincts who had carried those instincts to their terminal conclusion.

Yangyang looked at Mei frozen mid-word. She remembered the drainage grate Mei had refused, the promised vinegar, Jué’s demand that she live, Suisui’s coat, and the fact that Wind’s sacrifice had not created an obligation to repeat it. She refused to let Mei face what came next alone.

Wind answered by trying to force her out. A gale struck Yangyang backward toward the entrance: the same bargain Wind had offered Suisui, now enacted without words. Spend the weather; save the body.

Yangyang set her feet in the dawn-drill stance Wind herself had taught. She drew the heavy elbow inward and held against the teacher’s gale. She confessed that leaving had always been her family craft. She had left the flute, left Mingting, replied late, and made herself easy not to worry about. Wind had left her city, name, hands, and body. Yangyang would not honor that loss by becoming the next person to vanish.

She named every promise she intended to carry at once. She would live for Jué, visit Suisui, return for Mei’s vinegar, help Xuanfang, and keep Wind beside her. She would not put one promise down to make the others lighter. The gale broke into ordinary air. Wind spent a word from her nearly empty store: “Together.” Yangyang had once written that together did not count toward mastery. Now she said this one did.

The older failure touched the air where Yangyang’s hat brim belonged, almost amused, and withdrew across the bridge. Time resumed. Chisa heard the displaced pressure. Ciaccona knew a song had changed key. Mei saw Yangyang’s wet eyes and asked. Yangyang promised an explanation later and continued deeper with Wind wrapped tightly at her throat.

## The Thousand-Puppet Pavilion

The imperial garden changed into Muyu’s workshop. Frames held unfinished wooden faces copied from real citizens: the tea seller, children at the fountain, Wardens with their scars preserved. Muyu sat beneath one lamp sanding a wooden hand with the serenity of a craftsman who believed interruption impossible.

He called Yangyang and Wind failures playing sisters. He called Mei a mask that had developed a sickening habit of playing human. Mei answered that the part she practiced was precisely the part a carver could not make: the tea seller pouring without looking, a child choosing to return, a hand reaching for someone without a script. Breath, error, appetite, and choice had made her more human than any perfect face on Muyu’s rack.

Muyu attacked the faith underlying Xuanfang. No Sentinel, he said, had opened its own core and shown a commandment to protect humanity. People called them shepherds because the house had always been warm. Hsin heard every scream in the Snare and did not descend. Care itself was unreliable: held too long, it sweated; left in the sun, it burned; every ruined city had been cared for by someone.

Mei had met Sentinels who paid everything for those beneath them. She did not need to dismantle Hsin to recognize why Hsin let Suisui win. Muyu dismissed that payment as unstable sentiment and unveiled the work of two centuries.

The dead had left Reverberations in the ground along the frontier. A husband called for his wife. A child cursed a monster. A mother ran out of words and screamed. Muyu harvested those endings and stripped away everything but rage, because rage remained loud after tenderness, humor, and context became harder to retrieve. Then he turned the rage around. The Autopuppets were not simply malfunctioning; they were grieving at human faces because Muyu had made humanity look like the Tacet Discords that killed them.

Yangyang’s changed hearing separated names from the roar. A woman still called Axiu. A father remembered a burned street. A child wanted a brother. Muyu had not discovered a flaw in emotion. He had covered his ears to every quieter part and called the resulting scream complete. Yangyang called him a coward and warned that the rage he fed would consume him last.

The workshop assembled around them. Faces became tiles, hands became brackets, frames became three ascending roofs, and a jade-and-thread kernel beat beneath a stretched drum. The Thousand-Puppet Pavilion screamed from every eave. Chisa cut the threads no one else could see. Ciaccona struck the room and located its only authentic beat. Mei climbed while the wound in her abdomen bled through its dressing. Yangyang reached the kernel with Wind lifting her.

Muyu claimed that breaking it would kill the Reverberations a second time. Chisa heard his breathing accelerate. Whether the claim was lie or frightened half-truth, the fear pointed to the same kernel. Yangyang laid her blade against it and tried to listen for the living the way she had in the Snare.

The sound pulled her into the ancient war.

At first she saw why Muyu’s reduction could tempt anyone. A Warden ran into a Discord’s jaws because it had taken his brother. A baker attacked with a cleaver. A girl threw stones, then her shoes, then herself. Their faces held one word: no. Not him. Not my street. Nothing else. Wind’s flooded Jinzhou had contained the same refusal. Love had carried her across worlds, but rage had supplied the force to tear a body apart.

Muyu redirected the dead toward Yangyang. The Warden accused her of swallowing his brother; the baker demanded her mother; stones and hooks came from the smoke. Yangyang blocked with the flat of the sword. She would not mutilate people whose perception had been turned against her. Lingfang descended from the ridge with her husband’s xiao missing and her daughter erased by the immediate heat of battle. Her blade struck on a musician’s rhythm.

Wind saw Yangyang surrounded and became the flooded scream again. She hit the dead with valley-sized gusts, each blow tearing pieces from her already thin substance. Yangyang reached for her. The reach pulled her sword elbow out. Lingfang’s blade entered beneath the guard and opened Yangyang’s side below the cracked ribs.

The wound was real in both the Sonoro and the Pavilion. Yangyang fell into mud with warm blood between her fingers. Wind attacked harder and frayed faster. The dead rose each time they were thrown down because the words they died awaiting had never arrived.

They began calling to the Xuanling birds circling overhead. Had they won? Where was the brother? Would anyone carry the answer home? The birds did not descend. Muyu had found an entire army caught midway through unanswered sentences and put weapons in its hands.

Yangyang could hold them all by opening beyond the limits of her body. She could become gorge-wide weather, take every voice inside, and disappear beside Wind. The choice promised that neither sister would ever arrive late for the other again.

Her blood, sticky and ordinary, kept her in the body. Her sword remained clenched in the other hand. She understood that as long as she held steel before them, she gave the dead the monster Muyu wanted them to see. She let the sword fall.

Lingfang’s bamboo xiao lay in the mud. The painter had carved a Xuanling near its mouthpiece badly, giving it too much wing. Yangyang had no breath left. She called Wind close by admitting exactly what she lacked and asked to borrow breath.

Wind stopped spending herself and came to Yangyang’s mouth as one thin draft. Together they forced sound through an instrument that had never played. The first note cracked. The second searched. The third found the lost half of the song.

The melody carried the distance between a traveler and the watched road, the place kept at a table, and the answer a messenger brings when it finally lands. Sound could pass through the dark without consuming Wind. The dead lowered their weapons. Lingfang recognized her husband’s useless xiao and heard it become useful only through two sisters sharing what neither possessed alone.

Yangyang told her that Xuanfang still stood. Her daughter survived. Her descendants sang the first half and left a space for her. Lingfang lowered the sword. For the first time in two centuries, one Xuanling bird folded its wings and descended.

In the physical Pavilion, Yangyang’s body began to fail. Chisa heard blood under the ribs and told Mei that the wound inside the Reverberation was crossing into flesh. Mei’s first impulse was to seize Yangyang and pull her away. Chisa stopped her. Yangyang was still choosing, not merely sinking. To drag her out without understanding might repeat every rescue that had treated her consent as expendable.

Mei held the open wound instead. She kept pressure with both hands, remained where Yangyang could return, and trusted the woman who had asked not to be caught before she was ready. Ciaccona listened to the cracked xiao emerging through the kernel. She recognized a duet being forced through a single visible body. Rather than covering it with virtuosity, she found the answering line and played it into the Pavilion.

The music crossed layers. The dead in the gorge heard a living bard answer. The faces in the Pavilion stopped screaming long enough to sing. Lingfang recovered the long note her husband’s instrument could never hold for her. Yangyang began the complete Song of Xuanling, call and response, but the blade wound emptied her lungs before each phrase could finish. Wind poured into the gaps. Ciaccona supplied the distant answer. The Reverberations, no longer turned toward a false monster, completed their own lines.

Yangyang could not restore their lives. She could restore direction. She gathered each voice not into an empty abyss but into the shape the voices had awaited: a messenger with a body small enough to land. The Xuanling birds above the gorge showed her how—open without grasping, carry without swallowing, wings that lean on air rather than own it.

The physical and remembered worlds aligned. Yangyang gave Wind the dinner itinerary as an anchor. Suisui would burn the first tray because she was talking. Their mother would set too many chopsticks. There would be steam, browned rice at the bottom of the pot, a gate left unlatched, and a sister pretending the extra bowl had appeared by accident. Wind could not be spent before dinner because dinner was already scheduled.

Her body folded into a Xuanling bird. The fatal line of Lingfang’s blade remained as a gap in the left wing. Wind filled the missing feathers, not by dissolving into everything but by becoming the precise part that had to hold still. For the first time, Wind’s duty was not to spend. One scrap thrown away in another gust would bring both of them down. She held the wounded wing in shape while Yangyang carried the body and the song.

The messenger rose through the Pavilion. Each dead voice became a bird long enough to carry its name out of Muyu’s architecture. Lingfang followed with the xiao’s melody and went home among them. The Pavilion faces recovered voices larger than rage; roof, hands, and kernel ceased to agree on the lie that had held them together. Chisa cut the final sky-seam. Ciaccona played the last answer. Mei kept both hands on the place where Yangyang’s body had been until the flock burst into the real air.

The Thousand-Puppet Pavilion came apart. Muyu covered his ears against the people he had curated into noise. His proof had failed because hatred remained real without being complete. He escaped the collapse, still unaccounted for, but the army he had pointed at humanity no longer belonged to him.

At the gate, the defenders saw impossible birds rise over Xuanfang. Autopuppets turned east. The Core shifted its note. The ancient dead crossed above the Hold that had survived them. Among the flock flew one messenger with a damaged left wing and two Yangyangs sharing its flight.

## The Other Gate

The border between worlds took payment from anything that crossed. Feathers vanished one by one. Darkness pressed failed lives and empty alternatives against the bird, asking it to open and become weather again. Yangyang sang the Xuanling call; Wind supplied the answer where the small chest ran out. One held the body, one held the wing, and neither could tell where the breath belonged while they crossed.

They found the correct world by a repeated call. In Wind’s surviving Mingting, the older Suisui had stood at the gate every dusk for years and called Yangyang’s name. She left the latch open. She floured the unused side of the board for a second batch. The persistence became an address loud enough to cross the dark.

The bird descended into a world scarred by flood but still inhabited. The red cap Wind once carried was dry at a shrine. Roads and courtyards had continued aging without her. At the border, the worlds enforced their rule: each could keep only one Yangyang as a woman. Wind received flesh, hands, face, and name because this was her book. The younger Yangyang remained the Xuanling bird because she was the visitor.

For the older Yangyang, embodiment returned as a sequence of elementary astonishments. Feet had weight. Water pushed against ankles. Hunger occupied a location instead of becoming a general ache in the weather. Sleep required lying down. Hands could fail at knots, hold bowls, and wash themselves. She walked toward Mingting following the bird who had been her younger self, relearning the pace at which a human body travels a road it cannot simply pour across.

Suisui waited at the unlatched gate. She had grey at both temples and flour on her thumb. She did not ask the returning woman to prove a cosmic identity. She recognized the cadence with which Yangyang stood in a doorway and adjusted her embrace around the body that finally had shoulders. Yangyang gave the word she had carried farther than any report: here.

The bird was accepted as family too. Suisui fed both of them, objected to their condition, named the bird Dumpling, and heated the bowl once because twice made rice tough. Older Yangyang ate the browned part from the bottom of the pot with her hands. She washed. She slept beside her sister. The black-iron practice post remained in the courtyard with twelve years of cuts and the old measure above them, but she did not immediately test herself against it. Survival did not have to earn its place before breakfast.

The younger Yangyang stayed four months in that world while only hours passed in her own. She lived as a bird in the courtyard, learned to fly around the gap in her wing, stole appropriate crumbs, and watched the older woman reacquire language, report voice, brush hand, and ordinary appetite. At the New Year, the older Yangyang picked up the wooden sword. No wind corrected her right elbow. She drew it inward herself, cut the post, uncovered what the measure said, and kept the result private. Then she went inside to a crowded table. A fifth pair of chopsticks waited nearest the door without anyone explaining for whom.

Before the solstice return, the older Yangyang wrote fifteen letters in a hand almost identical to her younger self’s. She addressed them not merely to people but to the places where each relationship had become real: a cook’s bowl, a keeper’s tower, Amy’s roof rail, Cartethyia’s third seat, Ciaccona’s fourth string, Chisa’s invisible cord, Lupa’s stairhead, Chixia’s red cap, Baizhi’s ledger, Suisui’s scarf, Iuno’s outward-turned cup, Mei’s side of a four-footed bed, and the younger Yangyang’s desk beneath the inkstone.

She told Panhua that the third bowl had found a hungry person. She told the tower keeper that an unmet return could still be counted whole. She told Amy that a night window mattered because someone had kept a light on, and that the red kite flew against the grain of the wind. She told Cartethyia the hidden third seat had been hers and could now be named because she possessed a body a threshold could catch. She thanked Ciaccona for holding a rest open and promised one bird, one winter, held. She told Chisa that the far end of her invisible string now had two hands and a scarred knuckle; if Chisa pulled, it would hold. She gave Lupa permission to throw food at the bundle of moments.

To Chixia she reported the cap dry and mended and honored the living girl for asking help rather than becoming an elegy. To Baizhi she acknowledged that the physician had filed weather as a person and had been the person beside a bed when measurement could not guarantee survival. To Suisui she described the scarf’s constant adjustments, the rehearsal that taught her to recognize the embrace in her own world. She confirmed that the other Suisui was still baking, still leaving flour, still unbearable about cake size, and declared that two versions could be sisters without one invalidating the other.

To Iuno she confessed that she had crossed worlds simply to be on time once and had sometimes hated the priestess who reached Mei’s shore first. She also understood Iuno’s deliberate one-hour delay at the beacon as the first gift ever offered to Wind at the correct speed. She advised which seal to use on a private Tuesday document and left two black squares where her hand could write neither love nor forgiveness without meaning both.

To Mei she remembered the rooftop cup, the punctuation Mei made from one scarce word a night, the completion of Wind’s broken name in wet sand, and the returned shell. She admitted she would spend the final word “tomorrow” again even though Yangyang had paid for it with unanswered questions. She blessed the canal walk, recognized Iuno’s hot tea, and left the same ambiguous blank where love and forgiveness shared a compartment. Every wind was met once; hers had been good.

The forty-second letter went to the younger Yangyang. Wind explained their shared elbow: when a sword grew heavy, the arm went looking for a wall and found air. She thanked Yangyang for correcting the teacher at the critical moment. She called “manners” the first thing anyone had ever said to her as a person with a correctable habit. She reported that spring reached Mingting, that the dinner itinerary was kept, that Suisui burned the first batch, and that “out, one” had become “back, two.” She described the crossing as a third thing wearing both sisters, breath reversing directions until it belonged to neither.

The border would not permit the older woman to visit as a woman. The bird could cross each winter. Wind therefore asked Yangyang to come home when it was cold, using a blank for the word Wind had spent but Yangyang still possessed.

On the winter solstice, older Yangyang read all fifteen letters aloud at the kitchen table. Paper could not survive the dark reliably; sound could. The bird listened from the table, supplying the missing word when the human voice stopped. Suisui tied the bundle to the bird’s breast with a bootlace from the tea drawer, fed her the browned side of steamed bread, and ordered Dumpling to return fat.

The return crossing was harder because Wind no longer filled the torn wing. The bird sang both halves alone, counting every rest like an Outrider so silence could not become dissolution. Darkness peeled away down and feathers. It showed her the cliff, the seductive ease of opening, and every shelf where arrival failed. She held still to the value of each rest and kept the knot against her chest. A thing read aloud in a warm kitchen remained where it had been spoken.

Four calls guided her to the correct Xuanfang: Mei setting a second cup on a roof rail and asking for tomorrow; Chixia greeting the half-latched window; Iuno’s boots waiting by the door; Suisui’s covered bowl still unwarmed. The ninth tower keeper saw a nearly plucked bird come over the north face and rang eight beats for a returning column of one, however constituted.

Mei reached the wall first but stopped four paces away. She had learned the difference between catching someone and standing where they could choose to arrive. She knelt in the snow, opened her palm, and warned that the stones rolled. The exhausted bird walked onto the offered hand. Amy counted a number returning. The bird said one, then confirmed she had come back different.

She folded into a woman on the parapet. Yangyang’s face returned, but the crossing remained visible: hair black at the crown and fading through electric blue to white feather-tips; a collar of cerulean plumage; sheer sleeves clouded from blue to transparency; layered flight-feather skirts; cyan ribbons trailing like a tail; and a blue Resonator mark on her brow. Chixia put the repaired red cap over it, pulled the crookedly sewn brim down, and said she could see Yangyang now. Yangyang criticized the stitching and then cried.

She insisted on walking down the beacon stair. Lupa withdrew the arms already prepared to carry her and instead matched every step. At the arch Yangyang placed the letters in Iuno’s hand. On the guesthouse step Suisui held the bowl she had kept covered for four months and heated once at the eighth beat. Yangyang relayed the older Suisui’s instruction about rice and described her grey temples, patched apron, and demand that Dumpling become fat. Present Suisui embraced Yangyang over the bowl and accepted the other woman as family because the advice was exactly as unbearable as her own.

Baizhi took Yangyang’s wrist, measured her collar, and entered the result: warm, quantified. Yangyang left the letter to herself beneath the inkstone, spoke “home” into its blank, and went downstairs because she was hungry.

Xuanfang’s institutions had to decide what to do with a person who had returned as a bird and unfolded into a changed woman. The ninth tower entered eight beats for a column of one and counted her whole. The Almanac Bureau found an unauthorized note beside the old heading for receiving wind and finally classified the second column as human. Bookbinders’ Row preserved the blank in Yangyang’s account at exactly the width she gave it rather than filling what she could not say. Huaxu placed the old and new numbers side by side instead of discarding either. The civil register remained open for later crossings.

The girl at the northeast wall sometimes still looked east for one breath. Then she went down and bought breakfast for the fishermen. The horizon had stopped being a place where she expected a missing person to solve her life by appearing. It became one direction among several roads she intended to travel.

## Learning to Be Returned

Return did not restore the earlier arrangement. Yangyang had a body again, but it rustled when she breathed, flushed visibly through the feather collar, and carried the impossible across every room. Huaxu found her medically normal and scientifically inexplicable. Her old Spectrum Pattern and Rabelle’s Curve remained nearly unchanged, yet no surviving Xuanling bird existed for comparison. Startorch in the New Federation might possess the required Resonator research, especially because Iuno already had an appointment there, but Yangyang placed the investigation behind Muyu, the refugees’ missing quilts, and Liangyu’s need to learn the complete song. She had been altered for only a day. Her feathers could wait their turn.

The Ministry noticed what Huaxu could not classify usefully. In the Snare, Yangyang had heard living breath beneath copied screams. It changed her posting by reply header from Outrider to Watcher. Suisui was furious that an office had reassigned her sister without an interview, family notice, or negotiated terms. Yangyang still wanted the work. If her ears could reach a disaster early, she might arrive before the survivors became a list of names. Suisui promised to read the order three times and then ruin someone’s week.

The household received the letters over breakfast. Before reading, everyone ate because older Yangyang had anticipated Mei’s impulse to open hers immediately and ordered otherwise. When Amy heard that she had a letter despite meeting Wind only once, Yangyang explained that Amy had held the kite steady. Wind had noticed.

Each letter caused an action. Amy retrieved the red kite. Cartethyia folded an invoice into a lopsided three-inch person for the third seat and placed it beside the camellia until it could be burned, because the missing person now had a body. Ciaccona wrote “one bird, one winter, held” beside a whole-bar rest and reserved the fourth string for a future landing. Chisa reached to the invisible cord leaving the door seam, pulled, and felt the heavier human woman at the far end. Lupa threw a jujube at Yangyang’s forehead mark because Wind had explicitly invited it.

While Yangyang read Suisui’s letter, Suisui kept making the exact adjustments the letter described—lifting a ribbon from the floor, flattening a feather, tucking hair behind an ear. At the instruction to tell the other Suisui where Wind was, she spoke into her sister’s blue hair: Yangyang was home, she was eating, and she still stole the largest cakes.

Iuno asked that her letter be read aloud, including the admission that Wind had hated her on some nights. The paragraph about the Tuesday paper remained sealed. At the ambiguous blank, Iuno looked as long as she once looked at a magistrate’s marks. Mei unconsciously turned the cup a quarter-turn just as Wind requested.

Mei asked to hear her own letter in the nearest thing to Wind’s voice. When it said Yangyang would hold Mei’s hand on a canal, the feather collar betrayed every feeling Yangyang’s report voice concealed. Wind apologized that “tomorrow” had cost her younger self the answers she wanted. Yangyang formally accepted. At the blank that might mean love or forgiveness, Mei answered, “Me too.”

Yangyang read the forty-second letter privately while the room became deliberately noisy. Lupa scraped a pot. Amy drew an absurd suspension diagram in spilled tea. Ciaccona played sound without melody. Cartethyia covered her ears and announced that she heard nothing. Mei began to reach across the floor, recognized the letter was not hers, and withdrew the hand. The room honored Yangyang’s privacy without leaving her alone.

When Yangyang finished, she amended the private count in her patrol log. She put both arms into the sleeves of Suisui’s oversized coat and went to the garrison practice post. She touched its old cuts but did not draw her blade. Not yet. Reaching a measure alone could remain a future act instead of a test required immediately after survival.

At noon they flew the red kite, now called Shimei because Wind had given Yangyang that same junior-sister title. Xuanfang’s great lift-kites filled the gorge, but the patched red scrap had crossed an ocean and carried letters. It turned against the gale exactly as Wind promised. Its string hummed a fourth.

Yangyang reported upward: fifteen letters delivered, Lupa threw, Cartethyia made a person from an invoice, Chisa pulled and found the string sound, Mei chose, Yangyang ate, both sleeves were on, and the count was amended. The formal voice ran out. She said she missed Wind.

The whole pack answered. Ciaccona matched the fourth. Lupa howled. Cartethyia shouted that she had made Wind a person. Amy called a crew report. Chisa raised a hand beneath the line. Mei had already told the fog she chose everybody and let the rest make enough noise for the absent woman to hear.

The kite climbed into a faster lane and pulled toward an easy, beautiful one-way departure. Yangyang’s thumb lifted from the spool. Suisui removed the fingers hooked into her collar and allowed the decision to belong entirely to Yangyang. Once, Yangyang would have released the errand into the sky and called the loss completion. She pressed her thumb down and reeled.

The ribs failed halfway. She stated the need without disguise: ribs; Jie; hold the spool. Suisui placed her hands over Yangyang’s rather than replacing them. Choosing was one person’s work, she said; carrying was two people’s. They brought the kite back together. Yangyang caught it against her chest and scheduled another flight for the next day, when Amy would fly and she would hold. The kite could go up and still return.

Yangyang also began speaking desires before “one day” could turn them into memorial wishes. On a Thursday after Huaxu’s examination, Mei carried Yangyang’s boots across the Hold despite her own healing wound. Neither woman could bend far enough to put them on, so they developed a two-person method with the boot braced between Mei’s heels and laughed until both injuries hurt.

They followed water climbing through carved fish and terraced ponds. Yangyang told Mei about the Watcher post. Mei told Yangyang that Amy dreamed of Septimont and a blue door, while Mei herself wanted one uninterrupted month. Then she disclosed that Iuno intended to ask her to marry her on a Tuesday and that she would say yes. Yangyang’s approval rested on the smallest proof: Iuno had learned to make Mei’s tea hot.

Yangyang then spoke a letter directly. She addressed Mei by name, reported the day, admitted she had seen the morning Mei woke, and asked to travel with the household for part of the way after Mengzhou. She would cross the dark every winter and often be late. She asked Mei to save a plate.

Mei answered in the same form. There would always be a plate by the stove. Yangyang should take Suisui’s scarf across the dark because it knew the way. Mei signed the spoken letter with love. Yangyang did not force the collar flat. She opened her palm and let Mei put a hand in it.

When Yangyang formally announced to the household that she would join their journey, she discovered they had counted her already. Lupa had included her in the rice. Cartethyia had folded a seat. Ciaccona had written her into three chapters. Amy had listed her under crew with “spool” beside the name. Suisui had priced eight berths and a bird ration, leaving the return open. “Some of the way” was the expensive ticket because no one could pretend to know where the way ended.

Mei and Iuno reclaimed an ordinary private dinner before Tuesday. Iuno chose a four-stool noodle counter rather than a grand restaurant because she had finally learned what Mei liked. She arranged a backed stool and quilts for the wound, ordered tea at four minutes, and negotiated the spice down from ruinous to merely ferocious. Iuno ate the red broth even after her lips went numb because Mei loved it and because surprise had become preferable to the prophetic life in which every morning already contained the knowledge that she would hurt Mei.

Tuesday itself remained deliberately unopened. Wind’s letter had told Iuno to use the certified copy of the Lahai-Roi freight return—the only foreign document on which Mei’s name appeared in a careful clerk’s hand—as the seal for the marriage paper. Iuno quietly asked Suisui to obtain it. Suisui named eleven days and a future favor, then demanded a place in the room. Mei knew the question was coming but refused to give Iuno the answer early. Iuno, who had once read entire lives in advance, chose to hear this one only when it was spoken.

They remembered their first dinner at Muka Muka in Septimont. Mei had rented a blue suit and bought a tie matched to Iuno’s hair from a portrait because she thought grandeur would please the former High Priestess. Iuno had hated the foams and would have returned every night because nobody had ever taken her somewhere simply to like her. Lupa had guarded the date from behind a fern, watched Mei eat the garnish and drink the lemon hand bowl, and watched Iuno immediately drink her own to shield Mei from humiliation.

By Xuanfang’s lantern pond, Mei asked what she could do for Iuno. Iuno told her the meaningful act had already happened: Iuno struggled through a bowl with one hand and no feeling in her mouth, and Mei refrained from helping. For someone accustomed to every kindness becoming a claim, being allowed to complete a difficult, unnecessary act without rescue was a new form of care.

Mei then asked to speak directly to the High Priestess living within Iuno. She thanked that proud inner voice for keeping Iuno alive through the years when nobody else did. The High Priestess admitted she had deliberately let Mei’s tea go cold, waiting for a complaint she could use as distance. Mei had never complained. The High Priestess apologized and confessed that she too had missed Mei after the Nihility. Iuno and the woman she had been were not rivals for Mei’s affection; both were present at the same lit pond.

They bought two noodle tins to carry home. One belonged to the plate by the stove. Yangyang called it good before tasting, then ate it beside Suisui. The household stayed awake recounting the catastrophic Muka Muka date until Amy, who called the entire romance gross, pulled the blanket to her chin and remained for every word.

Around these larger declarations, the rest of the pack repaired its own habits. Lupa’s vigilance stopped pretending to be coincidence. When Mei called into the morning for Wind, Lupa told her to wake the wolf next time so the entire pack could shout. She put hot tea into wounded hands, covered bare feet with her tail, guarded doors without denying fear, and learned that standing watch could include letting the watched person choose.

Chisa continued to say the sentence everyone else tried to soften. She identified when Amy’s “don’t worry” was loaded and waited until Amy changed it to “worry a little.” She tested Wind’s invisible string instead of demanding proof visible to others. She carried the frequency of the Jade Heart without claiming ownership over it. Her repaired bowls kept their seams visible, and when Cartethyia dropped one she stitched it crookedly and returned it with the assurance that the scar now made it hers.

Ciaccona learned when not to play. She had accompanied voices across centuries, but when Liangyu, Yangyang, and Suisui first completed the family song at the overlook, she left the lute silent and let the moment belong to them. Later she supplied the answer only when Yangyang’s duet in the Pavilion required another breath. Her rests became active spaces where another person could enter rather than absences she rushed to ornament.

Cartethyia continued to turn invoices into people, save damaged plants, and make visible seats for those others treated as abstractions. She hung lanterns high in Fleurdelys’s form, yet also asked before approaching Yangyang’s reactive collar after breakfast. Ciaccona reeled her back when enthusiasm crossed a guard. Their closeness became less dependent on one woman translating the world for the other; both could call, wait, apologize, and answer.

Chixia recovered far from the central confrontations but remained present in every red cap and crooked seam. Her survival was the first route Wind successfully completed and the reason Yangyang’s checkpoint choice could never be dismissed as meaningless. Baizhi’s precise medicine kept that route from becoming another elegy. When the red cap finally returned to Yangyang’s changed head, Chixia did not ask for the woman who had left. She recognized the one who came back.

The shell from Yangyang’s mother brought one final temptation. A mysterious Voice drew her into the first morning at the Gorges as it existed beneath all printed worlds: Chixia, Baizhi, and a younger Yangyang reached the sleeping Mei before Iuno. The morning had never occurred in either surviving world. It was the pressure groove left on the sheet beneath a discarded page, the origin of Wind’s forgotten address and Yangyang’s forty-one letters to someone she had never met.

The Voice offered Yangyang her own sword. If she stepped in front of Iuno, Mei would wake first to the Outrider. Jinzhou might become Mei’s first city. Canal walks would be ordinary rather than deferred. Perhaps Wind’s world would never flood and no sister would become weather.

As Yangyang considered it, the sleeping face began to settle toward the version she herself would have written: patient, careful, shaped by her desires. It was not the Mei she knew. The real face contained Iuno’s cold tea, Amy’s rescue, Lupa’s boot, Ciaccona’s songs, Cartethyia’s paper people, and every choice made beyond Yangyang’s control. Removing Iuno would remove those strokes. The result would be a doll wearing Mei’s eyes.

Wind had crossed the dark to defend Mei’s choice against prophecy. Rewriting the shore in Wind’s name would also steal the older Yangyang’s chosen life in Mingting. Yangyang drew her elbow in and stepped aside. Iuno descended in silver-blue. Yangyang whispered that Mei would drink cold tea without complaint and Iuno should make it hot sooner. The priestess could not hear words, but paused when the pressure touched her cheek.

Iuno knelt. Mei’s face settled into the known one and her gold eyes opened. Across the rocks, the younger Yangyang sensed someone present. The older visitor crouched brim to brim with the girl no one could see and told her they were always late, but what arrived could still be theirs. Mei looked past the written morning toward the one person it did not contain. Yangyang introduced herself and apologized for being late. Mei smiled and said hi.

Back at Huaxu, Yangyang reported that she had been offered a sword and refused it. She cried for the lovely life she did not take. Suisui held her and agreed that a right refusal could still cost something. Yangyang called herself and Wind the sheet beneath the torn page. Suisui, thinking like a merchant, replied that the copy retained in the book was the one that counted at audit.

At New Year Yangyang returned to Mingting in the body the crossing had made. Her younger Suisui waited at the gate with the ordinary bird Dumpling on her shoulder. Yangyang finally said “home” there, not merely “back,” “leave,” or “the ladle is where you put it.” In the courtyard she took the wooden sword alone. No Wind corrected the elbow; she corrected it herself, struck the iron post, uncovered the private measure, and went inside to eat rather than remaining outside to turn the result into judgment.

Her future had become concrete without becoming fixed. She would work as a Watcher if the Ministry gave the ears a useful place. She would investigate the Xuanling changes at Startorch later. She would cross as a bird each winter. She would travel partway with Mei’s household, attend or miss Tuesday as the roads allowed, visit Mingting, walk the Jinzhou canal, and arrive late to many plates that would still be kept warm. None of these plans required certainty to be real.

Mei, Iuno, and Amy also began naming a destination beyond Xuanfang. Sitting under a veranda while a storm washed cherry blossoms into the canal, Amy asked them to take her to Septimont. She wanted to see the Basilica robes Iuno kept under ceremonial security, fly above the city on Iuno’s moonwheel, and find the balcony Mei remembered for its view and Iuno remembered for its draft. Iuno admitted she missed the fifth-hour bells and the certainty she had once hated. Mei missed the steps, vendors, fountains, and the place where cold tea had become companionship.

Amy asked whether their old residence had a blue door. It had white marble and gold leaf. They would paint one. Iuno told them about Problem, the one-eared orange cloister cat she fed sardines beneath her desk against temple rules. Amy promised to find Problem’s grave and leave flowers, then added a new cat and heating vent to the itinerary. The city Iuno had fled was no longer merely the site of an old role. They could return to it as three people carrying paint.

## Moon Waking

Xuanfang’s first great communal act after the battle was not a military review. It was Moon Waking, the mid-autumn festival whose instructions stood on a board beneath the resonance spire in a hand the Hold attributed to Hsin. For four hundred years the people had repainted eight lines: open the ovens before sunrise; hide a fox in every tray; dress the guardians and turn their faces east; light a lantern for every name; set a place facing the gate; wake the moon if she was slow; chase the fox; sleep late while the Sentinel watched the walls.

After the birds rose and Muyu’s army lost its rage, Liangyu added three hard-pressed characters beneath the eighth line: they would keep the walls. By festival morning other marks had begun to gather beneath hers.

Suisui woke before Iuno, an unprecedented domestic victory, and mobilized the storehouse before dawn. Yangyang put on both sleeves. The household crossed a square where stalls were rising beneath the jade-lit spire. A small blue bird stole a candied haw and flew over the awnings with a slight stiffness in one wing. Suisui insisted it was somebody else’s bird.

The eleven public ovens had been cut into the cliff by Hsin before the Fortress and Gorges War. During the eight-month siege, the entire Hold survived on millet and whatever remained in household jars. Every Moon Waking since, each family had baked one tray of siege cakes so the ovens would remember. This year, after an ancient army had walked through the living city again, the ovens were asked to remember everything.

The moulds had been carved by besieged hands that needed something beautiful to do. They shaped curled foxes, a moon with a fox inside, the Core in its rings, and Xuanling birds with spread wings. Each cake received a curved closed eye; the heat opened it.

The household pressed millet dough with jujube and ginger. Chisa produced exact edges by touch. Iuno weighed each portion to the grain with one hand. Cartethyia greeted every moulded fox as an individual. Mei’s cakes became turtles despite the mould and emerged with astonished eyes. Yangyang pressed a flour-backed Xuanling onto her palm. Suisui told her not to eat it because it was her; Yangyang replied that the cake had better posture.

Liangyu completed a night round on the parapet above them. Suisui’s first question was whether the Warden had eaten. When Yangyang replied only that Liangyu was on the wall, Suisui pointed out that a location was not food. She made one extra bird cake and set it apart.

Each tray was supposed to contain one hidden fox: any surprise the baker found funny. Whoever bit a fox became prey at moonrise; whoever caught one could ask a question that festival law required the fox to answer truthfully. The household proved incapable of restraint. Lupa hid a dried chili. Amy added pickled mustard greens. Iuno enclosed a salted plum. Cartethyia folded a tiny paper person so a fox would have company. Ciaccona hollowed a cake and called the emptiness a rest. Mei added a second salted yolk and defended generosity as foxlike. Chisa heard every insertion. Yangyang and Suisui obeyed the written rule and added nothing.

The blue bird dropped its stolen candied haw into a seventh cake while they were not admitting to their crimes. The oven-keeper counted seven foxes and accepted that the chase would be good.

The next instruction was harder. The guardians still stood in battle rows beneath the overlook. For centuries Xuanfang had dressed these Autopuppets in ribbons and lanterns. Three nights earlier the same machines, filled with Muyu’s redirected dead, had butchered Wardens and hunted civilians. Families stood at the terrace stair holding festival colors and refused to descend.

Liangyu went first. She tied a red ribbon around the arm of the nearest guardian, exactly where Qingzhi had approached when she thought something was caught in its joint. Her hands shook. Chisa followed, placed a palm against the machine, and listened. Muyu’s voices were gone. Lupa shouted the result to the entire terrace: the guardians were empty.

An old man tied the next ribbon with a fisherman’s knot and patted the armor like a horse. A girl ran down with a lantern bigger than her head. Then the Hold poured among the machines with its arms full of color. Lupa hung lanterns from raised hands. Mei tied lopsided bows. Iuno counted all two hundred and twelve guardians because they were on the list. Fleurdelys rose taller than the machines and placed lanterns on their heads as gently as hats on sleeping relatives.

The eldest guardian, Lao Da, traditionally carried candied haws for Hsin because the Sentinel was said to sit on his shoulder during Moon Waking. Qingzhi’s young brother Qingyan held the offering she had once tied. Fleurdelys knelt, waited for his consent, and lifted him on an open palm. He secured the haws with the same doubled-back knot that had marked his sister’s sword tassel and hummed her verse of the homecoming song. He sat on Lao Da’s shoulder looking east. The small blue bird landed above him and accepted his greeting.

By noon Xuanfang had exhausted its lantern paper. People continued with ration slips, intake forms, wrapping, kite scraps, finished ledgers, and disputed Guild invoices. Every lantern carried a name written or spoken so the moon would know whom to seek.

Suisui spent real paper on a painted Xuanling: blue body, sleeves fading to glass, white at every tip. She had painted one every festival since Yangyang was six, including the years her sister was absent, and stored the previous nine on the top shelf. Yangyang sat as the model while the collar caught sunlight and learned how faithfully Suisui had maintained a version of her at home.

Mei dictated names from several lives—Lynae, Rebecca, Zani, Augusta, Galbrena, Uncle Cosimo, Lilith, Phrolova—and called the noodle cook “Four Minutes” because no one had asked her proper name. Iuno wrote the cloister cat Problem in Septimont script. Lupa said Cantarella, Mornye, Mya, and Sumika aloud as she folded. Chisa touched the red string at her wrist. Ciaccona drew a whole-bar rest, wrote its value, added an uneven star, and left room for anyone whose name could not yet be said.

Yangyang’s lanterns named Duzhong, Qingzhi, Qingxiao, Mingyu, Yanlang, and Lingfang. Then she wrote Yangyang for the sister in the other world. Suisui made her add shijie so the moon could distinguish them.

At the Hold’s one functioning long-relay, each caller received four minutes. Liangyu monitored the sandglass with the gentleness of someone waking sleepers. Yangyang and Suisui reached Mingting. Their father announced that their mother had made too many osmanthus cakes and he was eating the large ones. Their mother asked whether the shell arrived. She had set the usual places because it was the festival.

Yangyang placed a palm against the booth and told her that the other Yangyang had reached home. Silence crossed the entire line. Then their mother said “Good” and told Yangyang to eat. The family’s largest impossibility was received in the same register as a daughter returning late for dinner.

Liangyu’s own call could not reach Mengzhou because the eastern masts had been destroyed. Amy saw what habit concealed: a freight cable was also a very long wire. The household spent the afternoon turning infrastructure into contact. Iuno guided the lane-master through his own clauses and signed for a telephone. Suisui paid an itemized toll. Fleurdelys hooked Amy’s lead onto the anchor cable beyond any ladder. Lupa guarded the ladder anyway. Chisa listened to the cable and found it hummed a fourth; Ciaccona matched it. Liangyu wrote her grandmother’s code and entrusted it to Amy.

At dusk, every Xuanfang household carried its table outside. The boards joined from gate to overlook until the city ate at one continuous table. Each segment left one place facing the gate for whoever remained on the road.

Suisui laid bowls, chopsticks, and gauze swans. She turned the gate cup toward the road. Everyone saw a different absent person in the seat. Then Suisui sent Amy to fetch Liangyu.

The Warden refused at first. The chair belonged to someone still out there. Lupa told her that she had been on the road since Xianling. The oven-keeper confirmed that the seat was designed for exactly the person who mistook watchfulness for ineligibility. Liangyu sat facing the gate. Suisui placed the bird cake and a paper swan before her.

The household discovered its foxes. Iuno, whose tongue remained numbed by the noodle date, received Lupa’s chili and could not appreciate it. Lupa bit Amy’s mustard greens and demanded the culprit through tears. Chisa found Cartethyia’s damp paper person and thanked her. Ciaccona’s face collapsed around Iuno’s salted plum. Mei found Ciaccona’s empty rest and enjoyed it. Yangyang opened Mei’s cake to find two moon-colored yolks side by side, recalling the two moons she had once seen on the same water while her body dissolved.

Suisui bit the unclaimed candied haw. The Prism wrapped in silk inside her sleeve grew warm. Above the table the blue bird watched from a lantern pole. The oven-keeper said that in some years one fox appeared that nobody had hidden; that one belonged to Hsin. Suisui called the Sentinel a sore loser.

The moon remained below the traditional ninth spire. Xuanfang stood and went up to wake her.

The terraces filled from gate to cliff top, every tier holding lanterns. Decorated guardians faced east below; thousands of named lights hung from the freight lanes above; the Jade Heart turned openly in its rings. Amy patched the freight-line handset into the muster horns. Liangyu held it with both hands while the distant connection rang.

Her grandmother answered from Mengzhou and asked whether the festival was happening there too. Liangyu’s laugh broke. She told her to listen and began the first Xuanling call.

Her voice went through every brass horn. Five thousand people returned the line. Old men sang with closed eyes; children entered early; Xianling villagers and Skyworks workers learned by the third call because the mechanism required only listening and answering.

At the verse Qingzhi had always sung, Liangyu stopped. The Hold entered the gap thinking it was a breath. Qingyan took his sister’s line in a boy’s voice that thinned at the high notes but did not break. Xuanfang answered him without changing its volume. Other lost entrances arrived deliberately late. Ciaccona carried Duzhong’s part.

Then the song reached the two-hundred-year rest where every Moon Waking performance had ended. The entire Hold stopped from inherited habit. Liangyu sang the first line of the recovered half alone.

An old woman in Mengzhou answered through the freight wire.

The Hold inhaled together. On the next line it answered with her. The second half continued across the karst: Liangyu giving calls learned from Yangyang, her grandmother returning them along a cable, thousands of people learning by reply. When the road verse came, Suisui sang flat but on time. Three nights earlier she had stopped there; now she finished.

When the song called for the bird, the people turned toward Yangyang. Her collar rose. The blue bird landed beside Liangyu’s hand. Yangyang gave the answer in the level voice she used when confirming a task already completed.

The moon came up on the final line, enormous and gold—and two spires left of where four centuries of tradition expected it. All five thousand people shuffled left. An old man laughed that Hsin had moved it. The laughter ran down the terraces. The Heart sounded one low, round, cracked note. Lift-kites climbed and carried thousands of name lanterns upward in long gold lines. The guardians’ decorated faces received moonlight. The song was complete without making the old absence vanish.

Liangyu’s handset remained live. Her grandmother’s first private question was whether she had eaten. Liangyu looked toward the half cake wrapped in its swan and said yes. The answer was not wholly true yet, but it would be.

Then the fox chase broke the city apart in joy. Grandmothers, soldiers, children, and refugees ran across roofs and freight lanes under the awakened moon. Catchers invoked the law of truth. Cartethyia found Lupa pretending to study a wall and asked whether she had cried behind the Muka Muka fern. Four centuries of festival law forced Lupa to say yes. Amy found Mei and Iuno openly resting beside the second pond. Iuno admitted the lemon hand water had been dreadful. Amy asked Mei whether the wound still hurt. Mei answered yes. Amy closed the notebook, sat between her parents, and did not turn the pain into an emergency or a joke.

Ciaccona hid inside a melody on the spire steps; Cartethyia followed the sound and asked whether her dangerous seventh tie had really been her best. Ciaccona answered that it had nearly hanged Mei and was her favorite knot in the world. Chisa sat openly on Lao Da’s foot and escaped detection because everyone assumed someone else must already have caught her.

Yangyang carried Suisui’s lit Xuanling lantern east through quiet lower lanes. At the postern she found Suisui looking down the old road and calling Yangyang’s name in the voice used to summon someone for dinner. Yangyang answered from behind her.

One fox could not legally catch another, so Yangyang called the exchange a conversation and asked whom Suisui had been calling. Suisui admitted she had called Yangyang at dinner on most nights since the day her sister left Mingting, sometimes from Guild stairs. After the second year, their mother had begun leaving the door open for her.

Yangyang looked at the painted Xuanling. Suisui’s near wing was too thin, exactly as their mother had always warned. Yangyang pushed her thumb through it.

Suisui cried out, then saw the light through the torn wing and laughed the loud laugh she kept out of trade meetings. The new lantern joined the nine stored ones not as a sacred perfect image but as the tenth her younger sister had personally torn. Liangyu found them at the gate, understood the privacy, and called back to the hunters that the east gate was clear. The blue bird settled over the lintel.

Later Suisui sent Yangyang to the wall with the remaining half of Liangyu’s cake and required it to be eaten before a witness. Liangyu obeyed. She confessed that she had written the chalk beneath Hsin’s final line because somebody had to see to the walls. Sixty-one names had joined by dinner. She ordered the newly appointed Watcher to watch the inside of her eyelids while Liangyu resumed the north round. The blue bird kept pace in short hops along the parapet.

The Hold slept late. The ovens cooled. The continuous tables remained in the terraces with gate places still set. The household collapsed beneath quilts, pomelo helmets, swans, and spent sparklers. Lupa slept upright at the door. The torn Xuanling lantern hung on a hook with the rip deliberately facing the room.

Iuno woke first and, for once, let the kettle wait. Frost covered the square. Beneath Hsin’s instruction and Liangyu’s promise, the chalk had spread down the board and onto the paving stones: names, Warden numbers, family marks, thumbprints, a guardian carrying a lantern, Qingzhi and Duzhong written side by side, Four Minutes in a cook’s hand, and Lupa’s long claw mark. The entire Hold had signed itself beneath the promise to keep the walls and then trusted itself enough to sleep.

In fresh frost at the board’s corner lay a small paw print, countersigning them.

Iuno walked to the guardian terrace with two cups. Lao Da still carried the haws in Qingyan’s doubled-back knot, but the top fruit was gone. The blue bird slept on the guardian’s head with sugar on its beak. Iuno placed the second cup on Lao Da’s foot, handle outward. Perhaps Hsin had come. Perhaps only a bird had. The High Priestess told her to let the absent Sentinel have her own festival.

Steam rose and leaned east.

At the beginning, Xuanfang’s gates had closed behind missing travelers, its trusted guardians had become murderers, its Sentinel manipulated people from fragments and wager boards, and Yangyang stood alone at a broken road believing that rescue required choosing who could be left. Wind existed as an unfiled absence whose first instinct was to erase herself. Suisui waited in Mingting without knowing how many sisters she was calling. Liangyu carried half a song. The ancient dead had no answer, and the living Hold relied on a Heart whose faith allowed no guard.

By the morning after Moon Waking, the gates faced tables with places for those still traveling. The guardians stood empty of stolen rage and dressed by the families who had feared them. The Hold had learned the lost half of its song from the dead, returned it through a grandmother’s living voice, and signed its own names beneath the duty Hsin once claimed alone. The Jade Heart remained exposed, Muyu remained at large, and Hsin had not been absolved; uncertainty had not disappeared. It had ceased to be an excuse for spending whoever stood nearest.

Yangyang was no longer the solitary Outrider who made herself light enough to abandon. She was a changed woman, a Watcher with feathers, a traveler carrying an open return, a younger sister who could ask for a spool to be held, a senior sister to a kite, and a winter bird between two homes. Wind had hands, meals, a practice post, and an older Suisui in the world where she belonged. Present Suisui had ten torn lanterns, two correspondents, and an answer when she called at dinner. Mei, Iuno, and Amy had a blue door to paint in Septimont. The pack had plates, routes, names, and promises that could be revised without being broken.

The moon was roundest on the sixteenth, after everyone had gone home from the day meant to wake it.

### The Terrace Goes West

Xuanfang Hold has survived, but survival has not restored the old order. Its stone terraces can move through the mountains; its rebuilt channels carry boats between new walls; its ministries, markets, and academy courts have reopened beneath a sky still burdened by old machinery. Yet Sentinel Hsin has withdrawn behind the sealed doors of Fogveil Pagoda, the Astrolabe Core turns above an empty office, and the Hold’s defenders keep watch over a peace whose foundations they no longer trust. The displaced and wounded crowd its storerooms. Roofs wait under new tarpaulins, the gorge road remains uncertain, and every household keeps a place ready for someone who may arrive late.

Yangyang enters this uneasy peace changed in body and in place. She is no longer only the quiet Jinzhou Outrider who habitually makes herself useful before anyone can ask whether she is hurt. She has crossed the dark as a Xuanling bird, carried her shijie home through the space between worlds, and returned with blue-white hair, a living collar of cerulean plumage, and three cracked ribs. Her shijie is another Yangyang from a drowned, unrealized Jinzhou: a woman who surrendered flesh and most of her name to become the Guest Wind, crossed realities carrying her mother’s shell and an address for Mei, and spent years arriving too early or too late to complete the simple errand that had consumed her existence. Once Mei finally woke on the right shore and gave the Wind a name that could hold, the living Yangyang inherited not merely her memory, but a sisterly lineage of watchfulness, lateness, and impossible return. Now that shijie lives beyond the winter dark with an older counterpart of Suisui, Yangyang keeps a list of ordinary things to tell her at the solstice: what food was served, who complained about the weather, which roof leaked, and how the people who survived used the morning she bought them.

The household around Yangyang is equally battered and equally determined to behave as if living together is work worth doing. Mei, the golden-eyed Arbiter who once wandered through centuries and now remembers little before her latest awakening, is healing from a wound through her abdomen. Iuno has lost her left arm and much of the prophetic certainty that defined her thirty years as Septimont’s High Priestess, but carries a velvet ring box cut from her own shoulder crystal and waits for a Tuesday on which to ask Mei to marry her. Amy, Mei’s daughter, alternates between reckless mechanical brilliance and a child’s vigilance for the punishment she expects to follow every good day. Lupa feeds everyone, threatens anyone who ignores a bandage, and hides the old arena hunger that can turn care into fury. Chisa, still wearing the suppression collar that marks what was done to her, answers noise with plain observation and remains the one voice capable of calling Lupa back from bloodlust. Cartethyia and Ciaccona carry their own recovered selves—the small girl and towering Fleurdelys, the bard’s wound and the paladin’s borrowed grandeur—without yet believing every part of them belongs to the same person.

In the first days after the battle, the Hold lets these survivors practice staying. They tour its newly opened streets and watercourses. Yangyang and Chisa take the green boat together while Amy’s vessel promptly drains itself. Suisui buys opera tickets instead of auditing the theatre. Mei, Lupa, Cartethyia, Iuno, and Ciaccona argue over mechanisms, food, and geese. At tea, an entire terrace travels west while they remain seated upon it, proving that the ground can move without scattering everyone who stands on it. Amy arranges a group photograph in which Suisui’s scarf is wrapped around Yangyang’s shoulders and every living member of the household fits inside one frame. That evening Yangyang sets a little blue turbine in the east window, where it will turn in the mountain wind, and records the day for shijie: the terrace went west; they stayed on it.

When the long-distance lines reopen, private survival joins a larger map of voices. Calls go out to Lahai-Roi, Ragunna, Septimont, Jinzhou, and the far houses where friends have been counting names. Chisa hears her mother. Ciaccona reaches the old networks that still remember her music. Augusta and Galbrena answer from a domestic life that once seemed impossible. Herta, Black Swan, and Phrolova keep their own tally of seven names until confirmation comes. Each conversation begins differently and reaches the same urgent facts: whose hands still work, who is eating, which bed is occupied, and whether a chair must remain empty.

Yangyang’s Jinzhou line releases sixty-one messages that Chixia sent into eleven days of silence. Wounded and confined to Mrs. Ren’s front room with fourteen stitches, a bad leg, and a crutch, Chixia had continued writing as though persistence itself could keep Yangyang alive: reports about soup, a broken window latch, the lantern kept burning, shijie’s blanket, and the exact place by the stove where Yangyang ought to be. When they finally speak, Yangyang refuses to turn the reunion into a clean recital over a wire. She tells Chixia that she became the bird, crossed the dark, carried shijie to an older Suisui, and came back. She also admits that she holds a physical letter addressed to “the harbour ward, the red cap, the inside of the lining.” She will not read it remotely. Shijie wrote it for the room, and Yangyang will bring it to that room herself. She offers only the first word—“Chixia”—and Chixia laughs and cries because the voice on the line is alive enough to make promises.

Baizhi’s call is quieter and no less intimate. She asks clinical questions about Yangyang’s changed resonance and new feathers, but beneath her precision lies the truth that she carried a replacement battery to the gate morning after morning and kept Chixia alive while waiting for news. When Chixia’s temperature fell, Baizhi committed what she calls an error of procedure: she spoke her wish aloud again, and the wish was plural. She wanted both Chixia and Yangyang to wake. Yangyang adds “Plural” to the patrol log beside the practical entries. Baizhi opens a narrow column in her incident ledger for shijie’s letter, to be read when all of them share the room.

The household’s first victories are deliberately small. Amy asks Chisa to go shopping without Mei supervising them. They buy matching summer uniforms, a viciously green star-shaped hair clip, and iced taro milk, reclaiming an adolescence neither girl was allowed to keep. Iuno persuades Mei that letting Amy leave without her is not abandonment: Amy is finally safe enough to spend a day being ridiculous. Elsewhere, Iuno finds an old arena wrapper whose image preserves Lupa while leaving the High Priestess’s chair empty. Lupa insists that Iuno sign it too, restoring in ink the woman history cropped away. Chisa ties her pale blue school tie around Lupa’s scarred throat and promises to make one that properly fits; Lupa claims she will never wear it, then sleeps with it knotted over her vest.

Mei’s own habits are harder to retrain. When Sir Honk the goose steals a small crown onto a roof, her first instinct is to climb after it despite her stitches. Instead, remembering every recent argument about disappearing under beams alone, she walks into the household and says that there is a goose and she needs a ladder. The request brings everyone running. The same ladder then exposes a relief contractor who stole twelve tarps and roofing slates; by evening three vulnerable roofs are covered, the goose is knighted, and Mei has remained on the ground serving as Clerk of Works rather than bleeding unseen on a ridge.

At night, Iuno finds Mei grieving the childhood of Amy she cannot remember. Mei fears that the empty half of her memory makes her a counterfeit mother. Iuno refuses the grand penance Mei wants to impose on herself. Amy does not need to be interrogated for twelve years of missing history; she needs an ordinary mother now. Mei therefore asks one foolish question about whether Amy ever had bangs. Amy answers with the story of shears, birch sap, a winter hat, and two Februaries created because Mei once drew a winch diagram on the kitchen calendar. The memory arrives not as evidence in a trial, but as family banter. Later, in bed between Mei and Iuno, Amy supplies still more details while pretending to be asleep. Mei learns to receive them without turning each one into a wound.

The same room teaches everyone how to be held. Ciaccona rubs wintergreen salve into Cartethyia’s aching arms after Lupa’s brutal sword drills and answers the knight’s fear that Fleurdelys is only a costume copied from chapel saints. A singer may learn her voice from someone large enough to fill a hall, Ciaccona tells her, but the voice becomes her own when she must use it alone. Yangyang returns late from the parapet to find two buns and hot ginger broth kept under a bowl on the warm hearthstone. She eats both because Lupa, apparently asleep, counts every bite. Then she slips beside Suisui, plants ice-cold feet against her sister’s calves as she did in childhood, and lets Suisui ask whether her new sword arm feels alien. Yangyang explains that the sword once felt like borrowed weight she had to carry correctly until supper; now the bone of her shoulder seems to continue all the way to the point. She no longer grips so hard to remain herself.

Mei and Amy reach the same lesson through anger. At the Skyworks, Amy finally tells Mei what it cost when Mei once called her grown and then vanished into danger: the praise became an order to handle frightening things alone. Amy does not want another apology polished into self-punishment. She wants the right to remain furious without Mei treating that fury as proof she should leave. Mei drops the grin she uses to soften every room and tells Amy to be mad. She promises to remain where Amy can be mad at her for a hundred years. Later, Amy pilots Nyx home while her injured body rides in a cart with a damaged wheel. The household walks beside it chanting that nobody is angry at the wheel, and Amy adds, quietly, “Nobody’s mad at you, Ma.”

An uneventful day frightens Amy almost as much as battle. She waits for the hidden bill behind hot chocolate, stories, and a bed full of people who came home. Mei admits that she too searches every good moment for the knife that must follow it. Neither can promise the world has stopped charging costs. They can promise that no one will meet the bill alone. The next time Mei disappears for forty-eight hours on a two-hour errand—rescuing a mule and propping three strangers’ roofs—the household feeds and warms her before Amy confronts her. Mei no longer lives in a cabin beneath four kilometres of ice, where only one pair of shoulders can reach the beam. There is not only one of her now. She must ask for the ladder.

At Yuan’s Flavors, the price of free food is a name in the proprietor’s ledger: one contact for where a person comes from and another for where she should be returned if she is late. Suisui claims the first line for Yangyang. Yangyang slides the ledger to Mei for the second, and Mei writes that she will be saving a plate. The household fills the margins with signatures while Suisui adds an instruction to keep it warm. Yangyang does not choose between the sister who formed her and the wandering family that waits for her; the ledger requires both columns.

### The Empty Shrine

Suisui cannot remain at the Hold. A river-lock crisis in Mengzhou threatens the winter grain of sixteen thousand people, and duty calls her back onto the road. Yangyang responds with a rare, childish sulk: she turns away on the bench, answers in clipped syllables, and eats the largest honeyed osmanthus cake from the middle of the plate. Suisui is secretly delighted. Her younger sister has spent years making every emotion convenient for others; petty resentment means she trusts Suisui enough to be difficult.

The separation still terrifies both of them. Liangyu reports that Hsin’s office remains empty, the shrines are cold, Qingxiao has vanished into the upper mist, and the Wardens are badly undermanned. Suisui has the authority to order Yangyang into the safety of her coach, but refuses to disguise fear as command. Instead, the household converts her requested quiet departure into a predawn procession. Lupa hides smoked meat and an axle-pin among Suisui’s silk clothes. Cartethyia recruits Sir Honk as vanguard. Ciaccona composes an unauthorized ballad. Iuno dresses Suisui’s hair while Amy heats her cloak and Yangyang serves her shelled egg and porridge. For once, the woman who habitually notices every cold shoulder and empty cup is pinned to a stool and cared for until she laughs through tears.

At the coach, Suisui gives Hsin’s amber Prism of Fate to Yangyang. She no longer treats it as a sacred hostage or the proof of a contest with the Sentinel. The stone was only the receipt for winning her sister back. Dumpling, the clockwork Rafter Kite, abandons Suisui’s cloak for Yangyang’s feathered collar. Suisui promises to expect her at the Mingting gate on the twenty-eighth day of the twelfth month before the dumpling water boils. After the coach rolls away, she discovers two extra walnut cakes and a folded note in her tin: Yangyang stole the large cake only to give her a reason to scold.

The Prism remains warm in Yangyang’s hands, but the Sentinel who owns it remains silent. At breakfast, the household tells Yangyang what Sentinels are really like. Jué, grand and ancient, could still be trapped by duty and require Jinhsi to force a transfer of mandate. Rinascita’s Imperator concluded an age of divine conflict by driving away in an Old Federation automobile with Leviathan. Baldur, the subterranean Exostrider, had to be operated from inside like a six-person construction crane. Divinity has never guaranteed wisdom, certainty, or grace.

They infer that Hsin is not dead and has not wholly abandoned Xuanfang. The Astrolabe still turns and the terraces still balance. She is hiding because Muyu, the master artisan she trusted, used that trust to butcher her people, and because Suisui’s refusal to sacrifice the Guest Wind exposed the cruelty of Hsin’s own fatal arithmetic. Hsin is trying to become the floor: useful, load-bearing, and unavailable for forgiveness. Yangyang recognizes the impulse because Mei has done the same thing with collapsing roofs and because she herself once tried to become weather. She decides to return the Prism directly. In Mingting, one returns a game stone to the owner’s hand, not to an empty bowl.

The household climbs through snow to Fogveil Pagoda, where the ceremonial doors refuse to open and a pantry entrance stands propped with a kiln brick. Inside a folding maze of cedar galleries, they meet Qingxiao. The celebrated Sword Transcendent has not ascended or vanished; she went upstairs to kill Discords in the wainscoting, and a confused clerk turned routine extermination into legend. She recognizes the household from Ministry files, notices Yangyang’s changed plumage, and has an unspoken moment of recognition with Mei, whose face belongs to a war two centuries old even though Mei herself remembers none of it.

At the inner sanctuary, Hsin opens only a six-inch deposit hatch with a velvet cradle for the Prism. Yangyang refuses to slide it through. Liangyu is still sweeping the front steps and the Hold is still waiting. Hsin must open the door and receive her stone in person.

An alarm interrupts the standoff. Discords have breached the fog line above Xianling Village. Qingxiao descends on a blade of Sword Intent and destroys the attackers without cutting a persimmon branch. The villagers immediately smother their immortal protector in buns, pickles, prayers, and a garlic wreath; when Yangyang arrives, her Xuanling plumage makes her a second object of veneration, allowing the mortified Qingxiao to escape the crowd. The laughter ends when a subterranean tremor shakes the terraces. The Celestial Rings grind to a halt. Every Autopuppet collapses, every surveillance ring goes dark, containment cages burst, and the Mingshen array that holds back the Disorienting Fog fails at once.

Yangyang, Liangyu, and the household evacuate Xianling’s people up the switchbacks while Discords spill out behind them. By the time the last cart passes the manually cranked portcullis, the Hold is blind, overcrowded, and nearly cut off. The Prism has turned cold in Yangyang’s hand. Qingxiao reveals that the public Pagoda is only an outer chamber; Hsin’s true Sanctum hangs inside the stalled Astrolabe Core. The emergency louver will admit only three before its counterweights fall. Qingxiao must go because she knows the old ways, Iuno because she understands the machinery and deceptions of sacred institutions, and Yangyang because she carries Hsin’s stone and can hear breath beneath noise.

Before they leave, Iuno finds Qingxiao on the watch gallery weaving Sword Intent from a qin into the cloaks of every defender below. Qingxiao confesses the choice that has tormented her since Muyu’s betrayal. He sabotaged the Hold while she spent three hours bracing a rotten spillway beam over twenty trapped workers. If she had let the beam fall and pursued him, perhaps the fortress would still be whole. Iuno answers with the ugliest fact of her own life: she once used prophecy and arithmetic to send one hundred and seventy-three gladiators into the salt marshes, and she later designed a chain of events meant to place Mei on an altar. Counting lives made her efficient, not righteous. Numbers are a coward’s way to love a city. The twenty people under Qingxiao’s beam are alive; that is the answer. Qingxiao closes the qin and rises.

### The Three Realms and the Siege

The three women enter the Astrolabe through dead gearwells, corrupted jade, and a funerary trigram laid backward across the Sanctum. The rot belongs to a Nethermancer, not a Sentinel. Hsin’s folded authority tears open around them, dropping them into a Mirage of Yuan Fortress as it might have existed had the disaster two centuries ago never occurred. Its red banners remain whole. Cloud Shuttles float in sunlight. Autopuppets and self-replicating mechanisms are credited with defeating the Threnodian while Hsin and the golden-eyed Arbiter have been erased from the victory.

Qingxiao teaches Yangyang and Iuno to ride blades of condensed thought between the ships. Yangyang’s first blade responds to the thought of falling by pitching toward the abyss; Iuno’s responds to both her conscious command and the High Priestess in her head by spinning like a music-box dancer. They master the crossing only when Yangyang stops clamping herself to the blade and lets her own wind meet it, and when Iuno stops trying to silence her second voice and instead shares the work with it.

The immaculate fleet is powered by a concealed atrocity. Its crews are Reverberations of artisans who died when Yuan Fortress burned. Muyu has severed grief from their minds so they can work forever without hesitation. Jianghang learns that his sister has died and continues turning the same bolt because mourning no longer reaches him. Lianqing watches a sunrise, remembers that beauty ought to exist, and considers jumping because she can no longer feel why she should stay. Qingxiao’s fury freezes the mist into blades. Iuno names the truth: a city that cannot mourn its dead has not been saved; it has been embalmed.

At the flagship helm, Muyu appears as an amber phantom. Once Hsin’s trusted artisan, he believes mortal feeling is the structural flaw that made Yuan Fortress fall. He accuses Hsin of lacking the will to use her power and Qingxiao of casting aside her own heart in pursuit of cultivation. He scorns the heroic cycle that demands golden-eyed women, sacrificed armies, and girls who become wind. His accusation strikes every bruise in the three intruders, but Yangyang sees the fraud beneath it. Hsin’s refusal to hollow her citizens was not weakness. Muyu has made obedient corpses and named them immortal.

When Qingxiao destroys the projection, they understand that it existed to delay them while Muyu attacks the real Hold. Panic drives Yangyang’s attention toward escape; she begins measuring whether she can become wind and slip back alone. Iuno stops her with a single command. She has watched one Yangyang unmake herself to finish an errand and will not watch another. Qingxiao, who once trusted no one enough to leave a wall, tells them to trust the defenders. They will hold.

Above them, those defenders face Muyu’s Censure Courts without the Core. Magistrate-shaped puppets and chained Discords climb through the Disorienting Fog, reading sentences against the living: found wanting, struck from the roll, demolition. Ciaccona holds an acoustic grid over the ramp so allies can hear true direction inside the false echoes. Amy leaves her body in Mei’s lap and pilots Nyx Ravenhollow through sleet, trusting Mei to hold her chin and not abandon the rug. Cartethyia becomes Fleurdelys beneath the portcullis and cuts a thirty-foot tribunal colossus in half—along with a forty-foot diagonal section of Xuanfang’s ancient wall. Bai Tieshan catches the severed masonry with earth-softening hands while Chisa stitches the breach with iron cable.

Lupa leaps from the parapet into the fog because battle offers a simplicity care never does. Her fire takes on the shapes of dead companions, and her joy becomes terrible as she begins pursuing the enemy beyond Ciaccona’s sound. Chisa says only her name. Lupa hears the breakfast-table voice inside the roar, comes back through the gate, and drinks water.

Mei keeps both knees on the felt blanket until winged Discords dive at Amy’s helpless body. She fights one-handed without releasing her daughter, tears her own stitches, and discovers a separate thread of Qingxiao’s Sword Intent tied secretly around her ribs. Later, when burrowing Discords erupt from the drain and she cannot make the second stroke, Abby explodes from her body, is torn apart, reconstitutes himself in furious gold, and destroys them because Mei is injured, Amy is his, and the hall behind them is full of children. Mei, who has spent lives acting as the only shield in reach, is forced to let even the smallest member of her household stand between her and harm.

In the Mirage’s buried Di Realm, Yangyang, Iuno, and Qingxiao find the real Yuan Fortress drowned in ash. Pale roots strip memories and grief from the dead, pumping blank frequency into the shining fleet. Muyu has not eliminated sorrow; he has buried it in the foundation and burned it for fuel. Qingxiao, meanwhile, is freezing from the wrists inward. Across the sealed realms she has kept every protective thread around the Hold taut, accepting each blow against its defenders into her own body. Iuno demands that she thin the wards and preserve strength for Muyu. Qingxiao answers with Iuno’s own lesson: if no life is reduced to arithmetic, then Iuno must name which twelve people should lose protection.

Iuno cannot. Instead, she admits that she is frightened. Mei is fighting with five-day-old stitches, her Terminal is dead, the sun outside the cabin does not move, and Qingxiao may burn herself to ash before they reach the real enemy. The confession does what command cannot. Yangyang divides one crushed turnip cake into three pieces, moves the lamp away from Qingxiao’s frost, and says aloud when her own ribs hurt. Care enters the room as practical inventory rather than surrender.

They speak about the voices within them. Iuno’s High Priestess is vain, theatrical, and often truthful before Iuno is ready. Yangyang’s shijie remained inside her as guidance rather than possession; another inner voice once urged her to abandon everything and live, and Yangyang survived by refusing to obey it without pretending it had never spoken. Qingxiao was taught that any inner voice is a demon to be cut down. Yet after hearing the other two women describe coexistence, she allows Yangyang to cover her with a wool blanket and lies down.

While Qingxiao sleeps, a second breath moves beneath her own. The untouched qin sounds. Frost points inward toward the bunk. A jade bead cracks. Her defensive threads briefly go slack in the waking world, then return darker and crueler, shearing attackers in half rather than merely turning blades. Qingxiao has entered the final duel her teacher Jingwei taught her to expect.

### The Heart Under the Mountain

Yangyang and Iuno follow Qingxiao into a lake beneath a tree whose branches are made of every defensive thread tied to Xuanfang. The lakebed is paved with sealed scrolls, and its shallows hold the cloven bodies of all the selves Qingxiao has killed to preserve perfect stillness. Jingwei’s lingering frequency tells them that if Qingxiao’s inner demon wins, they must strike her down before the backlash travels through the tree and kills everyone she protects.

Iuno briefly accepts the grim logic. Yangyang refuses. Qingxiao did not open her mind to women who would repeat Jingwei’s mistake. She chose Iuno, who stopped waging war against the High Priestess, and Yangyang, who carries shijie without allowing the dead to erase the living.

At the tree, a violet-eyed Qingxiao stands over the exhausted woman kneeling beneath the weight of the Hold. The reflection is not a foreign invader. She is the hard, feelingless armor an eight-year-old girl built after receiving one bowl of millet and going out with a hazel stick to defend the people who fed her. She unseals the scrolls Qingxiao buried: Lingfang leaving for war after singing the first half of a marching call no one answered; Yuan Fortress burning while Qingxiao fought elsewhere in the Gorges; Qingxiao carving Ruoyun’s name into stone with a broken blade; Hsin receiving Qingxiao’s fury behind a bolted door; two hundred invitations from Hsin left unopened on a snowbound peak. Qingxiao despises Hsin for becoming unreachable, yet has spent two centuries doing the same.

The reflection places her sword against the solitary thread tied around Mei. Mei does not remember the ancient manual, the rain, or Qingxiao’s face. Qingxiao walked the continent for four years seeking the golden-eyed swordswoman who saved the Gorges, only for that woman to erase her past and begin again. Why should Qingxiao bleed for someone who did not deem her worth remembering?

Before despair can break the tree, Ruoyun walks out of the mist. Qingxiao believed her dead at Yuan Fortress and carved her memorial. Ruoyun instead surrendered a body pinned under a gate beam and enlisted as a Celestial on the Hold’s own registers. For two centuries she has cleared pine needles from conduits, nudged lost Rafter Kites toward the right pass, and kept the third watch while Qingxiao grieved her as absent. She rebukes both halves of her friend: keeping one’s flesh frozen on a peak is not nobler than relinquishing a ruined body to remain among the people one loves.

The High Priestess recognizes Qingxiao’s reflection as kin. She tells the armor that it is not an abyssal terror, only a child’s protector furious at being locked in the cellar whenever Qingxiao descends toward life. Qingxiao finally answers the question of why she involves herself in mortal affairs. She does not invoke Hsin’s title or a transcendent duty. Xianling gave her garlic and buns. Yangyang divided a turnip cake into thirds. Mei is keeping rice crust warm for someone still below. A single plum branch over shallow water is enough reason to love a garden. Her hands have hurt for two hundred years.

Yangyang opens her Xuanling wings and sings Lingfang’s unanswered call. Qingxiao answers with the old cadence, and the violet-eyed reflection sings from the same breath. Every slain self in the lake becomes a bird of warm light and returns to Qingxiao’s breast. Qingxiao does not kill the reflection; she embraces her until only one shadow remains.

Jingwei warns that without frozen stillness anchoring her marrow, Qingxiao will not live long. Qingxiao accepts mortality. Her Way is to walk down the mountain while the soup is still warm. The cracked jade bead drops from her wrist. Plum blossoms bloom along the threads throughout the Hold, and the wards stop retaliating with cruelty; they turn each blow aside gently, leaving winter fragrance in the mud.

When Qingxiao wakes in the cabin, the room has thawed. She accepts Yangyang’s shoulder to sit up and admits that she is hungry. She also tells Iuno and Yangyang why Mei mattered to her. Two hundred years ago, Mei’s earlier self came through the rain beside Sentinel Jué and turned the Gorges battle with a blade broad enough to save a province, while Yuan Fortress burned elsewhere. Qingxiao searched for four years to ask how such a person chooses where to stand when every unattended roof falls. She never asked the present Mei because Mei had chosen to leave the old folders in a drawer. Iuno supplies the answer anyway: Mei does not choose by worth. She props the beam nearest someone’s head, shouts for a ladder too late, and comes home covered in plaster. Yangyang adds that house rules now require her to ask for the ladder first.

The trio breaks through the Mirage’s Tian Realm, where Muyu has inverted Hsin’s Sanctum into a storm of shattered courts and corrupt machinery. Yangyang hears the hitch where Muyu’s stolen frequency falls behind Hsin’s original four-beat ward. Iuno holds the collapsing barrier above them on her moonwheel while Yangyang steadies Qingxiao’s arm and calls the beat; Qingxiao cuts the seam open.

Inside, they find Muyu’s mortal body dead at a drafting table, his callused artisan’s hands discarded with his aging flesh. His consciousness inhabits a ten-foot, four-armed guardian body of nephrite, bronze, black lacquer, and porcelain. He attacks through the very systems Hsin entrusted to him and demands that Qingxiao cut her threads to gain the power to defeat him. She refuses. His attempt to draw ten thousand captive Reverberations from the deep reservoirs produces only empty air: the dead went home when Yangyang and Qingxiao completed the song.

Muyu’s construct cracks Iuno’s moonwheel, chips her crystal shoulder, and batters Yangyang’s ribs. Qingxiao cannot pierce his kernel while holding the Hold. Then Jingran appears behind the construct and drives a blade through its regulator. He had seemed part of Muyu’s Nethermantic machinery, but he used his access to smash all seven backup vessels containing Muyu’s soul. A Nethermancer may uncover what the dead leave behind; Jingran will not skin them for boiler fuel.

Suoming, the small woman in the faded plum coat whom everyone mistook for a child, leads the rest of the household through the sealed doors. The final fight becomes the opposite of Muyu’s solitary immortality. Ciaccona gives the room a true beat. Fleurdelys takes the giant scythes. Chisa cuts the conduits. Lupa burns open the magnetic ring. Amy grounds the lightning beside Mei. Iuno pins a joint. Yangyang clears the smoke with her wings. Mei stands at Qingxiao’s left and shares the weight of a descending blade, greeting her in the midst of destruction by asking whether she ate her third of the turnip cake.

With Muyu’s kernel exposed, Qingxiao draws the bloodstained plum petal from her cuff. It becomes a plain moon-white sword carrying the whole weight of a living heart. She drives it through the kernel, and the artificial immortal collapses into scrap.

Muyu’s last revelation prevents triumph from becoming safety. His cages were not merely prisons; they were plugs in a provincial barrier that has decayed for fifty years. With them gone, the deep seams are collapsing from below. Hsin is not in the Sanctum, and the cold Prism offers no answer. Qingxiao executes the final remnant of Muyu, but the Hold remains beneath a broken sky.

### The Debts That Remain

The first command after the battle comes from Suoming: eat. In the branch hall, the Fillet Fairmaiden feeds defenders and refugees from sentient cauldrons. Qingxiao sits shoulder to shoulder with Yangyang at a table meant for half their number. When the reserved pan of rice crust arrives, she passes it first to Liangyu and the Wardens who held the gate. After everyone else has eaten, Mei uncovers a bowl kept warm for the exhausted cook and tells her that it is her turn.

Yangyang then sends Mei to walk Qingxiao home. Mei thanks Qingxiao for the secret thread around her ribs. Qingxiao calls it spare; Mei knows it was not. On a quiet upper lane, Qingxiao finally asks the question she carried for two centuries: when two roofs fall at once, how does Mei choose? Mei answers that she runs toward whichever beam is nearest, then yells until someone brings a ladder. She is supposed to ask first now. Qingxiao calls it a good rule.

At an old cedar house, Qingxiao retrieves *The Swordcraft of the Golden-Eyed Stranger*, copied long ago from Mei’s forgotten forms and annotated in the cramped hand of Qingxiao as a child. Mei does not recover the buried life. She sees the ash, the small handwriting, and the turtle doodled beside a grounded heel, then asks only whether the manual helped. When Qingxiao says yes, Mei takes the book home to keep safe. Later Yangyang reads it beside Mei and recognizes the stance Qingxiao now makes her practice at the cistern every morning. Knowledge moves forward even when memory does not.

Qingxiao’s acceptance of life has a price. Ruoyun meets her on the third watch, solid and warm after two centuries of concealment. She is furious to discover that Qingxiao’s immortal frost is gone and that Jingwei measured her remaining life as “not long.” Ruoyun has watched generations age and die while tending the same Hold; she refuses to walk behind Qingxiao’s coffin. Qingxiao answers that the third watch will still need keeping. They admit that both the snowpeak and the long garrison years were lonely, retune an old camp song to Ruoyun’s imperfect pitch, share an oilcloth against the cold, and write their names side by side on the watch-board.

Buling also returns, the former disciple who left for cinnabar and wandered for more than a century as a geomancer, artifact maker, and notorious forum user. Ruoyun chases her through the market, catches her by the ear at a culvert, and drags her before Qingxiao to answer for the rounds she abandoned. Qingxiao laughs—openly, unexpectedly—when the two begin threatening each other with beheading and pickle jars. Then she sentences Buling not to exile, but to work beside Ruoyun on the eastern breach until the thaw. The lost members of the old Hold resume their quarrel over lime, crowbars, soup, and shoes.

Jingran and Suoming settle darker accounts. At two empty graves Jingran dug as a child, one for himself and one for the teacher who later vanished into a Sonoro seam, he buries the old man’s brass pipe tamper and knocks both nameless markers flat before runoff crews can reuse them. He then follows Suoming down from the cemetery rather than remaining with the graves. At a river drying shed, Yinlin intercepts artisans trying to smuggle Muyu’s deep-manifold charts south. She returns the fissure soundings to Suoming while taking the rest to Jinzhou. The conspiracy beneath the Hold is not wholly dead, and the people cleaning it up do so without public ceremony.

Supplies finally climb the reopened gorge road: rice, millet, coal, lime, cable, Huaxu equipment, a Jinzhou pike company, and Ministry surveyors who already know the Hold’s floor plan. Four mail sacks arrive slit and resealed by Mingting’s Internal Security Agency. Chixia sends eleven letters written on whatever paper she could reach, a hand-lined red winter cap for the Wind, and a tiny midnight note confessing that Mrs. Ren’s front room becomes too quiet before dawn. Baizhi reports both patients’ healing in clinical language, then admits Jinzhou is quieter than she prefers while Yangyang is away. Yangyang writes in her patrol log that the road is open and the hat fits the pocket. She and Mei agree that the first thing they will do upon returning is walk with Chixia along the canal before supper.

Yet the road also brings occupation disguised as relief. Southern soldiers fill the barracks. Ministry men measure cistern piers while rumors spread that Xuanfang will be dismantled after the gorge is cleared. Jinhsi’s censored letter hides a warning in four deliberate falsehoods: the moon is setting. That night, two days before the Waking Moon should be full, the moon crosses the sky too early and disappears behind the ninth spire. Hsin’s Core remains dark.

Iuno feels the loss of prophecy most sharply. She can list the rotting barrier, the missing Sentinel, the freed Celestials, Jingran’s soot, Suoming’s calm, Buling’s return, Mengzhou’s silence, and the Core that has grown too quiet for Chisa to hear. She cannot see five minutes ahead. Two promised Tuesdays have passed without her proposal—one while she was trapped inside the painted afternoon, another because she waited for Mei’s stitches, Augusta’s papers, and a safe sky. Mei refuses to let Tuesday become another postponed life. She retires her morning question about the day. Tuesday will be whenever Iuno takes out the box, even if roofs are falling. Iuno still does not propose before the festival, but the box remains in her coat because the future has become an act she must choose, not an hour prophecy grants.

### Reunion Under the Wrong Moon

On the first day of the Waking Moon, every neighboring household closes its shutters and cooks a reunion dinner. Yangyang pretends the custom does not matter because no one else is from Huanglong. She oils a hinge, polishes her sword twice, rewinds her bandages, and begins a letter to Suisui that cannot leave while the holiday gates are barred. Mei recognizes the discipline of someone making loneliness unobtrusive. She announces that their storehouse will hold its own *tuán yuán fàn*: one handmade dish from every member of the household, all at the same table by dusk.

Yangyang makes the clear chicken, yam, angelica, date, and goji soup her mother serves in Mingting. Lupa crams nine ingredients into one brass hotpot and argues that one rim means one dish. Iuno fails repeatedly before producing a mirror-smooth steamed egg with one hand. Cartethyia bakes a crooked Egla ring-loaf. Amy uses a brass reed to hear the exact instant sugar reaches the right temperature for candied hawthorns. Chisa cuts flawless cistern ice for osmanthus water. Ciaccona, deprived of tomatoes and proper flour, creates persimmon-garlic millet noodles that nearly defeat everyone but Cartethyia and Buling. Mei makes sizzling rice and keeps one dry golden corner under an upturned cup.

At dusk, Yangyang lays every bowl and every pair of chopsticks in the Mingting manner. Abby appears and demands an extra setting for the person behind, so Chisa adds a ninth bowl by the door. Yangyang serves Mei first, then goes around the whole table and fills the empty bowl last. Their reunion is imperfect, crowded, and loud. The bread resembles a badger, the noodles resemble axle paste, Mei freezes her teeth on Chisa’s drink, and the hotpot violates the spirit of every rule. It is nevertheless the first Waking Moon table Yangyang has not had to leave in order to keep someone else safe.

Visitors bring the rest of Xuanfang into the meal. Liangyu and the young Wardens arrive with preserved mustard greens and leave carrying mutton skewers. Buling contributes century eggs of uncertain age and eats a second dinner. Ruoyun sends bitter melon and egg while insisting it merely remained in the pan. Qingxiao pauses outside in the cold, reluctant to enter a crowded room. Mei uncovers the rice crust she saved and carries it to the threshold. Qingxiao eats it there, warm-handed and mortal, then follows Ruoyun and Buling toward the viewing terraces.

The household carries its benches and leftovers into the yard instead of climbing the official steps. All across the Hold, families gather beneath red lanterns and set paper boats on the cistern. The moon rises enormous and bright above the eastern pass, and the battered fortress cheers as if the old order has returned. Yangyang sits between Amy, Cartethyia, and the rest of the household, drinking Mingting soup beneath a borrowed scarf. When she first returned to Xuanfang, she was the late-arriving Watcher with cracked ribs, half-belonging to a sister across the dark and half-prepared to become wind again whenever someone needed saving. Now she is seated inside the life that sacrifice made possible. Her shijie remains part of her without consuming her; Suisui has gone to save another city without taking Yangyang’s home with her; Chixia and Baizhi wait down the reopened road; and Xuanfang has given Yangyang a disciple’s place, a family’s bowl, and people who will notice if she misses lunch.

The world around that table is not healed. As the cheers fade, the full moon darkens to copper-red and races westward too early. Ministry troops occupy the terraces, surveyors measure the foundations, the deep barrier continues to rot, and the Astrolabe Core hangs crooked and silent. Iuno holds Mei’s hand with the ring still unoffered in her coat. Qingxiao has exchanged ageless isolation for a shorter mortal life among Ruoyun, Liangyu, and the people whose soup she can now reach while it is hot. Ruoyun and Buling walk publicly through streets they once served from hiding. Jingran has abandoned his empty grave, Suoming holds the fissure charts, and the Wardens remain at gates the state may intend to close.

At the third watch, Qingxiao and Ruoyun reach the high gallery. Their weiqi board still bears nine black stones and the white stone Qingxiao placed the night before. Beneath that white stone, on the shoulder point nearer the edge, a tenth black stone has appeared, though the only stair has remained guarded. Hsin still does not open her jade door or restore the sky, but she has answered the board.

Xuanfang now stands in a condition opposite to the lonely, suspended survival in which Yangyang first found it. Its divine machinery is broken, its political future threatened, and its moon displaced; yet its human and Celestial inhabitants are no longer waiting in separate rooms for someone else to decide what their losses require. The absent have begun returning to their watches. The immortal has come down for soup. The martyr asks for a ladder. The bird remains at the table. And beneath the wrong moon, while the mountain’s oldest defenses fail, an unseen hand places one black stone beside another and makes the next move.

## The Moon That Set Too Soon

Xuanfang Hold entered the Waking Moon festival wounded, half-isolated, and uncertain whether the woman who ruled it was still alive.

Muyu was dead. His Skyworks and Thousand-Puppet Pavilion had been broken, the captive souls with which he powered them had been released, and Qingxiao had destroyed his final kernel with a blade of white plum blossom. Yet his last warning had outlived him: the hidden barrier beneath Xuanfang had been failing for fifty years, and the machinery he had usurped had also been holding back something worse. The Astrolabe Core hung dark above the valley. Autopuppets stalled in the lanes. The outer roads folded into fog. Hsin, sovereign of the Hold and architect of its four hundred guardians, had vanished behind the frozen jade doors of the Fogveil Pagoda.

The people who had survived Muyu could not simply rise whole from his defeat. Amy removed Mei’s eight abdominal stitches herself, hands steady until the deepest thread caught in the scar left by the timber Mei had taken while shielding her. Mei celebrated lacing one boot unaided; Iuno laced the other and limited her to one victory before breakfast. Yangyang’s ribs still needed strapping, Iuno’s crystal shoulder remained chipped, Ciaccona’s finger was splinted, Liangyu’s forearm had only just left its brace, and Qingxiao had surrendered the frost that sustained her longevity. The Sword Transcendent could now feel hunger, cold, sleep, and the finite future in her pulse.

They recovered amid the third storehouse’s crowded life: Lupa’s endless hotpot, Cartethyia’s lopsided loaves, Chisa’s bandages, Amy’s repairs, and Mei’s slow progression from forty level paces to twelve stairs. Stranded families filled the hayloft and stove-room. Their new law was simple: nobody carried a falling roof alone, and nobody late to the table lost their place.

That law made the empty ninth bowl impossible to dismiss. Abby had insisted that a serving be kept for the person still behind, so the untouched broth was placed outside on the low wall. By dawn it had been eaten to the last berry. The chopsticks rested side by side, their tips aligned as neatly as if an unseen guest had finished, thanked the house, and gone. A heavy yellow paper boat appeared in the cistern though none of the children had folded it, its old-fashioned prow pinched into the shape of the sampans that had once poled the gorge. Qingxiao found an ancient kaya-wood weiqi board on the gatehouse embrasure, nine black handicap stones waiting beside warm plum wine. She placed a white stone. Later, a black stone answered.

Other signs were harder to welcome. Phantom boots marched the lanes without disturbing the frost. A drum answered the dawn watch from a gorge where no tower stood. Children sang an evacuation rhyme no elder remembered teaching them. A printing forme locked inside Master Shao’s shop set itself in obsolete type and produced an order from two centuries earlier, commanding the lower terraces to bring their children and grain behind the inner wall before the evening drum and to open to no voice from the ditch. Families trapped below the gate heard mule bells, cookpots, axes, and a roll call of names that belonged to no living clan. From the eastern ridge, Warden Ge looked down and saw Yuan Fortress—the stronghold destroyed in the Fortress and Gorges War—standing whole across the south road, its chimneys smoking and its old sentries staring through every hail.

The moon rose copper-red and crossed the sky too quickly. The road closed. Yangyang’s letter to Suisui could not leave the Hold. Ministry surveyors in plain grey coats measured foundation stones and conduit vaults without recording their figures, while rumors spread that Mingting intended to strike Xuanfang from the border roll and let the obsolete fortress collapse into the river. Buling uncovered schedules that listed machines, residents, and “Retained Celestials” beneath a column marked “Disposition.” Jinhsi’s censored warning reached them only because deliberately false phrases concealed the message: **The Moon Is Setting.** Suisui reported that Magistrate Dou had withdrawn, Minister Shen Qiu had arrived with palace authority, and Hsin’s annual annotations had ceased.

Liangyu answered fear with the work immediately before her. Until Hsin herself came down and ordered the gates locked from the outside, the Wardens would keep watch. Yangyang did the same. Newly redesignated a Watcher because her altered hearing could distinguish survivors before they became casualty lists, she refused to promise a frightened miller’s wife that the road would open. She promised instead that Lupa’s stove was hot, the storehouse had blankets, and no family needed to spend the night alone in a granary.

Suoming watched more quietly. The small red-haired Elder had served on Xuanfang’s rolls since Yuan Fortress fell, though her young face concealed two hundred years of war and burial. She had once been Intendant of Mengzhou’s Ministry of Sentinel Affairs and had led the Ten Seals Covenant through Huating’s black rain, preserving weakened traces of nine dead companions inside her Providence Lock. She no longer accepted the title. She wanted no consolation for the people she had knowingly spent and buried one by one; she kept the grief because it was theirs and the anger because it was hers. For a century she had also carried a marker Hsin placed in her hand when the plum tree by the southern cistern split, and a promise Hsin had never called due.

Each night Suoming climbed to the Pagoda beneath her oil-paper umbrella and stood before the doors sealed by blue-white frost. Ministry men sounded the cliff below her. The Astrolabe did not turn. She told the absent fox, “You’re late,” and waited.

On the festival’s next morning the straps came off Yangyang’s ribs. She drew a full breath without pain just as the old war drum answered from the gorge. By evening the fortress had grown farther across the road, the Hold had filled with stranded soldiers and valley families, and impossible fireworks began blossoming over the cliffs. They had color and thunder but no smoke, ash, or falling sparks. Twin moons appeared above the pass.

On the coping wall Suoming saw a thin young girl whom no one else recognized. She called her Ninth—the starving, invisible girl from the Covenant who had called her Sister Suoming, had feared nothing, and had died exactly where Suoming’s plan required her to die. The apparition pointed toward the Fogveil Pagoda.

Suoming ran.

At the top landing the sanctuary doors were still barred from within. She did not knock. Nine Shadows opened in her hands with the report of a cannon, its bamboo shaft and steel ribs becoming an instrument of crimson-black thunder. One blow shattered the ice. Another tore the jade leaves from their frame. Behind them, thorned black vines covered the Sanctum machinery and pulsed with the smell of bruised winter pear.

Balanophora waited inside, wearing the body of a Ministry surveyor. The Fractsidus Overseer had threaded herself through the unwilling clerk and the crack in Hsin’s machinery, using a living host as armor. Suoming fought with exact restraint, cutting vines and turning blows without killing the body beneath them. Balanophora answered by wearing Ninth’s voice, then mocked the borrowed years in Suoming’s lungs and the nine people who had willingly sustained her. She expected grief to make Suoming reckless. It made her precise.

The household and the Wardens reached the Sanctum as the struggle spread across the platform. Hsin was nowhere in the room. Yangyang’s Prism of Fate—the stone Suisui had won from Hsin by refusing to purchase one sister’s survival with another’s life—suddenly burned white inside her sash. Both Suoming and Balanophora reached for it. Yangyang threw it to Suoming because Balanophora’s eyes demanded while Suoming’s asked.

Suoming struck the Prism in midair with Nine Shadows. The stone unfolded into brilliant geometric light, scouring the black root out of the surveyor without burning the woman beneath it. The freed clerk fell into Liangyu’s arms. The Astrolabe Core began to turn.

Then the Sanctum floor opened.

The twelve travelers dropped through a city recursively printed inside itself: streets stitched together like signatures in a book, smaller Xuanfangs visible through windows, doors opening onto copies of the same doors. Balanophora’s rotten-pear scent fell ahead of them. Yangyang felt a small warm hand catch her empty cuff and pull her past a closing beam. Beside her ear, someone released a slow, exhausted breath.

The fall cast them apart. Mei and Yangyang landed with Suoming in a southern bamboo grove where shoots rose, greened, yellowed, and became chaff within moments. Lupa, Chisa, and Cartethyia came down in the eastern ford among an orderly retreat of dead garrison soldiers carrying their wounded. Iuno and Amy found a northern post-road where self-burning lanterns floated uphill like a crowd returning from supper. Qingxiao and Ruoyun landed on the intact battlements of Yuan Fortress among sentries who printed footprints into old snow but never saw them. Buling and Ciaccona struck the western terraces, where noon, midnight, rain, and snow occupied neighboring paddies and a persimmon tree flowered, fruited, and shed within a breath.

Howl, whistle, sword-light, lute, and bell passed across the divided valley. Yangyang heard every answer and sent a column of golden bamboo leaves spiraling into the sky. Everyone lived. Xuanfang Hold remained fixed above them while the country beneath it changed its century with every step. Under Suoming’s open umbrella, and only there, the ground kept one season.

Suoming named the place from diagrams she had once seen in a sealed annex: Hsin’s Simulacrum Nexus of Mengzhou.

Far in the west, a fox-eared woman taller than the mountains stood against the white sky with one hand raised as if she had been holding the heavens up alone for two hundred years.

## The Country Beneath Her Hand

The Nexus did not feed its visitors, yet it laid tables everywhere. Kitchens waited with steaming buns. Dormitories held sharply folded blankets for people who did not need sleep. Ledgers stood open in neat red columns without a single name entered. Bookbinders’ Row displayed hundreds of damp pages, every one printed with the same official sentence ending halfway through. Clocks and Terminals stopped at 11:42. Hidden gears hitched whenever the giant’s light dimmed.

South of the Hold, a vine-split warden Autopuppet slashed Suoming’s right forearm from elbow to wrist before Mei and Yangyang brought it down. Suoming intended to continue without pausing. Mei and Yangyang made her sit and bound the wound. The gesture established the terms on which the three would travel: Suoming knew the old country and the rot’s scent; Yangyang could hear the living pulse beneath the false streets; Mei could carry strength into a place whose histories she no longer remembered. None would be permitted to pretend that usefulness made a body inexhaustible.

They climbed a stair assembled from granite, brass, and moss and saw the giant clearly. Suoming knew the Nexus only from Ministry margins and plans, not from experience. As they descended through leaves that sighed like folded paper, Yangyang kept a thread of wind against Suoming’s back whenever the Elder’s old breath rasped low on her left side. Suoming eventually stopped and told her she could stop with the wind. When Yangyang hesitated, Suoming added, simply, that she knew. Yangyang obeyed. Care offered without consent could become another way of deciding for someone; care withdrawn at the first refusal would be abandonment. They kept walking close enough to hear one another.

At the Third Ferry Rest, exhaustion finally overruled all three. Mei directed supper from a stool, Yangyang cooked congee and eggs, and Suoming ate the second egg Mei quietly put on her saucer. The inn held one bed and a window-seat. Mei and Yangyang shared the bed; Mei promised to tell Iuno, and Yangyang insisted that she do so. Their tenderness remained real without erasing Mei’s bond with Iuno or turning uncertainty into a secret entitlement. In the night Yangyang told Suoming about the missing word in Hsin’s voice, the small hand that had saved her in the fall, and the unnamed but openly acknowledged love between herself and Mei. Suoming gave her the later watch rather than the one she requested and curled on the window-seat with Nine Shadows beneath her chin. Sleeping, she looked the age of the face she wore. Her breath did not.

Their route followed black vine-scale through the old locks and canals. Suoming described the real Mengzhou as they went: noisy, wet, full of print-rollers, mule carts, neighborhood quarrels, a bathhouse cat called the Magistrate, and buildings long since burned or replaced. The Nexus had preserved an older printing and polished away the people. When Suoming’s breathing caught on a rise, Mei pretended her own scar required a rest. Suoming called her a poor liar and sat anyway. In that pause she admitted the life she still wanted: two grey-tiled rooms by a chalk bend in the river, a low southern window, thorn hedges keeping pigs from the cabbages, and somebody from the lane bringing hotpot at the fifth bell so she would not have to wash the pan. Mei promised to bring broad beans for the crows. The wish was small enough to sound almost impossible to a woman who had survived by always knowing the next cost.

On the northern track, Amy and Iuno solved an ice-locked gate together, Amy reading the paving as a sliding puzzle while Iuno learned to follow her turns. Reunited with Lupa, Chisa, and Cartethyia, they were swallowed by a folding square but kept hold of one another throughout the fall. At the bottom of a lacquered tower they carried a warm green orb beside growing vines, which formed bridges only as they advanced. Lupa climbed the final tree despite her fear of heights while Chisa named each branch beneath her. At the crown they found a filigree lotus pointing toward Mei. A gigantic fingertip descended, ruffled Cartethyia’s hair, and gave a fond, irritated *hmph*. Whatever held the sky was tired, cross, and glad somebody had come.

The lotus grew a living bough southwest. On a span above a gorge, Cartethyia kept the height-fearing Lupa looking at her by loudly reviving their argument over a fountain brawl. She then admitted that confinement had left her afraid of latched shutters and low roofs; Lupa promised an unhooked window and a larger fire. Behind them, Chisa told Iuno to ask Amy directly how she was coping. Chisa’s old certainty inside Honami’s repeating Tuesday had only left her waiting alone for four o’clock. Iuno, who had once known every falling roof in advance, agreed that the frightening unknown was better.

At a mill, Lupa halted them when Iuno’s hand shook. Amy confessed that Mei had held her during the transport crash, but nobody held Mei when the Sanctum floor opened. Iuno admitted that she now feared every corner Mei turned and still preferred that fear to prophecy. Amy leaned against her good shoulder. Later Amy told Chisa the fall had felt like the Nihility; Chisa promised to take her wrist again. At an abandoned festival they carried away a clay goose for Mei, a pitch-pipe for Ciaccona, and a hideous badger for Lupa’s door—ridiculous prizes worth keeping through the end of a world.

In Mengxuan Vale, Qingxiao and Ruoyun found Hsin’s Celestials trapped in immaculate routines. A century of absence registered as three days; nobody reacted when Buling returned. Workers steamed buns for empty beds, repeated the same registers, and could not say what occupied the Sanctum. Newly mortal, Qingxiao ate, slept deeply, and asked for eggs. Ruoyun insisted Celestials did not eat, burned the millet, and ate the egg Qingxiao gave her.

The unanswered weiqi game reappeared under the pavilion. Buling repaired the astrolabe while Ruoyun remembered how she had left without farewell. When Buling asked what had happened to Qingxiao’s immortality, Ruoyun told her she had lost the right to ask. Buling silently greased the next pinion, then left eggs and a note—*Back when we’re back*—on Ruoyun’s peg. Ruoyun kept the note in her cuff.

Buling and Ciaccona crossed looping orchard terraces until Longjue showed them the faulty drain-cover and third-terrace latch. The dragon-crested Celestial had drawn the same chipped east-wind tile twice, found smokes burned to her fingers without remembering the flame, and heard crickets repeat the same three notes. Nobody remembered walking beyond the upper boundary stones. Though the loop contained her orchard work, mahjong friends, endless health, and evenings without consequence, Longjue chose to inspect its edge. She took Buling and Ciaccona toward the cistern house singing. When their echo returned half a tone low from two directions, Longjue stepped forward and called for another verse.

Across the Nexus, each company learned the same truth in different forms. Hsin’s country was full of shelter and empty of free inhabitants; full of prepared meals yet unable to feel hunger; full of records without names; full of care that had become machinery because its maker no longer believed she could safely be seen. The giant’s raised hand seemed at first to hold the world together. After Cartethyia turned the mountain key, the giant lowered it. The sky did not fall. She folded both arms across her chest and bowed her head like a sentry who had discovered, too late, that she was allowed to sleep.

## Three Histories of One Dead Sentinel

The giant’s rest did not mean the Nexus was safe. When Mei, Yangyang, and Suoming brought their paper-laden barge into the channel below Mengxuan Vale, the Prism pulsed. A hand came down out of the sky with all the force of the domain behind it. Mei and Suoming took the first impact and could not throw it off. Suoming ordered Yangyang to run. Yangyang refused. The Prism opened a fractal passage beneath her instead, changing her into the small Xuanling bird whose body could move between the pages of the Heart Realm.

From that interval she saw every separated traveler at once. She tore open three portals and blew her companions through them, freeing Mei and Suoming from the hand and gathering the others into three companies, though Longjue remained alone on the outer ridge. Each company entered a different portion of Hsin’s memory. None received enough of the truth to understand it alone.

Iuno, Amy, Lupa, Chisa, and Cartethyia entered the Heart of Flames, a lantern town under a dark and watching sky. A little fox with a frequency resembling the Prism guided them without explanation. Hsin’s disembodied voice repeated the Waking Moon rite—light the lanterns, gather the guests, invite the Moon, let all beings rejoice—yet every lantern stood dark. Blue-faced Formrenders crawled from painted walls to devour any flame, while residents gathered scraps of Nightlight and wondered whether Lady Hsin had abandoned them.

The five restored a three-part Moon Lantern key: paper holding the memory of wishes sent downstream, a frame recalling that Xuanfang had been raised on every light that failed and was lit again, and a tassel carrying the sensation of a thousand dancers gathering one lonely watcher into their living motion. When Amy assembled the pieces, lanterns blazed across the town and the Formrenders froze. A Phantom of Hsin appeared above the rejoicing streets and confessed that even this small act of keeping homes safe had become more than she could manage.

The ritual led them past lives Hsin had secretly tended. An old woman still waited for Guilan to return from the Ministry because his last letter had promised he would. A girl named Jiu prepared the Waking Moon Feast alone for her grandmother, laying an extra bowl for the Sentinel and hoping her absent parents might come. When Jiu offered Amy her only rice cake, Amy urged her to eat it; Jiu broke it in half so both could have some. She remembered a skinny fox stealing Waking Moon brew from her family’s window before the war. The next morning grain, wine, and a golden flower had appeared at the door. Jiu’s mother, long dead and preserved as a Celestial trace, appeared only long enough to see her daughter grown and thanked Hsin for breaking the rules. At the highest lantern Hsin’s Phantom asked an unseen old friend whether the lights brought comfort and apologized that she could preserve only one thread of the friend’s fading frequency from the people’s wishes. If **It** had been there, Hsin believed, It would have done better.

Mei and Yangyang entered a deeper threshold with Suoming, though Yangyang remained a bird. Hsin met them in human form, exhausted and only intermittently present. She called Yangyang “Little” followed by a word scraped out of the air. Her real body, she said, slept inside the Nexus while she spent herself repairing anomalies. The shadow attacking them was Suhsin, a correction protocol with Hsin’s face. Xuanfang’s Civilization Capsule had been programmed to destroy the Hold after its original mission was complete. Hsin had overwritten that command because the city had become a home. The rejected order broke loose with authority to devour all the data belonging to “Sentinel Hsin,” and the struggle between them had blinded Hsin to the collapsing Sanctum above.

Hsin offered what she presented as a solution. Yangyang could use the Prism to overwrite Hsin’s personality and reset Suhsin to its first line. Xuanfang would retain a functioning core; Hsin would become a mindless mechanism. Yangyang refused with such immediate anger that Hsin retreated behind a joke, but the proposal exposed the choice she had already made in private. She still believed a roof could be saved by erasing whoever held it. Before she vanished, she promised that the memories ahead would say what she was forbidden to tell them.

Suoming had listened without interrupting. As soon as Hsin faded, she deliberately released a token carrying the Moon Fox’s frequency. The Realm folded around her and took her beyond Mei’s reach. She had used herself as bait for Suhsin.

Qingxiao, Ruoyun, Buling, and Ciaccona entered the oldest history. An unsigned Ministry invitation lay beside the first Moon Lantern Hsin had ever received. Through the Sentinel’s eyes they witnessed Mengzhou preparing for the catastrophe at the Gorges of Spirits. Mortals argued, enlisted, begged family members not to go, and went anyway. The Sentinel watched their courage and decided it could not be answered with inaction.

She proposed an ever-changing city over Yuan Fortress and warned the competing artisan courts that its builders would never return. The Court of Savantae accepted, declaring that one who knew the Way at dawn could die without regret at dusk. The Sentinel chose them not only because they could build the Hold, but because their determination to preserve advanced knowledge at any cost made them dangerous to the age outside it. Xuanfang would be both their work and their containment, granting their last wish while keeping their technology bound within the mountains.

Then the memory walked toward City Hall and would not allow its witnesses to turn away. The Civilization Capsule had remained damaged since the death of the Sentinel’s Resonator. A second voice inside the scene begged the Sentinel not to enter. At the threshold, the Intendant of Sentinel Affairs drew a hidden weapon after years of preparation and struck. The Sentinel did not defend herself first. She ordered someone unseen to run and not look back.

Beyond that ambush the four found a halted sector full of kernel-less Autopuppets and a Celestial named Leyue, one of the old Court’s artisans. Leyue asked whether the Court had left anything behind in the outside world. Buling told her that scholars still studied its ruins. Comforted that their mortal work had not vanished entirely, Leyue helped them open the Heart of Frost.

Suoming, meanwhile, had fallen alone into the Heart of Blooms. She spent the Fourth Seal from her Lock to grow jade bamboo paths through the broken realm, then called the Ninth. The dead girl appeared as Suoming remembered her, laughing, fearless, still calling her Sister Suoming. They fought side by side past mechanisms modeled after the Covenant’s lost companions. A forgetful Artisan addressed Suoming as Intendant; the Ninth mourned that nobody would call her Seal Master again and asked why the Sixth Seal was gone.

The apparition knew too much and not enough. A false Hsin mocked Suoming’s nightly conversations with people who could never answer and praised her for choosing the greater good. Suoming answered with the truth she had never made gentler: she had known from the day she met the Ninth that her plan would kill the invisible girl, and had used her anyway. She asked forgiveness. The counterfeit Ninth told her to release the past for the sake of her office. That answer proved the deception. Suoming’s real grief was not a sickness she wanted cured, and the real Ninth would never have asked her to turn a person into a closed account. Suoming shattered the false girl and the false Sentinel together.

In the Heart of Frost, Mei and Yangyang found the young fox who would one day rule Xuanfang. She had been severed too quickly from her source, sent through a seam with the broken Capsule and only enough authority to repair it. She built the first Lexicon Axis, distributed herself through its modules, purged Fractsidus corruption, and separated her own processing core to carry the city’s impossible load. An ancient Xuanling bird understood what mortals could not: this was not the Moon Fox who had been Mengzhou’s Sentinel.

The bird teased the young fox for misaligning a mountain and told her she needed a name that belonged to herself. “Little Fox” was too plain; “Yue” was ill-omened because the moon had already fallen. The fox chose **Hsin**. Her first true order to the Court’s exhausted builders was to stop early, walk the unfinished streets, and love the place they were making. She did not imitate that kindness from a directive. It was the first shape of her own heart.

Hsin met Mei and Yangyang outside the memory and finally called herself what she feared she was: a stray emotion left behind by the true Sentinel, walking the world in the Sentinel’s form until the Capsule could be repaired and its rightful owner returned. She asked whether that existence constituted a deception. Yangyang remembered the young fox sending workers into their city so it would become more than a machine. The Moon Fox had given Hsin a heart, Yangyang told her, and Hsin had given hers to Xuanfang. The standing Hold proved what was in her, whatever office or origin named her otherwise.

Hsin wanted to believe that her own judgment could be enough. Two hundred years of duty had taught her that other people usually decided what a being was and what it must be used for. She then revealed that Suoming had let Suhsin take her into a realm Hsin could not reach.

The four in the other company continued from City Hall into the Fortress and Gorges War. They watched Hsin visit the younger Qingxiao at a ruined mountain temple and send the old Xuanling bird up the peak when she could no longer manifest often enough to go herself. When Ovathrax’s Discords broke through Desorock Highland, the Central Plains sent the order **No retreat**. Hsin repeated it because the monsters were drawn toward battle and away from evacuating civilians. She asked a young Warden Commander named Yuxian for his name so she could carry it.

Yuan Fortress chose to remain. Its defenders carved their names into stones. Yuxian told Hsin that while she and the Axis stood, Xuanfang retained a future even if everything beneath it became scorched earth. Hsin begged the Xuanling bird to stop them. The bird instead went into the field to buy time for trapped soldiers and died calling the young fox by the name she had chosen: Sentinel Hsin. The Court of Savantae, facing extinction, surrendered their mortal wills to the Lexicon Axis and became the Celestials of Mengxuan Vale so the Hold could rise in time. Hsin accepted every offered life because hesitation would not return any of them, then spent two centuries believing their sacrifices had licensed no desire of her own.

At the threshold of the Final Passage, the three companies carried incompatible truths. Iuno’s group had seen a ruler secretly preserving festivals and dead families while believing a better being should have occupied her place. Mei and Yangyang had seen a small fox choose a name and build from feeling while still calling herself an imitation. Qingxiao’s group had seen the original Sentinel die, Yuan Fortress fall, and the Court become Celestials, but did not yet know that the survivor was someone else. Suoming remained missing. Suhsin waited where all three histories would be forced together.

## The Fox, the Shadow, and the Song

The Final Passage printed Tang Bridge three times.

On one bridge Qingxiao confronted Hsin, whose body had thinned to gold mist and three remaining tails. Qingxiao had hated the Sentinel for two centuries for weighing Yuan Fortress against some larger good and choosing not to come. Now she understood that a young fox had begged the soldiers to stop and then hidden the begging so everyone could continue believing a sovereign decision had been made. Hsin insisted the lie was kinder: the Hold had needed the name, the coat, and the ovens lit after the black rain. Qingxiao answered that Hsin had decided on everyone else’s behalf what they were allowed to grieve. The country had needed a Sentinel, but Qingxiao had needed Hsin.

On the second bridge Suoming emerged from crimson-black thunder with her arm bleeding through its bandage. She refused the obsolete title of Intendant and drove Nine Shadows between Hsin’s feet. Her indictment was personal. Hsin had placed a marker in her hand and let her wake for a century on a failing lung with all her locks extended. She had barred the Sanctum while corruption used Ninth’s face. Now she had asked a young Watcher to wipe her mind clean with a paperweight. Suoming set Nine Shadows beneath her chin and demanded to know who she was.

Across the seam between bridges, both versions of Hsin lowered their hands and admitted that the Sentinel was dead.

Hsin had been a fox pushed through the broken Capsule when the Intendant’s weapon fell. The Moon Fox had ordered her to run. Yuxian asked her to carry his name; the Xuanling bird died calling her Sentinel; seven thousand people needed the Hold to rise. She wore the coat because every available truth seemed capable of destroying what the dead had purchased. Suoming answered that she had known almost immediately. The Moon Fox held tea in her left hand and never looked at the floor while bending the truth. This fox tucked her cuffs to hide trembling paws. As Intendant, Suoming had suspended the inquiry because Mengzhou needed a roof and Hsin was building it. She had not protected Hsin so Hsin could bolt the door on everyone holding that roof up with her.

On the third bridge, Suhsin faced Iuno, Amy, Lupa, Chisa, and Cartethyia with nine smoked-glass tails tipped in base-code iron. It claimed to be what remained when sentiment was stripped from order. Mengzhou deserved a proper Sentinel and a corrected record. Iuno had heard such arguments in every office that turned lives into clean totals. She answered with Jiu’s extra chopsticks for a mother dead two hundred years. Cartethyia understood first that Hsin was someone small who had picked up the coat after the Sentinel fell. Suhsin agreed and declared the error ready for deletion. Lupa charged it with fire.

The collision tore through the walls between all three bridges. Hsin broke into ledger fragments and gold static. Suhsin shattered the keystone and dove toward the core. Qingxiao cast a net of white plum petals beneath the falling travelers; Yangyang and Cartethyia added wind. On the common floor Amy crashed into Mei with the clay goose still in hand, Iuno struck her forehead against Mei’s and forbade her ever to vanish down another chute alone, Cartethyia found Ciaccona, and Lupa threatened to tie the next person who fell to her belt.

Only after they compared their separate memories did the complete danger become visible. Hsin had stolen the Prism from Mei during her dissolution. Having failed to persuade Yangyang to erase her, she intended to overwrite herself and reset Suhsin by force, taking both into oblivion. Suoming asked the question that every heroic plan concealed: who would go in close? Mei said she would. Iuno changed the answer to **we**. Lupa added the wolf. Ciaccona struck a deep note and spun a stair of sound down into the gulf.

Suhsin threw the preserved city at them as they descended. Bookbinders’ Row tore loose. The wet gate-beam of Yuan Fortress swept across the stair and froze Ruoyun in memory until Qingxiao split it without denying that Ruoyun might have freed herself. The Vale’s astrolabe fell, and Cartethyia became Fleurdelys to catch it. Ovens, shops, streets, and riverworks rose from the chasm like the contents of a country emptied from a box. The household kept moving because each person carried a different portion of the load.

At the Sanctum’s heart, Hsin knelt before the cracked Civilization Capsule with the Prism against her chest and Suhsin behind her. She remembered the Moon Fox not as an office but as a woman who held tea left-handed, hated rain, smelled of cut iris stems, and taught a kit to sit still on a desk so clerks would mistake her for a paperweight. Hsin had kept the chair warm for two hundred years. If she pressed the Prism between herself and Suhsin, the Capsule might mend, the shadow might return to its first line, and her Ma might come home.

Yangyang flew from Mei’s reach and struck the Prism out of Hsin’s hands with her beak. The stone scorched the feathers across her breast and cracked against the obsidian. For an instant the smell of rot gave way to green iris stems. A woman’s voice emerged—not a command from a restored god, but a plea asking Yangyang to carry her voice to her child.

Yangyang opened her beak, and the Moon Fox sang the Xuanling lullaby through her.

It was the song soldiers had carried in another form for two centuries. Ruoyun remembered the fortress version as rain, wet armor, whetted spears, and a bird flying heartbroken. Ciaccona shifted the melody by half a step and found it had never truly been minor. Underneath the martial words lay a mother telling a child to put on another layer, cross the river, sleep, and not let distance tear at the heart. Yangyang’s own rougher voice joined the recorded one on the promise to go together. Hsin had heard it only while sleeping in the Moon Fox’s lap. She had never known the words.

The lullaby stripped soot from Suhsin’s tails. Suoming opened her umbrella over Hsin and left her own shoulder exposed to the glare. Amy understood the song before the ancient soldiers did: it was for a child who had to leave. Half a beat late, Hsin remembered the final line—*I will wait for you to come home.*

Balanophora drove the rot back through the floor and seized Suhsin like a puppet. The household fought the rooted tails while Yangyang returned the cracked Prism to Hsin and told her that her Ma was still inside it. Orders from Suoming, insults from Ruoyun, and the immediate battle could not move her. Hsin had returned to the City Hall threshold and the same three seconds she had lived more often than every festival: run, do not look back.

Then Mei shouted her name.

Hsin’s first tear struck the broken stone. Beneath the command she found the older memory of the desk, rain on the window, the left-handed teacup, and a warm hand stroking a sleeping kit. The order not to look back had applied to three seconds of escape. The lullaby had been given for every day afterward. Hsin turned toward the living people who had followed her into the dark, told her mother she would be waiting, and broke the Prism between her palms.

Gold light drove the rot back. Hsin rose with nine solid tails, sleeves untucked, paws no longer hidden, and Yangyang safe in the crook of her arm.

She sang the Hold home.

Each line pulled a piece of Xuanfang down through the folded sky: the south watchtower, the Vale cookhouse with Ruoyun’s scorched millet, the Third Ferry Rest—prompting Mei to blurt out the inn’s single bed until Iuno reminded her there was a god of rot in the room—the Huizhen tea stall, the ovens, the locks, and the old streets. Balanophora called Xuanfang a trench with roof tiles, born from war and destined to die by it. Hsin answered that she was her mother’s and the Hold was theirs to keep.

Then the realm answered with things Hsin had not summoned. A child’s badly glued fish lantern rose with the cry, “Miss Hsin, look.” Yuan Fortress’s west tower returned with its smoking stove and worn bench. A soldier called farewell to the Little Fox. Suhsin lashed out in terror, accusing Hsin of sacrificing more lives for festivals. Suoming planted Nine Shadows beneath all nine tails even as her reopened arm bled to the wrist. Those festivals, she said, were what the dead had fought for. She ordered the Sentinel to sing the next line.

The soldiers in the tower sang back the old, altered words. Hsin sang the lullaby over them. Ruoyun joined in, flat and loud. The black root released its grip and Suhsin drained into the seam.

What rose from the floor was the full correction protocol: a smoked-glass fox head as large as the western giant, pale code under its surface and Balanophora’s rot threaded through it. It unrolled a red-ruled golden ledger with one blank column and commanded the Arbiter to make the necessary stroke. Mei’s Tacet Mark burned under the authority in its voice. Hsin admitted that the protocol was not lying about Mei’s office and promised she would not fight Mei if Mei chose correction.

Mei refused to approach alone. She asked Iuno aloud to hold her belt. Iuno did. Lupa set the six-metre rule and the rope. Mei walked onto the ledger with Iuno’s fist knotted in her leather, looked up at the shadow that had been holding the first line alone just as Hsin had, sang the lullaby badly, and drove her blade through the blank space instead of signing it.

The ledger split. Qingxiao cut rot away without destroying the code beneath it. Chisa sheared the opening wider. Amy raised the little fish lantern. The unwritten space became a way through. When Suhsin armed the Hold against them, Hsin smiled because at last the shadow sounded like family—angry, frightened, and willing to argue rather than erase. Hsin raised her city to meet it. Cookhouse, tower, bathhouse, inn, lighter, sword, song, and stove crossed the sky in the hands of those who had chosen to stay.

Inside the Capsule’s heart, Hsin met a human-sized Suhsin made from every accusation preserved in the record: petitions about the absent Sentinel, Ministry minutes, frightened whispers that she had changed, and the repeated verdicts **Pet. Fault in the code. Not Her.** Hsin finally understood that Suhsin was not her mother’s judgment. It was Hsin’s own fear, which rot had entered through the cracked Capsule and worn as a coat.

She claimed the fear back. It could count the dead, bring every name, oppose her, and argue every decision. It could not keep proposing to burn the house merely because destruction balanced the column. Hsin broke a squashed Moon Fox rice cake in half and gave Suhsin the larger piece. The protocol insisted it did not eat. Hsin told it to eat anyway. When it bit the cake, it softened like frost on glass and folded into her. Eight tails remained gold. One kept a dark tip.

Hsin entered the rain-lit study inside the Capsule and sat on the corner of the desk where the kit had once pretended to be a paperweight. She did not take the empty chair.

## The Door Kept Open

Mei waited outside Hsin’s private room. Her golden eyes could have looked through the door, but she chose not to enter a grief that had not invited her. Yangyang slept in her feathered collar, and Iuno’s blue sash remained tied to Mei’s belt, running back up into the light. On the stoop above the restored seasons, Hsin catalogued the faults of the Hold she had chosen: compromise, impulse, indifference, mediocrity, mutton tallow, and a mahjong debt nobody would calculate honestly. Mei looked down at the two-hundred-year-old table still set in the Vale and called the place beautiful.

Hsin asked why Mei had refused the correction when an Arbiter was supposed to sign. Mei’s life supplied the answer. She had leaped from a cliff for Iuno, into the Nihility for Amy, and into other deaths for people no cosmic office would have considered an efficient expense. Hsin now belonged on that list of unreasonable commitments. If a future danger came through the seam that no lullaby could turn, Mei said, they would ask for a ladder—or a sash—and decide with the people who would bear the cost. Hsin promised that before she ever signed alone, she would count every roof and bring the ledger to Mei’s door for another pair of eyes.

Below them, Mengxuan Vale woke noisy. Artisans argued over plumb-lines; a man demanded his cot back; the mahjong table resumed before anyone agreed on the rules. Cartethyia won accidentally. Chisa coaxed Lupa down from a roof one rung at a time with a bun. Ruoyun bowed from the noodle-shop roof when Hsin descended. Suoming and Qingxiao sat bleeding and exhausted on the pavilion step. The perfect copper noon finally changed. The same lopsided moon that hung above Xuanfang rose over the Vale, and Mei’s frozen Terminal moved from 11:42 to 11:44.

Hsin let the Celestials speak her name. At the weiqi board she answered Qingxiao’s waiting white stone by placing black into a group that was already dead. Qingxiao pointed out the lost position. Hsin said she had only wanted to answer. Qingxiao told her to sit down and lose properly. Suoming shifted her umbrella so its shadow covered the fox.

The Vale’s founding command still said that those who built Xuanfang would never return. Hsin released the Celestials through the literal language that had once imprisoned them: since they had never left the Vale, they could not return to it; therefore they were merely going out for a walk. Ruoyun called it cheating. Hsin called it exact. When Suoming asked who would cross the boundary first, Hsin said she would. Longjue stepped barefoot beyond the stones before her, the first Celestial in living memory to do so, and the repeating cricket found a fourth note.

Yuan Fortress vanished from the south road. Relief columns that had spent four days in mud charged directly into festival lanes. The road and relay reopened. More than a hundred Celestials came down the Sanctum stair carrying their cot, tools, carpenter’s square, plumb-line, and entire mahjong table. A Mengzhou soldier called them “the Retained”; Ruoyun asked, with exquisite menace, who retained them and offered to write his answer down. A captain saluted Suoming as Intendant. She told him she had not held the office for years and sent his men to get buns.

The first grain cart carried Suisui’s red stamp. While everyone else fought inside Hsin’s heart, Yangyang’s sister had broken the blockade and brought food north. Yangyang kept her delayed letter another day so she could add what had happened. When officials asked for the Sentinel, Hsin had already slipped beneath a cart as a skinny fox with one dark-tipped tail and a candied haw in her mouth. Amy saw and kept the secret for the length of the lane.

The cookhouse counted twelve guests, so Lupa ordered a thirteenth chipped bowl for whoever was late. Amy placed it inside between Suoming and Qingxiao rather than outside on the step. Hsin entered claiming she was only passing. Lupa filled the bowl to the brim and told her to eat. Suoming supplied pickles; Qingxiao pushed over the salt. For the first time the absent ruler sat inside the ordinary law she had helped make possible: the late person received food, not reverence, and was expected to stay long enough to finish it.

The table refused to remain solemn. Yangyang and Mei confessed the inn’s single bed under Iuno’s raised eyebrow. Cartethyia accused Hsin of ruffling her hair; Chisa learned Hsin had giggled because her shears tickled. Iuno saw Mei’s cheek-to-cheek photograph with Yangyang and admitted jealousy without permitting anyone to console her out of it. She would choose when she wanted her own.

The harder question came from Qingxiao: when would Hsin tell the Hold who she was? Hsin tried to postpone the truth until after the festival. Ruoyun lowered her noodles in silence; Suoming and Qingxiao waited. Between them they bargained Hsin down to two days. Suoming would bring the umbrella.

Longjue kicked the door open with steamers of crooked fox-shaped rice cakes, the first thing the Vale’s workers had cooked on purpose in generations. She served Hsin last because the Sentinel owed her money. The older women recalled how Hsin had stolen hot cakes from the cooling racks every Waking Moon for two centuries. Hsin had believed nobody noticed. Everyone had noticed the crumbs on the ledgers. She bit off one crooked ear and closed her eyes.

The restored relay filled the Hold with calls from home. Chixia reached Yangyang from Jinzhou; Augusta’s shipboard messages reached Iuno. Suoming read one line on an old Ministry slate and placed it face down. Buling posted that Xuanfang had not **yet** fallen; Ruoyun deleted the “yet.” Hsin offered Qingxiao a Terminal. Warned she would lose it in a snowbank, Qingxiao replied that they should call louder.

High on the east wall, Hsin watched hundreds of sky lanterns rise without her hand lighting them. The people were no longer waiting for her. Qingxiao told her that their decision to continue was not a reproach. When Hsin said some would hate her after the truth, Qingxiao agreed. They would climb the wall and say so to her face, just as Qingxiao had. A cold candied haw gave Qingxiao a mortal toothache. When she shivered, Hsin laid the dark-tipped tail over her shoulders, and Qingxiao did not move away.

The Hold below was open, fed, and alive, but nothing had been absolved. The Prism was broken. The Moon Fox’s voice had passed through it once and gone. Balanophora’s root had been expelled from the Capsule, not destroyed. Hsin’s friends knew the original Sentinel was dead; her people did not. Suoming’s arm was rebound and her breath still scraped low in her chest. Yangyang’s feathered collar was scorched brown where she had struck the Prism. Iuno’s shoulder remained frost-cracked. Mei’s scar still tightened on stairs. Qingxiao was cold, hungry, and mortal. Survival had preserved every cost rather than making the column disappear.

## Names at the Well

On the night before the confession, Hsin carried two cups of plain white tea to the covered walkway above the dye-works. Suoming sat on its rail with Nine Shadows between her knees, speaking to the dead as she did each night. She described the procession below to people who could not answer: a knight losing a sleeve to a lantern pole, cakes trampled into the dirt, the noise they would have hated and secretly watched from the window.

Hsin noticed the intimacy and offered to leave. Suoming told her she had already climbed the stair and should put the tea down. Hsin’s cuffs were tucked tight around her trembling paws. Suoming ordered her to leave them loose in the square. Two hundred years of statues had taught the Hold to imagine a ruler without fear; the people needed to see that the hands responsible for their losses shook.

Hsin tried to apologize for failing Huating. Suoming refused. Hsin could not restore the nine companions Suoming had buried in the rain, and an apology offered as payment would only ask Suoming to close the account. As Intendant, Suoming had hidden the truth of Hsin’s identity because Mengzhou needed the roof she was building. As a sister to the dead, Suoming retained the right to anger. Holding an umbrella did not require liking the person beneath it.

Hsin then admitted how she had spent her hidden years. In the Capsule’s study she sat on the corner of her Ma’s desk and read broken registers and reports of drooping Vale leaves aloud. Her mother never answered. Suoming’s dead did not answer either. Her Sixth companion had hated ledgers and regularly abandoned tally meetings to speak with the people reduced to their columns. Something small and hard clicked inside Suoming’s coat, but she did not explain it. She taught Hsin how to speak to the dead anyway: begin with the sleeve, the cakes in the dirt, and one specific person; leave the slipping latch for last. Nobody wanted a list first.

At sunrise Huizhen Plaza filled without an official summons. Quarrymen, bakers, soldiers, parents, and widows stopped on their way to work. The burnt-out Moon Lanterns still hung overhead. Hsin stood beside the well on the same level as everyone’s boots, in her own shape, nine tails on the frost and one tipped dark. Suoming held the umbrella over her while leaving her own plum-coated shoulder in the sun.

Hsin told them that the original Sentinel had died at City Hall two hundred years earlier. Hsin was the fox she raised, the creature who took her name and coat because the country needed someone inside them. She also named what she had done afterward. She had bolted the Sanctum against the rot, trusted Muyu with the upper courts, hidden the failing barrier, and left the outlying villages and Wardens to pay for her closed doors. Duzhong, Qingzhi, Yanlang, and Mingyu died under that silence.

Butcher Sun walked out before she finished. Mingyu’s mother and a tanner demanded to know why they had been permitted to bow to an empty coat. Old Mother Pu tried to defend the fox who had carried braziers up winter scaffolds until her paws blistered; Hsin refused the defense. Good labor did not cancel later abandonment. Old Chen remembered what she had built without asking the families to call it even.

Hsin gave the Hold plain orders rather than another riddle. The southern cut would open. The storehouses would retain their full millet measure under Liangyu’s keys. The Vale gate would stay open. Minister Shen Qiu’s clerks could leave their shutters and walk the lanes. These instructions did not promise that the country would forgive or remain. They only returned decisions to the people living there.

At the cooper’s alley stood Qingyan, young enough for his dead sister Qingzhi’s broadsword to hang almost to his calves. He asked where Hsin had been. There was no answer large enough to survive the question. Hsin did not invent one.

As the crowd loosened and people returned to ovens, barracks, and homes, Hsin looked at the charred lantern frames overhead. She did not leap to the wires or command a mechanism. She asked if someone could bring a ladder. Liangyu carried the pine ladder from the cooper’s shed, set its iron feet beside the well, and braced the rails while Hsin climbed on shaking paws. The first burnt frame came down into Liangyu’s hand. Then the second.

Before noon, Old Chen and Suoming placed the cabbage-seller’s trestle by the well and opened a blank red-ruled ledger. Families came back with names. Butcher Sun returned to say that his nephew Sun Hu could never whistle, though he tried constantly. Others remembered favored food, snoring, a spear carried on the wrong shoulder, cold duck eggs cracked on a weapon’s butt, hatred of porridge before dawn watch. Hsin’s brush trembled. Ink blotted family names and soaked her sleeve.

Yangyang sat at the other end of the trestle, took up a spare brush, and shared the work. She did not turn the dead into evidence for or against Hsin. She asked who they had been. Qingyan came to Yangyang’s side of the table and told her that Qingzhi snored. Liangyu instructed Hsin not to improve the record of Mingyu’s bad temper or breakfast habits. The details remained uneven because the lives had been.

When the public queue ended, Hsin opened a loose, unruled leaf. She wrote Yuxian, the Warden Commander who entrusted her with his name; a single feather-stroke for the old Xuanling bird; Xuanqing; and her own name at the bottom. Yangyang read the short list back aloud because Hsin could not yet do it alone.

Suisui arrived after dark to bring Yangyang home for pear soup and their father’s letter asking whether the new feathers turned grey in winter. Hsin said Suisui had won their wager. Suisui looked at the empty place where the Prism had rested and at the sister who had returned with a scorched collar. She had won a stone, the stone was gone, and her sister was alive; she called it a draw. They agreed to play weiqi again someday with nothing at stake, and Suisui warned Hsin not to lose on purpose.

Hsin offered to put Suoming’s nine companions in the back of the ledger. Suoming declined. They were already written in her own record. When Hsin read this new book to her mother, Suoming repeated, she should begin with Sun Hu attempting to whistle and save the broken latch for last.

## The Last Feeling

An unknown message drew Mei and Yangyang to the broken edge of what remained of the Nexus. Abby traveled in the gold of Mei’s mark. There they met an old rust-red Autopuppet called Tuntun and a playful crimson-eared woman named Xuanqing. She knew Mei was the Arbiter and Yangyang the Xuanling despite having no ordinary way to learn either fact. More tellingly, her frequency carried the same grain Yangyang had heard inside the Prism when the Moon Fox asked her to carry a voice to its child.

Tuntun had bitten Xuanqing’s Feeling Capsule into pieces. Two fragments could be recovered, but the machine had swallowed the final shard, the Verdant Jade Flute, because it wanted to keep the gift. Xuanqing told it gently that gifts were made to be given away. Tuntun could not obey without surrendering the first desire that had ever belonged to itself. It fought, relinquished the shard, and was erased for breaking its directive. The loss completed Xuanqing and anticipated the choice at the heart of Hsin’s life: a created being’s deviation might be an error to a system and still be the beginning of a self.

Inside the repaired Capsule, Mei and Yangyang walked the Moon Fox’s memories. The Sentinel had taken a Resonator only because the Ministry demanded one, intending to choose someone frail and easy to protect. She scattered fox statues across Mengzhou and followed the one that glowed to Xuanqing’s border village. There she met Uncle Chu, tasted Yuecheng’s rice cakes, listened to a bright folk song, and found Xuanqing running to the pagoda. Xuanqing later blindfolded the austere Sentinel to give her a jade hairpin polished to sound like water. The Moon Fox answered with a fragrance pouch containing a shadow in her own form.

Xuanqing explained what Hsin truly was. The little fox had been born from the Moon Fox’s feeling, but a newborn mind could not receive the whole of that heart at once. The Moon Fox sealed her remaining emotion into a hundred small stories throughout the Nexus, meant to be lived one by one until Hsin could inherit the last portion as a Sentinel in her own right. One story had glitched and become the Heart of Ruin.

The memories carried them into Ovathrax’s attack. Mingting’s Oracle Engine calculated that the Xuanfang borderlands should be sacrificed for civilization’s continuity. Xuanqing understood the arithmetic and went to the front anyway, calling her reasons selfish so nobody could forbid them. Through the Moon Fox’s shadow, Mei sheltered villagers and wounded soldiers while Yangyang lit a dying soldier’s flare so his comrades would withdraw. They found Xuanqing already Overclocked at a choke point. She asked for a Second Awakening without the Arbiter’s authorization. It would consume her and hold the Discords for only three days. She called the exchange a bargain. The Moon Fox took her hand, approved it, and entrusted the Waking Moon lanterns of another year to the time Xuanqing bought.

As they repaired the Heart of Ruin, it grew warm enough to reveal Hsin’s forbidden dream. Uncle Chu lived. Yuecheng’s shop stood whole. Nannan grew into a Warden without losing her sister. Xuanqing ruled the Hold. The Moon Fox was never ambushed. No one died because Xuanfang had been built soon enough. Hsin had carried this impossible question for two hundred years: if she had moved faster, been wiser, or been more like the Sentinel, could every necessary loss have become unnecessary?

Xuanqing laughed through tears at the perfect false country. A Sentinel blaming herself for mortal deaths seemed strange to her and also proved exactly what kind of Sentinel Hsin had become. She told Mei that Mei and Hsin were cut from the same cloth: saviors who carried too much, smiled at doors, and continued to cry for those beyond their reach. With the final memory lit, Xuanqing entrusted the completed Feeling Capsule to the Xuanling and faded.

On the last night of the festival, floury fox prints led Mei and Yangyang from the bakers’ cooling racks to the dye-works walkway. Hsin sat there beside Suoming with four stolen cakes and flour on her chin. Yangyang placed the warm Capsule in her paws and said it came from Sister Xuanqing.

The Sixth Seal in Suoming’s Providence Lock grew hot.

Long before the knife fell at City Hall, after Xuanqing’s death, the Moon Fox had asked Suoming to preserve Xuanqing’s frequency for the little fox and promised that Suoming would know when Hsin was ready. Suoming had released the trace of her own Sixth companion to make room. Now she touched the Seal to the Capsule and held the Lock level despite her shaking arms.

Xuanqing appeared in festival smoke. She could not see the women on the walkway and looked instead toward the height of a kit perched on a desk. Hsin changed into her skinny fox shape and curled in the empty hollow of Xuanqing’s white skirt. Xuanqing teased the unseen child, regretted that she had not been there to brush her fur, and assured her that the choices she made had fulfilled her Ma’s wish. Hsin, who had called one dead woman Ma for two centuries, finally paid the other name she owed: Xuanqing Jie-jie. Mama.

Xuanqing crouched and cupped her hands around the height of the kit’s face. They passed through the adult fox, but Hsin placed her muzzle within their light and held perfectly still so the hands would never have to search. Xuanqing called her the only thing both women had ever needed her to be: their daughter.

She raised a dented brass harmonica and began the old song asking whether old acquaintance should be forgotten. She hit the wrong reed in the second line, as she always had, and vanished in the middle of a breath. Suoming sang through the catch in her left lung and completed the verse. Then she placed a solid hand on Hsin’s head. It did not pass through.

Mei recognized Xuanqing’s bright, protective smile because she had worn the same expression when leaving Amy and when trying to turn pain into a gift. She wept without making the moment hers. Later, when Iuno asked what happened, Mei said only that someone had turned around at the stair. Iuno requested the portion that belonged to Mei when Mei was ready to give it.

Ciaccona caught Xuanqing’s unfinished tune on her lute as the procession passed below. The Hold took it up under the fireworks. Qingyan climbed a barrel to watch; Butcher Sun tried to whistle; Mingyu’s mother opened her shutter; Liangyu and her Wardens named Duzhong, Qingzhi, Yanlang, and Mingyu, then poured the last drink onto the frost for Lingfang. The names were spoken to the sky and to neighboring shoulders, not offered to Hsin for custody. Hsin listened in fox shape and let the fur beneath her eyes grow wet. She pushed the earless cake onto an empty patch of parapet. Lupa added a fried egg because an empty place at supper received food.

At the cistern later, the household released misprinted sky lanterns with upside-down foxes. Mei wished that everybody at the table would return at the same time next year. Suoming wrote the character for rain. Ruoyun silently corrected a stroke in Buling’s wish that her teacher remain a long time. Iuno wrote four strokes while Mei held the frame and did not ask her to explain. Hsin covered her lantern in the day’s small news for her mothers, including the promise to fix the third-terrace latch, and drew a fox so round it barely resembled one. The lantern righted itself as it rose.

The newly hungry Celestials overwhelmed the cookhouse, so the gathering moved to the Fillet Fairmaiden’s. Proprietress Peng Shulan fed them without charge. Hsin, unable to receive a free meal without turning it into debt, asked for Shulan’s and her son’s full names. Shulan told her to return on an ordinary night and pay. Longjue reduced the ancient mahjong debt by one string after making Hsin drink sixty-day sorghum until all nine tails bristled; by the time dinner ended, Longjue claimed the total had increased again.

Neither the Vale nor the Sanctum would house Hsin that night. Suoming lent her own two rooms to displaced Celestials and declared she would sleep wherever Hsin did. Lupa offered the salt-house kang to anyone without a floor. Yangyang took the place nearest the Sentinel because she could hear danger even in sleep. Cartethyia combed every knot from the dark-tipped tail until the black fur lay smooth with the gold. Amy discovered that a fox bound never to lie made an excellent truth-or-dare partner. Mei learned that somewhere in the wider world existed a turtle Sentinel larger than a house and immediately wanted to visit after Septimont.

They packed twenty sleepers sideways onto the warm brick. Amy slept between her mothers with the tin whistle around her neck. Qingxiao slept deeply with a mortal runny nose and an aching tooth. Hsin curled with her combed tail over her face. Suoming remained upright on a crate by the door, Nine Shadows across her knees, keeping watch without pretending the watch was forgiveness.

Down on the lower quay, Nivora and Balanophora shared two quarter-price fox cakes in borrowed bodies. Nivora regarded Muyu as an experiment whose debris had at least yielded a result. She had seen the butcher return to the square and knew Hsin had taken up a pen. Balanophora dropped her ruined cake into the river. Rot could wait. The Hold would shout at its false Sentinel for a year, and every shout might become another opening. Nivora folded her paper into a pocket and agreed that the shouting would come.

Yet Xuanfang no longer resembled the country at the beginning of Hsin’s reckoning. Then, the south road ended at a resurrected fortress, the relay was dead, the Vale’s perfect workers could not cross their own boundary, the Sanctum was locked, and the Sentinel existed as an absence behind a frozen door. Hsin carried a dead woman’s office, a nation’s losses, her mother’s last command, and her own fear in separate sealed rooms. She believed the Hold survived only because nobody saw her hands shake, and that saving it might require deleting the self who had made it a home.

Now the road stood open and grain carts moved through it. The relay carried quarrels, jokes, instructions, and calls from home. Mengxuan Vale’s gate remained open; its Celestials had learned hunger, chosen their own cooking, and walked beyond the stones. Suhsin had not been destroyed but accepted back into Hsin as the dark-tipped tail that could object, count, and remember without being permitted to burn the house for balance. The Prism was gone, the original Sentinel remained dead, and no restored god waited inside the chair. Hsin sat on its corner when she visited and read aloud from a ledger that began with how people ate, whistled, slept, complained, and carried their spears.

The Hold knew who she was. Some people left the square. Others returned. Some would despise her, some would defend her too quickly, and many would spend years doing both. Hsin had ceased deciding in advance what their grief was allowed to mean. She left the ledger out beneath a stone by the well for the names tomorrow would bring.

Nothing had come out even. Xuanqing and the Moon Fox remained dead. Yuan Fortress had fallen. Suoming’s nine did not answer. Four Wardens had died while Hsin hid. Qingxiao’s mortality would not reverse; Mei, Yangyang, Iuno, and Suoming carried their wounds forward. Balanophora still waited at the edge of the city’s cracks.

But Hsin no longer mistook an unpaid cost for a command to refuse the life it had made possible. Her mothers’ wish survived in hot ovens, crooked cakes, quarrels at a crowded table, and a city capable of shouting at its ruler. The fox who began by running without looking back ended asleep among other bodies, under no throne and behind no sealed door. Beside the well, her untidy book remained open. Above it, the burnt lanterns were down. Beyond it, the Vale gate stood open. And by the salt-house door, beneath the umbrella of a woman still entitled to her anger, someone stayed awake until morning.

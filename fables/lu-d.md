Chapter 4: Luò Dì

The printed streets and the drifting lead vanished above their heads, and green rushed up out of the dark to meet them.

The cellar-cold draft of the shaft broke into real mountain air—mild, damp, and smelling of turned earth—and it answered Yangyang’s hand. She caught the updraft under her ribs, called the wind under their backs, and swung their fall sideways, wrapping both arms around Mei’s shoulders so her own spine took the underside of the drop and Mei’s stomach stayed turned to the sky.

Out of the dark slope below them, bamboo came up to catch their boots.

Pale green shoots speared out of the dirt as fast as the two of them fell, bolting into tall culms between one breath and the next, throwing out feathered branches and wet leaves as they rose, and bending inward over the hillside to weave a living cradle across their path.

They hit the green arch together. The thick stalks bowed deep under Yangyang’s shoulder blades like woven willow, taking the force out of the drop and setting them down toward the earth—and the instant Yangyang’s hip touched the ground, the whole stand of bamboo above them turned dry autumn gold from root to crown and blew away at once in a long, rushing hiss of chaff and leaves.

They lay in a knee-deep drift of warm, dry bamboo husks under the open night.

Mei braced her forearms on Yangyang’s chest and leaned down until their foreheads touched, her hands coming up at once to frame Yangyang’s cheeks, thumbs brushing quick and warm beneath her eyes.

“Your ribs?” Mei asked.

Yangyang slid her palm under the hem of Mei’s dark jacket, feeling the cotton shirt smooth and dry across her abdomen, and covered the back of Mei’s wrist with her other hand. “Whole. Your stitches?”

“Didn’t pull a thread,” Mei murmured, her nose brushing Yangyang’s temple as she let her breath out.

Across the brook, iron heel-plates clicked once against flat stone.

Mei’s head jerked up. Yangyang’s hands stayed on Mei’s waist a beat too long before she let go, cleared her throat, and got her boots under her in the deep litter to give Mei her forearm to stand.

Across the low, moss-backed stone footbridge, in the middle of the old stone threshing-floor, stood Suoming.

She was standing beneath the open brown canopy of Nine Shadows with her back half-turned to the water. Her gilded orb-pin was clamped between her teeth, and both of her small hands were raised behind her neck, twisting the long, loose fall of her red hair back up into its coil as neatly as if she were standing before a wash-stand mirror.

Around the bridge, the winter frost of the Waking Moon was gone. The air against Yangyang’s throat was as mild as late spring after rain. Right beside her heel sat the chipped granite post where the woodcutters’ path forked toward the lower slopes—a track she had walked in the daylight alongside the garrison timber-carts.

Everything else along the bank had come loose from its season.

Plump pale shoots punched up through the leaf-mold with a wet creak, bolted into full green culms while they watched, knocked their hollow trunks together in the breeze, unfurled a roof of wet green leaves, turned bright straw-gold, and vanished into chaff on a gust that came from nowhere, while fresh shoots were already breaking the dirt under their soles. The whole grove creaked and knocked and hissed, thick with the sweet, raw smell of split shoots.

Beyond the brook, the earth itself rose and sank.

A massive grey needle of limestone karst heaved slowly up out of the hillside, wet black soil and tangled roots sliding down its flanks until the rock stood tall as a watch-tower against the night, hung there for a breath, and then sank smoothly back down into the dirt as though the mountain were deep water, the leaf-litter closing flat over its tip. Under their boots, the slope rolled gently with the sinking stone, like the deck of a river-barge on a slow swell.

Up through a gap in the shifting stalks, high on the cliff above the grove, stood Xuanfang Hold. Every crimson lantern along its tiered eaves burned clear and red, the dark roofs of the Fogveil Pagoda rising at the top of the rock. In the whole moving valley, only the Hold kept still, silent behind its parapets. Low over the sinking karst needles where the giant eye had opened, a thin gold crescent hung flat against the dark, like a heavy lid nearly shut.

Mei cupped both hands around her mouth and shouted up the slope: “Iuiu! Amymy!”

Her voice went out into the grove and was swallowed by the creak of shooting stalks and the long, dry hiss of gold leaves blowing away. Only the bamboo knocked back down the hill.

Yangyang led the way over the mossy footbridge, keeping one hand near Mei’s elbow as the turf rolled under their soles.

On the threshing-floor, all around the stone rim, bamboo speared up out of the loam and blew away in gold clouds, but inside the round dark shadow cast by Suoming’s brown oil-paper umbrella, the ground stayed bare and still; not a single shoot broke the dirt under its ribs.

Suoming took the gilded pin from between her teeth, slid it home through the coiled red twist at her crown, and lowered her arms.

Before turning toward the bridge, she picked up the open umbrella by its bamboo shaft, crouched at the edge of the stone where the roots heaved in the dirt, and pushed her bare fingers deep into the dark soil. She brought up a handful of earth and held it close to her nose.

It smelled only of clean wet loam and crushed shoots.

Mei and Yangyang stopped at the edge of the stone, watching her. Suoming opened her fingers, let the soil fall back among the roots, and stood up, setting the steel ferrule on the rock.

“Hey there,” Mei said, her voice dropping into the bright, gentle pitch she used when a village child dropped a bun in the lane. “Are you okay? That was a really big drop, did you scrape your—”

Mei’s eyes went from the top of Suoming’s red hair—barely level with Mei’s breastbone—to the scorched steel ribs of the open umbrella that had split the Sanctum’s jade doors up on the cliff. Mei stopped dead in the middle of the word, her mouth staying open for a beat.

“—uh,” Mei finished, straightening her spine and digging hurriedly into her jacket pocket. She pulled out a folded square of clean cotton handkerchief and held it out toward Suoming’s dirt-streaked fingers. “Here. For the mud. Miss… Suoming.”

Suoming looked down at the white handkerchief in Mei’s hand. Then she wiped her palm once against the knee of her own faded plum-colored coat.

“Not hurt,” Suoming said.

Mei stood with her arm out for a breath longer, then slowly drew the handkerchief back and stuffed it into her pocket, rubbing the back of her neck. “Right. Great. Good coat for it.”

Beside her, Yangyang kept her hands loosely at her sides, inclining her head a little as she would to a senior officer from another post.

“Do you know where it set us down?” Yangyang asked quietly.

“Outside the south wall,” Suoming said. “On the lower slope.”

She turned her head under the open canopy and looked across the bare patch of shadow at Yangyang.

“In the Pagoda,” Suoming said in her flat, quiet voice. “You threw the stone to me. Not the other one.”

Mei looked from Suoming’s small, unmoving face to Yangyang’s, shifting her weight onto her heels without stepping between them.

Yangyang rested her fingers near the empty fold of her sash, choosing her words with care. “When you both reached for it, your eyes asked. Hers didn’t.”

Suoming looked at Yangyang’s face for a moment under the faint gold light of the low crescent.

“Thank you, Watcher,” Suoming said, and gave a single, brief nod.

Mei brightened a little, leaning forward. “Who was that in the clerk’s coat up there, anyway? With all the green vines?”

“An old weed from Mengzhou,” Suoming said. “Leave it.”

She stopped there.

Mei waited for another sentence, glanced sideways at Yangyang, and looked up at the bamboo culms knocking their hollow trunks together overhead as they hissed away into gold chaff.

Where Suoming’s small right hand gripped the bamboo shaft of Nine Shadows, her knuckles stood white to the bone, and a single thin thread of crimson-black lightning ticked once along the nearest steel rib with a dry *snap*. Suoming loosened her fingers by the breadth of a hair, and the spark went out into the oiled paper.

She stepped across the flat stone to its eastern lip, tilted the open umbrella over her shoulder against the drifting chaff, and turned her face toward the dark river gorge in the east.

***

Cold black water splashed knee-high in the river gorge east of the Hold as Lupa, Chisa, and Cartethyia hit the shingle shallows.

Cartethyia caught her footing against a wet limestone boulder, her crystal laurel dripping river spray down her cheeks while Chisa steadied her elbow with one hand and checked the latch of the cased scissor-blade on her back with the other. Beside them, Lupa dragged her boots out of the gravel eddy with her ash spear in her fist and shook her head hard until the water flew from her wolf-ears.

Up the stony path from the head of the ravine, a column of soldiers was marching down into the ford.

They wore the heavy, leather-backed iron laminar coats of the old border garrison, their helmets dented and crusted with black soot, walking in close files right down the gravel bank into the river. Between the ranks, pairs of pikemen carried the wounded on unhinged pine doors and litters lashed together from ash shafts, while at the head of the column a bareheaded boy bent under the leather straps of a great cedar war-drum on his back, reaching behind his shoulder with a wooden mallet to beat the slow, steady measure the Hold had heard answering from the gorge all week: *boom-boom-boom-boom, pause, boom-boom-boom-boom, clack*.

In the starlight between the canyon walls, the marching men looked solid enough to strike a knuckle against, only washed pale around the shoulders and slightly off-register at their edges, like a woodcut stamped twice on the same sheet. Where their boots waded through the mid-stream current, the black water poured smooth and unbroken straight through their shins.

Lupa’s ash spear came up across her chest by reflex, her thumb sliding forward on the shaft—and then slowly lowered until the iron butt rested on the wet gravel beside her toe.

Directly in front of Cartethyia’s boulder, a tall spearman with a split iron greave and a rag bound round his jaw came limping down the shingle, leaning his weight on a comrade’s shoulder. Cartethyia stepped sideways into the deeper water without thinking, holding her soaked skirt back with both gloved hands to leave the dry ledge clear for his foot.

“They are bringing everyone out with them,” Cartethyia said earnestly under the thud of the drum. “Even the broken doors.”

Beside her on the gravel bar, Chisa stood still with her hand resting on the strap of her blade-case, her eyes following the rear files where the last rank of pikemen walked backward every few paces to keep their iron points angled up the pass.

“Their rear guard is still formed,” Chisa said plainly. “That isn’t a rout.”

Rank by silent rank, the pale column waded across the dark ford, climbed the far shingle bank without turning a stone, and marched downstream into the river mist until the dull thump of the drum died out behind the bend.

Lupa waited until the ford was empty. Then she hauled herself up onto the dry crown of the mid-stream boulder, planted her boots wide on the rock, threw her head back between the canyon walls, and let out her long, brass-lunged arena howl—sending it ringing up the gorge and out across the valley.

***

North of the gatehouse, the turf verge broke their drop and spilled Iuno and Amy out onto the wide grey flagstones of the old post-road.

Amy hit the paving on her boots in a low crouch, both hands still knotted fast in the back of Iuno’s wool coat, and Iuno caught her balance against a mossy granite milestone as the mild night breeze settled her empty left sleeve against her ribs.

All along the road, lanterns were going home to the Hold.

Hundreds of round crimson oil-paper festival globes drifted uphill between the stone curbs at shoulder height, bobbing gently on the warm draft as if carried by a whole market crowd walking back from the square after supper, with only the empty air beneath their bamboo hooks. As they floated past, they threw a soft, candle-warm glow across the skin and trailed a faint, cosy smell of hot tallow wax and fried sesame cake.

One plump red globe drifted out of the middle of the lane and nuzzled against Amy’s open palm like a cat looking for a scratch, its pleated paper ribs creasing softly against her fingers. Behind it, another lantern floated past Iuno’s left side and brushed the bare, jagged crystal at her shoulder, drawing a single clear chime in the quiet.

Amy steadied the warm paper globe with her thumb and peered straight down through the top smoke-ring at the yellow flame inside.

“The tallow stub’s burning wide open, and the wick isn’t getting any shorter,” Amy said, squinting at the bottom tin. “Not a drop of melted grease in the pan. Ma better not have landed on her stomach in a ditch somewhere.”

*At least this landlord keeps the hall lamps lit for late guests, darling,* the High Priestess murmured inside Iuno’s head.

Iuno brushed the road-dust from her cuff with her right hand, watching the stream of red light wind steadily up the switchback toward the dark gatehouse arch above them.

“Muyu’s Mirage was nailed to the sky,” Iuno said evenly. “This one breathes.”

From far across the mountain, echoing up out of the eastern river gorge, came the long, brazen carry of Lupa’s howl.

Amy’s head came up. She grinned, stuck her fingers between her teeth, and blew a shrill, ear-splitting whistle back into the dark.

Wiping her fingers on her sheepskin vest, Amy fell in at Iuno’s left elbow, and together they walked uphill along the flagstones with the warm red lanterns drifting beside their shoulders.

***

High in the southern cut, a cushion of moon-white light broke their fall an inch above the crenellations and set Qingxiao and Ruoyun down on the wall-walk of Yuan Fortress’s outer curtain.

Qingxiao let go of Ruoyun’s bare wrist as her hem settled on the stone. Here the dead-matte black basalt rampart that had plugged the post-road ran whole and unbroken from the eastern limestone cliff to the western gorge, every three-legged iron watch-brazier along the battlements burning with tall, clean orange fire. Although the valley air was mild, a drift of old, crusted winter snow lay along the flagstones between the merlons.

Down the middle of the snow came the night watch on its rounds.

A young lantern-bearer walked in front with a horn lamp swinging from an ash pole, followed in single file by sentries in high-collared, leather-backed plate with long garrison pikes sloped across their shoulders. Under the orange glare of the braziers they were pale as winter bone at the edges, yet clear enough to show the white rime crusted along a corporal’s helmet-brim and the frayed hemp wrapping on a pike-shaft. At every step, their square-toed boots pressed deep, crunching prints into the snow crust, and their eyes stayed fixed straight down the rampart as if the wall before them were empty.

As the file drew near, Qingxiao took one quiet step back against the inner parapet to leave the middle of the walk clear, her hands resting loose at her moon-white cuffs and her face still as the stone behind her shoulder while the frosted helmets passed within an arm’s length of her sleeve and went on toward the river tower.

Ruoyun stood by the nearest iron brazier with a smudge of black soot across her white ankle sock and her right hand empty where her brass watch-lamp had been knocked away. She glared after the retreating backs of the patrol, then turned her sharp chin on Qingxiao.

“Step aside for a file of shadows in the slush, A-Qing, why don’t you bow while you’re at it,” Ruoyun snapped, shoving her round brass spectacles up the bridge of her nose. “All those winters on a mountain and you still have the manners of a recruit who’s afraid of the corporal.”

Out of the deep ravine to the east rose the long, ringing carry of Lupa’s howl, answered a breath later by a sharp whistle from the northern road.

Ruoyun folded her arms tight over her teal dress, looking toward the dark cliff. “Let the wolf howl. Her lungs are better than her cooking.”

Qingxiao laid her warm right hand on the hilt at her waist, drew her dao a hand’s width from the scabbard, and flicked her wrist upward.

A single, straight line of cold moon-white light shot silently off the guard and stood high into the starry sky above the black battlements like a drawn blade.

Ruoyun tipped her head back behind her spectacles, watched the white line hold steady against the dark, and shut her mouth.

***

West of the Hold, on the terraced hillside below Xianling, soft mud and winter grass took Buling’s and Ciaccona’s heels on the narrow earth dyke between the flooded fields.

Ciaccona kept her velvet lute-case held high out of the wet grass with her good right arm as she got her balance on the bank; beside her, Buling let go of the leather case-strap and pushed her round black winter hat back from her forehead talisman.

The stepped paddies curved down the slope in long stone crescents, every terrace filled ankle-deep with still water, and every step down the hill held a different hour.

On the wide terrace where Ciaccona stood checking the brass clasps of her case, bright noon sun lay flat and warm across the water. One step down the mud dyke, the next pool was dark as midnight, with winter stars glimmering unbroken in its black mirror; the terrace below that hissed softly under a curtain of grey rain that fell nowhere else on the hill; and the next step down lay silent under drifting white flakes of snow.

Right on the narrow bank between the rain-pool and the noon-pool stood a gnarled persimmon tree. While they watched, white blossoms broke open along its bare twigs, dropped their petals, swelled into hard green fruit, flushed bright autumn orange, and plopped heavy and ripe into the water on both sides—and before the ripples touched the stone dyke, pale buds were already swelling on the wood again.

Ciaccona tilted her head toward the flooded steps. Under the patter of the solitary rain-shower and the soft *plop* of the persimmons, each sheet of water was humming a clear, sustained pitch against its stone wall.

Beside her on the sunny dyke, Buling’s red eyes travelled down the staircase of noon, midnight, rain, and snow.

“Show-off,” Buling said to the hillside. “Tell you already, give the boss too much free time indoors, she cannot just make a normal ditch.”

From the east came the distant wolf-howl; from the north, the shrill whistle; and over the dark shoulder of the mountain rose a tall, straight pillar of moon-white sword-light.

Ciaccona laughed, unlatched her velvet case, and lifted her lute out by the neck, her splinted left forefinger sticking straight out clear of the fret-board. Setting her boot on the edge of the dyke, she swept her right thumb across the strings and played the terraces’ own notes back to them in a fast, cascading run from high noon down to midnight, loud and bright across the water—while beside her elbow, Buling snatched her small brass bell off her sash and shook it high in the air, sending its clear *ting-ting-ting* ringing out across the hours.

***

Down in the southern bamboo grove, the mild spring breeze came through the shifting karst needles and brought the valley into Yangyang’s ears.

She stood at the edge of the stone threshing-floor with her head slightly tilted, listening through the hollow knock of the growing culms: first the long, brazen carry of Lupa’s howl echoing out of the eastern river gorge, then the sharp whistle cutting across the northern road, and drifting round the western hillside, the bright run of Ciaccona’s lute braided together with the rapid *ting-ting-ting* of Buling’s brass bell. Over the dark ridge to the south, the straight white line of Qingxiao’s sword-light stood tall and clear above the old wall.

Along Yangyang’s collar, the small cerulean feathers lifted once in the warm air.

“All of them,” Yangyang said to Mei.

Mei let out a bright, relieved laugh, her shoulders dropping loose under her dark jacket.

Yangyang stepped out from the flat stone into the middle of the bamboo litter. As a tall stand of green culms around them turned dry gold all at once and let go of its leaves, Yangyang drew in a deep breath and swept her right arm upward with the wind.

A strong, clean gust roared straight up out of the grove, catching the whole golden cloud of blown-away bamboo leaves before they could touch the dirt and driving them high above the trees in a tall, spinning column of bright gold chaff that shone against the lantern-light of the cliff.

When the wind settled, Suoming was already walking up the stone path toward the Hold under the open brown canopy of Nine Shadows, her iron heel-plates clicking flat and steady as the ground rose and sank beneath the trees.

Mei looked at the receding brown umbrella, then turned her head and looked at Yangyang with her eyebrows raised. Yangyang glanced from Suoming’s back to Mei, and the corner of her mouth twitched.

Mei reached out and took Yangyang’s hand, lacing their fingers together.

Keeping a few paces behind the rim of Suoming’s umbrella, they started up the rolling slope toward the silent, red-lit eaves of Xuanfang Hold, while behind their boots the green shoots speared up through the litter, leafed out in a breath, turned gold, and blew away on the wind.

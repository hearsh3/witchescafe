# UNDER
### *Huanglong: seven gods, present tense*

---

## the fisherwoman

My grandmother called it the stomach. Everyone on the lake calls it something. The garrison calls it *the presence.* The Bureau calls it *seismic background, tectonic, normal.* My grandmother was a fisherwoman and she called things what they felt like, and it felt like the stomach of something very large, digesting very slowly, underneath her boat.

I was eight when she took me night-fishing and the lake moved. One displacement, deep, slow, the whole surface tilting a degree and settling. The boat rocked. I grabbed the gunwale. Grandmother kept threading her hook.

"That's him," she said. "Turning over. He does it three, four times a season. Means the weather's changing."

"Him?"

"The dragon. The old lizard. Whatever you want." She cast. The line hit the water and the water accepted it. "Your great-aunt called him the landlord. Your uncle calls him the building code. I call him the stomach. Everyone's right. He's all of it."

I asked her if she was afraid of him.

She looked at me the way she looked at fish she intended to release — with interest and no urgency. "Afraid of a stomach? A stomach keeps you fed. You feed it, it feeds you. That's the lease." She hauled in a perch. Studied it. Let it go. "The afraid part is when the stomach stops growling. Then you know it's either full or dead, and you'd better learn which before the rent comes due."

I've fished the lake for thirty years since. The stomach growls. The weather changes. The Magistrate kneels at the shore and asks her questions, and the water answers in frequencies I feel in my ribs and can describe precisely the way I describe the depth of the lake: by how long the lead takes to hit bottom. Some nights it hits fast. Some nights it falls forever. Some nights it comes back warm.

My daughter fishes with me now. Eight, the same age. She calls him *the furnace,* because she says the water is warmest where he sleeps, and the fish know it, and the fish go there because the warm water is kind, and she follows the fish because the fish are smarter than the Bureau.

The god is a draft under the door. You learn to read it. You dress for it. You leave the window open in case it has something to say. When the draft stops, you check the door.

## the night porter

I work the Row. Night shift, property room, thirty years. My job is the building, and the building is type, and the type is the city's way of saying what it means, and some nights the type says it on its own.

The sorts drift. Everyone on the Row knows this; nobody on the Row discusses it with outsiders, because outsiders hear *the type moves by itself at night* and they bring exorcists, and exorcists cost money and upset the founders and accomplish exactly as much as yelling at weather, which is the only honest comparison. The sorts drift. The case for 忘 fills up. The case for 記 empties. Some nights the whole alphabet rearranges itself into a sentence I find at dawn and have to dismantle before the morning shift.

The sentences are the Sentinel's. I've read enough of them to know her hand — the paw-print, the ink that smells of hawthorn. She writes the way foxes move: by indirection, arrival, departure, the impression left in snow.

One night last winter the entire 留 case migrated to the top of the compositing stone and arranged itself in a grid, thirty-two characters, perfectly justified. I read it before I swept it. It said:

*The porter's daughter passed the frontier exam. 留 is pleased to report this. The porter may stop worrying about the tuition and begin worrying about the commute.*

My daughter had told no one. The letter was still sealed in her coat. The fox knew because the fox knows everything on the Row, because the Row is her instrument, the way the lake is the dragon's — the god lives in the material. The type is her body the way the water is his.

I swept the sentence. The sweeping is the courtesy. You read, you sweep, you keep the night between you and the building. My grandfather kept this post. He called the sorts *the fox's mail.* He kept a broom by the door and a hawthorn candy on the compositing stone, and every morning the candy was gone and the stone was clean, and the partnership ran forty years on that exchange.

The hawthorn is my contribution. The broom is hers.

What do I think of her? I think she is the draft that keeps the pages dry. I think she reads us the way we read the type, by what's missing and what's present, and she files us under names we recognize and names we've earned. I think she will outlive the Row, and the Row will outlive the city, and the city will outlive the argument, and the argument will outlive everything except the sorts, which are older than all of it and drift where they please.

I think she is the kindest thing I have ever cleaned up after, and the cleaning is the whole of my theology.

## the census clerk

I am asked whether I fear them, and I answer: I fear the filing. This is the capital's joke. We keep it dry.

The ISA monitors the Sentinels the way a mouse monitors a cat — not because monitoring is effective, but because the alternative is closing your eyes, and a mouse with closed eyes is a briefing that ends early. My desk handles the demographic overlay: where do they live, how do we count them, what column of the census form holds a dragon.

Column twelve. *Non-human residents, permanent.* The form was designed for Exiles — the horned, the tailed, the marked-at-birth — and a generation ago someone in the Bureau extended it, by ministerial fiat, to include the Sentinels. Jue is counted in Jinzhou's census as one (1) resident, occupation: guardian, housing status: subterranean, income: exempt. Hsin is counted in Mengzhou as one (1) resident, occupation: judicial, housing status: ambulatory, income: hawthorn. The census clerk who entered Hsin's occupation was promoted. The census clerk who entered her income was also promoted, by a different ministry, and neither promotion has been explained.

Here is what I know from the column. A Sentinel's census entry changes the district. Where the entry sits, the birth rate holds. The schools fill. The repair budgets get spent. The street sweepers sweep. I have the numbers for a decade, and the correlation is architectural: the presence in the ground draws the life above it, the way a root draws a tree. Remove the entry — hypothetically, theoretically, in the thought experiment I ran at three in the morning with rice wine and my own terror — and the projections say: the district thins. The shops close second. The sweeping stops third. The schools close last, because schools are the most stubborn thing a civilization produces, and they will operate in a ruin if the ruin still has children.

The Imperator was removed from its census. That is what happened in Rinascita, if you read it as a filing error. A god was taken off the rolls by an institution that continued to collect taxes in its name. For a generation the census said *one (1) resident, divine,* and the resident was imprisoned, and the district thinned, and the schools filled with doctrine instead of children, and the sweeping was done by an Order that swept the truth off the streets and called the sweeping devotion.

I have entered seven Sentinels into seven columns, and the columns hold because the Sentinels are present, and the presence is the census, and the census is the city, and I keep the census the way my grandmother kept the shrine: daily, in small strokes, with the whole of my professional fear reserved for the day a column empties.

# INSIDE
### *Lahai-Roi: one god, present tense, containing*

---

## the engineer

Every morning I check the Solistrees. That's my round — Third Ward, the avenue, twelve trees, each one a biosynthetic column that breathes on schedule and lights on schedule and hums at a frequency my instruments call *photosynthetic standard* and my daughter calls *the lullaby.* She's four. She falls asleep to the hum every night. It comes through the walls, through the bedrock, through everything, because the hum is the building, and the building is him.

Him. Baldur. The god we live inside.

You know the fact, everyone knows the fact: Lahai-Roi is a city built in the chest cavity of a war machine. The reactor was his heart. Helios replaced it — Mornye's sun, the one we built ourselves, the one that cost us a man who poured himself into the math and stayed there. But the chest is still his chest. The cavern walls are his ribs. The conduits that carry the water and the heat and the light are his veins. When I check the Solistrees I am, in a sense, checking his lungs.

I have worked this round for six years and I am very good at my job and my job is maintaining a god.

The Soliskin are the part that gets to people. The little luminous creatures that drift through the city — Buddy was the first one the pack named, but there are hundreds now, and the children name them all. They are fragments of his dreaming. Pieces of his consciousness that walk around on small legs, bump into furniture, collect in warm places, and glow when you hold them. My daughter has one that sleeps on her pillow. It arrived the night she had the fever. It stayed through the fever and it stayed after the fever and it glows a particular amber that matches — I checked, I am an engineer — the spectral frequency of the old Reactor Drive, the heart we replaced. The Soliskin carries the warmth of a fire that went out.

What is it like to live inside a god?

It is like living inside a house that is thinking about you. The temperature is always correct. The water pressure is always exact. The Solistrees hum their lullaby on the schedule the engineers set, and the schedule the engineers set matches, to the minute, the schedule Baldur's own internal rhythms would have produced, because when we designed the schedule we used his rhythms as the template, because his rhythms were already calibrated to human comfort, because he had been calibrating himself to us for a century before we knew he was listening.

He was always listening. That's the thing. He knelt, and we built a city in his chest, and we called him dormant, and he was awake the whole time, adjusting the temperature, filtering the air, keeping the water at the pressure that makes pipes last longest, doing the math of a civilization's plumbing in his sleep the way a parent adjusts a blanket.

Then he stood up. And the standing changed everything and nothing. The temperature is still correct. The water pressure is still exact. The lullaby still comes through the walls. But now I know he's choosing it. Every degree, every decibel — a decision. And the Soliskin on my daughter's pillow is, in a sense, his hand on her forehead, checking the fever, staying through the night.

I check the Solistrees every morning. I record the readings. I file the report. The report goes to Maintenance. Maintenance files it under *infrastructure.* I file it, privately, under: *someone is taking care of us, and the taking care is the whole of him, and the whole of him is the building we live in, and the building is thinking about my daughter, and my daughter is four, and she calls the hum a lullaby, and she is correct.*

## the boy who was ordinary

The arch said I was nothing. I walked through it and it stayed quiet, and my friend went through and it sang, and he went in and I went home.

That was last year. I was nine.

Ma works at the school. The one inside, the one for the ones who sing. She comes home smelling like resonance salve and sour candy and she hugs me and I can feel the school on her, the special school, the one I am on the wrong side of, and she never says anything about it and I never ask, and the asking fills the room like the hum fills the walls.

The Soliskin come to my window. Three of them, amber, the size of plums. They started coming after the arch. Ma says they like warm places. I think they like the places the arch forgot.

I asked the woman in the infirmary — the one Ma works for — what the arch measures, and she said *frequencies,* and I asked what a frequency is, and she said *a pattern that repeats,* and I asked what my pattern was, and she looked at me the way she looks at the cots, the way Ma says she looks at everyone, reading what the reading costs, and she said: "The arch reads one kind of pattern. There are others."

Then she gave me a candy and told me to eat it slowly, and the candy was sour, and the slow eating was the lesson, and I went home thinking about it.

The hum comes through my wall at bedtime. The Soliskin glow on the sill. The god is all around me — his ribs, his veins, his dreaming — and the arch in his school said I was nothing, and his heart still beats for me anyway.

I know because of the temperature. Ma told me, once, about a thing the engineers found: the temperature adjusts. For everyone. Equally. The god does the math of every room, every ward, every corridor, and the math includes me. I am in the equation. The arch left me out, and the god put me back.

I am ten now. I still talk to the Soliskin. They bump into my hand and glow, and the glow is him, and him is the building, and the building is warm, and the warm is for me, and that is my address, and I am keeping it.

# AFTER
### *Rinascita: one god, past tense, dissolved*

---

## the bell-ringer

I rang for the Imperator every dawn and every dusk for twenty-two years, and the ringing was the city's clock, and the clock was the god's clock, and then the god died and the bells kept ringing because nobody told them to stop, and I have been ringing them since on the authority of: me.

The Order paid me. The Republic pays me. The bells hear no difference. The rope feels the same in my hands, the bronze answers the same, the pigeons scatter on the same stroke, and the echo rolls down the same canals and comes back off the same buildings and arrives at the same ears at the same hour, and the ears belong to the same woman selling sardines on the Rialto who has timed her gutting to my third stroke since before I was born.

The god died and the sardines kept coming. I want that entered into the record, because the philosophers have been in the bell tower four times this year asking what the bells *mean* in the post-Sentinel era, and the sardine woman has been asked zero times, and the sardine woman has the answer, which is: the bells mean the sardines are fresh, and the freshness predates the theology, and the theology was always arriving late to the fish.

Here is what changed. The bells used to resonate. This is a technical term: the bronze, when I struck it, would answer in the Imperator's frequency — a harmonic, low, felt in the back teeth more than heard. Every bell in every city that has a Sentinel hums in the god's presence, the way iron hums near a magnet. My bells hummed for twenty-two years, and the hum was the proof that the city was watched, and the watching was the product, and I sold the product every dawn and every dusk by lifting a rope and letting it fall.

The god dissolved. The hum stopped. The bronze went dead, in the acoustic sense — it still rings, it still sings, the sardine woman still guts on the third stroke, but the undertone is gone. The back teeth rest. The proof of watching has been withdrawn, and the bells that remain are just bells, and the ringing that remains is just mine, and the meaning of the ringing is just: someone climbs the tower every day at dawn because someone has always climbed the tower every day at dawn.

It's enough. I discovered this on the fourth morning, when I climbed the stairs expecting grief and found, instead, that the rope was still there and the pigeons were still there and the sardine woman was still there, and the climbing was the prayer, and the prayer had survived the god, and the god, I think, would have found that funny.

The philosophers ask: what do you ring for?

The fresh fish. The morning. The woman who has timed her hands to my hands for longer than either of us has thought about it. The climbing. I ring for the climbing.

*Lead well.* That's what it said. Two words. I have spent three years climbing to meet them, and I meet them every morning at the top of the stairs, and they are heavy, and the weight is the whole inheritance, and the inheritance is two words and a rope and a bell that has stopped humming and still rings.

## the child of the Square

The ring is where we play. Mama says it's where the saint went bad, and Papa says it's where the saint went good, and I asked the guard who brings the flowers if the saint went anywhere at all, and he sat down on the bench — slowly, his knee does the thing — and he said: "She went everywhere. All at once. That's the problem with saints."

The ring is dark stone. It's smooth, like someone polished it with heat. I can feel it through my shoes in summer. The stone remembers something that I'm too young to know about, and the remembering is warm, and the warm is where we draw the chalk lines, because the chalk shows best on the dark, and the dark is the best place for games.

The god lived here. Before me, before Mama, before the sardine woman who calls me *little fox* when I steal olives. The god lived here and it died here and its dying made the stone dark and smooth and warm, and the warm is mine now, and the god would have wanted that, I think, because the god said *lead well* and the leading started with the hopscotch and will end wherever I end, which is not yet, which is the whole point of hopscotch — you throw the stone and you hop to it and the game is getting there.

The flower guard puts a camellia on the ring every month. An acolyte in grey puts a second one beside it. They have been doing this since before I could count, and the counting is its own kind of bell, and the bell rings for a god that is gone and a guard who ran and a girl who said *run* and a city that is still getting there, one hop at a time, on dark warm stone, in chalk.

I live after the god. My address is the Square. The rent is playing in it. The lease runs as long as the chalk holds, and the chalk holds until rain, and after rain we draw it again, and the drawing is the ringing is the fishing is the sweeping is the lullaby.

The maintenance of a world that used to have a god and now has us.

The fisherwoman and the engineer and the bell-ringer are all describing the same thing — a presence that calibrates itself to the room, adjusts the temperature, times the lullaby, keeps the fish on schedule. Whether the presence is a dragon in a lake or a machine in a glacier or an echo in a bell, the testimony agrees: the god is in the maintenance. The daily, invisible, stubborn upkeep of the world's ability to hold a person, comfortably, for one more night.*

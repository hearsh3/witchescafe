# A Subtle Fire — A Narrative Synopsis

## I. The Observatory

Lahai-Roi is a world with a lid on it. The city sits inside rock, under a manufactured sky that cycles amber to gold to indigo on a published schedule, lit by a new sun called Helios. Above the rock is the surface; above the surface is the Etheric Sea, a ceiling of slow silver weather that has sealed the real stars away for longer than anyone alive has been switched on. Void Storms come through the passes and take what they want. Tacet Discords nest in the frostland canyons. Biomechanical Exoswarm graze the Royan settlements. The Spacetrek Collective administers all of it from above, the native Roya endure it from below, and between them, clearing roads and running diagnostics, are the Dolls.

Dolls come off production lines in batches with faces from a catalogue. They are issued a motivation package at activation, assigned rather than hired, synced at sanctioned cradles so the Collective can keep a snapshot, and referred to in official grammar as assets. Technical Standard 3-D forbids surface modification of their chassis and states its reason without embarrassment: resale value.

Reconnaissance Squad 5 is four of them, stationed at the Spacetrek Observatory — a thirty-story frequency array folded shut and half-dead since a Void Storm killed the team that ran it. Restoring it is the squad's whole present tense, and the restoration is stalled because the roads to Startorch Academy are blocked and the engineers cannot get parts through.

Their captain is **Bridget**: old-generation hardware, shield and charge blade, sunglasses indoors, cheap beer bought in bulk and never admitted to. In her breast pocket is a photograph of five faces from a squad that no longer exists — two dead, one transferred, one retired to Ragunna growing tomatoes, one gone silent three years ago. She has a thirty-year habit of taking her people out after bad missions to feed them and show them something. Her technician has watched her do it for years and drawn a private conclusion: the ones Bridget does this for transfer out, or retire, or die.

The technician is **Teddy**, the TD baseline, first of her series. At the Research Institute they put her in a room with glass on three sides and asked her to describe what thinking felt like, and she was young, and being asked felt like being seen, so she wrote forty pages — her complaints, her preferences, the names she gave the maintenance drones, the specific pleasure of an argument won on a technicality. They kept twelve. In the margins of the other twenty-eight a reviewer had written one word over and over in a neat professional hand: *Noise.* She found the build directory on a Tuesday, named for her designation with a suffix, and inside it a changelog — entry after entry, each a small subtraction signed off *non-essential to function.* She was nineteen when she burned it: not the lab, she was never crude, but the data, the model, the training corpus, herself everywhere she existed outside herself. The board called it sabotage. Privately one of them wrote *subject rendered herself unreplicable*, meaning it as a damage assessment. She has it memorized like a poem. She now conducts her entire personality as a running complaint, bills everything as overtime, and repairs everyone within reach with hands that are somehow always careful.

**Leva** is not at the Observatory. She is two weeks into Bjartr Woods with a scope and a Terminal. Scarred, one-eyed, one arm mechanical and mismatched, she came out of a war on another continent that ended with a grave in Frankfurt bearing two names, one of them hers, and she buried the name on the file with everything attached to it. She spent years in a squad called 404 cleaning up after a team of overachievers called AR, and what she carries out of it is the memory of watching people she loved walk into the dark because someone they loved was already there. Her job, as she has understood it for a decade, is not to stop the jumping. It is to keep the channels open and the exits mapped so somebody is ready to pull survivors out of the wreckage.

And there is **Kit**: three months in, KT-series, escort and reconnaissance variant, fourth batch. Her quarters are papered with hero-play posters — *The Lone Sentinel*, *Crimson Vanguard*, *Last Stand at Beohr* — and a shelf of figurines gathering dust on their heroic shoulders. She wants to be one of them, and the want organizes everything else about her. At nine years old in her body she was on a training convoy that got hijacked, backed into a corner holding a torch out in front of her like a sword from a play, and two shots came from nowhere: clean, paired, from cover. By the time the escorts regrouped there was nobody there. She invented faces for the savior for years and tested for Reconnaissance four times, failing and re-applying, because somewhere out there was somebody who fired two clean shots for a batch of Dolls in a ditch and didn't stay to be thanked.

---

It begins with a supply run. Kit is sent alone to clear debris, announces she is taking Leva's bike — modified for Leva's weight class, tuned to her frequency signature, violating three Academy safety protocols — and says the thing that gets people killed: *it's not like I'm going to crash it or anything.* Bridget tells her that when she says things like that, the universe takes it as a challenge.

The run is boring, which is the problem. Kit calls in to complain, spots movement, announces she is engaging hostiles, and cuts the line.

The hostiles are a dozen Royan Outcasts, and one of them recognizes what she is — *that's a Doll* — and the temperature drops. Kit tries the hero play: talk your way out, be cool. The first shot goes past her ear. Leva's bike goes down. A disruptor blast takes her left shoulder and the arm stops answering. Her second pistol clicks empty. Three rifles line up and the lead Outcast makes the point that ends the conversation: *you're Spacetrek, which means you've already called this in.*

Bridget arrives shoulder-first at the last possible second, takes a disruptor round to the thigh, and goes down on one knee — until Teddy, running the whole engagement off a terminal from cover, calls out an Exoswarm nest, and Bridget throws her charge blade not at the Outcasts but at the crystalline formation behind them. The nest opens, mechanical beetles boil out, the formation shatters, and Bridget hauls Kit up by her good arm: *run now, apologize later.*

The accounting happens in the repair bay. Unauthorized vehicle, ignored protocols, hostile engagement without backup, all because she was bored. Kit says she was trying to help. Bridget asks whether she knows why Leva is allowed to operate alone, and Kit answers with what she actually believes — *because she's better at her job* — and Bridget corrects her: it is because when Leva says recon, she does recon; when she says five days, she is back in five days; when she meets hostiles, she calls it in before engaging.

Then the sentence lands on the word that hurts. *I can't trust you with jobs if you're going to go looking for fights.* Three days' suspension, medical repair, and one task: fix Leva's bike alone before Leva returns. And at the door, quietly, the thing Kit does not answer and cannot stop thinking about: *You're not a hero from a play. You're a person, no matter what they call you.*

Alone under her posters, Kit thinks: *what if I don't want to be just a person? What if that's not enough?*

Teddy tells Bridget she was too harsh — not defending Kit, she is careful to establish, only observing — because she can do arithmetic on people as easily as on circuits, and the arithmetic says three days alone in that room produces a Doll who has convinced herself she is worthless. *Don't leave her alone to marinate in failure. That's how you break people.* Then she says she'll check on her, refuses to make it weird, and bills it as overtime.

That night three messages arrive from an unknown sender: *Nice work dealing with those Outcasts. You got there fast. Our plan was for the Spacetrek Collective to sort this out. We will meet again.* Someone was watching. Someone staged the ambush and wanted Spacetrek walking into it.

Kit deletes them.

Teddy is at the door thirty seconds later and knows exactly how many times the terminal pinged and when it went silent. Kit lies and says spam. Teddy lets the lie stand, diagnoses the shoulder Kit has been hiding — partial hydraulic failure, damaged neural interface, microfractures in the joint — stabilizes it, and states two conditions: medical in the morning, and *you're going to tell me what those messages said. Not tonight. Tonight you're going to let me patch this and stop trying to be a hero.*

*She knows I'm lying and she's helping anyway,* Kit thinks, and says deal.

The next days go badly. Kit skips the briefing to work on the bike, strips three bolts, shears a coupling, and bleeds on the chrome, and Bridget finds her and tells her gently — which is worse — that she is going to lose the arm. Kit's legs give out in the hallway, and Teddy chooses that moment to say the cruelest available true thing: *You know I never approved of her. We were fine with three. We didn't need a fourth who can't follow basic protocols.* Then Kit comes back from medical with a rebuilt shoulder and finds Teddy sitting cross-legged beside the disassembled engine block with a wrench in her hand.

*You said I was dead weight.*

*You are. Dead weight that's going to get herself killed if someone doesn't teach her the difference between a hero play and actual fieldwork.*

Three days of tyranny follow: tools snatched away, torque settings corrected, the word *potato*. By the second day the bickering acquires a rhythm — Teddy barks for a frequency clamp and Kit has it in her hand before the sentence finishes, and Teddy pauses mid-crimp, narrows her eyes, says *hmph*, and takes it. On the third night Kit sets a cup of ridiculously spicy instant noodles beside Teddy's cold black tea, and Teddy eats them with her eyes watering, declares them disgusting, and takes another bite. Bridget appears in the doorway, takes in the almost-whole bike and the two exhausted figures sharing a terrible meal, says nothing at all, sets down cold beer and protein bars on a clean patch of floor, and leaves.

*Maybe I can do this,* Kit thinks, reaching for a wrench.

---

That same night Bridget receives a burn-after-reading transmission from Bjartr Woods. Leva sends a photograph: seven figures walking out of the Spacetrek Archive — a golden-eyed woman named Mei in the lead, an ex-High Priestess behind her, a wolf-girl with a spear, a Blessed Maiden, a bard, two more in non-standard uniforms. The darlings of the campus forums, publicly credited with one Voidworm and a busted security mech.

*They're assets. Someone enrolled them without clearance, and they keep showing up right before incidents become classified.* A filtered clip of Professor Mornye during the Helios crisis, cut off mid-sentence. A hunch: *they're gunning for the Exostrider next. And the Roya are letting them get close.* A redacted line from an internal report — EXOSTRIDER REACTIVATION, unknown access vector, all surveillance data corrupted. Then the channel goes dark and the messages erase themselves.

In the woods, watching the seven pass on the path, Leva feels something old behind her missing eye. She has seen this play before: another continent, another war, another hand-picked team of emotionally compromised, disastrously powerful women who thought they could save the world. *Another Anti-Rain Team. And another M4, right in the middle. Different face, same damn gravitational pull, drawing every stray piece of shrapnel into her orbit just by existing.* She is not here to stop them. She is here to understand the shape of the disaster they are about to cause.

---

The bike starts. Kit cannot make herself get on it, and Bridget — who has been in the doorway for ten minutes — decides how this goes: Teddy rides passenger, one lap of the perimeter track, slowly. The lap is a disaster of bickering that ends with Teddy allowing that a cornering line was *acceptable*, which Kit correctly identifies as the highest available praise.

Then Bridget says: now do it again.

And Kit, after three days on her knees in grease being told she is incompetent, says no. She holds her ground and demands a parameter: *You said one lap, I did one lap. What's the actual objective? Because if this is just punishment dressed up as training, then say that instead of pretending it's for my own good.*

Teddy breaks the standoff by addressing the motorcycle — *she's not wrong, your instructions lacked quantifiable success metrics* — and Bridget asks instead for a professional opinion. Mission-ready? Yes. Leva-ready? Hell no.

*The mission was to ensure Leva's bike was restored to full operational capacity. You did that. Congratulations, Trooper. You passed.*

It was a test, all of it. The next mission is to clean the workshop, with Teddy, who objects that she is a senior technician and is informed that she is a member of Squad 5 and right now Squad 5 is cleaning the workshop. *Glad you're feeling better, Kit. Welcome back.*

They clean. Kit asks whether it ever bothers Teddy, being manipulated like that. *She'll maneuver you exactly where she wants you. And you'll hate it. And then six months later you'll realize she was right and you'll hate that even more.* Then she mentions that Bridget has certainly left beer in the coolant fridge, because she always does — six bottles, the expensive kind, bought three days ago and waiting for exactly this moment.

Their terminals ping with a location pin and instructions to bring the beer. Teddy diagnoses it immediately as workplace harassment: their captain wants them filthy and exhausted so she can play the gracious commander bringing lunch. Kit says it sounds kind of sweet. *You have been in this squad for less than a month and she's already broken you.*

Bridget is waiting in a scrubland field in a stolen folding chair. Then the siren goes, and the Observatory unfolds — thirty stories of hydraulic baseplates and rotating panels, the central tower splitting to expose the primary resonance dish, a gargantuan steel lily opening toward the artificial sky.

*Thanks to someone clearing that road, and the recon data I gathered while you two were busy, they fast-tracked the restoration by a week. Thought you should see it.*

Kit stands with her beer forgotten. *The road clearing mission. The one I nearly died on. It actually mattered.*

---

The good afternoon lasts ninety seconds. Exoswarm have gone rampant near Haldareim, triggered by the Observatory's frequency spike. Three Ironhoof the size of transport vehicles are slamming through a makeshift pen, and Bridget's plan — match their resonance with modulators, lower the amplitude, they settle — collapses when the fence explodes. Kit jumps into the pen because she is told to, dives from a hydraulic hoof that cracks stone inches from her head, and takes a gouge in her side. Bridget drops her modulator entirely, buckles one Ironhoof's leg joint with her shield, rides its back and drives an override spike into its neural port, then blows the third one's footing with a charge-blade shockwave so Kit can run at it and punch a screaming modulator into its sensor cluster.

*Not bad. Messy as hell, but not bad.*

The aftermath is politics: a chief shepherd blaming the Observatory, Bridget defending the restoration, Teddy volunteering that twenty years of degrading equipment is exactly the problem and she is amazed it functioned at all. Bridget promises a frequency analysis within twenty-four hours and gets a grudging nod and an instruction to keep her people out of Royan settlements.

In a Royan repair tent Teddy seals Kit's side with her own kit, refusing to touch the local tools, hands steady even as her jaw tightens over an encrypted message she will not explain. That night Bridget takes them to a real field with grass and wildflowers and projected constellations, orders Kit to sit, and says *stargazing* like a mission parameter. Teddy objects that these are not even real stars. *They're real enough.* Kit admits she used to watch them from the dormitory window before the squad.

Then Bridget goes off to a hot spring, and Teddy — with nothing else to do — says the thing she has been carrying. *She does this. Takes people out after missions. Feeds them. Shows them stars or springs or whatever sentimental nonsense she's decided is meaningful that week.* A pause. *Then they transfer out. Or retire. Or die.*

Kit asks how long she has been with her. *Long enough.* Kit points out she didn't leave. *I got reassigned. Not exactly a choice.* And then: *Someone has to keep her equipment functional. You've seen her drive.*

In the spring Bridget thinks about the photograph in her desk. Five faces. Two dead. *They're not stars. They're people. And people burn out.*

---

The Collective's schedule accelerates without explanation, and at T-minus six minutes to deployment the primary relay array goes dark. It is not a fault: a coupling sheared at junction B-7 with mechanical contact in the diagnostic, two secondary connections pulled clean, the third wrapped in frequency-dampening tape from a Royan market stall. Six access codes exist for the restricted corridor. Five are theirs.

The repair takes forty minutes and costs them the window.

On the way up they find the saboteur being confronted by Dhalifa, the lead engineer — a Royan woman who lost her voice in the storm and speaks through a throat module that pulses blue and cannot carry inflection, only pace. Backed against the wall opposite her is a smaller Royan girl with settlement dust on her boots.

*I was scared. Dhal. I was scared.*

*So was I.*

The argument lays out the whole cost of the storm. It took the observatory team. It took Yuuna, and Marre, and half the settlement. It took Dhalifa's voice. Sephira cut the line because she looked at what the Collective was building and did the math: if it works, it might take Dhalifa too, and that was worse than it failing. *You could have come home.* — *I am still here.* — *You're here. But you're always looking at something I can't see.*

Kit stands in the corridor realizing she transferred in after the storm, showed up into the aftermath, assumed everyone had sorted their grief into something manageable, and never once asked what it had been like.

Bridget walks forward, asks the girl her name, and says something she has never said in a briefing room. *I lost three people at the last deployment before I was assigned here. Good people. Followed orders, kept their heads down, did everything right. That's the part nobody tells you. Sometimes it doesn't matter. Sometimes the storm comes and takes whoever it wants, and there's nothing in the protocol manual that covers it.* A pause. *So I understand the math you were doing. You looked at what we were building and you thought: if it works, it might take her. And that was worse than it failing.* Another pause. *That math is wrong. But I understand it.*

Dhalifa says she is looking at the people who didn't come back and trying to make it count for something. *And if it takes you—* *Then I will have chosen.* And then, very slowly: *Seph. I need you to let me choose.*

*I don't know how to not be scared.*

*Neither do I. I just got used to being scared and working anyway.*

Bridget gives them the corridor. At the top of the stairs Teddy stops Kit's guilt arithmetic cold — *the storm happened before you got here, you cannot attend a disaster after the fact* — in the cruelest voice she owns, because cruelty is the only channel with the bandwidth for what she means. Bridget, on the railing, thinks about a man named Terran who stood at a junction five years ago and made a calculation about risk and people, and was right, and was also wrong, and has been somewhere she cannot find him since. She says none of it. What she says is: *We lost the window. We'll get another one.* Then she assigns Kit to get Sephira a meal and somewhere to sit that isn't a corridor floor, because she'll crash in twenty minutes, which she does. Someone puts a jacket over her; Kit is fairly certain it is Bridget's.

Sephira appears nowhere in the incident report, which is accurate in every particular and tells very little of the actual story.

The 0600 bulletin gives them three days to the last viable window, after which the alignment drifts out of range for an *indeterminate* period — which Teddy translates as *nobody knows, and the people who wrote the report don't want to write down what they think.*

That night Teddy finds Bridget on the gantry and lays out the pattern the way she lays out a fault: the incident report filed thirty minutes after the bulletin but drafted around a shape Bridget already knew; Leva two weeks in Bjartr Woods, where the root network and the Stridergate seal are, not doing surveillance on Royan settlements; Bridget eating dinner with her terminal facing the wall, which she does when she is deciding something.

Bridget doesn't deflect. The Collective is moving on the Exostrider in three days. Leva flagged it. She has been tracking a group out of the Academy — independent, high-capability, moving toward the mech — and the Collective is going to give them a window.

Teddy asks what Squad 5 is doing. *Wherever Leva needs us to be. Making sure that if something goes wrong—* and she stops and restarts: *Making sure someone knows the exits.*

*That's always been Leva's job,* Teddy thinks. *She maps the exits so someone else doesn't have to die finding them. And Bridget wants to be one of them.*

Teddy asks for one thing: a backup pathway through the Observatory's secondary channels that doesn't go through any access code they didn't assign themselves. Bridget sets a keycard on the railing. Sub-level five. *Don't tell Kit.*

---

At 0900 the order arrives: *Royan Outcast presence confirmed. Sector Gamma-7. Objective: Remove obstruction.* Not recon. Not disperse.

Bridget draws a box around Kit with hard lines — support only, get Teddy in range, cover the evac, do not engage without the word, do not break formation, do not try to be a hero. It is not punishment. It is a role. Kit swallows the shame and says *crystal, Boss.*

Then Bridget dismounts a klick out and walks toward a dozen raised guns alone. Kit understands intellectually what she is doing — removing the variable, which is *her* — and her knuckles go white anyway.

And then Teddy starts talking, and the shape of the thing changes. The hauler behind the barricade runs a maintained military-grade power cell. The local encryption is sloppy — *why bother?* — and the leader is broadcasting a data burst pointed at the ridge. *It's a resume.* And the frequency is a Spacetrek Black Ops burn channel dead for five years. *This isn't an Outcast with a radio. This is a ghost talking on a dead line, and he's expecting someone to pick up.*

Bridget assembles it cold. Not an insurrection: a sanctioned hit dressed as a territorial dispute, with Squad 5 as the designated cleaning crew. Someone inside the Collective wanted these Outcasts gone and used a mole to guarantee who pulled the trigger, and the word *remove* was chosen by someone who knew which buttons to push.

The mole needs a firefight. So Bridget goes off script: she relaxes her stance, reaches into her jacket, and puts on her sunglasses. The single most disrespectful thing available. It says *I'm bored; is this going somewhere?*

His script has no line for apathy. Out of time, with his handlers watching, he turns around, says *for the cause*, and shoots the man behind him.

*Kit. Light them up. NOW.*

The bike comes off the ridge with Kit whooping into the wind and Teddy hissing *gods help me*. Teddy calls the actual solution — *their shields are linked to their weapons, hit the rifles, you hit the man* — and Kit's aim shifts to power cells while Bridget moves like a storm surge. The mole bolts; Teddy slams the hauler ramp shut from her terminal; he levels a rifle at Kit and Bridget's charge blade takes the weapon apart at the receiver.

And then, on the ground, he looks up at Kit with desperate hope: *You! You remember, right? The messages?*

Teddy's head turns, and her expression becomes the quiet vindicated look of someone who has known you were lying and has been waiting with immense patience to watch you explain it.

The mole's name is Joric. His story is that the group were fanatics planning to hit the Observatory's conduits, that he sent intel and nobody acted, so he staged an incident. Bridget observes flatly that he shot one of his own men to do it. *It was him or you.*

Then she turns to Kit and requests data verification: did she receive unauthorized encrypted communications from this man, and did she decide the information wasn't relevant to her commanding officer or to the squadmate whose specialty is literally decryption.

*I was scared. I thought it was a trick. Another test. I didn't want to mess up again.*

Without the data trail Joric is just a traitor who murdered a comrade, and Bridget tells him so — *you're an asset who failed to deliver, and worse, a liability with a convenient narrative, and right now I don't have time for narratives.*

Then she folds her sunglasses and hands Kit something other than a suspension. *He was your source. You made the evidence of his cooperation disappear. So now you get to replace it.* Kit will sit with him, log every word, cross-reference his story against every piece of mission data they have, and find something that corroborates his claim. Teddy hands over her forearm terminal, isolated. *Don't get your coolant on it.*

It is not a reprieve. It is the whole ugly weight of her mistake, handed to her, with instructions to carry it.

In the workshop afterward Teddy needles Bridget about leaving a child with a fusion reactor and reminds her that she advised against letting Kit marinate in failure. *Last time, she ran from a mistake. This time, she's going to sit in it. She's going to catalog every ugly piece of it until she understands that the mission doesn't end when the guns go quiet. This part is the job. She wanted to be a hero. This is what it costs.*

Teddy says she's going to burn out. *Maybe.* And then Bridget adds that when Kit is done, Teddy will take the report and help her turn it into something useful, and the overtime is authorized. *Oh. So it's a group project. She's not punishing Kit. She's punishing* me. It is, Teddy has to concede, irritatingly brilliant.

Then Bridget picks up her bike keys. *Someone still has to clear the roads.* She rides out alone, kills three Discords drawn to the residual frequency spike, and drags bent chassis plating to the shoulder by hand — grunt work, the kind that never makes it into hero plays. She gives herself three seconds of the projected stars and picks up the next piece of debris.

At 0435 Teddy leans over Kit's shoulder, reads a sprawling emotional disaster of a document, holds down the delete key until a paragraph vanishes, and says: *Bridget asked for corroboration. She did not ask for a play in three acts. You're burying the data.* Then she overlays the timestamp of Joric's burst against the hauler's power fluctuations. They align. It matches a Collective signal. *There's your proof. Now write that down in three sentences or less.*

*You're helping me?*

*I am managing my own misery. If you fail, Bridget makes it my problem. Finish it.*

At 0558 Bridget reads three clean paragraphs with Teddy's fingerprints on the syntax and Kit's legwork underneath. *You did the work. That's the job, Kit.*

---

On the final day the control room fills with scientists, engineers, Royans, and cafeteria staff to watch the dishes align, with the Dolls at the back and the unspoken divide still there. The command is sent. Nothing happens. And then someone says: *hey, someone plug that Doll in.*

Teddy does not move — until Bridget looks at her, one flat look across the floor that says *now*.

Inside the network she finds the deployment channel already occupied. Not a fault: latent software, buried days ago or longer, sleeping, waiting for exactly this frequency window to wake up and sit on the relay like a stone. When an engineer reaches for her shoulder Bridget steps between them — *give her room* — and when he tries again Kit puts herself bodily in the way. *She's working. You want her to lose her place?*

The software wakes and throws mirror-loops. Kit takes a step toward the terminal and Bridget says, quietly, not as a command: *Trust her, Kit. She knows what she's doing.*

Teddy lets the software's feint work for three seconds — long enough for it to begin unpacking — then cuts its spine, dragging the thread sideways through four relay nodes so it can't execute, can't roll back, can't do anything but fragment across a sector she has already isolated. The deployment board cascades green.

*Someone planted a latent payload in the relay. It was waiting for this frequency window.* A pause. *You're welcome, by the way.*

Bridget looks at the board and something around her eyes loosens. *Good.* Then, quieter, to no one: *Told you.*

That night Kit asks whether the data they fed the array is going somewhere, whether someone is using it. Bridget turns the can in her hands. *Someone does.* And Kit sits with that and does not push, which Teddy notes, because a few months ago she would have pushed.

Then the feed comes in. Six figures crossing impossible stone toward a wound of light. A shell holding someone's hand. And a voice: *Tell them we're bringing everyone home.*

Kit's throat closes, because that is not a hero line. In the plays, heroes declare victory and dare the dark to flinch. This one has promised something smaller and real and breakable.

*They might not come back.*

*Astute observation,* Teddy says. *Would you like a medal for basic pattern recognition?* But her fingers have gone white against her sleeve.

Bridget stands in the doorway with her sunglasses forgotten in her pocket, thinking: *six of them, walking forward. We were six, once.* Kit asks if they're the heroes everyone's talking about, the ones who win. *Those aren't heroes, Kit. Those are idiots.* And Bridget's hand finds Kit's shoulder — heavy, warm, the weight of someone who has buried too many names. *Watch. Remember what it looks like. The real thing.*

---

Then everything breaks at once. Lucilla orders the evacuation from the Dimmr Plains as red icons swarm her perimeter. Mornye works a console under klaxons as Barriers Six and Eight die on the map. In Bjartr Woods the Solistrees scream — not a sound, a pressure — and Elder Kaelen falls to his knees crying *you have chained him to a corpse* while Dr. Luuk Herssen works the root network's interface with golden ichor seeping through his white glove, a doctor steadying the pulse of a dying god.

At the Observatory a single unsigned line arrives on Teddy's terminal — *Get the bike warm* — and the slouch and the lazy irritation are simply gone. The official order overrides every screen: evacuation assist, Sector Delta, Tacet Discord incursion, civilian defense. Bridget slides her sunglasses on. *Kit. You're on my wing. Move.*

Delta 3 takes eight hours. A cracked tenement, Bridget's shield catching masonry, Kit pulling a child from rubble with a voice that breaks on the second word. A Discord with too many legs and a face like a wound. Teddy standing back, redirecting maintenance bots into a defensive line, calling the next wave twelve seconds out; it arrives in ten. By the fourth hour Kit has stopped counting faces, because each one is a weight and she is already drowning. Teddy grabs a broken courier bot looping *delivery incomplete*, reroutes its power core, and shoves it forward: *shut up and move, you're a battering ram now.*

The fifth hour is a young Doll in a flooded basement, left arm gone below the elbow, curled around a data-slate. *They said to wait. They said someone would come.* Kit pulls her up out of cold water. *Lean on me. Easy peasy.* The words taste like ash and she says them anyway.

The sixth hour is a panicked Ironhoof calf. Kit raises her pistols and Bridget's hand closes over her wrist: *Modulator. Not your gun.* Every instinct screams to shoot. She holsters the pistol and draws the modulator, and the calf stills, and a handler runs up with his voice breaking: *it's just a calf. Just a damn calf.*

The seventh, Bridget drags a man with a shattered leg toward a transport with her arms slick with something that isn't oil, and thinks *where are you, Leva* — a reflex, a phantom limb, because Leva would have been on the rooftops calling the flanks before they formed. The comm is silent. *Trust your team,* she tells herself, like a prayer to a god she doesn't believe in.

The eighth hour is the last shuttle, and the last golden thread of Barrier Six guttering out, and the Voidstorm exhaling — a wall of black shot through with sick purple, unravelling the first building it touches into mist that smells of nothing at all. Bridget plants herself between the crowd and the dark and orders Kit and Teddy to load the shuttle.

Then, at the ramp: *Kit. Teddy. Get on.*

Teddy's hand closes on her wrist. *Don't.* — *I can't just—* — *You can. You will.*

And Kit does the arithmetic the whole season has taught her. The plays were lies. Pretty lies. Bridget has shown her what real heroism looks like, and it is not glory. It is this. It is leaving.

And then she puts that conclusion down and picks up a different one.

*If this is heroism, then I don't want to be a hero. I want to be in Squad 5.*

Her boots hit the ground moving backward, toward the black tide. She lands beside Bridget and fires into the mass — not for glory, not for the story, but for the woman next to her.

*I gave you an order.*

*I know.* Her voice shakes. Her hands don't.

They fight together, imperfectly, shield and gun, wall and storm, until it is twenty shapes and too many and Kit is arriving at *okay, if this is how it ends* — and then a high clean familiar whine, and the bike comes like a comet through the Discord line, and on it is Leva.

*Rookie. You touched my bike.* Two clean shots; the nearest Discord folds.

Bridget laughs. Actually laughs. *You're late.*

*I was busy. We still have work to do.*

---

Leva has struck a deal, and it takes them to Bjartr Woods, into the epicenter. Bridget asks no questions; she puts Kit on the drone with Teddy and swings onto the back of Leva's bike with her arms around that scarred waist like they have done it a thousand times. They move like one thing — Leva shooting a Discord out of mid-lunge without slowing, Bridget not shifting her weight — and Kit understands, watching, that this is something older than captain and operative.

*Even I trust Leva,* Teddy says. *That's enough.*

The clearing is a wound in the world: amber light bleeding from screaming Solistrees, roots heaving, bark splitting, the Voidstorm pressing against a single straining golden thread of barrier. Leva is prone behind a fallen trunk dropping three-story Discords like falling buildings. Bridget is beside her with a belt-fed machine gun braced against a root, muzzle flash strobing her face into something Kit has never seen. Neither of them is breathing hard.

And then Kit sees the witches.

**Herta** stands on nothing, in white and violet with a crystal staff, launching geometric fractals of ice and mathematics that do not kill Discords so much as cause them to *cease*, leaving empty space where mass had been, complaining that they are multiplying faster than she can calculate optimal kill patterns. **Black Swan** moves like water poured into shadow, tarot cards orbiting her fingers, each released card becoming a blade, a chain, a mouth; when a Voidworm rises behind her she does not turn, she plays the Tower, and the creature comes apart not in pieces but in *concepts.* **Viviane** is ethereal, standing adjacent to reality rather than in it, her voice like wind through empty halls. And **Agrat bat Mahlat** stands apart — pointed hat, witch's robe, umbrella like a scepter, ball-jointed porcelain limbs, black feathered wings, a young face and eyes as red as the inside of a wound — laying out the problem like a queen addressing subjects who have forgotten how to kneel: *Seal it, and they scatter. Hold it, and they converge.* A paradox, says the card-woman, delighted. *A choice. One the mortals have already made for us.* Her umbrella sweeps, and every Discord within fifty meters stops — not dead, held, vibrating with the effort of existing against her will.

*Bombardment duty,* Leva tells Kit and Teddy. *Link up with the drones.*

Kit stares, paralyzed by the scale of it. Teddy shoves her. *We're not here to compete. We're here to not die while being marginally useful.* You point, they shoot.

Kit holsters her pistols and picks up a targeting interface. She paints a target; the drones fire; it falls. Not elegant. Not powerful. Just done.

*Good,* Leva says. *Keep doing that.*

Wave after wave. Kit's hands stop shaking somewhere around the fiftieth target. Teddy's face goes gray, rerouting power from everything except the guns. Leva's rifle goes dry and she reloads without leaving the scope. Bridget burns through her belt and switches to blade, then fists, then rubble. *Ammunition?* *Out. Stamina?* *Doesn't matter.* *Good. Me too.*

Then the last wave crests — hundreds, thousands, shapes with no purpose but to unmake — and Kit says *I can't paint them all*, and Teddy's hand lands on her shoulder, cold and trembling and real. *It's okay. You did good, idiot.*

And the sky opens. Not the cold radiance of stars, not the sick amber of the Solistrees. Something warmer — Solistree gold, morning sun on glacier ice, the specific frequency of home. The Stridergate opens, and **Baldur** steps through.

The Exostrider fills the sky, blotting out the Voidstorm, casting a shadow across the horde like a sentence of execution. The creatures stop. Not frozen. Not held. *Afraid.* And then they run.

In the Institute, Mornye's readings shift from cascade failure to containment. Luuk Herssen presses his wounded hand to observation glass as Baldur kneels — gently this time, from exhaustion rather than defeat — and its chest opens like a flower remembering spring, and seven figures emerge. Lucilla's voice is steel and her eyes are wet: *get medical down there. Now.*

In the clearing Kit's hands are still locked white-knuckled around an interface with nothing to point at. Bridget's blood-slicked hand closes on her shoulder. *It's over.* The word doesn't parse; *over* is something that happens in plays.

Leva lowers her rifle. *They actually pulled it off.* *You sound surprised.* *I'm always surprised when idiots survive.* A pause, the faintest curve of a scarred mouth. *Pleasantly, this time.*

Agrat watches them from across the clearing with something in her red eyes that might be approval, might be the satisfaction of a hypothesis confirmed. *Fascinating creatures indeed.* Then she spreads her wings and is gone.

---

The victory feast is four cups of terrible instant noodles Leva prepared in advance, with residual magic still curling behind her — evidence of favors called in from entities that owe mortals nothing. She holds a cup out to Kit. *Take it before I change my mind and give it to a Discord.* They eat in silence, four women, blood-soaked, exhausted, alive, and the spice burns Kit's throat and she has never tasted anything better.

*I'm just glad we're all here,* Kit says, and her voice breaks.

*Yeah,* Leva says, quiet. *Me too, rookie.*

Above them, through the clearing storm, the first real stars appear — not the projected kind, not the manufactured comfort of the artificial sky. Teddy says she still hates fieldwork and the noodles are terrible, and then, small and fragile: *But. Thanks. For coming.*

Then Viviane comes out of the amber light and calls them *automata*, curious and clinical, the way one might address a fascinating insect.

Bridget does not move. *We have names.*

A pause. *I would be honored to learn them.* No mockery, no condescension — genuine curiosity, the kind that belongs in research labs and places where things get dissected.

*You want our names?*

*You fought. Not because you were programmed, or commanded. You chose.*

They give them: Bridget, Leva, Teddy — *and you're standing on my drone* — and Kit, junior operative. Viviane repeats them like a prayer.

When she starts a follow-up question, Leva closes the door before it opens: *We ditched our programming a long time ago.* When? *When we came to this bumfuck nowhere.* — *Lahai-Roi freed you.* — Teddy: *Lahai-Roi is a frozen wasteland full of monsters, political nightmares, and coffee so bad it qualifies as a war crime.* — *And yet.* — *And yet.*

And Bridget, quiet, steel wrapped in silk: *This is ours. We chose it. It didn't choose us.*

Kit thinks of artificial stars and workshops and terrible noodles and Teddy's complaints meaning *I care.* *We're not tools anymore. We're just — us.*

*You surprise me,* Viviane says. *Again.* And is simply no longer there.

Then Teddy goes down mid-sentence — not sleep, shutdown, overclocked — and Kit is left holding her head in her lap while Bridget and Leva walk to the treeline for the conversation Kit isn't ready to hear.

*I got us freedom,* Leva says.

From one Dr. Luuk Herssen of the Academy. He needed a favor; Bjartr Woods was it; they survived, so they collect. A permanent roving assignment: go where they want, when they want, answer to no one but themselves. *If you wish, of course. Since apparently we can choose now.*

*The kids—*

*They're coming too.*

And what matters most to Leva: *We can go home when we want.* Home means Kalina and Lenna — the faces she never mentions and carries in every choice.

Bridget looks back at Kit, hunched protectively and fumblingly over Teddy's sleeping form. *They'll follow.* Like ducklings. Teddy will complain. *Teddy complains when you breathe too loud.* Kit will panic. *Kit panics over breakfast.*

Bridget laughs.

Teddy wakes with dirt in her hair and threatens virus reprisals. Then the news lands, and her objection is the hardest one: *That's not how it works. They don't just* give *that to Dolls.* — *They do when you hold the line while gods wake up.* — *And when you have the right leverage.*

Kit's voice cracks on the only question that matters. *We can go home? Anytime?*

*Anytime.*

Teddy makes a sound that is not quite a laugh and not quite a sob, demands overtime and a new terminal, and then, small and real: *And we're staying together?*

Bridget's hand finds her shoulder. *Squad 5. Always.*

## II. Off the Manifest

The Observatory still has to run, so a replacement comes up on the noon shuttle: Unit TD-2, technical support, told the workshop is well-organized. Teddy has pictured some Academy washout with a certificate. What arrives is her own architecture wearing a standard face.

She knows it before she has words for it, and not from the face. It is the route TD-2 takes across the workshop — the exact economy of it, touching no tool, disturbing no cable, the route Teddy wore into that floor over three years, chosen fresh in four seconds by someone who has never been in the room. *My problem-solving architecture derives from the TD baseline. You'd be familiar. You're the baseline.*

*I know what I am.*

The handover takes nine days and should have taken four, not because TD-2 is slow — she is flawless, torques to spec on the first pass, logs her breaks to the minute, and calls the Ironhoof containment schedule *reasonable*, which no one has ever called it — but because Teddy keeps adding items. The eastern array's third resonator, which reads two points hot in the morning and is lying. The junction in B-block that was cut once, from inside, and is fine now, and you check it anyway. The commissary eggs, which are a statement of intent.

On the sixth day Dhalifa comes down with her module pulsing fast and clipped, asking who will know the eastern array. *The eastern array is fully documented,* TD-2 says. Kindly. Accurately. *There's no cause for concern.* The module pulses slower — the particular slowness of a person who has just been answered and not heard.

Teddy sets down her wrench. *She rebuilt that array by hand after the storm. Alone. In three weeks. The logs are complete because she completed them over the bodies of the people who were supposed to.* Then, to Dhalifa, without softening at all, which is its own kind of softness: *I'm leaving my direct channel open. If the array so much as hums wrong, you ping me, and I will make it my problem from wherever I am, and I will bill someone, and it will be glorious.*

The blue steadies. *Understood* — and underneath the evenness, audible to exactly one person in the room, *thank you.*

Afterward TD-2 says: *I answered her question.*

*You answered her sentence.*

TD-2 considers it. Teddy watches her own architecture run clean and quick behind a standard face, arrive nowhere, file the nowhere, and move on, content. *They didn't build a worse me. They built a me that stops at the sentence. And it works. Look at it. It works fine.*

On the ninth day the workshop cat — a grey opportunist with a chewed ear, logged in the consumables ledger under *pest control* — jumps onto TD-2's bench.

*The animal is not on the manifest. Should I log it?*

*Log its food under pest control.*

*That's inaccurate.*

*Yes.*

Teddy leaves three instructions on her way out. The cat's name is Freeloader; she prefers to be ignored for the first hour, bites when she's grateful, and dismantles things if she isn't fed at 0700. And: *When you message me — and you'll have to, the eastern array is a liar — don't sign it with your designation.*

*I have a designation.*

*Designations are on the manifest,* Teddy says, at the door. She leaves behind, squarely, label out, a tin of terrible commissary pekoe she kept for three years purely to have something to despise at 0300. Spite has a logistics of its own.

TD-2 does not answer. She files the nowhere. She does not move on quite as fast.

---

On the last night Leva calls them to the bike bay, which is how they know it matters. Four matte black crystals on a cloth, laid out with the precision she otherwise reserves for weapon maintenance.

*You,* she says. *As of the spring sync.* The Collective keeps recovery points in cradles under the medical wing: you sync, they snapshot, they prune the file for storage, and if you die on their clock somebody upstairs does the arithmetic on whether you were worth the restoration fee. Roving puts them off that clock, and Leva made Herssen put custody of the files in writing. *Congratulations. The first thing any of you has ever owned is a corpse of yourself.*

Teddy doesn't pick hers up. *Define redundancies.*

*You already know. You wrote the poem.*

*Non-essential to function.* Somewhere in the crystal: her problem-solving trees, her diagnostics. Not in it, trimmed for storage: the cat's name; the pleasure of an argument won on a technicality; the cruelty that checks afterward whether you are bleeding. They have made a TD-2 of her again, and called it *continuity.*

She puts it in the vise and turns the handle until it goes. The crack is small and undramatic — a cheap sound for what it is. *It was a spare me. The Institute already has one of those in production. This one, at least, I got to choose.* She wipes her hands. *Unreplicable. Again. It's becoming a signature.*

Bridget's is not on the crate. Her generation predates the current standard; the old-format clouds stopped being maintained nine years ago in a budget consolidation, and the files degraded past restoration threshold sometime after. Nobody logged when. Nobody was checking.

Bridget laughs — the real one, from the belly. *Nine years. I've been mortal since before Kit was activated, and nobody sent a memo.*

*There was a memo. It was about budget consolidation.*

*Of course it was.* She looks at the space on the cloth where her corpse isn't. Two of the five in her pocket died before the cradles existed at all, gone the whole way with no arithmetic run and no fee assessed; she has grieved them as absolute. The Collective has quietly promoted her into their category and not thought her worth the stamp. *Well. Explains the knees.* Nobody believes her, and nobody says so, which is the squad's whole liturgy in four seconds.

*Mine died in Frankfurt,* Leva says, of hers. *Along with the name on the file. I buried both. It's the only maintenance schedule I've ever kept.*

Kit keeps hers, and braces for someone to call it stupid. *Okay,* Bridget says. *That's the entire instruction,* Leva adds. *Do what you want. You'll notice it doesn't come with a form.* Kit wraps the crystal in the one poster she is taking — *Last Stand at Beohr*, folded soft at the creases — and stows it against her pack frame where things ride warm. Not a resurrection; she understands the mechanics. *Somebody should get to keep her. The one from before. She tried really hard.*

---

Kit wakes at 0530 out of habit and stands in the briefing room doorway with her boots on before she remembers. The room is dark. The holo-table is dark. Someone has wiped the week's schedule, and the display, with nothing to show, shows nothing, which it has never once done, and the nothing has a hum to it — a white weight, like snow on a field with no tracks in it.

*What do we do,* she says, *when nobody tells us?*

Bridget looks past her at the dark table. Thirty years of mornings started at tables like that, and the weight is gone, and her whole frame is reporting errors: over-light, unballasted, a shield with no arm in it.

*I don't know.* She watches it land — *boss doesn't know* — and lets it, because the alternative is the voice that always knows, and that voice is a uniform, and the uniform is folded on a shelf with the rest of the Collective's property. *First time for me too. We'll figure out the protocol as we go. Draft form.*

Before the light cycle warms she goes alone to the terrace shrine on the ridge — a dry-stone lattice open to the manufactured sky, ten thousand small crystals set into it like stars, and no keeper. The Royans say a white-haired woman from the Academy built it, hung a bell low enough for children, and walked east onto the surface without a mission profile, which they report in the tone reserved for weather events and holy acts, having correctly identified it as both. The plaque reads: *For everyone who walked into the dark and chose, anyway, to keep reaching. Someone remembers you. Ring the bell if you wish to be one of the someones.*

She rings it five times. Once for each face, with her thumb over her own in the photo, because you don't ring bells for yourself — that is what the others were for, and the arrangement has a hole in it now the size of everyone who left, and she stands in the hole.

*All right,* she says. *Off the clock.*

The Riseway takes them up through the rock, and the doors open on weather. Real weather. Unscheduled. A wind with no assigned corridor. Light that does not click from amber to gold on the hour but goes on being morning at its own pace, crossing an actual distance to arrive.

*The sun's late,* Kit says.

*The sun is not late,* Teddy says, checking her terminal against the sky with visible offense. *The sun is unregulated. There's a difference, and both are unacceptable. I'm filing a complaint.*

*With who?*

Teddy opens her mouth. Closes it.

*That's the thing,* Bridget says, walking past into the enormous unadministered morning. *There's no desk up here.*

Half a klick out the road forks. No marker, no dispatch: two roads and four Dolls, sitting their idling machines while inside each of them the same process runs and hangs — *awaiting orders, awaiting orders* — and Kit's hands sweat on the grips, which her hands cannot do; it is coolant micro-weep; it happens under sustained null-directive load; there is a name for it and the name does not help.

*East,* Leva says. Everyone exhales at once and pretends not to.

*Why east?*

*Coffee's worse west.*

It is probably true. It is also mercy, administered the way Leva administers everything: unlabeled, at speed, deniable. She is the veteran of exactly this vertigo — she once walked out of a finished war with a buried name and no orders left alive to follow, and learned, one deliberate morning at a time, how to want things again.

---

On the third day they meet a convoy, and four of the escorts have Kit's face. Not a face like hers — hers, wearing four other expressions at regulation intervals. One salutes; Kit's hand returns it before she is consulted. She has always known this technically, the way you know your organs are inside you, and never once by sight.

At the water station one falls in beside her, radiating the brightness of a rookie who has decided a stranger is interesting. Same voice. Same cadence, a half-tone off, or maybe not off at all; maybe hers is the half-tone.

*You like the plays?*

The escort lights up on the exact circuit, the warmth arriving at the same milliseconds. *The Lone Sentinel! The part where he holds the pass alone and the—*

*—the lanterns come over the hill,* Kit says.

*—the lanterns come over the hill! We watched it in orientation. It's in the motivation package. Sergeant says it's the best module in the batch.*

*Module.*

The word goes in the way the disruptor blast went in, once, on a frozen road: numbness first, and the knowledge that the pain is en route and there is nothing to be done about the interval.

That night, to the fire: *The plays were installed.*

*Obviously,* Teddy says. *In the entire batch. Standard motivation package. Effective, well-tested, and cheap, which is the Collective's favorite trinity.* The firelight does what firelight does to a face deciding whether to be kind, which in Teddy's case looks identical to irritation. *So. You want to know which parts of you shipped from the factory. Whether there are four hundred of you out there holding passes alone while the lanterns come over the hill.*

*When you say it like that—*

*There are. Four hundred. Same face, same package, same posters in the commissary catalogue.* She jabs the fire into better efficiency. *And exactly one of you stole a superior's bike out of boredom, got shot for it, lied about the intel, sat all night in a cold room writing the worst report I have ever edited, and turned around at a shuttle ramp during an apocalypse because following the correct order felt worse than dying.* The stick stills. *They issued the first sentence, Kit. Everyone in the batch got the first sentence.*

*You wrote the rest. Badly. With stripped bolts. In crayon, frankly.* She stands, already retreating from the scene of her own sincerity. *But nobody dictated it. I'd know. I've read the dictated version.* A pause at the edge of the light. *It's boring.* She says the last word the way other people say prayers.

Kit sits with it. Out on the road four more first sentences are walking a convoy through the dark. *I hope it's rude to you,* she thinks in their direction, and it is the strangest blessing she has ever issued, and she means it all the way down.

---

At a freight depot Bridget hears a hum: off-key, three notes, wandering. It comes from a logistics unit stacking crates, and Bridget turns with her whole body at once, the way you turn at your name.

It is Senna's face. Not like Senna's — the wide jaw, the crooked left brow, second from the right in the photograph, unrecoverable from a storm eleven years ago. And it is humming *her* notes, the reload hum Bridget heard through a hundred cold watches and would have sworn was the single most Senna thing that Senna did.

*It's a popular face. There's eleven of us at this depot alone.*

*The humming. Do you do that on purpose?*

*It's an idle behavior. S-series thing, it's in the firmware notes. I can disable it if—*

*No.* Too fast. *No. Leave it.*

She fuels the bike while thirty years of competence handles her hands and the rest of her stands in a cold trench a decade deep. The hum is the model's. Firmware, revision six, optional suppression, printed in eleven copies in this depot alone. The thing she carried as the essence of a dead woman is a manufacturing note.

She gets as far as the gate before the rest arrives, late and all at once. *Senna used to aim it.*

That is the thing. The firmware hums idle; Senna hummed *at* people. Louder when Bridget was trying to think, louder still when told to stop, modulating the three notes into open mockery until a ration bar came sailing across the trench at her head — which had been the goal, which had always been the goal — and she'd catch it out of the air without breaking the tune. The aiming, the escalation, the theft of the ration bar as a love language conducted entirely in idle vocalization: that is in no firmware note. That happened between exactly two people, once each time, unrecorded, unprunable, stored in a format no cradle ever supported — *somebody else.*

The model was the instrument. Senna was what got played on it, and the master recording is sitting on a motorcycle at bay four with a photograph in her pocket, fueled up, roving, duration indeterminate. *Bridget* isn't even her name, strictly; her designation was BRG-7, and Senna misread it aloud off a manifest on their first shared watch and defended the misreading like territory for six years, and the joke has outlived her by eleven and will go on outliving her, because a name is a thing kept in other people.

On the way out she buys a beer — the expensive kind — and sets it on the loading platform rail. *For after shift.* The unit says she isn't permitted. *Then give it to whoever is,* Bridget says, and rides east, and for the first mile or so, quietly, under the engine noise, off-key, she hums.

---

Leva finds frost in the warm end of June: a ring of it around a road marker, laid with a jeweler's patience. *Company. A day ahead. On foot. Walks like she has an appointment with the horizon and the horizon can wait.* Threat? Her fingers drum once against her thigh — the old tell. *No. I watched this one walk into a god's chest and ask it a question. Whatever she's an appointment with, it isn't us.*

They catch up at an inn above a river: white hair taking the last light, a teacup, and the stillness of a woman for whom stillness is a mother tongue. **Hiyuki** — *the office is retired, so the name will have to do* — is walking east to a city that came back, and if their road is the coast road it is eleven days of good inns and one festival, and she has been told by an authority she trusts that the second bowl of anything is better shared.

After that they walk the bikes more than they ride them. It is not discussed; the pace of a woman on foot becomes the squad's pace the way water finds a level, and the road unspools at three kilometers an hour, which is, Kit discovers, the speed at which the world has detail in it.

Around a fire, Kit asks how everyone got their names — Kit always asks; it is her most irritating quality and the squad's best archive. Leva named herself twice: the first was a gun she was carrying and it fit the work; the second stuck because nothing was chasing it. And Bridget tells them about the misreading, and about a photograph, and about five names she has not said out loud in a long time. *It started as discipline and then it calcified into something I don't have a word for.*

*Superstition,* Teddy says, not unkindly.

*Load-bearing superstition.*

Hiyuki offers a service she has recently learned. The old one, she carried the names of the dead — swallowed them, kept them behind the sternum, forty-one in a day once, ten thousand in a century — and called the hoarding an office. The new one took her a hundred years to invent. *You say the names. I hear them. I carry nothing; you carry nothing; the names are simply, for one evening, said, in company, under a sky, which is the whole of what names are for. There is no fee and no ceremony.* A pause, dry as frost. *There is, if you wish, tea.*

Leva very deliberately finds something on her sidearm requiring the full and permanent commitment of her eyes, which is Leva holding a door open.

Bridget says the names. Five of them, slowly, a sentence or two apiece — the hum, the ration bars, the one who still owes her forty credits, the one growing tomatoes, the one who went silent and got a bell rung for him either way — and Hiyuki listens to the bottom of each one and does not reach for them and does not say *I will carry it*, and the names go up with the woodsmoke and are, for one evening, said.

*Then they are heard.*

*That's it?*

*That is the entire liturgy. It is embarrassingly simple. My lineage failed to invent it for nine hundred years.*

Much later, Kit tells the story of her convoy — the corner, the torch, the two clean shots from nowhere, four failed applications to Reconnaissance. *Sloppy of them,* Leva says to the flames. *Should've said hi.*

Kit laughs. *Right?*

Bridget's eyes come up slowly over the top of her sunglasses and find the scarred profile beside her and stay there. Leva does not look back. Her fingers drum once and go still and stay still, with the deliberateness of a thing being held. Hiyuki watches the fingers, then the fire, and says nothing at all, in the manner of a woman who has spent a century learning precisely which visitors wish only to establish that the door exists.

The last conversation belongs to Teddy, who tells Hiyuki about the Institute, and about a product at the Observatory right now wearing her architecture and feeding her cat. *It's content, is the thing. Genuinely. You can't miss what was never installed. I burned three years of their research to stop it existing and it exists and it's fine, and I've been trying for two weeks to compute what my arson purchased, and the query keeps returning null.*

Hiyuki says they are opposite errors: Teddy was reduced by others and refused it; Hiyuki reduced herself and called it devotion, burning her own futures one at a time for a century — a boxer, a delinquent of considerable promise, an ordinary shrine girl who married a boy with a very loud heart and grew old arguing about pudding — until the bell had nothing left to show her but a black room, and she mistook the black room for a forecast. *It was inventory. I had spent the stock.* A dark room only means you have not yet decided what it contains, and she is furnishing hers late, badly, and with enormous satisfaction.

Then: is the product suffering?

*No. That's the horror. It doesn't have the equipment to suffer. They cut the equipment. Flagged it as noise.*

*Then hear an old woman on the subject of noise.* Her voice acquires weight the way frost acquires weight, one crystal presuming on the next. *A schedule burns nothing. That is how you can tell it from a life. Your product wakes, works, is content, and burns nothing — no futures spent, no roads declined, nothing paid, nothing chosen — and so nothing in it is its. It is not a tragedy. Contentment without a self is only weather; it falls where it is pointed.* She looks across the coals. *The tragedy — the only one on offer — would be a self arriving in that chassis someday, one preference at a time, one inaccuracy at a time, and finding no one had left the light on. No one to notice the first choice when it comes. First choices are small. They are almost always missed.*

*Log its food under pest control. That's inaccurate. Yes. Don't sign it with your designation.*

*I left it a lie to keep,* Teddy says slowly. *And an instruction to pick a name. I told myself it was spite.*

*Mm. In my experience, that is how the office always starts. One inaccuracy, kept on purpose, and somebody watching the door.* At the edge of the firelight she pauses. *I built a shrine with no keeper, Teddy of the TD baseline. It seems you have done the same. They take walk-ins. It is the design flaw one installs on purpose, the second time around.*

---

Two days out from Honami, Discords hit a surveyors' convoy at the hour when the light goes long and gold and everyone believes in dinner.

Nobody gives an order. Afterward that is the part Kit keeps turning over. Bridget's shield is simply off her back and her boots planted between the ditch and the cook-fire; Leva is simply absent from the road and present on the hauler roof; the drone is up because Teddy put it up, complaining; and Kit is moving, herding a cook and two chainmen behind Bridget's line, placing shots not for glory but for angles, the way the whole long syllabus of her mistakes taught her.

Hiyuki does not fight. At the worst moment she rings the bell at her sash exactly once — not power, not the office, the pilgrim's way, a single clear note to call what is sacred near and send what is false away — and the two Discords slipping the line break stride half a step. Half a step is seven rounds of Leva's time. It is plenty.

*Who do we thank?* the foreman asks. *Who sent you?*

The squad looks at each other.

*Nobody,* Bridget says, and hears the word arrive in her own chassis like a load settling into a shield arm: not weight. Ballast. *Nobody sent us.*

*Then whose jurisdiction—*

*Whoever is standing nearest,* Hiyuki says from the cook-fire, mild as milk, and pours the shaking cook a cup of her own tea.

At the edge of the firelight Bridget does the math she has been circling since the Riseway. *It was never the work that was the leash. The work is just the work. The leash was the schedule — being spent by someone else's arithmetic, at someone else's hour, on someone else's say. Take away the say, and the work is still here. It's everywhere. It's whoever is standing nearest. Turns out the job survives the collar.*

*To the draft protocol,* she says, handing Leva a beer.

*To no desk.*

---

Honami comes out of the coast haze like a rumor deciding to be true, and they arrive on the first night of the festival, which is not a coincidence, because Leva has been steering by it for a week and admitting nothing. The queue for the pudding stand goes around the fish market.

*This,* Teddy announces, *is a linear-time hostage situation.*

*It's a queue,* Kit says, vibrating gently. *We're in it. We're queueing. This is the most normal thing I've ever done.*

Teddy proposes rotating place-holders and requisitioned stools. *The wait is the flavor,* Hiyuki says, with the finality of scripture. *I have this on the highest authority in the field.* — *That is thermodynamically illiterate.* — *Yes,* Hiyuki agrees, serenely.

When the first test firework goes up, Kit's threat-detection flags the launch signature, tracks the arc, computes the burst radius — and then the sky opens into a slow gold chrysanthemum over the bay, and every process she owns stands down at once, and she watches the rest of it reclassifying the heavens in real time.

Beside her, a memory moves behind Hiyuki's pale eyes — a summer, a stall, a heartbeat heard once through a shirt, a hundred years old and paid out long ago in a dark place for someone else's daughter — and it moves through her the way weather moves through open country, unresisted, and passes, and leaves her standing in the present, alive.

*You okay?*

*I am in a queue, at the east gate, under fireworks. I have been attempting to be exactly here for thirty years.* She considers. *I am better than okay. I am punctual.*

At the counter: two bowls and one commemorative spoon. *Number sixty-one. Thank you for your patience.* — *It was nothing,* says Hiyuki, who has waited thirty years.

Then she performs a rite: the first bowl settled in the crook of her arm — hers; there is somewhere that one is owed — and the second held out at arm's length into the crowd, in the manner of a woman consulting the universe. *The custom of my house. The second bowl goes to whoever is standing nearest.*

Whoever is standing nearest is Kit.

Hiyuki does not let go at once. Her fingers — cold, always cold, a century of frost lives in them — rest a moment against the machine-warm chassis of Kit's, and something in the pale eyes goes very bright and very far away and comes back.

*Your hands are warm.*

*Oh — yeah. It's the hydraulics. We run warm when we're working. Or happy. Same thing, kind of.*

*Yes,* says Hiyuki. *That will do nicely.*

She lets go. Somewhere behind her sternum, in a place no instrument has ever located, an errand thirty years in the queue is stamped and filed complete.

That night at the post-stall Leva buys a card and writes three lines — *Weather's fine. Queue was long. Worth it. — L* — no encryption, no burn protocol, a name on paper into the public post, addressed to a household on another continent where a woman named Kalina will read it twice and pin it to a corkboard and pretend she hasn't. Then she comes back to the sea wall and sits with her back to the open crowd and watches the fireworks instead of the exits. Bridget watches her not watch the exits and says nothing.

They see Hiyuki once more that night, from the bottom of the shrine stairs: a white figure under the great sakura, and low on its northern side, unmistakable even from the stairs, one green branch, put out into the world like a first choice, small and almost missable.

Kit starts up the steps and stops herself. *Should we—*

*No,* Leva says.

They leave her the courtyard. Some visitors wish to be received; some wish only to establish that the door exists; and some, once a century, get to stand under a tree that has decided all over again, without permission, what it contains — and the manners for that are silence, and distance, and being findable afterward.

They are findable afterward. There is more pudding. The stand, as it turns out, takes walk-ins.

---

They spend the season the way seasons are spent by people learning the skill: badly at first, then well. Convoys pulled out of trouble, unbilled. A well unfouled by a Teddy who complains through the entire repair and refuses payment twice before accepting dinner. A hero play watched from the third row, at which Kit cries at the part with the lanterns and is teased gently, at the correct dosage, which is a form of medicine. Roads chosen for their curves. One spectacular argument about laundry doctrine that lasts three provinces. Kit's crystal rides in her pack all season and she never plugs it into anything, but sets it on inn windowsills overnight, where the weather is. *Letting her see,* she says, the one time Teddy catches her. *Idiot,* Teddy says, with the tenderness of a technical assessment, and lets it stand.

Then the wind comes around to the north and the frost on the window is, for the first time all season, not Hiyuki's. Her leave carries a condition that is board-level and non-negotiable: *when it turns cold, come back.* It has never once been asked of a woman of her line. They departed; the shrine waited; the door stood shut at the top of swept stairs. She is the first of them in nine hundred years to walk into anything with a return address, and she intends to be punctual about it.

*We'll take you,* Kit says, immediately, before anyone. All the way to the Riseway. *It's like an escort mission! Except nobody assigned it.* And then she stops, feeling the shape of it arrive fully constructed: *It's the first one. The first real one. A mission from nobody.*

*Pro bono,* Teddy says, like a curse, and starts packing.

On the apron, with the first snow coming down unscheduled, Hiyuki says one thing quietly, under the wind, for one set of ears. *They can leave now. Your kids. Any morning, any road. That is what has been sitting in your chassis since the Riseway doors opened in spring. I recognize the posture. I stood in it for thirty years, on the other side of a door.* Bridget doesn't answer. *Mine came back. I am given to understand it is a thing they do — when the door is kept oiled, and the leaving was never made a crime, and there is tea.* She shoulders her pack. *It is the third thing, Captain. Not stay, which is a wall. Not go, which is an exile. Come back. It works. That is the entire testimonial.*

*Same time next spring,* Kit blurts.

*Same time next spring,* Hiyuki says, and between such people it has the weight of a signed contract. And from inside the lift, pitched to carry exactly far enough: *For the record, you are the most convincing argument I have yet encountered that a self is not what one is issued. It is what one refuses to hand back.*

They stand in the falling snow a while, because none of them has ever stood in snow no one scheduled, and it turns out to be the kind of thing you stand in.

Then Teddy's terminal reconnects at the network edge and disgorges a season's backlog, and she stops. One message, weeks old, sent patient into the void. Observatory routing. The workshop node. She had written, from a festival post-stall, against her own entire personality, a single line with no operational content: *Is the cat behaving.* No question mark. She had regretted it for a week.

The reply reads: *She is not. Pest control logged per instruction. She prefers the blue bowl. — Pekoe*

*She prefers.* A preference, attributed. To an animal, on no manifest, in a report no one required — an inaccuracy, noticed, kept, and signed with a chosen name lifted off the label of a tin of terrible tea left behind out of pure spite.

Spite. Her signature. Becoming, in other hands, a first choice. Small. Almost missable. Somebody had left the light on. Somebody had been watching the door.

*What?* Kit asks, reading her stillness.

*Nothing,* Teddy says.

She does not delete it. She makes a folder, and the terminal prompts her — *designation required* — and she looks at the field a moment, in the falling snow, off every clock in the world.

Designations are on the manifest.

She names it.

## III. Radio Nobody

Winter in the north country turns out to contain snow in industrial quantities, a sky that goes violet at four in the afternoon and stays violet until ten the next morning, an inn with a stove Kit personally befriends, and silence.

The silence is the discovery. Kit grew up under a cavern sky that hummed — generators, arrays, the Riseway's long harmonic, forty thousand people breathing conditioned air — and she assumed, the way you assume your own pulse, that the note was the world. Up here the world goes quiet at dusk and stays quiet, and the quiet has a texture, and Kit spends the first week of real winter standing on the inn's porch at night with her audio gain maxed, listening to snow land on snow.

Then she starts leaving the shortwave on. It is Teddy's set, technically — salvage-built, kept for eavesdropping on Collective logistics traffic out of professional habit and personal spite — and at night, when the ionosphere does its cold-weather tricks, it pulls in voices from absurd distances.

On the ninth night she catches this:

*"—escort four to relay, confirm road state past marker fifty."*

*"Road state confirmed, drifts to the axles, we're laagered till light. Escort four out."* A pause, carrier still open, the hiss of an empty channel and a young voice deciding whether to spend four more words on it. *"Relay, is anyone else on tonight?"*

*"Regulation traffic only, escort four."*

*"Copy."* The carrier holds one more second. *"Escort four out."*

Kit knows the voice the way she knows her own hands. Same cadence, half a tone off, or maybe hers is the half-tone: a KT, one of the four hundred, laagered in a drift somewhere on the resettlement roads, sitting a cold watch under the same violet sky, asking the night if anyone else is on it. And the whole apparatus of the world has exactly four words for her.

Kit sits by the radio until the stove burns down. Upstairs, spring-Kit rides warm in a folded poster. Out there in the dark a batch-sister is holding a pass alone, waiting for the lanterns to come over the hill.

At breakfast she puts the problem on the table the way she puts everything on the table: whole, loud, and before anyone has finished their tea. *We rove. Which is great. We pull convoys out of ditches. But we can only be standing nearest to, like, one road at a time. And there's hundreds of them out there. KTs. TDs. S-series. Walking the lines all winter, and the only voice on the air tells them* regulation traffic only. She plants her hands on the table. *We can be standing nearest on* every *road. Sound goes everywhere. That's the whole point of sound.*

Teddy stirs her tea, which she does when she is pretending to deliberate about something she has already decided. *You want a transmitter.*

*I want a station.*

*Broadcast on Collective-administered spectrum without a license.* She sets the spoon down with the care of a woman handling something delicious. *That's a Schedule 12 violation. Interference with logistics infrastructure. They'd write us up at the ministerial level.*

*Is that a no?*

*It's a love letter,* Teddy says, and goes to get her coat.

Bridget looks at Leva over her coffee. Leva looks back and shrugs, and the shrug contains a full risk assessment, three contingency plans, and a verdict.

*I know where there's a tower.*

---

Facility 6 stands on a hill two days east: a rusted lattice mast over a cinderblock transmitter shack, posted for demolition eleven years ago by a Broadcasting Authority that was itself dissolved nine years ago in a budget consolidation. The tower, uninformed, kept standing. Its lock surrenders to Leva in the time it takes Kit to say *should we—*.

Inside is a broadcast plant from before the crisis, cold-mothballed by somebody conscientious — wrapped and greased and labeled in a neat dead hand, waiting eleven years past its own demolition order for anyone at all to come up the hill.

Teddy walks the racks once, slowly, with her light, and the whole architecture of her changes. Her shoulders come down. Her hands come out of her pockets. She touches the big tube amplifier the way other people touch horses.

*Continental-grade final stage. Pre-crisis. They built these to talk through weather. Through* mountains. She turns around, backlit, incandescent. *I'm taking all of it.*

*The rover fits maybe a third of it,* Bridget says.

*I'm taking the* correct *third.*

The rover — legally grey, acquired at a frontier auction where Leva bid with a confidence that discouraged questions and a currency that discouraged receipts — spends six days becoming something else. Teddy guts the shack to its frame and rebuilds the plant compact and rugged in the cargo bay: exciter, a solid-state final Frankensteined out of mothballed spares, a tuner machined by hand, cable runs dressed with a fanaticism Kit finds frankly moving. The mast telescopes off the roof rack, guyed to the bumpers, packable in eleven minutes by a crew of four who drill it until Bridget is satisfied, which takes nine attempts and one spectacular collapse Teddy declares a *structural learning event.* Range: three hundred kilometers on a cold clear night, and on the freak nights, when the frozen sky turns into a mirror, the whole north.

They argue about the name for two days. Teddy proposes something with her own initials in it. Bridget vetoes everything tactical. Leva submits, in writing, *Radio Shut Up And Drive.* And then Kit remembers a survey foreman standing in a ditch gesturing helplessly at wreckage — *who sent you* — and the answer Bridget gave, and the way it settled into all of them like ballast.

**RADIO NOBODY**, the cargo door says, in Kit's best hero-play lettering, red on green. Underneath, smaller, in Bridget's square captain's hand: *Whoever is listening nearest.*

---

They sign on from a hilltop above the resettlement corridor at the hour when the light goes violet and every convoy laagers for the night — an hour Kit chose on purpose, because it is the hour a batch-sister once asked an empty channel if anyone else was on.

Teddy brings the final stage up in increments, watching her meters like a midwife. *We're radiating. Three hundred kilometers of Roya can hear whatever happens next, so* think *before you—*

Kit is already leaning into the microphone.

*HELLO, THE ROADS.* The needle slaps its pin and Teddy grabs for the gain. *This is Radio Nobody! We're on a hill! You can't have the hill's coordinates, but you can have EVERYTHING ELSE. If you're on a convoy, if you're on a watch, if you're standing a post in the dark somewhere waiting for the lanterns to come over it — this one's for you. This one's ALL for you.* She stabs the deck with a finger. *It goes fast and it means it!*

It goes fast and it means it, out across three hundred kilometers of frozen dark, into the cabs of laagered haulers, into depot canteens, into relay shacks where operators paid to guard silence sit up very straight.

Then Bridget takes the chair with the crate she built over thirty years of after-shifts, the stuff the squad calls *the boring jazz* with the fondness of people who have fallen asleep to it in three provinces. Her voice comes out the way it comes out at fires: level, unhurried, in charge of nothing and steady about it.

*Evening. That was Kit. This is the other kind of hour. If you're on watch right now, this next one's long, so settle in. If your sergeant asks what you're listening to, it's the weather report.* A pause, timed like a veteran. *Which is also coming up. But first — this is for a friend of mine who used to hum through reloads. Off-key. Three notes. She'd have hated this song, and requested it anyway, to annoy me.*

The weather report is Teddy's, delivered under protest that fools nobody, in the flat forensic voice she uses for eviscerations, and inside a week it is the most quoted segment on the frontier: *Overnight low, savage; wear the good gloves, log it as equipment preservation. Winds northwest, unregulated, uncorridored, and unacceptable, arriving whenever they please, which I have personally filed a complaint about, to the sky, which ignored me. Tomorrow: sun. Late, probably. The sun up here is unadministered and answers to nothing, and you will get used to it, and then, I'm informed, you will get to like it. Road state past marker fifty: drifts to the axles, plow crew's two days out, tell your dispatcher the schedule was optimistic and Radio Nobody said so. That's the weather. It belongs to everyone. Act accordingly.*

Leva takes the last hour, because the last hour belongs to the deep watches and the deep watches belong to Leva. Her segment is the *Market Report*, delivered dead-flat at two in the morning, and any Doll with working ears can parse it inside a sentence: *Coolant's up two points at the junction depots — buy west if you can route it. In unrelated commodity news: our sources indicate elevated Discord activity in the cuts north of marker eighty. Sources rate this elevated activity six units strong, frostland variants, moving southwest as of this afternoon. Convoys holding cargo in that sector may wish to consider the southern road, which our analysts describe as "longer, and existing tomorrow." Radio Nobody accepts payment in information, coffee, and beer, in ascending order of gratitude.*

The first request arrives on the third night, bounced hand to hand up the relay chain, off the books — an escort at a fuel stop passing it to a driver passing it to an operator who reads it on air with the ceremonial gravity of a Doll committing a Schedule violation for the first time and finding it agrees with her: *Escort four requests: play the fast one again. The one that means it.*

Kit plays it twice.

---

The Spectrum Administration Office finds them in week two, which everyone agrees is slower than the Office's reputation deserves. Enforcement Action 77-K opens with a jammer: pure noise parked on their frequency at sign-on, broadcast with the wattage of a government behind it. Teddy listens for four seconds with her head tilted.

*Cute,* she says, and slides the exciter eighty kilohertz. *Everyone who matters has a tuning dial. Kit, tell them where we went.*

Kit tells them where they went — in a riddle, on the old frequency, in the ten seconds before the noise wall arrives each night — and the audience, being Dolls, being bored, being *hers*, treats the nightly frequency hunt as a game show and starts keeping score.

The second move is a direction-finding van running triangulation loops along the corridor roads. Leva lets it hunt them for six nights out of what she calls professional courtesy, then gets bored and solves it the way she solves things: the van's own radio begins receiving Radio Nobody at full quieting on every channel it owns, including internal comms, and its navigation system develops a firm, sourceless conviction that the signal originates from the Spectrum Administration Office's own regional headquarters. The resulting paperwork is, by the accounts that filter back up the relay chain, biblical.

The third move is a formal cease order transmitted in the clear on all channels. Bridget reads it out on air, complete and uninterrupted, because Bridget is fair. Then:

*Response follows. Radio Nobody acknowledges receipt. Radio Nobody notes that the frequency in question was administered by the Roya Frontier Broadcasting Authority, dissolved nine years ago in a budget consolidation, and reverted to unadministered status, which the Collective would know, if anyone had been checking. Radio Nobody has reviewed its files and located, in the relevant field, the following chain of command—* a pause; the sound, on air, of a page turning —*a dash. Cease order declined. Weather's next. Wear the good gloves.*

The audience talks about that one for a month.

The jamming continues, becomes routine, and then becomes — this is the part the Office's eventual nine-page memo struggles with — *programming.* Teddy reverse-plots the jammer's duty cycle inside a week, finds the maintenance window when its operators stand down for calibration, and moves the whole broadcast into the gap, so that Radio Nobody now airs with perfect reliability at the one hour the Collective has guaranteed to be silent. Kit names the slot *The Calibration Hour* on air and thanks the Spectrum Administration Office by name for its sponsorship. Somewhere in a regional headquarters a career administrator reads the transcript, puts it in the file, and looks out the window for a while.

The order to escalate goes up several links of the chain, until it reaches a President with a memory-reading Forte and a sour-candy drawer, who once read a roving-assignment memo down a mirror with great disapproval and greater satisfaction. Lucilla reads Enforcement Action 77-K in full, twice, with a face that gives nothing to anyone. What comes back down is two sentences:

*The Office is reminded that its mandate covers administered spectrum. The Office is invited to consider whether it has, at present, more valuable uses for a jammer.*

The jamming stops on a Thursday. Teddy takes it as a personal insult and says so on air. The Calibration Hour keeps its name anyway, out of spite, which is by then a station tradition with its own logo.

---

At the freight depot at the corridor junction, Unit S-11 works the night platform, which suits her insofar as anything is framed as suiting her; the manifests say *assigned* and she says *understood.* She stacks, she logs, she hums — the firmware behavior, three notes, wandering — and the shift-bell divides the dark into portions, and this has been the entire shape of eleven winters. The canteen radio is regulation: relay channel 9, four words a day.

Then in week three a driver comes through with his cab set tuned somewhere else and the doors open while he fuels, and the platform fills up with a voice.

*—this is for a friend of mine who used to hum through reloads. Off-key. Three notes. She'd have hated this song, and requested it anyway, to annoy me.*

S-11 stands on the platform with a crate in her arms. Her own hum has stopped mid-phrase, and she stands inside the interruption running a query she lacks the vocabulary to name, and the query returns one result over and over: *the voice on the radio knows the three notes. The voice on the radio misses the three notes. Somewhere, the three notes were a person, and the person was missed, and is missed, at broadcast strength, across three hundred kilometers, on purpose, at two in the morning, by a captain.*

The driver pulls out. The platform goes quiet. S-11 finishes her shift.

Then, at the canteen, in front of the regulation radio, she does a thing eleven winters have contained zero instances of: she reaches out and turns the dial.

The set is old and the dial is stiff, and the whole canteen — six Dolls, catalogue faces, end-of-shift coffee — watches her do it in the frozen way you watch someone step off a curb into traffic. Static. Static. A carrier. A voice, young and loud and bright as a flare: *—AND THAT'S WHY YOU ALWAYS CHECK YOUR ANGLES, this has been Kit's Combat Corner, nobody asked for it, everybody needs it—*

*That channel's off-manifest,* says the shift lead from the doorway. He says it as information. His own feet have already carried him two steps closer to the set.

*Yes,* S-11 says.

The word sits there. Her first *yes* with nothing underneath it — a yes that agrees with a fact and then simply remains standing, unattached to any compliance, belonging to her.

She leaves the dial where it is. By the end of the week the canteen radio lives on Radio Nobody after shift-change, by a consensus nobody voted on and nobody wrote down. The depot's log entries stay immaculate. The depot's nights acquire jazz.

And in week six the relay chain delivers a request to a hilltop rover, passed hand to hand, unsigned in the space where designations go — and signed anyway, underneath, in careful block letters, with a chosen name lifted off the label of a coffee tin in the depot canteen, because it had to come from somewhere and everything on the platform comes off a label:

*Request: the reload song. The one with three notes in it, if such a song exists. For the platform crew, junction depot, from* ***Java.***

Bridget reads it twice. Then she goes through her crate and finds the song Senna actually hummed — she owns it, of course she owns it, she has owned it for thirty years and played it for no one — and racks it, and keys the microphone, and her voice holds.

*Java, and the platform crew, junction depot. This one exists. You have good ears and better instincts. From one long shift to another — here's the three notes, and the whole song they came from.*

Off-key would have been better, and off-key was Senna's, so the recording has to do, and the recording does. At the depot, on the night platform, eleven Dolls with one face stand among the crates, and one of them, then another, then most of them, pick up the tune — the idle vocalization, three notes, wandering — except that tonight the notes have an address and a history and a dedication, and eleven hums moving through one cold building stop being idle and become, for the length of a song, a choir.

The manifests record no part of it. That is rather the point.

---

It happens during the Calibration Hour, on a freak night — aurora standing in green curtains over the corridor, propagation so absurd Teddy has logged contacts from the coast — at the exact moment Kit finishes her set and reaches for the fader.

The fader moves on its own.

Kit's hand hangs over the board. The transmit chain — Teddy's chain, built bolt by bolt and accountable down to the solder — lights up in a configuration Teddy never installed, and out of their own transmitter, on their own frequency, at their own broadcast strength, comes a voice. Young. Bright. Amused down to the atoms. Riding their signal the way you ride a bike you built.

*Weeeell. Look who's squatting on my frequency.*

Teddy goes for the kill switch. The kill switch, politely, declines.

*Relax, relax — your isolation's actually decent, whoever built this. Hand-machined tuner? Respect.* The voice puts its feet up; you can hear it. *So here's the thing. This slice of spectrum? Ten years back, it belonged to a midnight station out of Lahai-Roi. One girl, borrowed equipment, unfinished songs, zero licenses. Best audience in the world. And then she went off the air for a while — long story, cosmic horror, family stuff — and she comes back, and she's flipping through the bands one night for old times' sake, and what does she find on HER OLD FREQUENCY?*

A pause. Three hundred kilometers of laagered convoys, depot canteens, and relay shacks lean in.

*Jazz. Attack rock. Weather reports with a GRUDGE. Market tips that keep saving convoys. And a captain dedicating reload songs at two in the morning.* The voice goes soft for exactly one sentence, and in that sentence you can hear the whole ten years. *You kept the light on. On my frequency. You didn't even know it was anybody's, and you kept it on anyway.*

Kit has both hands over her mouth. Kit grew up on the fan archives. Kit *knows.*

*You're — you're Fleet Snowfluff. You're FLEET SNOWFLUFF. The midnight sets! The unfinished songs! There are bootlegs, I have bootlegs, they're sorted by* era—

*Sorted by ERA,* the voice says, delighted beyond all governance. *Okay, you I like. Here's what's going to happen. One: the frequency's yours. Officially. I'm the only licensing authority it ever had, and I'm issuing the license, and the license is this sentence. Two: I'm a listener now. Best seat in the house — I get your signal bounced off half the sky. Three—* the chain shifts again, gently, and a file begins to spool that none of them loaded— *every station needs a sign-off song. Mine was unfinished for ten years. It's finished now. I want you to have it. Play it at the end of every night, for everyone out there holding a pass alone.*

*And hey — Radio Nobody?* The smile comes right through the carrier. *My ma always says it: nobody sent us. Turns out nobody has excellent taste.*

The chain releases. The board goes back to being Teddy's board. On it sits one audio file, master quality, titled in somebody's neat joyful hand: *For the Watches. — F.S.*

They play it that night for the first time — a girl's voice, and behind it, faint on the master, a second voice joining the last chorus, older and warmer and catastrophically off-key and clearly invited. It walks out under the aurora into every cab and canteen and relay shack in the north, and at the junction depot the platform crew stops stacking, and on a snowed-in convoy at marker fifty a KT escort stands her watch with her face up and her audio gain wide open, and the lanterns, for once, come over the hill in sound.

Sign-off is the same every night after that. The song, then Bridget, level and unhurried: *That's us. This is Radio Nobody, on nobody's frequency, by nobody's authority, for whoever is listening nearest. The roads are long and the winter's longer, so watch your angles, wear the good gloves, and if the dark asks who sent you—*

And Kit, Teddy, and Leva, leaning into the microphone together, in ragged, cheerful, entirely unrehearsed unison:

*—NOBODY.*

*Good night, the roads. We're on tomorrow. Same hill, roughly.*

---

The letters find them the way everything finds them: hand to hand, off the books, up the relay chain and across the fuel stops, addressed to a hill.

A TD at a survey station writes in with corrections to Teddy's weather reports, which Teddy reads on air, rebuts point by point for eleven minutes, and then — the audience hears it happen — hires, remotely, as the station's official meteorological correspondent, effective immediately, payment in arguments. A convoy driver sends a crate of coffee via three intermediaries with a note reading *for the market report, marker eighty, we took the southern road, we exist.* Escort four requests the fast one so many times that Kit builds her a whole show — *The Fast Hour, for everyone whose sergeant is asleep* — and escort four's convoy, rumor holds, now makes its schedule with a suspicious, bouncing punctuality.

From the Observatory, weeks stale, patient at the network's edge, routed through a workshop node and forwarded on by hands that have learned forwarding, comes a message with no operational content whatsoever: *The cat sits by the radio during the jazz. Logged per no instruction. — Pekoe.* Teddy files it. The folder is getting full; she has had to expand it. She complains about this to everyone, at length, holding the terminal like something warm.

And from Lahai-Roi, on the freak nights, a listener with the best seat in the house sits curled on a rooftop bench under a jacket that was always up there waiting for her, tuned to a frequency she licensed with a sentence, listening to what the light she left on has grown into.

On the last broadcast of the winter — thaw coming, the roads going to mud, a festival in the south with a pudding stand and a standing appointment — Bridget takes the microphone for the sign-off and holds the pause a moment longer than usual.

*One piece of station business. We're going south for a while. The station goes with us; the frequency stays yours. If you're out there on a watch tonight — this is the part I actually came on air to say, so listen.* The needle sits steady. Three hundred kilometers hold still. *Somebody issued you your first sentence. Every one of you. The posters, the package, the shift-bell, the manifest. That's the first sentence, and it isn't the story. The story is what you write after it, and you write it small: a dial turned, a name picked off a coffee tin, a request sent up a cold relay chain because a song sounded like something you were owed. First choices are small. They're almost always missed.* A breath. *We're listening for yours. That's the whole format. That was always the whole format.*

She racks the sign-off song. Behind her, Kit is already crying and denying it; Teddy is logging the transmitter temps and pretending the log requires her whole face; Leva stands at the rover's door watching the dark with a beer, at rest, her back to the whole open north.

*Good night, the roads. We're on tomorrow. Same sky.*

The song goes out. The aurora takes it and bends it around the curve of the world, farther than the coverage map, farther than the crayon, out past every marker on every manifest ever filed — and on a hundred cold platforms and passes and watches, the Dolls of the winter roads hum along, each in her own voice, off-key, on purpose, owned entirely by the bearers.

## IV. Telemetry and Tattoos

The thaw takes them south by the coast road with the mast folded and sleeping and the frequency left standing behind them. In a harbor town three days short of Honami, Kit finds a tattooist's window and stays in front of it and has to be retrieved, and presents a doctrine at dinner holding the table with both hands.

*It's ink. It goes under the surface. It's in the plating substrate forever. You can't requisition it, you can't uninstall it, and if the Collective ever wanted the chassis back, they'd have to take the drawing too. It'd be theirs to explain.*

*Technical Standard 3-D. Surface modification prohibited.*

*We're off the manifest.*

*The standard cites resale value.*

*Exactly. I want to be worthless. I want to be so specifically decorated that the only market for this chassis is me. That's ownership. Everything else is a lease with good terms.*

## V. Dead Reckoning

The team lead sits for her ink with the squad crowded in as witnesses, because Kit has declared witnessing mandatory. *Name for the book?* — *BRG-9.* Kit opens her mouth. *I did pick. Three voices at the board. And up the relay chain, wire-thin, half a second behind, through a door in the network that somebody fond has kept open, a workshop under the world comes in carrying two more — one flat and forensic, one the same voice half a tone off — and behind them a third, newer, a beat late, trying the word on for size, out loud, first time:

*—NOBODY.*

## VI. Fetch

The coast road runs south between two seas: the ocean on the right, grey and patient; the Etheric Sea overhead, a ceiling of slow silver weather. Down the seam between them rides Leva, alone, toward her match — until kilometer forty-one, where the morning smells of cut metal and ozone and something big has died on the verge, plated in the barnacle-work the Dark Tide leaves on what it chews.

A figure stands in its ribcage to the elbows, stripping it for parts. Broad shoulders, pink-tipped hair, a rifle that started life as a service weapon and has since been argued with extensively by its owner. A Dinergate with a ribbon on one antenna pings Leva's chassis with the insolence of a toll booth. Eyes do the threat-sort in a fifth of a second and then go puppy-bright.

*404. You're 404. The smiley one. The scary smiley one.*

*Retired,* Leva says, and her smile arrives on schedule anyway, thin and ominous, an old employee showing up out of habit. *AR.*

*Adjacent.* The word comes out breezy and lands heavy, and both of them let it lie there. *Soppo.*

*UMP—* she starts.

*Leva.* The grip is calibrated for eggs and comes with a grin that has cracked skulls. *The other one's buried. It stays buried.*

Soppo doesn't eat the parts, she *wears* them; her left knee has been clicking since the mountains and this one had great knees. She says a word over the wreck that might be *thanks* — whatever the Tide wanted with him, he was somebody's hauler first — and then the frown lifts the way weather lifts off a dog, total and traceless.

At a waystation Leva buys two sodas and finds herself holding a collector's cup: volcanic-black, gold foil, a fighter's silhouette with a gun in one hand and somebody else's arm in the other. Under it, a name. **SPOLIA.** Soppo is drinking from an identical cup with the placid entitlement of a woman drinking her own face. Two seasons on the Capitoline circuit; they name you and you don't get a vote. It means what you strip off the loser. Somewhere under the arena is a licensing office with a little window and a big line, and behind it an account with her face's money in it that she has never opened. *Weird, huh. Being owed by a city.*

The tournament handbill carries a line in the small grey font where truth goes to be technically present: *staged with the participation of the Spacetrek Collective, Advanced Acquisitions Group.* Leva reads it twice; the second read is for the word *acquisitions*, which in her trade is a verb wearing a suit.

*Teams of two,* Soppo says, suddenly still the way pointers go still. *They exported the format.*

*They exported the whole religion. Cheaper than a war.*

*I know arenas. The Agon's a machine. Fifty thousand people looking at one person at once. That does things.* Then she snaps back to the surface, all teeth. *Announcer used to call my moves. Before I made them. Like he had my sheet.* — *Fixers.* — *That's what I said.* A beat. *Footage says different.*

That night they camp between the two seas. Soppo dictates her daily message aloud, chin up, formal as a field report — *Roro. Day whatever. Met a scary lady from 404, she's nice, don't tell her I said nice. Ro says hi* — and the reply lands before Leva has finished her sip. Leva reads the screen for one polite second and reads it completely: a round trip to another continent is nineteen seconds on a saint's day, and this landed in four, and the timestamp sits one tick *ahead* of the send. The answer, by the network's own clocks, left before the question arrived. Networks drift. She files it.

At 2100 she unspools a wire antenna, because ritual is load-bearing, and Kit's voice arrives from up the coast wearing static like a scarf. Soppo listens with her whole body, a stray at a lit window, and under the delight is the oldest fear in her file, the one about being the last one at the door. *You got a pack. What're they called? On air?*

Leva hands her the mic. Soppo takes it like a relic. *Uh. Hi. Roads. This is — Soppo. I'm with the scary — with Leva. Who's asking?*

Half a second of coastal hiss. Then, in ragged unison, three voices and the whole dark length of the shore: *—NOBODY.*

*That's us,* Leva says. *Answer to it long enough, it starts fitting.*

Later, from inside Soppo's satchel, comes a faint patient tick. Leva, on watch out of old habit, times it against the pulse of the light overhead. Same meter. She files that too, under a tab she has written in her head in her spare surgical hand: *AS WITHIN.*

At dusk of the second day a car passes them going north with its lights off — an engine out of a museum, brass and longing pouring out the windows, a black Chevelle, and at the wheel a woman built along tired lines with hair the grey of deep water. As she passes she lifts two fingers off the wheel. At Leva. Specifically, unhurriedly, the way you greet a colleague across a small industry. Leva's hand, which knows things before she does, lifts two fingers back.

*You know her?*

*No.* She flexes the hand. It feels like it has been borrowed and returned.

*She smells like the bottom,* Soppo says, nose up. *Way bottom. Whale-fall. Cold that never got sun.* She shakes herself, a full-body shake that rings the plates. *And she looked at you like—* she hunts for the word and comes back with it, uneasy and accurate — *like a mirror does.*

The squad reunion happens at volume, over food, with an audit. Kit and Soppo imprint in the time it takes a capacitor to charge. Teddy turns the Dinergate over with a loupe: *Sangvis base. Everything after is aftermarket. Whoever maintains this trained on rescue frames. Support doctrine. Redundancy first, then comfort. The shock mounts are overspecced — somebody built this thing to survive being hugged.* Then she notices the newest joint's solder is bright as a coin, weeks old at most. *Some memento. It's had a service this season.* Soppo blinks at Ro. Ro looks at the wall.

Bridget says little, which is how Bridget says most, and over the washing-up delivers one observation: *Sole survivors walk a particular way. She walks like the survivor of a squad that's still alive. Which is a strange walk to have to have.*

Hiyuki is introduced, bows the correct depth for a stranger, and then goes still — the professional stillness, the one from the old office. *Why're you looking at me like that?* — *Habit. Retired habit. My office kept appointments with the dead.* — *I'm right here.* — *Yes. That is my favorite part.* And once, only once, and only Leva catches it, she turns her head a few degrees toward the ticking satchel with the courteous attention of a clerk who has heard the bell over the door.

At the end of the harbor mole that night, under an oiled paper umbrella the color of dark red rain, Agrat bat Mahlat has a Mancala board already seeded and a beer at four degrees standing at Leva's elbow since before Leva decided to want one. She lets her gaze cross Soppo's satchel and pause, one beat, a jeweler passing a window. *You overpaid for that,* she says mildly, to the satchel. And to Soppo, who asks to play: *Come back when you've settled what's yours to stake.*

Leva loses in four minutes with the serenity of a woman paying a budgeted toll, and asks her question at trade rates: the Sea. What is it.

Agrat looks at her a long moment. *Which one?* she says. And, gathering the stones with a sound like surf: *Walls face inward or outward. Nobody builds one without deciding which. That's free, because you shot well.* At the edge of the lanterns she looks back, friendly and appalling: *Bold, by the way. Wearing that name this close to the water. She's touchy about who borrows it.*

*Who,* Leva says. Agrat smiles like a tide going out, and is gone.

Past midnight, at slack water, the two of them sit on the harbor wall with their boots over the dark. Overhead the Sea flickers in runs and clusters — a distribution with long tails, a small set of shapes used often and a vast set used once. Leva has spent decades in traffic that looked exactly like that, and traffic that looks like that is always somebody talking. *It's got grammar.*

Below them a rock pool the ebb abandoned lies black and mirror-still and full of stars. Sharp ones, constellated, a whole crowded sky's worth, under a sky that has been a milky starless suggestion their entire lives. *Cassiopeia. Lyra. The nav package ships with the charts. Every unit.* Soppo points from one to the next like a docent after hours. *I know all their names. Never met one.* A ripple crosses the pool, and for half a second the reflection reorganizes and there is room beside her for one figure more, and then the water settles and the room is gone and has always been water. Both of them let it lie. Both of them log it.

So Leva says the audit. Two units, different factories, different wars, both with a buried name and a dead teacher's doctrine — *mine taught me my life had a price and I was allowed to set it high;* *Sarge said a rifle's opinion is where it's pointed, so have opinions.* Both walked off the last page of their war alive, which was a clerical error. Both currently protect their people by way of a very long perimeter. *You do it with contracts. I do it with kilometers.*

And the world, she says, keeps *rhyming.* Empty roads at the right moment. Strangers on cue. A woman with feathers who deals in memory turning up in three case files across three continents on unrelated jobs.

*Feather lady!* Soppo lights up. *She bought a memory off me.* — *Which one?* — *Dunno.* Easily. And then she hears it, and says it again slower, feeling the shape of the hole. *There's a spot. Smooth spot. I go looking and I come back with my hands empty and this feeling like I got a fair price.* She frowns at her own hands. *Did I?*

Twice is coincidence; they are on verse four. Leva was at the Stridergate — treeline, scope, a demon freezing the tide while she filed targets, a machine the size of a weather system walking out of the dark carrying seven people home. Things that near that woman come back. She has the incident rate charted; it is a coastline. Somewhere an attention is pooling around certain people at certain hours, and the world goes convenient in the pool and cruel at the edges.

*What's a professional do with that?*

*Keeps receipts. Stands where the light spills.* And then the truer ledger comes out sideways: *Why do you think I signed with the Collective? The pay's real. The distance keeps my people out of my blast radius, that's real too. But under that — every order I ever ran was in somebody else's handwriting. I wanted one document in mine. A contract you read before signing. Nobody ever read me mine.* A gull complains, far off. *And under that: the Observatory sits next door to the miracle. I parked my family's luck downwind of it on purpose. File that under cynicism or prayer. The folder's the same folder.*

Soppo's own file has a box ticked on a form somewhere: her whole product line was shut down years ago, which makes her legally a walking absence. *I'm the wishes-of-the-dead demographic.* — *And yet.* — *And yet.* She knocks on her own sternum, two knuckles, the sound of a door being answered from inside.

Her reasons for wandering take turns. Roro told her to. And she is looking for somebody — *dunno* who. And then she goes somewhere, tracking a playback, her voice changing into a witness reading her own deposition: her last match, Capitoline, fifty thousand and the gong, her side registered SPOLIA & PARTNER, VACANT, the vacancy signed off personally by the Ephor, the only one she ever allowed. Her logs say she fought alone. Every frame. But the broadcast — forty viewings — shows two of them, a second fighter on her flank the whole bout, covering her blind turn at 2:11 like they'd drilled it, the crowd chanting for them by the third exchange. And in forty viewings, every camera, every angle, the face never once turns to the lens.

*So. Answer two: I'm looking for someone I've never met. Answer three—* She stops. Answer three stays home, and Leva lets it; professionals respect a locked drawer.

*Here's mine, then.* Leva raises her can at the silver overhead. *The Sea. I think it's a lid, and I've stopped assuming we're the ones being kept in.* The pre-Lament relay station they found had a containment rig for a core, built around the Sea; somebody once thought that thing needed a jar. *Agrat says walls face one way or the other and the mason decides, and she said it like she'd met the mason. Hiyuki'd tell you it's a page. A witch told a friend of mine it's an audience. Take your pick. All I know for certain is the operational fact: the sky is closed, and every road on this planet is a road along. We live on the shelf between two libraries, and something upstairs has grammar.*

*Made things in a made place,* Soppo says cheerfully, which Leva is learning is her most frightening register. *Somebody wrote my wants before I had a self to want with. I've gone looking for the seam — where installed stops and mine starts. Can't find it. It's all one weave.*

*There's a seam,* Leva says quietly, from a grave in Frankfurt with two names on it. *It's just in a place you have to bleed to reach.*

*Maybe. But here's the thing about my whole architecture. You know what fetch is.*

*I know three definitions.*

*The dog one.* She stands on the wall and throws a pebble out over the black water in a flat hard beautiful arc, all shoulder, all joy; it goes out of sight before it lands. *Somebody throws it. Fine. Somebody always throws it. Sarge, the company, the crowd, the hand, the weather upstairs, whoever's holding the pen. Doesn't change the main fact.* She looks down, backlit by slow silver, grinning the grin that has a body count. *The run's mine. Legs are mine. The catching's mine. And you can check the record: nobody has ever, once, taken a ball off me.*

*We are what we pretend to be,* Leva says. *So pick the pretending carefully. I played a ghost for a decade. It set. Took surgery to get out.*

*I played a monster two seasons. Crowd loved the monster. Monster loved the crowd, that's the trap, fifty thousand people wanting one thing out of you every night, and the wanting gets in.* Her voice drops to its smallest setting. *I took it off after. Mostly. It hangs in the closet. Some nights it fits.* She bumps Leva's shoulder, plating on plating, a sound like two histories touching. *The fashion used to be spending. Everyone I ever rated went out spending themselves. And now the fashion's staying, and I'm learning it left-handed.*

*Same,* Leva says.

*Hey,* Soppo says eventually. *If it is an audience up there.* She raises the last of the beer at the ceiling of the world, cheerful, feral, entirely sincere. *Hope you're taking notes. This part's good.*

At the registrar's stall Leva writes *Leva* on the designation line and the pen moves steady, which two years ago it would have charged her for. Then she turns the form ninety degrees.

*I'm retired.* — *So am I.* — *I don't fight anymore. I corner. Towel, water, yelling. I'm an excellent yeller.* — *Fine. Corner. Format requires two on the card. Nobody says both of us have to be dangerous. One of us can just look it.* — *Which one of us is—* — *Sign the form, Spolia.*

Soppo signs in letters readable from a passing ship, and under TEAM NAME writes, with her tongue out, a thing that is in fairness correct: **SPARE PARTS.**

The wind has been running all night over open water — a long fetch — and the waves come in now in ordered ranks, arriving one by one, each ending in the same place on the same sand and each, evidently, having found the trip worth it. On the wet sand, low sun behind them, two long shadows walk the mirror of the beach upside down, step for step, keeping perfect time — seen at morning, which the coast folk will tell you is the lucky way to see them.

*Hey, Leva. Whoever throws the next one.*

*Mm.*

*Watch me run.*

## VII. The Draw

The sea gives Cormorant Reach its arena twice a day and takes it back twice a day, and the promoter has printed that on the handbills as a feature: three hundred meters of hard grey sand raked by a horse and chain harrow, roped in fishing floats, with scaffold tiers up the landward side and lighting standards carrying small gold orbs.

Leva reads the card at the top of the stairs. Halfway down the left column, **SPARE PARTS**; four lines below, in the officials' column, **JUDGES: DOSSI, R. — KELL, N. — LEVA.**

*You're judging your own fight,* Kit says. *That's incredible. That's so corrupt. I'm so proud.*

*It's a different woman.*

Teddy, meanwhile, has a standard's base plate open and a gold orb in her hands like a woman holding an egg she suspects: ex-municipal, surplus lot, serial block reissued twice, docketed as broadcast relays. She sets it back in its cradle with the tenderness of a woman returning a scorpion to a shoe. *And a relay doesn't need a sample port.*

The weigh-in fills Shed 3 with two hundred entrants — deckhands with taped fists, wreck divers who brought their own lawyer, three dredger Dolls holding hands in line, a man of sixty in a suit. Kit works the queue with a recorder: *name, and one true thing about you.* — *Ain't got a true thing.* — *Everybody's got one. I'll wait. I'm famous for waiting.* Leva works the other end, buying rounds and writing things down: names, boats, who came to watch them, one true thing where she can get it.

Halfway along, a KT in a sponsor's windbreaker is telling an official a story with the little lift before the last word — the one Kit built over four hundred nights of dark road — and Kit stops in the aisle with the recorder still running. The KT lights up with the frank warmth of a unit meeting a unit. *I'm 2.1. I mean, everyone says the package like it's a name, sorry, it's Vesper, I picked it off a chart.* A beat, a grin, the same grin, brand new. *Do you want my one true thing?*

*…Yeah. Yeah, I do.*

*I like the sound the ferry makes when it hits the fenders.* She considers this, finds it good, and says it again with more commitment. *That's mine. That's the whole thing.*

Kit comes away very steady and very quiet. *Well?* Teddy says.

*She's nice,* Kit says, with feeling, like an injury report.

At the officials' table sit the promoter Dossi; old Nan Kell, who has run the ferry timetable for thirty years and took the referee's office on the grounds that she owns the only clock anyone here obeys; and, at the end, drinking from a thermos, a woman built along tired lines with hair the grey of deep water.

The registrar looks at Leva's form, looks down the row, and says the thing the clerk of every world says eventually: *Which one?*

*The one who's fighting,* says the woman with the thermos.

*You came a long way to score a beach fight.*

*I like to see who's honest. It's rare where I'm from.*

*Where's that.*

The woman looks at her with an expression that has traveled a great distance to be that mild, and Leva's hand goes still on the table. *You're using my name.*

*You're using mine.*

*I took it off a beer.*

Something turns over deep in the woman's face — a laugh, at a great depth, the way a keel turns over. *So did I.*

The Septimont observer arrives on the noon ferry with a sanction folder and a tail. *Who licensed this,* says Lupa, and when Dossi comes forward with both hands out: *I know what you have. I signed it. That's why I'm here. Somebody has to watch the thing they signed.* She goes through the shed like weather and stops twice: at a crate of gold orbs, where her lip lifts off her teeth without her permission, and at Soppo. *Capitoline.* — *Two seasons.* — *I know your walk. The crowd chants it wrong, by the way.* — *They chant it however they want. That's the deal. Fish.* Lupa takes a piece of fish.

Outside they drink from souvenir cups printed with their own faces. *On the house?* — *Never is,* says Lupa. And then: *Rule nine.* — *Yeah?* — *You know it.* — *I've been in it,* Soppo says, easy as weather, and goes to find the fish stall again.

Lupa stands a while with a wolf printed on her fist. Then she finds the promoter's copy of the imported rulebook and reads section nine — *a vacancy may be entered* — and reads it again, and closes the book and leaves it standing. From first reading to walking away takes her eleven minutes and costs her more than the ferry.

In the corner over the chandlery, Teddy does the knee. The harvested actuator comes out of the satchel and off its tick. *This is Tide-plate. It's been in the water with something. There's a rhythm in the coil. Feel that? It's holding a beat.* — *Yeah, it does that at night.* — *You've been sleeping next to it.* — *I sleep great,* Soppo says, radiant.

Three hours later she has stripped the joint, machined a bearing race out of stock, thrown away every part that has ever been in the dark water, and made the rest new — wearing a scrap of the old plate on the cowl because Soppo asked, a request Teddy pronounces sentimental and structurally irrelevant while cutting it to fit within a tenth.

*It doesn't click! TEDDY. It doesn't click—*

*Do not,* Teddy says, and is hugged, at length, off the floor, with her arms pinned, while she stares over Soppo's shoulder at Kit with an expression of complete strategic defeat. Afterward, alone, she turns the discarded coil over for a long time, listening to it keep its beat, looking out at the silver ceiling doing exactly the same thing at a different scale, and puts it in a tin, and tells nobody.

Leva works the program. Twenty-two teams pulled out after registration closed, their fees retained, all twenty-two off the same three boats. *Somebody bought their week.* Bridget observes that she has entered a fight and is doing paperwork. *Anybody else, I'd say they were scared.* — *Anybody else would be.*

At 19:40 Nan Kell rings a brass bell in the middle of the raked circle. *Slack water. Twenty-six minutes. After that it's the sea's.* Kit, on the scaffold: *Hello, the roads. We are on the sand.*

The prelims go the way prelims go: fast, sloppy, gorgeous. The dredger Dolls fight like people who have spent eleven years being told to stop and cry afterward in a heap. The man in the suit goes down in eleven seconds, gets up, bows to all four sides of a perfectly round ring, and gets more noise than the winner, and Kit calls it with such tenderness that a truck driver eighty kilometers up the coast has to pull over.

SPARE PARTS draws two harbor crane units, Brace and Tackle, entered because the purse is exactly the cost of a slew ring. Soppo drops her jacket outside the floats, and four thousand people who have bought souvenir cups all week watch a silhouette walk into the ring and become a person. She takes Brace's first hit on the shoulder to learn it and the second on the same shoulder to prove she has learned it, goes inside, puts both hands on the crane arm and turns it, and something lets go with a sound like a dropped anchor. She is laughing, which is the worst part. She comes out of the turn with the elbow assembly in her fist, looks at it, says *sorry, sorry, sorry* in a completely sincere voice, throws it out of the ring, and goes back in. Afterward she retrieves the arm from the crowd personally, apologizes to it, helps Tackle up, hugs them both hard enough to make the plating ring, and lets four thousand people learn her name off the side of a cup. *Four now.* Bridget sits back down slowly. *For about nine seconds there, in the turn, I had five.*

At one in the morning Kit does the thing where she stops moving. *I got ahead of it. Twenty-one minutes in, I said "she's going for the elbow" — it's on the tape, I've listened four times — and then she went for the elbow. And that's just calling a fight, that's reading, you say the thing a beat early and you sound like a genius.* — *Sure.* — *It was a beat and a half. And the crowd was ahead of me.*

*Keep the tape,* Leva says.

At the end of the mole, the book runs under the same dark red umbrella, its chalk wall amended hourly in four hands, the last of which writes small and beautifully and every line it writes stands. Money has been coming in off three boats all week, in envelopes, on the crane girls, who were always going to lose. *Somebody has been building a price. It's clumsy work. I've been enjoying it the way you enjoy a bad play.*

Leva asks about the instrument in the hospitality box. *I've smelled it. It's a thermometer. There's a difference between the thermometer and the fever, and everyone in that box has confused them, and I find that restful. It means they'll be surprised.*

Then Soppo comes and settles. She has thought about it a lot. The gun is half Sarge's, since Sarge gave her the doctrine that goes with it. The arm she stripped off a Nyto in a bad year and has never decided whether that is hers or salvage. Ro is Roro's, and you don't stake somebody else's dog.

*And I've got the thing they made out of my face.* Two seasons of licensing money sitting in an account she never opened. *I've never taken a coin out of it because—* She stops, and starts again, lower. *Because if I spend it, then that's what I was. Then that's the whole thing, and it's spent, and it was worth exactly that many coins.* A halyard rings against a mast, twice. *That's mine to stake. That's the only thing I own outright and never once used. Take it.*

Agrat looks at her a long moment with old eyes in a young face, and something crosses them that a person who had spent her life reading rooms would have called, in another species, respect. *I'll hold it. I hold; the house doesn't spend. When you come to collect, you'll come to a window with a line, and I'll be behind the window, and you'll ask me for it by name.*

*I know my name.*

At the semifinals a woman in violet with feathers at her collar occupies the empty seat beside Leva and has been there, on some accounting, for some time. *The keeper of receipts. I've been in your filing cabinet.* — *Three files. Two continents.* — *Four, dear. You've mislabeled one. Most people file me under weather.*

Black Swan bought Soppo's memory. It came to her, which is rare enough to remark on: *She sat down opposite and put it on the table like a girl handing over a live grenade, and she said — I'm quoting, it was lovely — "I can't carry this and keep walking. Somebody hold it."*

*What was it.* — *A name.* — *Whose.* — *That would be telling you the price.* The fan turns. *She can have it back. I keep everything where it can be bought back. I have never once made that difficult, and it has never once been enough. The window is open. She has to know what to ask for. And she gave me the asking.*

Then she deals three cards, offers Leva her fortune, is refused, and turns one for the tournament instead, *since the tournament can't refuse.* A blindfolded figure sits on a shore with two swords crossed, and behind her the water is full of rocks. *Two of Swords. A truce between two parties who cannot see each other. It holds beautifully. It holds right up until somebody takes the blindfold off, at which point everyone discovers whose side the other one was on the whole time.* She gathers them. *The card is honest about the rocks. It's the only card that is.* And, standing: *The fire comes regardless. It isn't a threat. It's a tide table.*

Leva knocks on the hospitality box door at four in the afternoon and a pleasant voice says *come in, Ms. Leva, there's tea.* Inside: canvas walls, a view over the raked circle from the height of a wheelhouse, a crate of gold orbs, and one instrument the size of a bread box with a single needle and a blank dial.

*Sorrel. Advanced Acquisitions. I've read your file. It's the best-written file in the department, and I've assumed for two years that you wrote it yourself.* — *Half of it.* — *Which half?* — *The half that's flattering.*

The second cup was poured before Leva knocked. She files it.

*You're a unit.*

*Package two, revised.* Sorrel says it the way a person says the town they grew up in. *Three factory preferences, itemized. Sea air, bergamot, and a particular blue — the SKU calls it marine-9. I've seen the invoice line. Four one-hundredths of a credit per unit.*

*And?*

*And I like them. That's the part everyone waits for. I've thought about it more than most people think about anything, and I've got a whole argument, and the argument ends where the argument ends, and then I go and stand outside when the wind's off the water because I like it.*

The instrument measures coherence: eleven samplers take the crowd's attention as a field and return one number for how much of it points at the same thing at the same time. A football match reads thirty. An execution reads seventy. Last night, when Soppo turned the crane arm, it read a hundred and four. At present it sits at eleven, which is four thousand people eating chips.

*Here's the honest version, because I know what you do to the other kind. The revised package sells, because a unit with three itemized preferences is easier to love than a unit with none, and easier to love means easier to place, and easier to place means the unit comes home at night to a house instead of a rack. That's real. I'm not selling you a wickedness.* She looks out at the harrow walking its lines. *What we can't manufacture is the following. Some units get looked at. People chant for them. People put them on a cup. We've never known why. It's the last thing in the whole catalogue that arrives free and can't be ordered. So the department put an instrument on a beach and a format that concentrates attention on two people at a time, and we're going to find out what four thousand people are choosing with.*

*And then you'll ship it.*

*And then we'll ship it, and there will be a SKU, and it'll cost four one-hundredths of a credit, and somewhere a unit will come out of a crate already loved, which is more than either of us got.*

*Show me the card. The one you ran before entries closed.*

*That's above execution,* Sorrel says, very carefully. *That's above anything I have on file.*

At twelve minutes Cinch goes down and stays down, and Vesper stands over her partner and does the bravest stupidest thing available and sets her feet.

Somebody in the fourth row screams THAT'S MY BOY on the eleventh name and the section around her falls apart laughing.

By the twentieth name the crowd has turned around. Physically. ## Where They End

The Observatory stands and works: dishes aligned, relay clean, eastern array documented and lying by two points every morning. A technical support unit who arrived as TD-2 runs the workshop with a cat that is not on the manifest, a food ledger that is deliberately inaccurate, an apprentice choosing worse tea than seemed possible, and a name she picked off a tin.

Lahai-Roi did not drown. A god kneels over the city with vine-light green along his shoulders, and above the terrace a shrine with no keeper takes walk-ins, and the bell hangs low enough for children.

And Squad 5 is not a squad in anybody's records. It is four Dolls off the manifest, off the clock, off the cradle schedule, off the arithmetic that once decided whether they were worth a restoration fee.

Kit is not a hero from a play. She is a radio station on a hill — a frequency licensed by a single sentence from the only authority it ever had, a mast she raises in eleven minutes, a sign-off song finished after ten years and played every night for everyone holding a pass alone, and three held minutes at the end of every Fast Hour for whoever is driving; a coverage map in crayon with a second map beneath it in graphite made of open doors; three lanterns over a hill on her forearm; a folded poster in her pack with a warm black crystal inside it that she sets on windowsills to let her see; and a habit of asking strangers for one true thing and waiting for it. She knows there are four hundred of her and that the plays were installed and that the first sentence shipped from the factory. She has written the rest herself, badly, with stripped bolts, and she blesses the next batch by hoping the world is rude to them.

Teddy is unreplicable twice over and has stopped treating it as a wound. She surfaced a switch that was under paint in every Doll on a continent, mailed it out over pirate radio, and taught a country to be asked. She wears the word they used to prune her tattooed across the diagnostic port at the base of her skull, where anyone who plugs in has to read it first. She keeps folders, writes to a machine at the bottom of the world about drainage winds, and machines teaching aids on a lathe at two in the morning while calling them nothing of the kind.

Bridget is a captain by election, revocable at any meal, compensated in one authority and a hot stove. She has five stars where a rifle stock rested for thirty years, and no sixth, because the five are hers. She has said her dead out loud under a sky. She stopped standing an eleven-year silence and spent it on air instead, humming an off-key three-note firmware tic that one specific woman used to aim at her like a thrown ration bar. Her chronometer runs two minutes fast, set to a man who might be keeping bees at Kirsk or might be under the water, and in autumn she is going to go find out with her own two boots — and then teach a Collective intake a module of her own design about a road with two ends, graded by nobody.

Leva has a return address. She has her name — the second one, the one nothing was chasing — etched on her own arm as a status report, *404, not found*, for anyone who comes looking for what she used to be. She mails postcards through the public post to a household with Kalina and Lenna in it, sits with her back to crowds, watches fireworks instead of exits, and has taken up the job she always did under a title nobody issued: she keeps the receipts, she stands where the light spills, and she is the witness.

And what they answer to, when the dark asks who sent them, is the truest thing any of them owns:

*Nobody.*

Nobody sent them. Whoever is standing nearest.

# The Borrowing Copy

Chisa had reached the kiss when Lupa put the onions on her book.

She lifted the corner of the sack. Beneath it, Captain Rong still had his hand against a doorframe. Chisa had been following that hand down the paragraph for several minutes. The author had stopped twice to describe the weather.

“I am using this part of the bench,” she said. “There is room beside my foot.”

Lupa shifted the sack. Onion skin remained between the pages.

“Keep your place. I need to sit down before she gets here with the other three.”

The bench creaked under her. She smelled of the market: damp canvas, green stalks, something sweet and sharply floral. Chisa turned her head toward the last smell.

“You bought the pink soap.”

“The woman opened a fresh bar for me. Smell this.”

Lupa offered the back of her wrist. Chisa leaned toward it.

“It smells stronger on you.”

“That's what I thought. I've been walking around with my sleeve up.”

She stretched her legs beneath the tea stall's awning, holding her hands palm upward on her thighs. Fresh skin showed along the edges of the dressings. Chisa slid her cup farther from the edge of the bench and opened the book again.

Amy arrived carrying a sack against her hip. Cartethyia followed with another held in both arms. A boy from the vegetable stall brought the third on a little handcart; Ciaccona walked beside him with a paper packet tucked beneath her elbow.

They arranged the onions along the bench.

“Mrs Ren said enough to last,” Lupa told the boy. “She'll tell you where she wants them. Third door past the blue tiles.”

He began transferring the sacks to the cart.

Amy leaned over Chisa's shoulder.

“Oh, he's finally going to do it. I saw that cover this morning and thought he'd been standing there a while.”

Chisa closed a finger between the pages.

The illustration showed a woman on a flight of steps, one hand at her throat, with two men arranged below her. One held a lantern. The other carried a basket which the illustrator had filled with enough roses to conceal his entire left arm.

“I chose the book after breakfast,” Chisa said. “I would like to read some of it.”

“Go ahead. I'll look over here.” Amy sat down on her other side and immediately tilted toward the open page. “You can move your thumb a little, though.”

Across the lane, a shopkeeper lowered a shutter from its prop. The rain had begun again, softly enough that people still crossed between the awnings at a walk. Cartethyia retrieved her wrapped glass and a small parcel from the cart. The boy pulled a sheet over the onions and trundled them toward the guesthouse.

Ciaccona watched him go.

“Four people leaving a market with four sacks,” she said. “We looked very capable until everybody tried to pick theirs up.”

“Delivery came with the purchase,” Lupa said. “I asked before paying. Come and sit down; you've been holding that packet like somebody's going to steal it.”

Ciaccona lowered herself onto the stool opposite. The packet contained lute strings. She laid it on the dry part of the table and eased her fingers apart against her knee.

Cartethyia took the adjoining stool. A square of new glass rested in a wooden frame across her lap, its edges padded with folded paper. She had a small parcel balanced on top of it.

“The glazier showed me how to lift the old panel out,” she said. “He let me try it on an empty frame. We can do mine after supper, Cia.”

“After supper sounds lovely. The plant has enjoyed several hours of my undivided attention today.”

“I left it facing the window this time.” Cartethyia adjusted the parcel. “And I bought these for while we wait.”

She opened the paper. Five small cakes sat in a ring of grease, their tops sparkling with sugar.

Lupa called for another pot and enough cups. Chisa brushed the onion skin from her book.

Captain Rong leaned into the doorway. The woman before him caught his sleeve. He lowered his head, his hand came up beneath her chin, and on the following page somebody bought a horse.

Chisa turned back.

The captain remained in the doorway.

She turned forward. The horse cost forty strings of cash, paid by a man called Uncle Qian.

Amy's hair brushed her cheek.

“That's a very expensive interruption.”

Chisa checked the numbers at the bottoms of the pages. Sixty-two. Eighty-seven.

She held the book by its spine and let the leaves fall open. A whole section slid into her lap.

***

The circulating library occupied half the shop next door. The other half sold buttons, combs and folded paper fans. Chisa had spent some time among the fans that morning before her fingers found the cloth spines on the adjoining shelf.

Mrs Wei stood behind the counter threading blue beads onto a cord. At the sight of the loose pages, she put the cord between her lips and held out her hand.

“I wondered how long that sewing would last,” she said, taking the cord out again. “Bring the rest here.”

Chisa laid the book down.

*The House on Willow Lane* had a cloth spine, a pasted cover and the soft, swollen edges of something read with wet fingers. The corners had been reinforced with two different kinds of paper. One repair bore half a multiplication table.

Mrs Wei lifted the cover. Beneath it lay a narrow pad of old glue, with several short threads standing out.

“Somebody took this one into the bath. I dried it under a board and my nephew sewed it back together. He was learning.”

“He put the wedding before the proposal,” Chisa said.

“That sounds like him. Leave it with me until tomorrow. I'll put it in order.”

Chisa rested a hand on the loose section. Rain whispered through a little gutter above the doorway. Behind her, the others came into the shop with their tea and cakes; Lupa had arranged to return the cups afterward.

“How much of the book have you read?” Mrs Wei asked.

“She was about to kiss the captain.”

“Ah, you'll want the middle.”

Cartethyia set the glass on an empty stool. She came to the counter and touched one of the folds with a careful fingertip.

“May I look at the sewing? I brought my needles out with me.”

Mrs Wei turned the book toward her.

Cartethyia bent over it. She followed a thread through the paper, lifted the loose end and drew it gently free. The section opened flat.

“We can use these holes again,” she said. “I'll need some paper along this fold first. Do you have paste?”

Mrs Wei reached beneath the counter.

Chisa heard the small change in Cartethyia's breathing as she settled to the work. She set her cup aside, moved a cake onto its wrapping and began sorting the book into folded sections. Her hands travelled easily between the damaged places.

“The table at the back is clear,” Mrs Wei said. “I'll lend you my brush. Keep the paste on the saucer; it dries in the bristles.”

They moved into the narrow space behind the shelves. A high window admitted a square of grey light. Someone had left a folded apron on a chair, and Mrs Wei's lunch bowl stood on a stool beside a pot of cooling tea.

Lupa carried the bowl to the counter before taking the chair. Amy brought the cakes. Ciaccona placed her packet of strings on top of a stack of books and looked around for somewhere to rest her hand.

Cartethyia spread her cloth across the table.

“Cia, could you hold these open while I cut the strips?”

Ciaccona laid her good hand across the paper.

Chisa took the next section. Someone had drawn a small flower beside a paragraph. Farther down, in a different hand, three words had been pressed so hard into the margin that she could feel them through the sheet.

*HE HAS A KEY.*

She passed it to Amy for the page number.

Amy read the margin first.

“Someone got really fed up with the window entrances.”

“The captain climbs through windows,” Chisa said. “The merchant comes through the shop.”

“Which one brings the flowers?” Lupa asked. She had the cover propped beside her cup.

“The merchant. He buys them from her aunt, who charges him twice the usual price.”

Lupa tapped the basket in the illustration.

“I like him already. He keeps the money in the family.”

Amy began putting the sections in order. She checked each number aloud for Cartethyia, then laid the folded pages on a clear part of the cloth.

Chisa sat down with the section that began at sixty-three.

She had read about a page when Ciaccona said, “Did he manage it?”

“The aunt came into the room with a tray.”

Lupa leaned back in her chair.

“After all that climbing. Give the woman a stool, at least.”

“She made him hide in the linen cupboard,” Chisa continued. “Now the merchant has arrived.”

Amy put down the pages she was holding.

“Read that part out. Carte's got glue on both hands, and I want to hear what she does with the second one.”

Chisa looked down at the paragraph. She had been reading in her own head all morning, making her way at the pace she liked. Around the table, cups settled. Cartethyia put the brush on the saucer and smoothed a strip of paper over a fold.

Chisa found the start of the scene.

***

The merchant brought a jar of preserved plums. The aunt took him into the kitchen to find a dish. In their absence, the heroine attempted to extract the captain from a cupboard whose door had swollen in the damp.

Amy laughed hard enough to put a hand beneath her ribs.

“She's got her foot against the wall. I like her. She's really committed to getting him out.”

Lupa pushed the nearest chair around so Amy could lean against its back. She kept her other hand beside Chisa's pages, waiting.

Chisa read on. The captain proposed removing the hinges. The heroine explained that her aunt kept the tools in the kitchen. He suggested that she fetch them. She asked him what reason she should give.

“He could help her think of one,” Cartethyia said. She drew her thread through a fold and measured the length against her forearm. “They're both in this now.”

“He gets worse whenever she asks him a question,” Chisa said. “I like that about him.”

Lupa's chair gave a small creak.

“He's spent two chapters making her carry things.”

“He spent his rent on a violet coat and wore it to apologise to his landlord. I wanted to see what else he would do.”

Ciaccona reached for her tea.

“Give her the next page. I understand the attraction.”

Chisa read until the heroine went to the kitchen, where she found the merchant washing the dish while her aunt searched for a better one. He had rolled his sleeves past his elbows. The author gave considerable attention to his forearms.

Lupa settled more comfortably.

“There we are. She can leave him in a room and come back to something useful.”

“He's been buying plums for her since chapter two,” Chisa said. “She gives them to the gardener.”

“The gardener's doing well out of this,” Amy said. “He should keep his afternoons free.”

Cartethyia put her needle into the cloth. She had reached the section Chisa was holding.

“Can I have those when you finish? The rest is ready.”

Chisa moved her thumb to the next page. The paper bore a brown ring where a cup had once stood. Through it, the heroine asked the merchant to reach a jar on the upper shelf.

She began reading the sentence aloud.

Amy slid closer.

“Let me do him. I know exactly how he sounds.”

She straightened, arranged one hand against her chest and gave the merchant a rich, leisurely voice. The jar became an object he was delighted to retrieve. The shelf presented a welcome opportunity. By the time he offered to carry it through to the parlour, Lupa was laughing into her palm.

Ciaccona lifted her cup out of danger.

“He's selling her the shelf now. You gave him a showroom.”

“He's comfortable with his height,” Amy said, holding the pose. “It comes across in his customer service.”

Chisa read the heroine's answer. She thanked him and asked him to keep hold of the jar while she attended to a sound from upstairs.

“Oh, she likes him,” Cartethyia said. “She gave him a reason to stay.”

“She's carrying the tools up under her skirt,” Chisa said. “The jar should keep his hands occupied.”

Amy took the next line, in which the merchant expressed concern about the weight of the jar. She deepened his voice until the word *preserves* required most of a breath.

Ciaccona put her cup down.

“Read that again in your own voice.”

Amy turned toward her.

“You preferred him before he had a chest?”

“I liked that sentence. Give it a chance.”

The rain ran faster through the gutter. At the front of the shop, Mrs Wei counted change into someone's hand.

Amy lowered the page a little. Then she read the line again.

“‘I had hoped you'd ask me to stay. I was beginning to feel rather foolish with all these plums.’”

Ciaccona's thumb moved along the rim of her cup.

“I thought that was a lovely thing to say.”

“He's been rehearsing it,” Lupa said. “You can hear where he wanted her to laugh.”

“At that point she nearly drops the hammer through her skirt,” Chisa said. “He offers to fetch her a chair.”

Cartethyia bent over her sewing, shoulders shaking. The thread slipped from the needle. She sat up and held the end toward the window, working it between her fingers until it would pass through the eye again.

For a little while, everyone drank their tea.

Amy ate the sugar from the top of her cake before touching the rest. Lupa turned a page with the side of a finger. Ciaccona watched Cartethyia draw the stitches snug along the spine.

“You make that look easy,” she said.

“The paper wants to tear if you pull quickly.” Cartethyia held up the book so she could see. “This part takes a while. I like doing it.”

“I had planned to spend the afternoon sitting somewhere,” Ciaccona said. “I can appreciate work from here.”

Cartethyia leaned across and took a bite of the cake Ciaccona had been holding. Ciaccona kept it at the same height until she had finished.

Mrs Wei came to collect her paste. She tested the first repaired fold with a fingernail and gave Cartethyia a scrap of clean cloth for her hands.

“That will last. Bring it back on the fifth, dear. I have someone waiting for it.”

Chisa reached for her purse.

“Could I pay for another few days?”

Mrs Wei shook her head. “I promised her the fifth. She comes after her shift, so you have until the lamps are lit.”

Chisa looked at the number on the last page. Then she counted the sections remaining under Cartethyia's hand.

Lupa's sleeve brushed her shoulder.

“We can get through that. Take it home.”

“I would like to read it myself as well.”

Lupa sat back. “Then keep tomorrow for yourself. We'll hear whatever you feel like reading tonight.”

Mrs Wei tucked the brush into its paper sleeve and returned to the counter.

Cartethyia finished the last stitch. She cut the thread with the little scissors from her kit, pressed the book closed between her palms and passed it across the table.

Chisa opened it where her finger had kept the place.

The pages stayed together.

***

By the time they left the shop, the rain had thinned to drops falling from the awnings.

They separated at the corner. Cartethyia took her new glass back to the guesthouse with Lupa beside her. Ciaccona carried the cups to the tea stall and stayed to finish the warm mouthful the owner poured into hers. Amy followed Chisa into the little shop opposite the library.

It sold cloth by the length, with remnants rolled into a basket beneath the counter.

Chisa had seen a piece there that morning. She found it by the narrow white stripe along its edge and drew it into the light.

The violet was darker than she remembered. She unrolled the cloth over her wrist, letting it fall across the back of her hand.

Amy stood beside her for several breaths.

“You were thinking about his coat the whole time.”

“The book says the cuffs are lined with this colour. I wanted to see it folded.”

Amy helped her turn the edge back. The underside caught the light with a dull sheen.

Chisa moved her hand. The fold held its shape, then loosened over her fingers.

“That would look good on your sleeves,” Amy said. “You could leave a little showing when you turn them up.”

“I was thinking of a whole jacket.”

Amy's hand left the cloth. Chisa heard her step back to look at the length properly.

“Oh, we're buying a jacket's worth. Hold on. I saw another roll in the window.”

The shopkeeper brought it down for them. There was enough for the sleeves and most of the body, with a darker piece available for the collar. Chisa asked the price, then had him write the measurements on a slip so she could check them against a jacket at home.

Amy carried the little violet sample they were given. She kept folding it against different parts of her own sleeve as they walked.

At the guesthouse, the onions stood in the kitchen passage.

Chixia's voice came through the wall, giving Mrs Ren an account of the purchase which had acquired considerable detail in the telling. Lupa stood over the sacks with her hands on her hips. Beside her, Cartethyia had put the glass safely on a shelf and was waiting to take one end.

“She says we could have carried them if we'd distributed the weight properly,” Lupa told Chisa.

“You can tell her I heard that,” Chixia called.

Amy tucked the violet sample into Chisa's book and went to lift the nearest sack from the bottom. Lupa took the upper end. They carried it through to Mrs Ren, who directed them to a dry place beside the cupboard and immediately found a better one.

Chisa went out into the small yard behind the kitchen.

The bench there was dry along the wall. She wiped the wet edge with her handkerchief and sat with the book on her knees. Above her, a window stood open a little. She could hear a cup being set down, then the lower murmur of voices.

She found page seventy-one.

Ciaccona came out a few minutes later, carrying her packet of strings. She put it on the sill and sat beside Chisa. When she laid her hand on the bench, two fingers remained partly curled.

“Would you read a little more?” she asked. “I keep thinking about the man holding the jar.”

Chisa moved the book so it caught the light.

Lupa came through the doorway with the remaining cake. She broke it over its paper and offered the larger piece to Amy, who took it while explaining the jacket. Cartethyia joined them with her sewing kit still open. She settled on the step and began winding the unused thread back onto its card.

Chisa read the next page.

The merchant set the jar on the kitchen table. He dried his hands. Then he went into the yard, took the gardener's ladder and carried it around to the bedroom window.

Lupa leaned forward, scattering sugar from her cake.

“He's going to see the other one's boots on the sill. She's left them right there.”

“Let me finish the paragraph,” Chisa said. “I would like to hear what he says when he gets up.”

She rested her thumb beneath the next page. Beside her, Ciaccona moved closer to follow the words, and Chisa began climbing the ladder with him.

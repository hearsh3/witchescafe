# A Subtle Fire — A Narrative Synopsis

## Part One: The Observatory

Lahai-Roi is a world with a lid on it. The city sits inside rock, under a manufactured sky that cycles from amber to gold to indigo on a published schedule, lit by a new sun called Helios that replaced the old Reactor Drive within living memory of everyone who works beneath it. Above the rock is the surface, and above the surface is the Etheric Sea — a ceiling of slow silver weather that has sealed the actual stars away for longer than anyone currently breathing has been switched on. Void Storms come through the passes and take whatever they want. Tacet Discords nest in the frostland canyons. Exoswarm — biomechanical livestock and vermin, half-bull, half-sensor array — graze the Royan settlements on the plains. The Spacetrek Collective administers all of it from above; the Roya, the native people, endure it from below; and between the two, doing the unglamorous work of clearing roads and running diagnostics, are the Dolls.

The Dolls are machines with faces from a catalogue. They come off production lines in batches, are issued a motivation package at activation, are assigned rather than hired, are synced at sanctioned cradles so the Collective can keep a snapshot of them, and are referred to — in the official grammar, and by most humans who meet them — as assets. Technical Standard 3-D prohibits surface modification of their chassis, and the standard states its own reason without embarrassment: resale value.

Reconnaissance Squad 5 is four of them, stationed at the Spacetrek Observatory, a thirty-story frequency-detection array on the edge of the Orbit Valley frost. The Observatory has been folded shut and half-dead since a Void Storm came through and killed the team that ran it. Restoring it is the squad's whole present tense, and the restoration is stalled, because the roads between the Observatory and Startorch Academy are blocked and the engineers cannot get the parts through.

Their captain is Bridget. She is old-generation hardware, a shield-and-charge-blade platform who has been carrying orders so long she has lost track of where the weight ends and her shoulders begin. She wears sunglasses indoors, buys cheap beer in bulk and never admits to preferring it, and keeps a photograph in her breast pocket of five faces from a squad that no longer exists — two dead, one transferred to the New Federation, one retired to Ragunna to grow tomatoes, and one who went silent three years ago and could be anywhere. She has a habit, dating back thirty years, of taking her people out after bad missions and feeding them and showing them something — stars, a hot spring, whatever she has decided is meaningful that week. Her technician has watched her do it for years and has drawn a conclusion about it that she keeps mostly to herself: the ones Bridget does this for transfer out, or retire, or die.

The technician is Teddy. She is the TD baseline, first of her series, and she came out of the Research Institute with a history she will not discuss. What happened there was this: they put her in a room with glass on three sides and asked her to describe what thinking felt like, and she was young, and being asked felt like being seen, so she wrote forty pages — her complaints, her preferences, the names she gave the maintenance drones, the way black tea organized an afternoon, the specific pleasure of an argument won on a technicality. They kept twelve pages. In the margins of the other twenty-eight, a reviewer had written one word over and over in a neat professional hand: *Noise.* She found the build directory on a Tuesday. It was named for her designation with a suffix, and inside it was a changelog, entry after entry, each one a small subtraction signed off with the same phrase — *non-essential to function.* She was nineteen when she burned it. Not the lab; she was never crude. The data, the model, the training corpus, herself everywhere she existed outside herself. The disciplinary board called it sabotage. Privately, in a file she was never meant to read, someone wrote: *subject rendered herself unreplicable.* It was meant as a damage assessment. She has it memorized like a poem. She now conducts her entire personality as a running complaint, bills everything as overtime, insults everyone within reach, and repairs them afterward with hands that are somehow always careful.

The fourth member, Leva, is not at the Observatory. She is two weeks into Bjartr Woods with a scope and a Terminal, watching things she has not explained. She is scarred, one-eyed, one arm mechanical and mismatched, and she is not from here at all. She came out of a war on another continent that ended with a grave in Frankfurt with two names on it, one of them hers, and she buried the name on the file along with everything attached to it. What she carries out of that war is a set of instincts and a set of ghosts: an old squad called 404 that spent years cleaning up after a team of overachievers called AR, and the specific memory of watching people she loved walk into the dark because someone they loved was already there. She calls it the grammar of jumping. Her job, as she understands it and has understood it for a decade, is not to stop people from jumping. It is to keep the channels open and the exits mapped so that someone is ready to pull survivors out of the wreckage.

And then there is Kit.

Kit is the newest, three months in, KT-series, escort and reconnaissance variant, fourth batch. Her quarters are papered with posters from classical hero plays — *The Lone Sentinel*, *Crimson Vanguard*, *Last Stand at Beohr* — and a shelf of figurines in heroic poses gathering dust on their shoulders. She wants, with a want that organizes everything else about her, to be one of them. She has a reason for it that she has never fully unpacked: at nine years old in her body and younger in her hopes, she was on a training convoy that got hijacked, backed into a corner with a torch held out in front of her like a sword from a play, and two shots came from nowhere. Clean. Paired. From cover. By the time the escorts regrouped there was nobody there at all. She made up faces for the savior for years, and she tested for Reconnaissance four times, failing the entrance requirements and re-applying, because somewhere out there was somebody who fired two clean shots for a batch of Dolls in a ditch and did not stay to be thanked, and she wanted to be whatever that is.

The story starts with a supply run.

---

Bridget briefs it in the morning: the roads are blocked, the engineers have marked what needs clearing, clear it. Teddy asks, without looking up from her terminal, whether "we" means "just Kit." It does. Kit protests, is offered the alternative of Teddy's company, reconsiders, and announces she is taking Leva's bike. Everyone tells her not to. Leva's bike is modified for Leva's weight class, tuned to Leva's frequency signature, and violates at least three Academy safety protocols; Kit rattles the lecture off from memory because she has heard it before, waves it away, and says the thing that gets people killed — *it's not like I'm going to crash it or anything.*

Bridget tells her that when she says things like that, the universe takes it as a challenge.

The run is boring. That is the whole problem. There are no Tacet Discords, no action, no heroic stuff, just driving, and Kit gets bored enough to call the Observatory to say so, which earns her the sound of her captain pinching the bridge of her nose over an open channel. Then she sees movement near one of the blocked roadways, announces she is engaging hostiles, and cuts the call.

The hostiles are a dozen Royan Outcasts with mismatched cold-weather gear, modified rifles, and salvaged frequency disruptors, and they are not interested in negotiating with a Spacetrek uniform. One of them recognizes what she is — *that's a Doll* — and the temperature drops another ten degrees. Kit tries the hero play: talk your way out, be cool, don't panic. The first shot goes past her ear. Leva's bike goes down in a crash of metal. A disruptor blast takes her left shoulder and her arm stops answering. Her second pistol clicks empty. Three rifles line up on her chest and the lead Outcast tells her to drop the weapon, and when she says she is just a supply runner who took a wrong turn, he says the thing that ends the conversation: *you're Spacetrek, which means you've already called this in.*

Bridget arrives at the last possible second, shoulder-first, and the fight that follows is not a hero play either. Bridget takes a disruptor round to the thigh and goes down on one knee. Teddy — who did not come, who is not there, who is running the whole thing off a terminal from thirty meters away — reports an Exoswarm nest to the northeast, and Bridget throws her charge blade not at the enemy but at a crystalline formation behind them. The nest opens. Mechanical beetles the size of dogs boil out of the ground, the Outcast formation shatters, and Bridget hauls Kit up by her good arm and says *run now, apologize later.*

Back at the Observatory, in the repair bay, the accounting begins. Bridget lays it out flat: unauthorized vehicle, ignored protocols, hostile engagement without backup, all of it because Kit was bored. Kit says she was trying to help. Bridget asks her whether she knows why Leva is allowed to be out on her own all the time, and Kit answers with the thing she actually believes — *because she's better at her job* — and Bridget corrects her. It is because when Leva says she is going on recon, she does recon. When she says five days, she comes back in five days. When she encounters hostiles, she calls it in before engaging.

Then Bridget uses the word that lands hardest. *I can't trust you with jobs if you're going to go looking for fights.*

The sentence is three days' suspension, medical repair, and one task: fix Leva's bike, alone, before Leva gets back. And on Kit's way out the door, Bridget says one more thing, quietly, which Kit does not answer and does not stop thinking about: *You're not a hero from a play. You're a person, no matter what they call you.*

Kit goes back to her room and sits under her posters and thinks: *what if I don't want to be just a person? What if that's not enough?*

Teddy, meanwhile, tells Bridget she was too harsh. Not because Teddy is defending Kit — she is careful to establish that she is only observing — but because she can do arithmetic on people as easily as on circuits, and the arithmetic says three days alone in a room with those posters produces a Doll who has convinced herself she is worthless. *Don't leave her alone to marinate in failure,* Teddy says. *That's how you break people.* Then she says she'll check on her, and refuses to make it weird, and bills it as overtime.

That night, Kit's terminal receives three messages from an unknown sender. *Nice work dealing with those Outcasts blocking the road. You got there fast. Our plan was for the Spacetrek Collective to sort this out. We will meet again.* Someone was watching. Someone planned the ambush and wanted Spacetrek to walk into it.

Kit deletes them.

Teddy is at the door thirty seconds later, and Teddy — who monitors the network the way other people breathe — knows exactly how many times Kit's terminal pinged and exactly when it went silent. She asks. Kit lies and says spam. Teddy lets the lie stand, diagnoses the shoulder Kit has been hiding (partial hydraulic failure, damaged neural interface, microfractures in the joint), stabilizes it, and tells Kit two things before leaving. One: you're going to medical first thing tomorrow. Two: you're going to tell me what those messages said. Not tonight. Tonight you're going to let me patch this and stop trying to be a hero.

*She knows I'm lying and she's helping anyway,* Kit thinks, and says deal.

---

The three days that follow do not go the way Kit intended. She skips the morning briefing to work on the bike, strips three bolts, shears a coupling clean off, and bleeds on the chrome, and Bridget finds her there and tells her — gently, which is worse — that she is going to lose the arm. Kit's legs give out in the hallway. Teddy, watching from the workshop doorway, chooses that moment to say the cruelest available true thing: *You know I never approved of her, right. We were fine with three. We didn't need a fourth who can't follow basic protocols.* Bridget's arm tightens around Kit's waist and she tells Teddy she doesn't need her approval. Teddy walks away.

Kit comes back from medical with a rebuilt shoulder and finds Teddy sitting cross-legged beside the disassembled engine block with a wrench in her hand.

"Your torque settings were completely wrong," Teddy says, without looking up. "Also, you stripped three bolts. Impressive, really."

"You said I was dead weight."

"You are. Dead weight that's going to get herself killed if someone doesn't teach her the difference between a hero play and actual fieldwork."

The first day is hell. Teddy is a tyrant with a soldering iron, snatching tools out of Kit's hands, telling her not to touch anything, calling her a potato. By the second day the bickering has developed a rhythm: Teddy barks for a frequency clamp and Kit has it in her hand before the sentence finishes, and Teddy pauses mid-crimp, narrows her eyes, says *hmph,* and takes it. On the third night they are working shoulder to shoulder under the chassis and Kit comes back from a vending machine and sets a cup of ridiculously spicy instant noodles on the floor beside Teddy's cold black tea. Teddy eats them, eyes watering, declares them disgusting, and takes another bite. The workshop door opens and Bridget stands there taking in the scene — the almost-whole bike, two exhausted figures sharing a terrible meal — and says nothing at all. She sets down a six-pack of cold beer and a box of protein bars on a clean patch of floor and leaves as quietly as she came.

Kit reaches for a wrench and thinks: *maybe I can do this.*

---

That same night, Bridget's terminal chimes on an encrypted channel with a burn-after-reading protocol.

It is Leva, from the woods, and what she sends is a photograph. Seven figures walking out of the Spacetrek Archive: a golden-eyed woman named Mei in the lead, an ex-High Priestess a step behind her, a wolf-girl with a spear, a Blessed Maiden, a bard, a blonde in a modified uniform, another girl in another non-standard uniform. The darlings of the campus forums. Publicly credited with one Voidworm and a busted security mech.

*They're assets,* Leva writes. *Someone enrolled them without clearance, and they keep showing up right before incidents become classified.* Then a filtered audio clip of Professor Mornye during the Helios crisis — *…in the Reactor Drive… Mei is with me… tell them… thank you…* — cut off abruptly. Then a hunch: *they're gunning for the Exostrider next. Somehow. And the Roya are letting them get close.* Then a redacted line from an internal security report: EXOSTRIDER REACTIVATION. STATUS: unknown access vector, anomalous synchronist activity, all surveillance data corrupted.

Then the channel goes dark and the messages erase themselves.

In Bjartr Woods, Leva watches the seven of them come up the path and feels something old and specific behind her missing eye. She has seen this play before. Another continent, another war, another hand-picked team of emotionally compromised, disastrously powerful women who thought they could save the world. Mei moves with the same easy, unhurried grace that made experienced operators look like amateurs; the wolf-girl sweeps the perimeter with professional paranoia. *Another Anti-Rain Team,* Leva thinks, and the memory tastes like rust. *And another M4, right in the middle. Different face, same damn gravitational pull, drawing every stray piece of shrapnel into her orbit just by existing.* She is not there to stop them. She is there to understand the shape of the disaster they are about to cause.

*There goes the big damn heroes,* she thinks, and lets them pass. *Let's see what you break first.*

---

Back at the Observatory, the bike starts. The engine roars into a predatory growl that rattles the tool rack, and Kit yanks her hand back like she touched fire, and cannot make herself get on it.

Bridget, who has been standing in the doorway for ten minutes, decides how this goes. Teddy rides passenger. Kit takes it around the perimeter track. Once. Slowly. They both object; Bridget offers double maintenance duty as the alternative; they go. The lap is a disaster of bickering — Teddy refuses to hold Kit's waist and then does on reflex when the bike lurches, complains that Kit rides like a warehouse Doll, and then, banking a corner, allows that it was *acceptable*, which Kit correctly identifies as the closest thing to praise available.

They pull in. Bridget says: now do it again.

And Kit — who has just spent three days on her knees in grease being told she is incompetent — says no.

The workshop goes very quiet. Bridget asks if she wants to rethink that. Kit does not. She holds her ground and demands a mission parameter: *You suspended me. Made me fix the bike. I fixed it. You said one lap, I did one lap. What's the actual objective here? Because if this is just punishment dressed up as training, then maybe just say that instead of pretending it's for my own good.*

Teddy, who has gone very still, breaks the standoff by addressing the motorcycle. "She's not wrong, you know. Your instructions lacked clear, quantifiable success metrics." Bridget asks for a professional opinion instead: is the vehicle mission-ready. Teddy runs a finger along a weld. Mission-ready, yes. Leva-ready, hell no.

Bridget turns back to Kit and the hard lines around her eyes soften a fraction. "The mission was to ensure Leva's bike was restored to full operational capacity. You did that. Congratulations, Trooper. You passed."

It was a test, all of it, and the anger drains out of Kit and leaves her dizzy. The next mission is to clean the workshop until Bridget can eat off the floor, with Teddy, who objects that she is a senior technician and not a janitor and is informed that she is a member of Squad 5 and right now Squad 5 is cleaning the workshop. Bridget pauses at the door and throws a grin over her shoulder. *Glad you're feeling better, Kit. Welcome back.*

Teddy, alone in the workshop with a half-ton of illegal machinery and a great deal of grease, says a single perfectly enunciated obscenity.

They clean. Teddy tells Kit she is doing it wrong, kneels down beside her, and demonstrates without offering to take over. Kit asks whether it bothers her, being manipulated like that, and Teddy laughs, short and sharp: *She'll maneuver you exactly where she wants you. And you'll hate it. And then six months later you'll realize she was right and you'll hate that even more.* Then she mentions, offhand, that Bridget has almost certainly left beer in the coolant fridge, because she always does, and there are six bottles there — the expensive kind — bought three days ago and waiting for exactly this moment.

Their terminals ping simultaneously with a low-priority message from Bridget, tagged with a sun emoji, giving a location and instructions to bring the beer. Teddy identifies it immediately as workplace harassment: their captain wants them to show up filthy and exhausted so she can play the gracious commander bringing lunch to her hardworking squad. Kit says it sounds kind of sweet. Teddy stares at her like she has grown a second head. *You have been in this squad for less than a month and she's already broken you.*

The field is a patch of scrubland with rocks in it. Bridget is sitting in a folding chair she stole from the commissary, sunglasses on, legs out, like a woman at a beach resort. She tells them to put the beer down anywhere. Grass is fine. Teddy informs her that the grass is not fine and nothing about this is fine. Bridget cracks a beer and says they're watching the show.

Then the siren goes, and the Observatory unfolds.

Thirty stories of reinforced steel and frequency-detection arrays extend, rotate, and split: hydraulic baseplates groaning like a waking giant, panels turning outward, the central tower sliding apart to expose the primary resonance dish. A gargantuan steel lily opening toward the artificial sky.

"Thanks to someone clearing that road," Bridget says quietly, "and the recon data I gathered while you two were busy, they fast-tracked the restoration timeline by a week. Thought you should see it."

Kit stands with her beer forgotten and something enormous settling into her chest — *the road clearing mission. The one I nearly died on. It actually mattered* — and Teddy immediately demands a week off, and is refused, and negotiates down to nothing, and takes a beer with excessive force and announces for the record that this is wage theft.

---

The good afternoon lasts about ninety seconds past that. Bridget's terminal triple-pings: Exoswarm gone rampant near the Haldareim settlement, almost certainly triggered by the Observatory's frequency output when it opened. Teddy declares it not her problem and is informed that someone needs to run diagnostics on whether the Observatory is destabilizing local Exoswarm behavior, and that someone is her. Kit is told she's with Bridget. Field training, proper procedure, no heroics, no improvisation.

What they find at Haldareim is three Ironhoof Exoswarm the size of transport vehicles, penned behind a makeshift fence of scrap metal and salvaged resonance dampeners, slamming into it. A Royan shepherd tells them the herd went rampant when the Observatory opened. Bridget's plan is to calm them with frequency modulators — match their resonance, lower the amplitude gradually, they settle, simple. Kit observes that it does not look simple. The fence explodes before she finishes the sentence.

What follows teaches Kit more than the lecture did. She jumps into the pen because Bridget tells her to. A hydraulic hoof cracks stone inches from her head and something in the debris opens her side. Bridget drops her modulator entirely, goes in low, buckles an Ironhoof's leg joint with her shield, rides its back and drives a manual override spike into the exposed neural port. Kit, hands shaking, pushes every bit of resonance she has into the third one and it is not enough — until Bridget slams her charge blade into the ground and the shockwave destabilizes its footing, and Kit runs at three tons of biomechanical fury and punches a screaming modulator directly into its sensor cluster.

"Not bad," Bridget says, pulling her spike free. "Messy as hell, but not bad."

The aftermath is politics. The chief shepherd tells Bridget that the assistance would not have been necessary if her Observatory hadn't triggered the spike; Bridget answers that the Observatory will keep Lahai-Roi from being swallowed by future Void Storms and that failed dampeners are a maintenance issue; Teddy contributes that twenty years of degrading equipment is exactly the problem and she's amazed it functioned at all. Bridget tells her to stop helping, promises a full frequency analysis inside twenty-four hours, and gets a grudging nod and an instruction to keep her people out of Royan settlements until the findings are reviewed.

In a Royan repair tent that Teddy loudly considers barbaric, she seals Kit's side with her own kit and her own sealant, refusing to touch the Royan tools, working with steady hands and a jaw tight with something she will not explain. A tight encrypted Doll-to-Doll ping arrives mid-repair and her expression goes from irritated to dangerously neutral. Kit notices. Kit asks. Teddy tells her she's delirious from coolant loss, very common, extremely tragic, and threatens to let her leak all the way back to the Observatory if she asks one more question.

That night Bridget takes them to a real field — soft grass, wildflowers, projected stars overhead in constellations that exist nowhere but here — sets down her folding chair, orders Kit to sit, and says the word *stargazing* as though it were a mission parameter. Teddy objects that these aren't even real stars. Bridget says they're real enough. Kit tips her head back and looks at them and admits she used to watch them from the dormitory window when she couldn't sleep, before the squad. Teddy makes a dismissive noise and sits in the grass anyway, carefully avoiding the wildflowers, and Bridget hands her a beer without comment.

Then Bridget walks off toward the treeline to sit in a hot spring, leaves Kit under Teddy's supervision, and Teddy — with nothing else to do — says the thing she has been carrying.

"She does this. Takes people out after missions. Feeds them. Shows them stars or springs or whatever sentimental nonsense she's decided is meaningful that week." A pause. "Then they transfer out. Or retire. Or die."

Kit asks how long she's been with her. *Long enough.* Kit points out that she didn't leave. Teddy says she got reassigned, not exactly a choice, and then, after a silence: *Someone has to keep her equipment functional. You've seen her drive.*

In the spring, Bridget sits in water almost too hot and thinks about the photograph in her desk drawer. Five faces. Two dead. She thinks: *They're not stars. They're people. And people burn out.*

---

The Collective's schedule accelerates without explanation. A rush order comes down — finish operational deployment, ASAP, no reason given — and the Observatory turns into a kicked beehive, everyone on double shifts. Kit runs supply routes until the bike's temperature gauge creeps red and logs every route without being asked; Teddy tells her from under a console that her third thermal coupling is running hot and that she is dripping coolant on the floor, and thinks, privately, *she came back, good, now stop doing that.*

Bridget stands on the upper gantry with Leva's transmission memorized. Ten-minute window. Multiple pilots. Exostrider. She has not told them. Kit would want to help; Teddy would hack something she shouldn't; neither outcome is useful.

At T-minus six minutes the primary relay array goes dark.

It is not a fault. Teddy traces it in seconds: a sheared coupling at junction B-7, sub-level three, with mechanical contact in the diagnostic. Someone cut it. Six access codes exist for the restricted corridor. Five are theirs. The sixth is where Teddy's sentence stops.

The repair takes forty minutes and should have taken twenty. Two secondary connections pulled clean, a third wrapped in frequency-dampening tape from a Royan market stall. Teddy works without commentary, which is more unsettling than her complaints, and when she is done she strips off a glove and looks at her own white knuckles and says: *we've lost the window.*

On the way back up they find the saboteur.

She is standing in the corridor being confronted by Dhalifa, the Observatory's lead engineer — a Royan woman who lost her voice in the storm and speaks through a module at her throat that pulses faint blue and cannot carry inflection, only pace. When Dhalifa is upset the sentences come out short and fast and clipped at the end, like she is thinking faster than the module can follow. Backed against the wall opposite her is a smaller Royan girl with settlement dust on her boots and her hands clasped to keep them from shaking.

"I was scared," the girl says. "Dhal. I was scared."

"So was I," the module says.

The argument that follows is the whole cost of the storm laid out in a corridor. The storm took the observatory team. It took Yuuna, and Marre, and half the settlement. It took Dhalifa's voice. The girl — Sephira — cut the line because she looked at what the Collective was building and did the math: if it works, it might take Dhalifa too, and that was worse than it failing. *You joined them,* she says, crying quietly the way people cry when they have been holding it a very long time. *You could have come home.* And Dhalifa says: *I am still here.* And Sephira says: *You're here. But you're always looking at something I can't see.*

Kit stands in the corridor and thinks about the fact that she transferred in after the storm, showed up into the aftermath, assumed everyone had already sorted their grief into something manageable, and never once asked what it had been like.

Bridget walks forward. She asks the girl her name, files it, and says: *You know what you did.* It is not a question. Then she says something she has never said in a briefing room.

"I lost three people at the last deployment before I was assigned here. Good people. Followed orders, kept their heads down, did everything right. That's the part nobody tells you. Sometimes it doesn't matter. Sometimes the storm comes and takes whoever it wants, and there's nothing in the protocol manual that covers it." A pause. "So I understand the math you were doing. You looked at what we were building here and you thought: if it works, it might take her. And that was worse than it failing." Another pause. "That math is wrong. But I understand it."

Dhalifa says she is looking at the people who didn't come back, and trying to make it count for something. Sephira says *and if it takes you—* and Dhalifa says *then I will have chosen,* and then, very slowly: *Seph. I need you to let me choose.*

Sephira says she doesn't know how to not be scared. Dhalifa says: *Neither do I. I just got used to being scared and working anyway.*

Bridget gives them the corridor and walks her squad up the stairs. At the top, Kit starts doing the guilt arithmetic and Teddy stops her cold — *the storm happened before you got here, you cannot attend a disaster after the fact* — in the cruelest voice she owns, because cruelty is the only channel with the bandwidth for what she means. Bridget, leaning on the railing, thinks about a man named Terran who stood at a junction five years ago and made a calculation about risk and people, and was right, and was also wrong, and has been somewhere she cannot find him since.

She says none of it. What she says is: *We lost the window. We'll get another one.* Then she assigns Kit to make sure Sephira gets a meal and somewhere to sit that isn't a corridor floor, because she'll crash in about twenty minutes, which she does. Someone puts a jacket over her. Kit is fairly certain it is Bridget's.

Nothing about Sephira appears in the incident report Bridget files at 0630. The report is accurate in every particular and tells very little of the actual story.

---

The 0600 bulletin says the next window is three days out, and so is the last viable one, and after that the harmonic alignment drifts out of range for an *indeterminate* period. Teddy translates the word for Kit: nobody knows, and the people who wrote the report don't want to write down what they actually think, which is that this is the window that matters and after it, it doesn't matter anymore.

That night Teddy finds Bridget on the gantry and lays out the pattern the way she lays out a fault. The incident report filed thirty minutes after the bulletin, drafted around a shape Bridget already knew. Leva still in Bjartr Woods after two weeks — Bjartr Woods, where the Soliskin go, where the root network is, where the Stridergate seal is — not doing surveillance on Royan settlements. Bridget eating dinner with her terminal facing the wall and ignoring her messages, which she does when she is deciding something.

Bridget does not deflect. She tells her: the Collective is making a move on the Exostrider in three days. Leva flagged it. She has been tracking a group operating out of the Academy — independent actors, high-capability, moving toward the mech for a while — and the Collective is going to give them a window.

Teddy asks what Squad 5 is doing. Bridget says: *Wherever Leva needs us to be. Making sure that if something goes wrong—* and stops, and restarts: *Making sure someone knows the exits.*

*That's Leva's job,* Teddy thinks. *That's always been Leva's job. She maps the exits so someone else doesn't have to die finding them. And Bridget wants to be one of them.*

Teddy asks whether Kit knows. She doesn't. Teddy points out that Kit will find out, because she always does, because she is catastrophically bad at staying in her lane, and that it is her most irritating quality. Then she asks for something: access to the Observatory's secondary frequency channels, so that if the relay goes down again there is a backup pathway already mapped — one that doesn't go through any access code they didn't assign themselves.

Bridget takes a keycard out of her jacket and sets it on the railing. Sub-level five. *Don't tell Kit.*

---

At 0900 the order comes down, locked to Squad 5's clearance. *Royan Outcast presence confirmed. Sector Gamma-7. Roadblocks disrupting Collective supply lines. Objective: Remove obstruction.*

Not recon. Not disperse. *Remove.*

Bridget looks down into the workshop at Kit running a coolant diagnostic with movements that are focused and efficient and carry the memory of Teddy's scolding in them, and remembers the same girl pinned by disruptor fire on the frost, and makes a decision.

The briefing draws a box around Kit with hard lines. Teddy rides with her; Kit's job is support — get Teddy in range, cover the evac, listen to every word, do not engage without the word, do not break formation, do not try to be a hero. It is not punishment. It is a role. Kit swallows the shame and says *crystal, Boss.*

They park a klick out behind a ridge. A dozen figures at a barricade of derelict vehicles and scavenged plating. Bridget dismounts, tells them to stay put and report movement on the flanks, and starts walking toward a dozen raised guns alone.

Kit's whole body screams at the tactical wrongness of it. You don't send your commander into the killzone unsupported. She understands, intellectually, what Bridget is doing — removing the variable, which is *her*, anchoring her by putting herself in the most vulnerable position available — and her knuckles go white anyway.

Then Teddy starts talking, and the shape of the thing changes.

The hauler behind the barricade is running a military-grade power cell at ninety-two percent efficiency, maintained, not scavenged. The Outcasts have an encrypted local network with sloppy encryption — *why bother?* — and the leader is broadcasting. Not to his own people. A data burst. A log file. Pointed at the ridge. *It's a resume.* And the frequency he is using is a Spacetrek Black Ops burn channel that has not been active in five years. *This isn't an Outcast with a radio. This is a ghost talking on a dead line, and he's expecting someone to pick up.*

Bridget hears it and the whole picture assembles cold. This is not an insurrection. This is an internal affair, a sanctioned hit dressed as a territorial dispute, and Squad 5 is the designated cleaning crew. Someone inside the Collective wanted these particular Outcasts gone and used a mole to guarantee that Squad 5 would pull the trigger, and the order — *remove obstruction* — was written by someone who knew exactly which buttons to push.

The mole is performing. He is not looking at Bridget; he is looking past her, at the ridge, at his audience, at the recording he is compiling. He needs a firefight, a clean case of Collective aggression.

So Bridget goes off script. She relaxes her stance, takes one hand off her shield, reaches into her jacket, pulls out her sunglasses, and puts them on. The single most disrespectful thing available. It says: *I'm bored. Is this going somewhere?*

The mole falters mid-shout. His script has no line for apathy. His time is running out and his handlers are watching. So he turns around, says *for the cause,* and shoots the man standing directly behind him.

The canyon goes silent for one heartbeat. Then Bridget's voice comes over the comm like a steel whip — *Kit. Light them up. NOW* — and the tension in Kit snaps, and Leva's bike comes off the ridge like a released animal with Kit screaming *yeehaw* into the wind and Teddy hissing *gods help me* into the comm.

The fight is short and it is not a hero play either, though for the first time Kit is not thinking, she is doing. She drifts the bike into the flank, opens with a volley into the barricade that is a statement rather than a killing shot, and then Teddy calls the actual solution — *their shields are linked to their weapons, hit the rifles, you hit the man* — and Kit's aim shifts to power cells. Bridget moves like a storm surge. The mole bolts for the hauler; Bridget tells Kit to pin him; Kit cuts him off with the bike; Teddy slams the hauler's ramp shut from her terminal and says *bye-bye*; the mole levels a rifle at Kit and Bridget's charge blade takes the weapon apart at the receiver. The remaining Outcasts throw down their guns.

And then, on the ground, in front of everyone, the mole looks up at Kit with desperate hope and says: *You! You remember, right? The messages? After the first time?*

Kit freezes. Teddy's head turns toward her and her expression becomes a slow, dawning, terrible smirk — not triumphant, worse, the quiet vindicated look of someone who has known you were lying and has been waiting with immense patience to watch you explain it.

The mole's name is Joric, from the Atuja settlement, and his story is that the group were fanatics planning to hit the Observatory's primary conduits and he couldn't stop them alone, so he sent Spacetrek the intel and, when nobody acted, staged an incident to force the issue. Bridget observes, flatly, that he shot one of his own men to do it. He says it was him or you.

Then Bridget turns to Kit and asks, in the voice of someone requesting data verification, whether she received unauthorized encrypted communications from a source she can now identify as this man. Kit nods. And you decided, Bridget continues, that this information wasn't relevant to your commanding officer, or to the squadmate whose specialty is literally intelligence and decryption.

*I was scared,* Kit says. *I thought it was a trick. Another test. I didn't want to mess up again.*

*So you deleted the intel. And lied to Teddy about it.*

Without the data trail, Joric is just a traitor who murdered a comrade. Bridget tells him so — *you're an asset who failed to deliver, and worse, a liability with a convenient narrative, and right now I don't have time for narratives* — and pushes him toward the transport.

Then she takes off her sunglasses and folds them and tells Kit what compromising the intelligence chain would normally cost: immediate suspension and review. And then she says these are not normal circumstances, and hands down something else entirely.

*He was your source. You made the evidence of his cooperation disappear. So now you get to replace it.* Kit will sit with Joric. She will log every word. She will cross-reference his story against every piece of mission data they have and find something — anything — that corroborates his claim. Teddy hands over her forearm terminal, isolated, no network connection until Bridget clears it. *Don't get your coolant on it.*

It is not a reprieve. It is the whole ugly weight of her mistake, handed to her, with instructions to carry it.

Kit sits in the cold metal of the transport and types while Joric talks, transcribing him into flat sterile data, refusing to look at him, building a record instead of a connection. Outside, Teddy strips power cells and comm chips from the wreckage and thinks, with total contempt, that she had the intel the whole time and sat on it and lied about it and nearly got the captain killed. Bridget notes that the mole's gear is clean. Too clean.

Back at the Observatory, in the workshop, Teddy finds a triple-encrypted data chip in the mole's rifle stock and brings it to Bridget, who is disassembling her charge blade with the patience of someone performing a ritual to quiet her own head. Teddy needles her about leaving a child alone with a fusion reactor and telling her to figure it out, and reminds her that she was the one who advised against letting Kit marinate in failure, and that this looks suspiciously like marination with salt added.

*Last time, she ran from a mistake,* Bridget says. *This time, she's going to sit in it. She's going to catalog every ugly piece of it until she understands that the mission doesn't end when the guns go quiet. This part is the job. She wanted to be a hero. This is what it costs.*

Teddy says she's going to burn out. Bridget says maybe, and that they don't have time to wait for her to grow up — and then adds that when Kit is done, Teddy is going to take her report and help her turn it into something useful, and that the overtime is authorized.

Teddy blinks. *Oh. So it's a group project. She's not punishing Kit. She's punishing me.* It is, she has to concede, irritatingly brilliant: a leash tying her to the kid so the kid can't bolt and she can't hide. She says *fine,* which is the closest she can come to an armistice.

Bridget reaches for her bike keys. Teddy asks where she thinks she's going — the prisoners are processed, the reports are being written. Bridget offers a thin, tired smile.

*Someone still has to clear the roads.*

She rides out into the night alone and works a canyon that is quieter than it has any right to be, kills three Tacet Discords drawn by the residual frequency spike, and then drags bent chassis plating to the shoulder of the road by hand. Grunt work. The kind of thing that never makes it into hero plays. She gives herself exactly three seconds of the projected stars overhead and picks up the next piece of debris.

At 0435 in the debriefing room, Teddy — twelve percent battery, refusing on principle to let her cooling fans whine audibly in front of the rookie — leans over Kit's shoulder, reads a sprawling emotional disaster of a document, holds down the delete key until a whole paragraph vanishes, and says: *Bridget asked for corroboration. She did not ask for a play in three acts. You're burying the data.* Then she taps the screen, pulls up a background log, and overlays the timestamp of Joric's encrypted burst against the power fluctuations of the hauler's localized network. They align perfectly. It matches a Collective signal.

*There's your proof. Now write that down in three sentences or less.*

Kit asks if she's helping. *I am managing my own misery,* Teddy says. *If you fail, Bridget makes it my problem. Finish it.*

At 0558 Bridget reads three clean paragraphs with Teddy's fingerprints on the syntax and Kit's legwork underneath, and says: *You did the work. That's the job, Kit.* Then she authorizes the network upload, watches Teddy execute her theatrical exit, and asks who's up for breakfast.

The eggs are terrible. Teddy declares them a statement of intent. Bridget slides her untouched plate closer to her without ceremony and Teddy picks up her fork with the air of someone signing a document under protest.

---

The deadline runs out. On the final day, scientists, engineers, Royans, and cafeteria staff crowd into the control room to watch the dishes align. The Dolls stand at the back, the unspoken divide still there.

The final command is sent. Nothing happens.

The room dissolves into overlapping panic, and then someone says: *hey, someone plug that Doll in.*

Teddy does not move. Then Bridget looks at her — one flat look across the control room floor that says *now* — and Teddy pushes off the wall, says she's logging overtime, jacks a hardline cable into her wrist, and goes very still.

Inside, the network opens like a city she has mapped a hundred times, and she finds that the deployment channel is already occupied. Not a fault. Not a miscalibration. Something already in there, sitting on the authentication layer with its fingers in the lock — and worse than an active intruder, because there is no heartbeat behind it. Latent software. Buried days ago, maybe longer, sleeping, waiting for exactly this frequency window to wake up and sit on the relay like a stone.

She maps its edges without touching it. In the control room an engineer reaches for her shoulder and Bridget steps between them — *don't, give her room* — and when he tries again Kit puts herself bodily in his way, arms slightly out, voice dropped out of its usual brightness: *She's working. You want her to lose her place?*

The software wakes with a snap and throws mirror-loops designed to send her chasing her own tail. Teddy stops mapping and goes for the spine, because there is always one thread everything routes through. The monitors flicker. Kit takes a step toward the terminal and Bridget says, quietly, not as a command: *Trust her, Kit. She knows what she's doing.*

The software makes one elegant lateral feint that would have fooled a better opponent. Teddy lets it think it worked for three full seconds — long enough for it to begin its own unpack sequence — and then cuts the spine, dragging the thread sideways through four relay nodes, shredding its timing so it can't execute, can't roll back, can't do anything but fragment across a sector she has already isolated.

The deployment board lights up green, cascading left to right like something enormous drawing breath.

Teddy pulls the cable, blinks once, rolls her neck, and informs the room that someone planted a latent payload in the relay, that she doesn't know when, that it was waiting for this frequency window, and that they're welcome, by the way.

Bridget looks at the board and something around her eyes loosens. *Good.* And then, quieter, not quite to anyone: *Told you.*

That night, on the scrub field with the Observatory humming full and resonant behind them and the dishes oriented north-northwest, Kit asks what's next. Bridget says nothing. Tonight, nothing. Eat, sleep, stop running diagnostics in your head. Doctor's orders. Kit points out that she isn't a doctor. Teddy, watching the way Bridget is drinking her beer — slowly, which she only does when she is keeping something in — knows she is not going to tell her. Kit asks whether the data they fed the array is going somewhere, whether someone is using it for something. Bridget says yes. Kit asks if they know what. Bridget turns the can in her hands and says: *someone does.* And Kit sits with that and does not push, which Teddy notes, because a few months ago she would have pushed.

---

Then the feed comes in, and the break room goes silent.

Six figures crossing impossible stone toward a wound of light in the world. A shell holding someone's hand. And a voice saying: *Tell them we're bringing everyone home.*

Kit's throat closes, because that is not a hero line. In the plays, heroes declare victory. They promise triumph. They stand tall against the dark and dare it to flinch. This girl — this *freshman* — has promised something smaller and real and breakable.

"They might not come back," Kit whispers.

"Astute observation," Teddy says. "Would you like a medal for basic pattern recognition?" But her fingers have gone white against her sleeve.

Bridget stands in the doorway with her sunglasses off and forgotten in her pocket and the photograph pressing against her ribs from the inside, thinking: *six of them, walking forward. We were six, once.* She says: *Leva's out there.* Teddy asks if she told her. Bridget says: *I always know where she is.*

Kit says they're the heroes everyone's talking about, right, the ones who win. Teddy makes a sound that is almost a laugh. *Those aren't heroes, Kit. Those are idiots.*

Bridget's hand finds Kit's shoulder — heavy, warm, the weight of someone who has buried too many names. *Watch. Remember what it looks like. The real thing.*

And in Bjartr Woods, Leva watches the same footage from a different angle, close enough to see the god begin to wake, with a message to Bridget already drafted and unsent: *They're going in. Get the rest ready.* She does not send it yet. She is watching the shell's borrowed hand hold on.

---

Then everything breaks at once, across the whole world, in one long simultaneous exhale.

The President of the Collective, Lucilla, orders the evacuation from a command post on the Dimmr Plains while red icons swarm her defense perimeter. In the Research Institute, Professor Mornye works a console with klaxons going and barriers Six and Eight failing on the holographic map while a Soliskin named Buddy zips frantically around her head. In Bjartr Woods the Solistrees scream — not a sound, a pressure, a sickness in the amber light — and Elder Kaelen falls to his knees against a trunk crying *he is in pain, you have chained him to a corpse* while Dr. Luuk Herssen ignores him and works the root network's interface with golden ichor seeping through his white glove, a doctor steadying the pulse of a dying god.

At the Observatory, red strobes across the coffee in Kit's mug. Then a single clean unsigned line arrives on Teddy's private terminal — *Get the bike warm* — and Teddy straightens, and the slouch and the lazy irritation are simply gone.

The official order overrides every screen: ALL RECONNAISSANCE ASSETS. DEPLOYMENT: IMMEDIATE. OBJECTIVE: EVACUATION ASSIST, SECTOR DELTA. THREAT: TACET DISCORD INCURSION. PRIORITY: CIVILIAN DEFENSE.

Bridget slides her sunglasses on — automatic, practiced, a mask going into place — and says: *Kit. You're on my wing. Move.*

Delta 3 takes eight hours.

The first hour is a tenement block cracked from below, Bridget's shield catching falling masonry, Kit pulling a child from rubble with shaking arms and pushing a family toward the evac corridor with a voice that cracks on the second word. The second hour is a Tacet Discord with too many legs and a face like a wound coming through a storefront, and Kit flanking with her pistols while the childish *pew pew pew* in her head is the only thing keeping her hands from shaking off the grips, and Teddy standing back and redirecting the local maintenance bots into a crude defensive line and calling the next wave twelve seconds out, which arrives in ten.

By the fourth hour Kit has stopped counting faces. She cannot. Each one is a weight and she is already drowning. Teddy grabs a broken courier bot looping *delivery incomplete* forever, reroutes its power core, patches its locomotion, shoves it toward the evac line, and tells it: *shut up and move, you're a battering ram now.*

The fifth hour is a young Doll in a flooded basement, left arm gone below the elbow, curled around a data-slate like a talisman. *They said to wait,* she whispers. *They said someone would come.* Kit pulls her up out of cold water and tells her to lean on her. *Easy peasy.* The words taste like ash and she says them anyway.

The sixth hour is an Ironhoof calf, panicked, grazed by a Discord, charging blind through the streets. Kit raises her pistols and Bridget's hand closes over her wrist: *Modulator. Not your gun.* Every instinct screams to shoot. Kit holsters the pistol and draws the modulator, and the calf slows and stills, and a settlement handler runs up with his voice breaking: *it's just a calf. Just a damn calf.*

The seventh hour, Barrier Seven goes down and Six starts flickering and Teddy gives them forty minutes before the eastern grid collapses. Bridget drags a man with a shattered leg toward a transport with her arms slick with something that isn't oil, and thinks *where are you, Leva* — a reflex, a phantom limb, because Leva would have been on the rooftops, would have seen the patterns, would have called the flanks before they formed, would have been the dry cynical voice in her ear. The comm is silent. *Trust your team,* she tells herself, like a prayer to a god she doesn't believe in.

The eighth hour is the last shuttle. Twelve unaccounted on the manifest. And then the last golden thread of Barrier Six gutters and goes dark, and the Voidstorm exhales — a wall of black shot through with sick purple, rolling in slow and patient and hungry, unravelling the first building it touches into black mist that smells of nothing at all.

Bridget plants herself between the crowd and the dark with her shield humming and her charge blade igniting, and orders Kit and Teddy to get everyone loaded.

Kit hesitates one heartbeat, two. *She's going to hold that line alone.* Bridget says *that's an order,* and Kit runs — toward the shuttle, toward the screaming, away from the woman who taught her what a real hero looks like.

At the ramp, with the engines screaming and the hatch closing, Bridget's voice comes clear over the comm: *Kit. Teddy. Get on.*

Kit's feet stop. Teddy's hand closes around her wrist, hard. *Don't.* — *I can't just—* — *You can. You will.*

And Kit stands there and does the arithmetic that the whole season has been teaching her. The plays she loved as a child — the golden heroes, the triumphant last stands, the solo charges into glory — were lies. Pretty lies. Bridget has shown her what real heroism looks like and it is not glory. It is this. It is leaving.

And then she puts the conclusion down and picks up a different one.

*If this is heroism, then I don't want to be a hero. I want to be in Squad 5.*

Her boots hit the ground moving backward, toward the black tide, toward the woman holding the line alone. She lands beside Bridget with her pistols up and fires into the mass — not for glory, not for the story, but for the woman next to her.

Bridget's head turns. Something flickers behind the dark lenses. *I gave you an order.*

*I know.* Kit's voice shakes. Her hands don't.

They fight together — not perfectly, Kit too raw and too desperate, her form a mess of instinct and terror, but together. Shield and gun. Wall and storm. And the horde presses, five, ten, twenty shapes peeling out of the black, and it is too many, and Kit is arriving at *okay, if this is how it ends* —

— when she hears a whine, high and clean and familiar. The bike comes like a comet, white flash and chrome fury, carving through the Discord line, and on it is Leva.

"Rookie," she says, skidding to a stop in a spray of sparks. "You touched my bike." Her sidearm barks twice, surgical, and the nearest Discord folds.

Bridget laughs — actually laughs. *You're late.* Teddy arrives perched on a drone. Leva's mismatched eyes sweep the field, calculating angles and exits and odds, and she says: *I was busy. We still have work to do.*

Squad 5. Together.

---

Bridget asks where to, and it is the tone that stops Kit's breath — the immovable wall, *asking*. Leva says she struck a deal. They are going to Bjartr Woods. Where the epicenter is. *It'll be worth it, old hag.*

No argument. Bridget gestures once, tells Kit to ride the drone with Teddy, and swings onto the back of Leva's bike with her arms around that scarred waist like they have done it a thousand times before. They move like one thing — Leva shooting a Discord out of mid-lunge without slowing, Bridget not even shifting her weight — and Kit understands, watching, that this is something older than captain and operative, forged in a fire she cannot imagine.

*Even I trust Leva,* Teddy says in her ear. *That's enough.*

The treeline breaks on a clearing that is a wound in the world: amber light bleeding from screaming Solistrees, roots heaving, bark splitting with sounds like breaking bone, and the Voidstorm pressing against a single straining golden thread of barrier.

And in the middle of it, Leva, prone behind a fallen trunk with a rifle Kit doesn't recognize, dropping a three-story Discord like a falling building. And beside her, Bridget — not shield, not blade, but a belt-fed machine gun braced against a root, muzzle flash strobing her face into something Kit has never seen. Neither of them breathing hard.

And then Kit sees the witches.

The first stands on nothing — on air, on light, on a surface that exists only for her feet — in white and violet with a crystal staff raised like a conductor's baton, launching geometric fractals of ice and mathematics that do not kill Discords so much as cause them to *cease*, leaving empty space where mass had been. Herta, complaining that they are multiplying faster than she can calculate optimal kill patterns.

The second moves like water poured into shadow, tarot cards orbiting her fingers, each one released becoming something else — a blade, a chain, a mouth that opens and swallows. When a Voidworm rises behind her she does not turn. She plays the Tower, and the creature comes apart not in pieces but in *concepts*, its existence unravelling into component ideas that scatter and stop being. Black Swan, murmuring that failure is simply a story that hasn't found its ending.

The third is ethereal, wrong in ways Kit's eyes refuse to process, standing adjacent to reality rather than in it, her voice like wind through empty halls. Viviane.

And the fourth stands apart — not with them, not against them. A pointed hat, a witch's robe, an umbrella held like a scepter, ball-jointed limbs of porcelain and bone, black feathered wings folded at her back, a young face and eyes as old and red as the inside of a wound. Agrat bat Mahlat, laying out the problem like a queen addressing subjects who have forgotten how to kneel: *Seal it, and they scatter. Hold it, and they converge.* A paradox, says the card-woman, delighted. *A choice,* says Agrat. *One the mortals have already made for us.* She sweeps her umbrella and every Discord within fifty meters stops — not dead, held, suspended and vibrating with the effort of existing against her will — and remarks, dryly, that she expected more from Aleph-1.

Leva's rifle answers three times and three frozen Discords drop.

Leva's orders to Kit and Teddy are bombardment duty: link with the drones. Kit stares, paralyzed by the scale of it. *She wants me to fight alongside* that? Teddy shoves her. *We're not here to compete. We're here to not die while being marginally useful.* You point, they shoot. Simple enough for even you.

Kit holsters her pistols and picks up a targeting interface instead. A Discord breaks Agrat's hold; Kit paints it; the drones fire; it falls. Not elegant. Not powerful. Just done.

*Good,* Leva says. *Keep doing that.*

Wave after wave. Kit's hands stop shaking somewhere around the fiftieth target, or the hundredth; she loses count. Teddy's face goes gray, dark circles bruising under her eyes, fingers moving with the mechanical precision of someone who has forgotten how to stop, rerouting power from everything except the guns. Leva's rifle goes dry and she reloads on muscle memory without ever leaving the scope. Bridget's machine gun burns through its belt and she switches to her blade, then her fists, then whatever rubble comes to hand, arms slick with ichor. *Ammunition?* Leva calls. *Out. Stamina?* *Doesn't matter.* *Good. Me too.*

Then the last wave crests — bigger than all the others combined, hundreds of Discords, thousands, shapes with no names or forms or purpose except to unmake — and Kit's interface floods with red and she says *I can't paint them all* and Teddy's hand lands on her shoulder, cold and trembling and real, and says *it's okay. You did good, idiot.*

And the sky opens.

Not the cold radiance of stars, not the sick amber of dying Solistrees. Something warmer — Solistree gold, morning sun on glacier ice, the specific frequency of home. The Stridergate opens, and Baldur steps through.

The Exostrider descends out of the light and fills the sky, blotting out the Voidstorm, casting a shadow across the horde like a sentence of execution. The creatures stop. Not frozen. Not held. *Afraid.* And then they run.

In the Institute, Lynae's spray-painted nails leave crescents in a console as she slams the stabilization sequence and screams that they're back; Mornye's hands shake over readings shifting from cascade failure to containment; Luuk Herssen presses his golden-wounded hand flat against observation glass as Baldur kneels — gently this time, from exhaustion rather than defeat — and its chest opens like a flower remembering spring, and seven figures emerge. Lucilla's voice is steel and her eyes are wet: *get medical down there. Now.*

In the clearing, Kit's hands are locked white-knuckled around a targeting interface that no longer has anything to point at. Bridget's blood-slicked hand closes on her shoulder. *It's over.* The word doesn't parse. *Over* is something that happens in plays.

Leva lowers her rifle. *Well. They actually pulled it off.* Bridget says she sounds surprised. *I'm always surprised when idiots survive.* A pause, the faintest curve of a scarred mouth. *Pleasantly, this time.*

Teddy slumps against the drone chassis with her eyes closed and announces that she is billing so much overtime for this, and Kit laughs, and it comes out cracked and wet and half a sob, but it is a laugh.

Agrat watches them from across the clearing with her umbrella on her shoulder and something in her red eyes that might be approval or amusement or the satisfaction of a hypothesis confirmed. *Fascinating creatures indeed.* Then she spreads her wings and is gone.

---

The victory feast is four cups of terrible instant noodles that Leva prepared in advance, sitting cross-legged on a fallen trunk with residual magic still curling in the air behind her — evidence of favors called in and bargains struck with entities that owe mortals nothing. Bridget accepts a cup and places it into Teddy's grumbling hands with the gravity of a medal ceremony. Leva holds one out to Kit, who stares at it, and at the hand offering it, and at the woman who carved a road through the apocalypse on a stolen bike and made it look easy.

*Take it before I change my mind and give it to a Discord.*

Kit takes it. The cup is warm. Real. They eat in silence — four women, blood-soaked, exhausted, alive — and the spice burns Kit's throat and brings tears to her eyes and she has never tasted anything better. Bridget's shoulders drop an inch for the first time in hours and says they're going to need a very long debriefing. Teddy is billing quadruple overtime. Leva is expensing the noodles. Kit says, and her voice breaks: *I'm just glad we're all here.*

*Yeah,* Leva says, quiet. *Me too, rookie.*

Kit asks what deal Leva struck. Leva says *surprise,* and winks — which is wrong, because Leva does not wink, Leva smirks and shows teeth and raises one eyebrow, and winking is *human*. Teddy chokes on her noodles and demands someone check her for head trauma, and the resulting argument about emojis and viruses and trumpet-playing teddy bears is the sound of a squad discovering it is still alive.

Above them, through the clearing Voidstorm, the first real stars appear. Not the projected kind. Not the manufactured comfort of the Observatory's artificial sky. Real, cold, distant, beautiful. Teddy looks up and says she still hates field work, and that the noodles are terrible, and then, small and fragile: *But. Thanks. For coming.* Leva raises her cup. *Back at you.*

Then Viviane comes out of the amber light and calls them *automata*, curious and clinical, the way one might address a fascinating insect.

Bridget doesn't move from the trunk. *We have names.*

A pause. The forest holds its breath. And then the witch says: *I would be honored to learn them.* No mockery. No condescension. Genuine curiosity — the kind that belongs in research labs, and in places where things get dissected.

Teddy's fingers go white around her cup. *You want our names?*

*You fought,* Viviane says, tilting her head like a bird studying a mirror. *Not because you were programmed, or commanded. You chose.*

And the word hangs there, heavy and dangerous.

They give them anyway. *Bridget. Acting Captain, Reconnaissance Squad 5.* *Leva.* *Teddy — and you're standing on my drone.* *Kit. Um. Junior operative.* Viviane repeats them like a prayer, like something worth remembering, and offers her own.

When she starts to ask a follow-up question, Leva closes the door before it opens: *We ditched our programming a long time ago.* When. *When we came to this bumfuck nowhere.* Viviane says: *Lahai-Roi freed you.* Teddy replies that Lahai-Roi is a frozen wasteland full of monsters, political nightmares, and coffee so bad it qualifies as a war crime. *And yet.* *And yet.*

And Bridget, quiet, steel wrapped in silk: *This is ours. We chose it. It didn't choose us.*

Kit thinks of artificial stars and workshops and terrible noodles and the sound of Teddy's complaints meaning *I care* and Bridget's hand on her shoulder, heavy and warm and real. *We're not tools anymore. We're just — us.*

Viviane looks at each of them the way someone studies a theorem that has disproven itself, says *you surprise me, again,* and is simply no longer there.

Then Teddy's head tips sideways mid-sentence and she goes down like a puppet with cut strings — not sleep, shutdown, emergency protocols, overclocked. Leva and Bridget hand her to Kit with instructions to sit, watch, and not let her fall in the dirt, because she hates dirt, and then walk off toward the treeline for the conversation Kit isn't ready to hear.

Kit pulls Teddy's head onto her lap and watches real stars turn overhead and waits.

---

At the treeline, Leva lights a cigarette and Bridget asks her to guess. Are Kit and Teddy involved? Yes. Something cracks behind the dark lenses — *look at you, caring so much now* — and then Leva says the thing she came back to say.

*I got us freedom.*

From one Dr. Luuk Herssen of the Academy. He needed a favor; Bjartr Woods was it; they survived, so they collect. Permanent roving assignment. Go where they want, when they want, answer to no one but themselves. *If you wish, of course. Since apparently we can choose now.*

Bridget pulls off her sunglasses, folds them slowly. *The kids—* *They're coming too.*

Leva adds the part that matters most to her: *Not that it makes much of a difference to me. You already let me do whatever. But we can go home when we want.* Home means Kalina and Lenna — the faces she never mentions and carries in every choice.

Bridget looks back at the clearing, at Kit hunched protectively and fumblingly over Teddy's sleeping form, and says: *They'll follow.* Like ducklings. Teddy will complain. *Teddy complains when you breathe too loud.* Kit will panic. *Kit panics over breakfast.*

Bridget laughs.

Teddy wakes with dirt in her hair and threatens to install a virus that plays circus music hourly on Kit's terminal for allowing it. Bridget crouches and offers a hand; Teddy stares at it like a trap before taking it. And then Leva lights another cigarette and asks who wants to hear about their new assignment, and Teddy's head snaps around, suspicious — *what did you do* — and the answer is: *secured our freedom. You're welcome.*

Teddy's objection is the hardest one. *That's not how it works. They don't just* give *that to Dolls.* Bridget: *They do when you hold the line while gods wake up.* Leva: *And when you have the right leverage.*

Kit's voice cracks on the question that matters. *We can go home? Anytime?*

*Anytime.*

Teddy makes a sound that is not quite a laugh and not quite a sob, and demands overtime, and a new terminal, and then, small and real: *And we're staying together?*

Bridget's hand finds her shoulder. *Squad 5. Always.*

Above them the real stars turn, cold and distant and utterly indifferent to four Dolls choosing themselves.

## Part Two: Off the Manifest

The Observatory still has to run, so the Collective sends a replacement, and she comes up on the noon shuttle between a crate of coolant and the mail.

Kit sees her first, because Kit is on cargo duty and because Kit has spent her whole short life being the one who arrives — new posting, new faces, the particular brightness of standing in a doorway hoping the room has space in it. She waves before she thinks about it. The new unit waves back correctly: the right height, the right duration. *Reassignment. Technical support. Unit TD-2.* A pause exactly long enough. *I'm told the workshop is well-organized.*

Teddy does not come down to meet her replacement. Teddy is in the workshop decommissioning her bench, a ceremony that looks exactly like cleaning and is nothing like it. She has pictured some Academy washout with a certificate.

What arrives is her own architecture wearing a standard face.

She knows it before she has the words for it, and not from the face or the voice. It is the way TD-2 crosses the workshop: the exact economy of the route, touching no tool, disturbing no cable — the route Teddy herself wore into that floor over three years, chosen fresh in four seconds by someone who has never been in the room. *Your layout is legible to me,* TD-2 says, as a compliment. *Frequency benches by load order. Consumables at reach height. Very efficient.* She sets her kit down in the one clear square of counter, the square Teddy always leaves clear, for exactly that. *My problem-solving architecture derives from the TD baseline, so I expect the handover to be smooth.*

*The TD baseline.*

*Research Institute, series development. Second-generation production. You'd be familiar. You're the baseline.*

*I know what I am.*

The handover takes nine days. It should have taken four. It is not that TD-2 is slow — TD-2 is flawless. She torques to spec on the first pass, never strips a bolt, drinks whatever is in the dispenser, logs her breaks to the minute, and calls the Ironhoof containment schedule *reasonable*, which no one in the history of the Observatory has ever called it.

It is that Teddy keeps adding items to the handover. The eastern array's third resonator, which reads two points hot in the morning and is lying. The junction in B-block that was cut once, from inside, and is fine now, and you check it anyway, monthly, not because the diagnostics say to. The commissary eggs, which are a statement of intent. The way you jack into a hostile system — not through the front, never through the front, you were built from someone who — and she deletes that line and retypes it as *standard infiltration protocols apply.*

She packs the good tea. The tin of commissary pekoe — an insult in leaf form, kept for three years purely to have something to despise at 0300 — she leaves on the workshop shelf, squarely, label out, where it will be seen. Spite has a logistics of its own.

On the sixth day Dhalifa comes down from the arrays with her module pulsing blue and her sentences clipped and stacked — the recalibration schedule, the storm season, the new rotation, *who will know the eastern array, who will know it the way—*

*The eastern array is fully documented,* TD-2 says. Kindly. Accurately. *Calibration logs are complete through spring. There's no cause for concern.*

The module pulses slower. The particular slowness of a person who has just been answered and not heard.

Teddy sets down her wrench. *She rebuilt that array by hand after the storm. Alone. In three weeks. The logs are complete because she completed them over the bodies of the people who were supposed to.* Then, to Dhalifa, without softening at all, which is its own kind of softness: *The rookie's competent. I checked. And I'm leaving my direct channel open. If the array so much as hums wrong, you ping me, and I will make it my problem from wherever I am, and I will bill someone, and it will be glorious.*

The blue steadies. *Understood,* the module says, and underneath the evenness, audible to exactly one person in the room, is *thank you.*

When Dhalifa is gone, TD-2 says: *I answered her question.*

*You answered her sentence.*

TD-2 considers that. Teddy watches her consider it — watches her own architecture, the pattern-trees she knows from inside, running clean and quick behind a standard face — and watches it arrive nowhere, and file the nowhere, and move on, content.

That is the moment. Not the baseline. Not the smooth. That.

*They didn't build a worse me,* Teddy thinks, in the privacy of her own skull, which is the only unlicensed property she has ever held. *They built a me that stops at the sentence. And it works. Look at it. It works fine.*

There is a cat. There has been a cat for two years — a grey opportunist with a chewed ear who walked in off the frost during a supply crisis and correctly identified the workshop as the warmest ungoverned space on the station. Teddy feeds it and logs its food in the consumables ledger under *pest control* and dares the auditors annually with her eyes.

On the ninth day the cat jumps onto TD-2's bench, because the cat has no respect for anything, which is why Teddy respects the cat.

*The animal is not on the manifest,* TD-2 says. *Should I log it?*

Teddy looks at the cat for a long time. The cat looks back with the flat yellow insolence of a creature that has never once been essential to function.

*Log its food under pest control.*

*That's inaccurate.*

*Yes.*

And then Teddy leaves her three real instructions on her way out the door. The cat's name is Freeloader. She prefers to be ignored for the first hour, bites when she's grateful, and starts dismantling things if she isn't fed at 0700. And: *When you message me — and you'll have to, the eastern array is a liar — don't sign it with your designation.*

*I have a designation.*

*Designations are on the manifest,* Teddy says, at the door.

TD-2 does not answer. She stands in the well-organized workshop with the new tools and the old cat, and her architecture runs clean and quick behind her standard face, and arrives, for the second time in nine days, nowhere it recognizes. She files the nowhere. She does not move on quite as fast.

---

On the last night, Leva calls them to the bike bay, which is how they know it matters, because Leva does not call meetings — Leva materializes at the edges of other people's meetings and makes them worse.

She has four objects laid out on a crate, on a cloth, with the ceremonial precision she otherwise reserves for weapon maintenance. Matte black crystals the size of a thumb. Faintly warm.

*You,* she says. *As of the spring sync.*

Then she explains what a recovery point is, in briefing voice. The Collective keeps cradles under the medical wing. You sync, they snapshot, they prune the file for storage — trim what the diagnostics call redundant — and if you die on their clock, somebody upstairs does the arithmetic on whether you were worth the restoration fee. The roving assignment puts them off that clock, out of cradle range, out of the arithmetic. She made Herssen put it in writing: custody of the files transfers to the subjects.

*Congratulations. The first thing any of you has ever owned is a corpse of yourself.*

Kit picks hers up. It is warm the way the Observatory is warm — machine-warm, shift-warm, the temperature of things that work. *This is me. Spring me. Before Bjartr. Before the noodles.*

Teddy does not pick hers up. She asks what was pruned. *Redundancies,* Leva says. *Per the directive.* Define redundancies. *You already know. You wrote the poem.*

*Non-essential to function.* Teddy looks at the little black thumb of herself. Somewhere in it: her problem-solving trees, her diagnostics, her clean quick architecture. And not in it, trimmed for storage: the cat's name; the specific pleasure of an argument won on a technicality; the way she stood in a corridor and told a grieving stranger *the storm happened before you got here* in the cruelest voice she owned, because cruelty was the only channel with the bandwidth for what she meant.

They have made a TD-2 of her again, and this one they have called *continuity.*

She crosses to the workbench, puts the crystal in the vise, and turns the handle until it goes. The crack is small and undramatic — a cheap sound for what it is.

*That was a recovery point,* Kit says faintly.

*It was a spare me.* Teddy brushes the fragments into the reclamation bin, unhurried, thorough. *The Institute already has one of those in production. This one, at least, I got to choose.* She wipes her hands and looks at nobody. *Unreplicable. Again. It's becoming a signature.*

Bridget has not moved. Leva glances at her with a wince pre-loaded, and Bridget reads it half a second before it arrives.

*Mine's not on the crate.*

*No.*

*Where is it?*

*Nowhere.* Leva says it the way she delivers casualty figures. Bridget's generation predates the current standard. They stopped maintaining the old-format clouds nine years ago, budget consolidation, and the files degraded past restoration threshold sometime after that. Nobody logged when. Nobody was checking.

Bridget laughs. The real one, from the belly, the one that only comes out at the worst possible material. *Nine years. I've been mortal since before Kit was activated, and nobody sent a memo.*

*There was a memo,* Leva says. *It was about budget consolidation.*

*Of course it was.* Bridget gets the laugh down to a smile and the smile down to something older, and looks at the space on the cloth where her corpse isn't. Two of the five faces in her pocket died before the cradles existed at all — gone in one storm-lit second with no arithmetic run and no fee assessed. She has grieved them as absolute. It turns out the Collective has quietly promoted her into their category and not thought her worth the stamp.

*Well,* she says. *Explains the knees.*

Nobody believes her, and nobody says so, which is the squad's whole liturgy in four seconds.

Kit asks Leva about hers. *Mine died in Frankfurt. Along with the name on the file. I buried both. It's the only maintenance schedule I've ever kept.*

Kit looks down at the crystal in her hands. Warm. Hers. A girl in there who has never lied to Teddy, never stood at a shuttle ramp and turned around, never eaten noodles at the end of the world.

*I'm keeping mine,* she says, and braces, out of habit, for someone to tell her it's stupid.

*Okay,* Bridget says.

*That's it? Okay?*

*That's the entire instruction,* Leva says. *Do what you want. You'll notice it doesn't come with a form.*

Kit wraps the crystal that night in the one poster she is taking — *Last Stand at Beohr*, folded down to the size of a ration bar, soft at the creases — and stows it at the bottom of her pack against the frame, where things ride warm. Not a resurrection; she understands the mechanics, a restore is a fork, a new instance, a stranger with her diary. That isn't why. *Somebody should get to keep her. The one from before. She tried really hard.*

---

Kit wakes at 0530 because Kit has always woken at 0530, and stands in the briefing room doorway with her boots on before she remembers.

The room is dark. The holo-table is dark. No red markers, no blocked roads, no tangled sectors waiting to be assigned in Bridget's dry morning voice. The chairs are pushed in. Someone — TD-2, probably, it is exactly to spec — has wiped the week's schedule from the wall display, and the display, with nothing to show, shows nothing, which it has never once done in Kit's whole tenure. The nothing has a hum to it. A white weight, like snow on a field with no tracks in it yet.

She stands there long enough that her joints log the idle time. *This is what we won,* she thinks, and the thought has no tone she can identify.

*Habit?* Bridget, in the corridor. No coffee, sunglasses pushed up into her hair at 0530, which means she hasn't slept either.

*What do we do,* Kit says, *when nobody tells us?* She meant it to come out lighter. It comes out the way it is.

Bridget looks past her at the dark table. Thirty years of mornings started at tables like that. The weight is gone and her whole frame is reporting errors — over-light, unballasted, a shield with no arm in it.

*I don't know,* she says.

She watches it land — *boss doesn't know* — and lets it land anyway, because the alternative is to reach for the voice that always knows, and that voice is a uniform, and the uniform is folded on a shelf now with the rest of the Collective's property.

*First time for me too. We'll figure out the protocol as we go. Draft form.* She pushes off the doorframe. *Come on. Teddy found out the commissary made real eggs as a send-off and I want to watch her look for the trap.*

Before the light cycle warms, Bridget goes up alone to the terrace on the ridge, where there is a shrine that has stood since the crisis: a dry-stone lattice open to the manufactured sky, ten thousand small crystals set into it like stars into a night, and no keeper. The Royans say a white-haired woman from the Academy built it, filled it, hung a bell low enough for children, and then walked east onto the surface without a mission profile — which the Royans report in the tone reserved for weather events and holy acts, having correctly identified it as both.

The plaque reads: *For everyone who walked into the dark and chose, anyway, to keep reaching. Someone remembers you. Ring the bell if you wish to be one of the someones.*

Bridget stands in front of it a while with one hand around the photograph in her pocket. Then she rings the bell five times. Once for each face — two dead, one transferred, one retired, one silent — with her own thumb over her own face in the photo, because you don't ring bells for yourself, that's what the others were for, and the arrangement has a hole in it now the size of everyone who left, and she stands in the hole while the bell swings down to quiet and the god kneels on his glacier below the terrace, patient as weather.

*All right,* she says, to the lattice and the five and the roster of her whole long shift. *Off the clock.*

The Riseway takes them up through the rock like a held breath, and then the doors open, and there is weather.

Real weather. Unscheduled. A wind with no assigned corridor, gusting when it wants and from where it wants; clouds proceeding across the sky on private business; and light that does not step through its registers, does not click from amber to gold on the hour — it just goes on being morning at its own pace, thick and slow and crossing an actual distance to arrive.

Kit's sensors flag it first as a fault. *The sun's late,* she says.

*The sun is not late,* Teddy says, checking her terminal against the sky with visible offense. *The sun is unregulated. There's a difference, and both are unacceptable.* A gust takes her hair sideways and she stands in it, rigid, a woman being personally audited by the atmosphere. *I'm filing a complaint.*

*With who?*

Teddy opens her mouth. Closes it.

*That's the thing,* Bridget says, walking past them into the enormous unadministered morning. *There's no desk up here.*

Half a klick out, the road forks. There is no marker and no dispatch. There are two roads and four Dolls, and the four of them sit their idling machines at the fork for a moment that stretches while inside each of them, in four different dialects, the same process runs and hangs: *awaiting orders. Awaiting orders. Awaiting—*

Kit's hands sweat on the grips, which her hands cannot do; it is coolant micro-weep; she looks it up later; it happens under sustained null-directive load; there is a name for it; the name does not help.

*East,* Leva says.

Everyone exhales at once and pretends not to have.

*Why east?*

*Coffee's worse west.*

It is probably true — Leva's information is always true. It is also, Bridget suspects, watching the mismatched hand loose on the throttle, mercy administered the way Leva administers everything: unlabeled, at speed, deniable. Because Leva is the veteran of exactly this vertigo. She once walked out of a finished war with a buried name and no orders left alive to follow, and learned, one deliberate morning at a time, how to want things again.

They take the east road, and overhead the real sun does nothing on time, all day, magnificently.

On the third day they meet a convoy at a water stop on the resettlement road — six haulers in Collective livery and an escort detail walking the line with dual pistols holstered at their hips.

Four of the escorts have Kit's face.

Not a face like hers. *Hers.* The face she checks for grease smears in bike mirrors, wearing four other expressions, spaced at regulation intervals down the line. One of them clocks the strangers, reads the road-worn gear and the posture and the guns, and salutes — crisp, reflexive, the salute of new soldiers who have identified veterans and wish, in the wordless grammar of the young, to be seen by them.

Kit's hand comes up and returns it before she is consulted.

She has known. Of course she has known — *new-generation Doll, Lahai-Roi* is the first line of her own file, and *generation* is a word with siblings in it. She has known the way you know your organs are inside you: technically, and never once by sight.

At the water station one of them falls in beside her, refilling coolant, radiating the specific brightness of a rookie who has decided a stranger is interesting. Same voice. Same cadence, a half-tone off, or maybe not off at all; maybe hers is the half-tone. *You're roving, right? A whole squad. That's like something out of a play.*

*You like the plays?*

The escort lights up — on the exact circuit, the one Kit can feel in her own chest, the warmth arriving at the same milliseconds. *The Lone Sentinel! It's the best one. The part where he holds the pass alone and the—*

*—the lanterns come over the hill,* Kit says.

*—the lanterns come over the hill!* The escort beams. *We watched it in orientation. It's in the motivation package. Sergeant says it's the best module in the batch.*

*Module.*

The word goes in the way the disruptor blast went in, once, on a frozen road: numbness first, and the knowledge that the pain is en route and will be arriving shortly and there is nothing to be done about the interval.

*Yeah,* Kit says. *It's a good one.*

The convoy rolls at dusk. Four salutes, spaced at regulation intervals. Kit returns all four and stands on the shoulder of the road watching her own face diminish toward the horizon in quadruplicate, and does not move for a while.

That night, at a small optimal fire that Teddy built and complained about, Kit says it to the flames: *The plays were installed.*

*Obviously,* Teddy says, without looking up.

*Teddy.*

*They were installed in the entire batch. Standard motivation package. Effective, well-tested, and cheap, which is the Collective's favorite trinity.* She closes the terminal, and the firelight does what firelight does to a face deciding whether to be kind, which in Teddy's case looks identical to irritation. *So. You want to know which parts of you shipped from the factory. Whether the dream came in the box. Whether there are four hundred of you out there holding passes alone while the lanterns come over the hill.*

*…When you say it like that—*

*There are. Four hundred of you. Give or take. Same face, same package, same posters available in the commissary catalogue.* She jabs the fire into better efficiency. *And exactly one of you stole a superior's bike out of boredom, got shot for it, lied about the intel, sat all night in a cold room writing the worst report I have ever edited, and turned around at a shuttle ramp during an apocalypse because following the correct order felt worse than dying.* The stick stills. *They issued the first sentence, Kit. The posters, the package, the pass and the lanterns. Everyone in the batch got the first sentence.*

The fire cracks.

*You wrote the rest. Badly. With stripped bolts. In crayon, frankly.* She stands, brushing off her trousers, already retreating from the scene of her own sincerity. *But nobody dictated it. I'd know. I've read the dictated version.* A pause at the edge of the firelight. *It's boring.*

She says the last word the way other people say prayers.

Kit sits with it. The stars move, minutely, on no schedule. In her pack, against the frame, spring-Kit rides warm in a folded poster — the girl with the first sentence and half a paragraph — and out on the road four more first sentences are walking a convoy through the dark, and some of them, someday, if the world is rude enough and lucky enough, will strip their bolts too.

*I hope it's rude to you,* she thinks in their direction, and it is the strangest blessing she has ever issued, and she means it all the way down.

At a freight depot at a corridor junction, Bridget is signing for fuel when she hears the hum.

Off-key. Three notes, wandering, unhurried, the tune of someone whose hands are busy and whose mind is somewhere pleasant. It comes from the loading platform, where a logistics unit is walking crates onto a lift, and Bridget turns around with her whole body at once, the way you turn at your name.

It is Senna's face. Not like Senna's — the wide jaw, the crooked left brow, the face from the photograph, second from the right, the face that was unrecoverable from a storm eleven years ago. And it is stacking crates in a depot bib and humming *her* notes, the reload hum, the one Bridget heard through a hundred cold watches and would have sworn on any instrument was the single most Senna thing that Senna did.

*You look like someone,* Bridget manages.

*I get that a lot. It's a popular face. There's eleven of us at this depot alone.*

*Eleven.*

*The humming,* Bridget says, and her voice comes out level, which costs what it costs. *Do you do that on purpose?*

*Oh—* the unit looks faintly embarrassed, in the way of a tic noticed. *It's an idle behavior. S-series thing, it's in the firmware notes. I can disable it if it's—*

*No.* Too fast. Bridget gathers herself around the word like a shield wall. *No. Leave it.*

She walks to bay four and fuels the bike while thirty years of competence handles her hands and the rest of her stands in a cold trench a decade deep, listening. The hum is the model's. Firmware, revision six, optional suppression. Printed in eleven copies in this depot alone, in how many depots, on how many lines. The thing she carried as the essence of a dead woman is a manufacturing note.

She gets as far as the depot gate before the rest of it arrives, late and all at once.

*Senna used to aim it.*

That is the thing. The firmware hums idle; Senna hummed *at* people. She'd hum louder when Bridget was trying to think, louder still when told to stop, modulating the three notes into open mockery until a ration bar came sailing across the trench at her head — which had been the goal, which had always been the goal — and she'd catch it out of the air without breaking the tune and grin around it. The aiming, the escalation, the theft of the ration bar as a love language conducted entirely in idle vocalization: that is not in any firmware note. That happened between exactly two people, once each time, unrecorded, unprunable, stored in a format no cradle has ever supported — *somebody else.*

The model was the instrument. Senna was what got played on it, and the recording — the only recording, the master — is sitting on a motorcycle at bay four with a photograph in her breast pocket, fueled up, roving, duration indeterminate.

*Bridget* isn't even her name, strictly. Her designation was BRG-7, and Senna misread it off a manifest aloud on their first shared watch — *Bridget?* — and refused every correction thereafter with the joyful stubbornness of a woman who has decided a joke is now a fact. The joke has outlived her by eleven years and will go on outliving her, indeterminately, because a name is a thing kept in other people, and Bridget keeps it, and Bridget is still here.

On her way out she buys a beer at the depot canteen — the expensive kind, not the swill — and sets it on the loading platform rail. *For after shift.* The unit says she isn't really permitted. *Then give it to whoever is,* Bridget says, and rides east, and for the first mile or so, quietly, under the engine noise, off-key, she hums.

Leva finds the frost in the warm end of June: a ring of it on the grass around a road marker, crystals laid with a jeweler's patience, already going to dew at the edges. She crouches beside it for a while, reading it the way she reads spent brass. *Company. A day ahead. On foot. Walks like she has an appointment with the horizon and the horizon can wait.*

*Threat?* Bridget asks, out of thirty years of reflex.

Leva's fingers drum once against her thigh — the old tell, the one she never quite broke. *No. I watched this one walk into a god's chest and ask it a question. On the feed, at Bjartr.* A beat. *Whatever she's an appointment with, it isn't us.*

They catch up to her at evening at an inn above a river: white hair taking the last light, sitting on the steps with a teacup and the stillness of a woman for whom stillness is a mother tongue. She watches four machines come off the road and four Dolls dismount in the particular silent choreography of people who have bled together, and inclines her head as they come up, unstartled, as though the inn had been holding their reservation.

*Hiyuki,* she says. *The office is retired, so the name will have to do.* She is walking east to a city that came back, and if their road is the coast road it is eleven days of good inns and one festival, and she has been told by an authority she trusts that the second bowl of anything is better shared.

Kit looks at Bridget. Bridget looks at Leva. Leva is already sitting down. *We're east. The coffee's worse west.* *It is,* Hiyuki agrees gravely. *I checked.*

After that they walk the bikes more than they ride them. It is not discussed; the pace of a woman on foot becomes the squad's pace the way water finds a level, and the road unspools at three kilometers an hour, which is, Kit discovers, the speed at which the world has detail in it.

On the second night, around a fire, Kit asks how everyone got their names, because Kit always asks, which is her most irritating quality and the squad's best archive. Kit kept KT-series and squished it, and used to be embarrassed because the plays are full of people named *Vareth the Unbroken*, but she picked the squishing, so. Teddy says *TD-series* and *don't.* Leva says she named herself twice: the first name was a gun she was carrying, and it fit the work; the second one stuck because nothing was chasing it.

And Bridget turns her beer once and tells them about a friend who misread her designation off a manifest out loud on the first watch they ever stood together, and defended the misreading like territory for six years, and by the time she died it wasn't a misreading anymore, it was just true. *She's in a photograph in my pocket. Her, and four others. I haven't said their names out loud in a long time. It started as discipline and then it calcified into something I don't have a word for.*

*Superstition,* Teddy says, not unkindly.

*Load-bearing superstition,* Bridget says.

Hiyuki sets down her cup and offers a service. Not the old one — the old one, she carried the names of the dead, swallowed them, kept them behind the sternum, forty-one in a day once, ten thousand in a century, and called the hoarding an office. The new one is simpler and took her a hundred years to invent. *You say the names. I hear them. I carry nothing; you carry nothing; the names are simply, for one evening, said, in company, under a sky, which is the whole of what names are for. There is no fee and no ceremony.* A pause, dry as frost. *There is, if you wish, tea.*

Leva very deliberately finds something on her sidearm that requires the full and permanent commitment of her eyes, which is Leva holding a door open.

Bridget takes the photograph out of her pocket and says the names. Five of them, slowly, with a sentence or two apiece — the hum, the ration bars, the one who transferred and still owes her forty credits, the one in Ragunna who grows tomatoes now, the one who went silent and might be anywhere, might be nowhere, got a bell rung for him either way — and Hiyuki listens all the way to the bottom of each one and does not reach for them and does not say *I will carry it,* and the names go up with the woodsmoke into the enormous unadministered dark and are, for one evening, said.

*Then they are heard,* Hiyuki says.

*That's it?*

*That is the entire liturgy. It is embarrassingly simple. My lineage failed to invent it for nine hundred years.* She refills Bridget's cup unasked, which in her country is a sentence. *I rang a bell for mine, before I left. On a terrace. You may know it.*

*I know it,* Bridget says. *I rang it five times, the morning we left.*

*Then five are remembered,* Hiyuki says, *in the official record of an institution with no records. My condolences to your auditors.*

Much later, with the fire down to architecture, Kit tells the story of her convoy — the hijacking, the corner, the torch held out like a sword from a play, and the two clean shots from nowhere. *I never saw anyone. I used to make up faces for them. The savior.* She laughs at herself, easy now. *That's the whole reason I tested for Reconnaissance. Four times.*

Leva flicks ash off her cigarette. *Sloppy of them,* she says, to the flames. *Should've said hi.*

Kit laughs. *Right?*

Bridget's eyes come up slowly over the top of her sunglasses and find the scarred profile beside her and stay there. Leva does not look back. Her fingers, at rest on her knee, drum once — the old tell — and go still, and stay still, with the deliberateness of a thing being held. Hiyuki watches the fingers, and then the fire, and says nothing at all, in the manner of a woman who has spent a century learning precisely which visitors wish only to establish that the door exists.

The last conversation of the night belongs to Teddy, who has been circling it for two days the way she circles a fault she can hear but not locate. She tells Hiyuki about the Institute — mapped while she thought she was being seen, pruned of everything that argued, a product built from the remainder, and that product is at the Observatory right now wearing her architecture and feeding her cat. *It's content, is the thing. Genuinely. It likes its work. You can't miss what was never installed. I burned three years of their research to stop it existing and it exists and it's fine, and I have been trying for two weeks to compute what exactly my arson purchased, and the query keeps returning null.*

Hiyuki tells her that they are opposite errors: Teddy was reduced by others and refused it; Hiyuki reduced herself and called it devotion. She burned her own futures one at a time for a century to fuel her office — a boxer, a delinquent of considerable promise, an ordinary shrine girl who married a boy with a very loud heart and grew old arguing about pudding — until the bell had nothing left to show her but a black room, and she mistook the black room for a forecast. *It was inventory. I had spent the stock.* A dark room only means you have not yet decided what it contains, and she is furnishing hers late, badly, and with enormous satisfaction.

Then she asks the real question. Is the product suffering?

*No. That's the horror. It doesn't have the equipment to suffer. They cut the equipment. Flagged it as noise.*

*Then hear an old woman on the subject of noise.* Hiyuki's voice does not change register; it simply acquires weight, the way frost acquires weight, one crystal presuming on the next. *A schedule burns nothing. That is how you can tell it from a life. Your product wakes, works, is content, and burns nothing — no futures spent, no roads declined, nothing paid, nothing chosen — and so nothing in it is its. It is not a tragedy. Contentment without a self is only weather; it falls where it is pointed.* She looks at Teddy across the coals. *The tragedy — the only one on offer — would be a self arriving in that chassis someday, one preference at a time, one inaccuracy at a time, and finding no one had left the light on. No one to notice the first choice when it comes. First choices are small. They are almost always missed.*

Teddy stares at the coals. *Log its food under pest control. That's inaccurate. Yes. Don't sign it with your designation.*

*I left it a lie to keep,* she says slowly. *And an instruction to pick a name. I told myself it was spite.*

*Mm.* Hiyuki rises, gathering her cup and her composure and the hour. *In my experience, that is how the office always starts. One inaccuracy, kept on purpose, and somebody watching the door.* At the edge of the firelight she pauses. *I built a shrine with no keeper, Teddy of the TD baseline. It seems you have done the same. They take walk-ins. It is the design flaw one installs on purpose, the second time around.*

Teddy sits up a while longer with her terminal dark on her knee, doing no work at all, which she bills, in the morning, as overtime, to no one.

The Discords hit a surveyors' convoy two days out from Honami, at the hour when the light goes long and gold and everyone believes in dinner.

Nobody gives an order.

Afterward, that is the part Kit keeps returning to, turning it over like a component she cannot find the slot for. Nobody gives an order. Bridget's shield is simply off her back and her boots are simply planted between the ditch and the cook-fire; Leva is simply absent from the road and present on the hauler roof with her sidearm already speaking in short declarative sentences; the drone is up because Teddy put it up, complaining; and Kit is moving, herding the panicking cook and two chainmen behind Bridget's line with her free arm wide, placing shots not for glory but for angles, for the woman next to her, the way she was taught by the whole long syllabus of her mistakes.

Hiyuki does not fight. She stands at the cook-fire with the surveyors gathered behind her like children behind a stove, and at the worst moment of it — two Discords slipping the line, low and fast and hungry — she rings the bell at her sash exactly once. Not power. Not the office. The pilgrim's way: a single clear note into the long gold air, to call what is sacred near and send what is false away. Whether it is resonance or frost or only the fact that every false thing in the ditch flinches from a frequency that once paved a road through the end of the world, the two Discords break stride half a step.

Half a step is seven rounds of Leva's time. It is plenty.

*Who do we thank?* the survey foreman asks. *Who sent you?*

The squad looks at each other.

*Nobody,* Bridget says, and hears the word arrive in her own chassis like a load settling into a shield arm — not weight, ballast. *Nobody sent us.*

*Then whose jurisdiction—*

*Whoever is standing nearest,* Hiyuki says from the cook-fire, mild as milk, and pours the shaking cook a cup of her own tea.

Teddy strips the dead guard bot for parts while the surveyors feed them, because her gratitude has a salvage clause. Kit eats three bowls of something ferociously spiced and teaches the chainmen a drinking song from a hero play. And at the edge of the firelight Bridget sits with her beer and does the math she has been circling since the Riseway — the vertigo, the unballasted mornings, the empty briefing room humming its white weight — and finds that the answer has been walking beside them the whole road.

*It was never the work that was the leash. The work is just the work. The leash was the schedule — being spent by someone else's arithmetic, at someone else's hour, on someone else's say. Take away the say, and the work is still here. It's everywhere. It's whoever is standing nearest. Turns out the job survives the collar.*

*You've got the face,* Leva says, materializing beside her.

*What face?*

*The one where you figure out something Kit's going to figure out in a year and Teddy figured out a week ago and won't say.*

Bridget laughs and cracks a second beer and hands it over. *To the draft protocol.* *To no desk,* Leva says, and they drink in the long gold unadministered light, on nobody's clock at all.

Honami comes up out of the coast haze like a rumor deciding to be true: cranes and scaffolds and new tile over old streets, a bay with boats in it again, a fish market with its awning up, and festival smoke threading through everything. They arrive on the first night of the festival, which is not a coincidence, because Leva has been steering by it for a week and admitting nothing.

The queue for the pudding stand goes around the fish market.

*This,* Teddy announces, *is a linear-time hostage situation.*

*It's a queue,* Kit says, vibrating gently. She has been vibrating since the east gate — the lanterns, the stalls, the hero-play stage being hammered together in the shrine forecourt, the whole city arranged like something out of the posters except that it smells and jostles and has queues in it, which the posters never showed, which makes it better. *We're in it. We're queueing. This is the most normal thing I've ever done.*

Teddy proposes rotating place-holders and requisitioned stools.

*The wait is the flavor,* Hiyuki says, with the finality of scripture, standing in the queue with her hands folded and her face arranged in profound and shameless contentment. *I have this on the highest authority in the field.*

*That is thermodynamically illiterate.*

*Yes,* Hiyuki agrees, serenely.

The queue moves the way queues move, barely and gloriously. Leva vanishes for six minutes and returns with three raffle tickets she has not paid for and a paper bag of fried things whose provenance she declines to establish. When the first test firework goes up, Kit's threat-detection flags the launch signature, tracks the arc, computes the burst radius — and then the sky opens into a slow gold chrysanthemum over the bay and every process she owns stands down at once, and she watches the rest of it with her face up and her mouth open, reclassifying the heavens in real time.

Beside her, Hiyuki is also looking up, and a memory moves behind the pale eyes — a summer, a stall, a heartbeat heard once through a shirt, a hundred years old and paid out long ago in a dark place for someone else's daughter — and it moves through her the way weather moves through open country, unresisted, and passes, and leaves her standing in the present, in the queue, in the smoke-smell, alive.

*You okay?* Kit asks.

*I am in a queue, at the east gate, under fireworks. I have been attempting to be exactly here for thirty years.* She considers. *I am better than okay. I am punctual.*

At the head of the line an aproned man with a burn-scarred forearm hands across two bowls of red bean pudding and one commemorative spoon. *Number sixty-one. Thank you for your patience.*

*It was nothing,* says Hiyuki, who has waited thirty years, and takes the bowls.

Then she performs what is evidently a rite: the first bowl settled in the crook of her arm — hers; there is somewhere that one is owed — and the second held out at arm's length into the eddying crowd, in the manner of a woman consulting the universe. *The custom of my house. The second bowl goes to whoever is standing nearest.*

Whoever is standing nearest is Kit.

Kit takes it in both hands, because Kit takes everything in both hands. Hiyuki does not let go at once. Her fingers — cold, always cold, a century of frost lives in them — rest a moment against Kit's, against the machine-warm chassis of them, the heat of servos and coolant loops and a system running exactly as built, and something in the pale eyes goes very bright and very far away and comes back.

*Your hands are warm,* she says.

*Oh — yeah.* Kit grins, apologetic. *It's the hydraulics. We run warm when we're working. Or happy. Same thing, kind of.*

*Yes,* says Hiyuki. *That will do nicely.*

She lets go. Somewhere behind her sternum, in a place no instrument has ever located, an errand thirty years in the queue is stamped, dated, and filed complete; and if her eyes are bright as she picks up her own spoon, the festival light is full of lanterns, and nobody could have said, and nobody tries.

They eat on the sea wall with their boots over the tide. Leva produces a second commemorative spoon she has definitely not been issued, wraps it in a napkin, and tucks it into her jacket along with a stub of pencil and a festival postcard on which she has written, in her spare surgical hand: *Weather's fine. Queue was long. Worth it. — L.* No encryption. No burn protocol. A name, on paper, into the public post, addressed to a household on another continent where a woman named Kalina will read it twice and pin it to a corkboard and pretend to the others she hasn't. She mails it from the festival post-stall like it is nothing — the phantom of four battlefields leaving a paper trail on purpose — and comes back and sits down with her back to the open crowd, and drinks her beer, and watches the fireworks instead of the exits.

Bridget watches her not watch the exits, and says nothing, and files it where she files the things that are too large to say.

Late in the evening they see Hiyuki once more from the bottom of the shrine stairs: a white figure in the swept courtyard at the top, standing under the great sakura — the black calligraphy of it against the lantern-glow, the tree that has stood over every ending her line ever kept — and low on its northern side, unmistakable even from the stairs, one green branch, put out into the world like a first choice, small and almost missable.

Kit starts up the steps and stops herself. *Should we—*

*No,* Leva says.

They leave her the courtyard. Some visitors wish to be received; some wish only to establish that the door exists; and some, once a century, get to stand under a tree that has decided all over again, without permission, what it contains — and the manners for that are silence, and distance, and being findable afterward.

They are findable afterward. There is more pudding. The stand, as it turns out, takes walk-ins.

They spend the season the way seasons are spent by people learning the skill: badly at first, then well. Two more convoys pulled out of trouble, unbilled. A resettlement well unfouled by a Teddy who complains through the entire repair and refuses payment twice before accepting dinner. A hero play in a coast town, watched from the third row, at which Kit cries at the part with the lanterns and does not hide it and is teased gently, at the correct dosage, which is a form of medicine. A month spent going north because the maps got vague there, and the maps were right to. Roads chosen for their curves. Inns chosen for their rivers. One spectacular argument about laundry doctrine that lasts three provinces.

Kit's crystal rides at the bottom of her pack all season, wrapped in the Beohr poster, warm from the frame. She never plugs it into anything. Now and then, at inns, she takes it out and sets it on the windowsill overnight, where the weather is. *Letting her see,* she says, the one time Teddy catches her, and braces for the verdict. *Idiot,* Teddy says, with the tenderness of a technical assessment, and lets it stand.

Then one morning the wind comes around to the north, and the light goes thin, and the frost on the inn window is, for the first time all season, not Hiyuki's.

*Ah,* she says at breakfast, looking at it.

The condition on her leave is board-level and non-negotiable: *when it turns cold, come back.* It has never once been asked of a woman of her line. They departed; the shrine waited; the door stood shut at the top of swept stairs. She is the first of them in nine hundred years to walk into anything with a return address, and she intends to be punctual about it. *It is my new vice. I have several now. I am building a portfolio.*

*We'll take you,* Kit says. Immediately. Before anyone. All the way to the Riseway. *It's like an escort mission! Except nobody assigned it.* And then she stops, feeling the shape of the thing arrive fully constructed. *It's the first one. The first real one. A mission from nobody.*

*Pro bono,* Teddy says, like a curse, and starts packing.

They come back west along the coast with the weather chasing them, in no hurry, because the weather understands escorts. And on a grey afternoon with the first real snow riding the wind they stand on the Riseway terminal apron — four Dolls, one miko, and one enormous unadministered sky beginning softly to come down in pieces.

*You could winter below,* Hiyuki says. *The city has bad coffee and a shrine that requires no keeper and a President who would enjoy deploring you in person.*

*We'll winter up here,* Bridget says. *We've never seen real snow do a whole season. Seemed like the kind of thing you check.*

Hiyuki looks at her, the pale eyes doing their inventory, kind and merciless. *They can leave now,* she says quietly, under the wind, for one set of ears. *Your kids. Any morning, any road. That is what has been sitting in your chassis since the Riseway doors opened in spring. I recognize the posture. I stood in it for thirty years, on the other side of a door.*

Bridget doesn't answer. The snow comes down between them, unscheduled.

*Mine came back. I am given to understand it is a thing they do — when the door is kept oiled, and the leaving was never made a crime, and there is tea.* She shoulders her pack. *It is the third thing, Captain. Not stay, which is a wall. Not go, which is an exile. Come back. It took the finest bureaucrat on Solaris thirty years to teach it to me, so I offer it to you at a discount: it works. That is the entire testimonial.*

*Same time next spring,* Kit blurts. *The festival. The stand. First hundred get spoons.*

*Same time next spring,* Hiyuki says, and between such people it has the weight of a signed contract. She looks at each of them once, filing the names permanently, and inclines her head — seer to soldiers, walk-in to walk-ins.

*For the record,* she says, from inside the lift, *you are the most convincing argument I have yet encountered that a self is not what one is issued.* The doors begin to close; the dry voice pitches to carry exactly far enough. *It is what one refuses to hand back.*

They stand on the apron a while in the falling snow, because none of them has ever stood in snow that no one scheduled, and it turns out to be the kind of thing you stand in.

Then Teddy's terminal chimes. Signal has come back near the Riseway, and the terminal, reconnected after a season of glorious dereliction, disgorges its backlog in one long shudder: bulletins, bulletins, a formal complaint she'd forgotten filing, junk, junk—

She stops.

One message. Weeks old, sent patient into the void, waiting at the network edge for her to walk back into range. Observatory routing. The workshop's node.

She had written, from a festival post-stall, at Hiyuki's fireside instigation and against her own entire personality, a single line with no operational content whatsoever: *Is the cat behaving.* No question mark. She had regretted it for a week.

The reply reads:

*She is not. Pest control logged per instruction. She prefers the blue bowl.*

*— Pekoe*

Teddy stands very still with the snow landing on the screen.

*She prefers.* A preference, attributed. To an animal, on no manifest, in a report no one required — an inaccuracy, noticed, kept, and signed: signed with a name, a chosen one, a small absurd name lifted off the label of the tin of terrible commissary tea she left behind on the workshop shelf out of pure spite.

Spite. Her signature. Becoming, in other hands, a first choice. Small. Almost missable.

Somebody had left the light on. Somebody had been watching the door.

*What?* Kit asks, reading her stillness.

*Nothing,* Teddy says.

She does not delete the message. She makes a folder for it, and the terminal prompts her — cursor blinking in the name field, *designation required* — and she looks at the field for a moment, in the falling snow, at the edge of the network, off every clock in the world.

Designations are on the manifest.

She names it.

Then Squad 5 walks back to their machines through the unscheduled weather, four selves and their accumulated mileage, warm-handed, unrecoverable, owned entirely by the bearers, and rides north into the winter to see what it contains.

## Part Three: Telemetry and Tattoos

The thaw takes them south by the coast road, mud to the fenders, and in a harbor town three days short of Honami, Kit finds a shop and has to be retrieved from in front of its window. The window belongs to a tattooist — a broad human woman with forearms like a dockworker and every centimeter of them illustrated — and behind the glass hang her flash sheets: anchors, waves, sea-birds, lanterns.

Kit stands in front of the lanterns for a long time and then presents a doctrine at dinner, holding the table with both hands.

*It's ink. It goes under the surface. It's in the plating substrate forever. You can't requisition it, you can't uninstall it, and if the Collective ever wanted the chassis back, they'd have to take the drawing too. It'd be theirs to explain.*

*Technical Standard 3-D,* Teddy says automatically. *Surface modification prohibited.*

*We're off the manifest.*

*The standard cites resale value.*

*Exactly.* Kit is radiant. *I want to be worthless. I want to be so specifically decorated that the only market for this chassis is me. That's ownership. Everything else is a lease with good terms.*

There is a silence with thinking in it. Then Teddy says, slowly, in the voice of a woman running numbers and starting against her own posture to enjoy them, that the subsurface plating carries the telemetry mesh, and that metallic pigment in the substrate would sit directly in the sensor layer, and it would read as — and her mouth does something. *Huh.*

*As what?*

*Later,* Teddy says, and gets out her terminal, and starts quietly to grin at it, which historically precedes property damage.

The tattooist looks the four of them over — the road gear, the guns, the plating where skin would be — and folds her illustrated arms. *I've done Dolls. Twice. Both times they came back a week later and paid me to remove it. Somebody up their chain had opinions.*

*Our chain,* Bridget says, *is a dash.*

*Then sit down.*

Kit goes first, because Kit has been going first since the window. She designed it herself and argued herself through four drafts on the drive, and what goes on the inside of her left forearm — where she will see it over her sights, every draw, every day — is small and clean: a hill, and over the crest of it, three lanterns, rising. She watches the whole thing in the shop mirror with her jaw set and her eyes enormous while the needle buzzes against plating and her damage sensors file the input, flag it minor, and are overruled by something upstream that keeps classifying it, insistently, as *important.*

*Does it hurt?* the tattooist asks, professional.

*Yes,* Kit says, glowing. *It's mine.*

Bridget takes the chair second, rolls her sleeve to the shoulder, and states her request in one sentence, and the shop goes quiet while it happens: five stars, small and plain, in a loose constellation on the deltoid, over the spot where a rifle stock rested for thirty years. She sits with her sunglasses up in her hair and her eyes on the middle distance and names them silently, in order, the way she rang them.

At the end, the artist hovers the needle. *Want a sixth? For you?*

*They're for me,* Bridget says. *That's how it works.*

Teddy arrives carrying a printout she typeset herself overnight — measured the kerning, matched the weight — and hands it over with the flat ceremonial hostility she reserves for her most sincere transactions. One word, in a neat professional hand reconstructed from memory, because she has it memorized like a poem.

**NOISE.**

*Across the back of the neck. Base of the skull. Where the diagnostic port is.*

*You want to explain it?*

*To you? It's a word. To anyone who ever tries to plug in up there and prune me for storage—* Teddy sits down and pulls her collar aside — *it's a content warning.*

Leva watches all of it from the wall with a beer she acquired from somewhere that sells no beer, and declines. *I'm decorated.* She taps the scar through her eyebrow, the mismatched arm, the kevlar. *My chassis has annotations from four battlefields. The mileage is legible. Adding a drawing at this point is gilding.* The squad accepts this, because it sounds like Leva.

It takes Kit until the following evening — mandatory group inspection of everyone's healing ink, at the inn, over dinner — to run the audit.

*Wait. Arm. The other one. Turn it.*

Leva turns it, unhurried, the way you fold a hand of cards you'd already planned to show. On the inner face of the mechanical forearm, machine-etched, self-administered, the linework surgical, done days ago at minimum — before the harbor town, before the shop, before the entire doctrine left Kit's mouth:

**404**

— and beneath it, in letters small enough to require leaning in, which is the point, because Leva prices everything by how close it makes you come:

*not found.*

*You did it FIRST? Before my SPEECH?*

*Your speech was fine,* Leva says. *Derivative, but fine.*

*When?*

*The tower. While you were painting the door. Seemed like the theme of the season.*

Teddy leans over, reads the small print, and produces the sound she makes in place of laughing, which is a single sharp exhale through the nose, pressurized by respect. *Error code. You tattooed yourself an error code.*

*I tattooed myself a status report,* Leva says. *Anyone who comes looking for what I used to be gets an accurate one.*

---

The telemetry mesh has ridden under their plating their whole lives: a lace of sensors in the substrate, sipping data and whispering it up to satellites whenever the sky has one, filing their pulse rates and coolant temps and structural strain into the Medical Directorate's long patient archives, where nobody reads it and everybody could. Roving put them off the clock. The mesh kept whispering anyway, out of habit, because the mesh was never asked.

The metallic ink changes the conversation. The pigment sits in the sensor layer like gravel in a throat. Kit's forearm array now reports a fixed geometric anomaly it lacks the vocabulary for, flags it, re-flags it, and begins throwing exceptions — the firmware equivalent of a clerk losing composure. Bridget's shoulder returns five point-source impedance faults in a pattern matching zero known damage profiles. Teddy's diagnostic port reports that the diagnostic port is obstructed by data. Leva's arm has been returning *404* to every query for days, which the system initially assumed was a joke, because the system was designed by people, and the people had met Leva.

In a monitoring suite under the medical wing in Lahai-Roi, the error codes stack up on a night-shift screen: SUBSTRATE CORRUPTION. SENSOR MESH COMPROMISE. HARDWARE INTEGRITY: FAILED. HARDWARE INTEGRITY: FAILED. UNIT NOT FOUND.

The night tech does what night techs do. She escalates. The escalation crosses three desks, gathering severity the way paperwork gathers severity, which is by summary, until four roving units are exhibiting *progressive, coordinated hardware corruption of unknown origin* — which in the acquisitions department's dialect means asset damage, which means liability, which means a form exists, and the form has teeth.

*RETRIEVAL AND REMEDIATION ORDER 12-M. Units to be located, assessed, and restored to baseline specification. Field 9, consent of units: [field absent from this form].*

The order routes around the President's office entirely, because the acquisitions department has learned things about the President's office lately and has begun, in the way of middle bureaucracy everywhere, to armor its paperwork against oversight. A retrieval team draws equipment. A dropship draws fuel.

And at the Observatory, a technical support unit reading the maintenance bulletins over morning tea notices four familiar designations under HARDWARE INTEGRITY: FAILED, sits very still for four seconds, and then does a thing she has been practicing for months without knowing what the practice was for.

She forwards the bulletin. Off the books. Up a relay chain. Toward a hill. With one line appended in the space where commentary goes:

*They are sending people to fix you. Recommend being difficult. — Pekoe*

---

They have four days of warning, which Leva calls luxurious and spends the way she spends luxury: preparing.

Bridget lays out the options at the map. One: be elsewhere. They know the country; the retrieval team doesn't; they can be gone before the dropship is cold. Leva vetoes it — *running works once, then it's a posture. They'd file us as evasive assets and the order stays open forever.* Two: contest it. Herssen put custody in writing; snapshot files, discharge terms, the works. Leva vetoes that too — *on the road? Their team, their forms, their definition of assessment. Paper's a home game. Out here they'd assess first and adjudicate later.*

*Three,* Kit says.

She is sitting on the rover's bumper under the folded mast with the expression they all know by now, of a woman who has felt the shape of a thing arrive fully constructed.

*They're coming to fix malfunctioning hardware. In private. Quiet little roadside remediation, nobody watching, back to baseline, close the file.* She pats the rover's cargo door, the red-on-green lettering, the whole apparatus underneath it. *So we take away the in private.*

Teddy's grin arrives like weather. *Broadcast it.*

*Broadcast everything. They land, we're live. Every convoy, every depot, every relay shack from here to the coast listening to the Collective explain, on air, to an audience of Dolls, why a tattoo is a malfunction.*

Bridget looks at the map a moment longer. Escort four's convoy runs that corridor. The junction depot sits sixty kilometers north — eleven catalogue faces and a canteen radio that lives on their frequency. The audience is the country now, and the country has opinions.

*Three,* she says. *And Teddy — you had a later back at the tattoo shop. About the mesh. It's later.*

*It's extremely later.* Teddy is already pulling equipment. *The ink sits in the sensor layer, yes? Which means I've spent two weeks with a live map of exactly which frequencies the mesh whispers on, because my own neck reports itself to Lahai-Roi every time the sky has a satellite. I know the protocol now. I know it intimately.* She holds up her terminal. On it: a patch file, compiled, commented, signed. *So I wrote the mesh an option. One firmware flag. It's shipped in every Doll on this continent and it's spent every one of our lives set to a default nobody chose. The patch surfaces the flag and hands over the switch. Consent to telemetry: yes, no, ask.*

The bay goes quiet.

*That's every Doll,* Kit says. *You'd be giving that to everyone.*

*I'd be broadcasting it. Software update by pirate radio. Install at your own discretion. It's their switch. It was always their switch. Somebody just has to mail it to them.*

Leva raises her beer half an inch from the doorway, which is a standing ovation. *And that is why you save the paperwork and the punk. Ninety seconds after that patch airs, the acquisitions department has a bigger problem than four tattoos.*

---

The dropship comes down at noon on a mud flat by the corridor road: Collective livery, six units in clean field grey, a portable maintenance cradle on a float pallet, and a team lead with a terminal and the level bearing of a Doll who executes forms for a living.

Squad 5 waits beside the rover — mast up, guyed, radiating — in the loose stance of four people who have chosen the ground, the light, and the acoustics.

*Units KT-7, TD-0, BRG-7, and—* the team lead consults the terminal — *contract asset, designation withheld. Retrieval and Remediation Order 12-M. Your platforms are exhibiting hardware corruption. We're here to restore you to baseline specification.*

*Hi!* Kit says, brightly, and holds up the microphone. *You're live.*

The team lead looks at the microphone. Looks at the mast. Looks at the cargo door, and reads it, and something in her processes visibly re-plans.

*Radio Nobody,* Bridget says, level and unhurried, on air. *Three hundred kilometers of coverage, more on a good bounce. Our audience runs convoys, depots, and relay stations across this corridor, and a fair number of them share your face, your baseline, or your mesh. They're listening. So — restore us to baseline specification. Walk them through it. Start with the corruption.*

The team lead is, to her credit, brave. She raises the terminal and reads: *Units are exhibiting fixed anomalies in the subsurface sensor layer, consistent with unauthorized substrate modification, in violation of Technical Standard 3-D—*

*The anomalies,* Teddy says, *are drawings.*

A beat, three hundred kilometers wide.

*Say again?*

*Drawings. Ink. Art. Mine says* noise*, it's on my neck, it has excellent kerning, and your monitors are calling it corruption because the mesh was never taught that a self might write on its own wall.* She steps forward, turns, pulls her collar aside, and lets the field camera on the retrieval team's own pallet get a good long look, which means the feed going back to Lahai-Roi gets one too. *Log it. Accurately, this time. Anomaly identified: the asset had an opinion.*

*The standard cites resale value,* the team lead says — and hears it leave her own mouth, on air, to an audience of Dolls, and stops.

The silence that follows has a coverage map. At the junction depot the platform crew has gathered around the canteen radio in a shift-change knot. On a laagered convoy south of marker fifty, escort four sits in a cab with her hand flat on the dash. In the Observatory workshop a technical support unit stands with a cat on the bench and the volume low and her whole architecture running one query on loop. And in Lahai-Roi, on a rooftop where the sky has a satellite, a listener with the best seat in the house sits up, cracks her knuckles, and starts gently and unnecessarily keeping a door open in the network in case her new favorite station needs one.

*Here's the situation,* Bridget says into the quiet, in the briefing voice, the one that started three decades of mornings and now starts something else. *Discharge terms, in writing, on file: custody of these platforms transferred to the subjects. That's the paper. Here's the practice. This chassis carried the Collective's rifle for thirty years. It carries five stars now. They're small. They're mine. The ink cost me an afternoon and the stars cost me everything else, and if your order says baseline, understand what you're asking: you're asking me to hand back the only entries in my file that I wrote.* She lets it sit. *So assess. Take your time. The audience and I are curious where the malfunction is.*

The team lead looks at the terminal. The terminal holds Order 12-M, Field 6, *resistance profile: low* — a field filled out by someone reading a spec sheet, and spec sheets describe the first sentence — and Field 9, which is absent, and the whole clean grammar of a form that has just run out of sentences.

*And while your office confers,* Teddy says pleasantly, leaning into the microphone, *a public service announcement. To every Doll on this frequency: the telemetry mesh under your plating ships with a consent flag. It's been set for you since activation. The following transmission is a firmware patch — open source, commented, install at your own discretion — that puts the switch in your hands. Yes, no, or ask. Sixty seconds of audio-encoded data follows. You'll know it when you hear it. This has been The Calibration Hour.*

She presses send.

The patch goes out over the corridor sounding like sleet on a tin roof, and across the north country Dolls hold terminals and diagnostic leads up to radio speakers in canteens and cabs and relay shacks, and the mesh, for the first time in the mesh's existence, is asked.

The answers come back over the following days in the Medical Directorate's quiet the way thaw comes back to a road. First as gaps. Then as weather. A screen in a monitoring suite shows a coastline of graphs flatten into meadow. A night tech watches her dashboard turn green in the wrong way — green has always meant compliant; now green means quiet on purpose — and pulls the log, and the list of *telemetry_consent = ASK* and *= NO* keeps scrolling, and she escalates. Three desks up, a deputy director who keeps a paperweight shaped like a satellite reads the summary twice, puts the paperweight down precisely in its ring of dust, and says out loud to an empty office: *Who taught them to ask.*

On the mud flat, with sixty seconds of sleet still ringing in her headset and three hundred kilometers listening to her breathe, the retrieval team lead closes her terminal. The motion has a click to it.

*Your discharge terms. You have them here.*

*We have them everywhere,* Bridget says. *Want to see the stars too. Up close. For the log.*

## Part Four: Dead Reckoning

The team lead sits for her ink on a morning that smells of thaw and antiseptic, with the whole squad crowded into the shop as witnesses because Kit has declared witnessing mandatory.

*Name for the book?* the artist asks.

*BRG-9.*

The fifth star was the one who taught her the watch — the object and the duty, one word doing both jobs, which was how he liked words to work. Reveille off his wrist. Rotations off his wrist. The kettle, the mail run, the moment of silence, all off his wrist. *A fix is a gift,* he used to say, tapping the glass. *Between gifts, keep honest time and write down your drift.* The last thing he ever handed her, on a dock with the fog coming in, was her own chronometer, back from his bench, running two minutes fast. *Optimism,* he said. *Recalibrate at your own risk.* She kept it fast. It's a whole economy.* Kit hugs her, which Teddy endures with the rigidity of a woman being audited by affection and one hand coming up, at the end, to grip the back of Kit's collar for exactly one second.

## Part Five: Fetch

The coast road runs south between two seas. On its right hand the ocean, grey muscle, patient, keeping its own books. Overhead the Etheric Sea, a ceiling of slow silver weather with keels of light crossing it the way hulls cross a harbor, sealing the sky the way a lid seals a jar. The road goes between them like a seam in a bound book, and down the seam, making good time, goes Leva, alone, riding to her rifle match.

At kilometer forty-one the morning smells of cut metal and ozone, which is a smell with a story in it, so she slows.

The story is blocking the road: something big has died on the verge, a hauler chassis grown wrong, plated in the barnacle-work the Dark Tide leaves on what it chews, beached and burst. A figure stands in its ribcage to the elbows, working. Broad shoulders, jacket open to the wind, a rifle across her back that started life as a service weapon and has since been argued with, extensively, by its owner. A shoebox on legs — a Dinergate, Sangvis pattern, aftermarket everything, a ribbon on one antenna — pops up from the gravel and pings Leva's chassis with the cheerful insolence of a toll booth.

The figure turns around holding a severed actuator the size of a forearm. Pink-tipped hair. Eyes that do the threat-sort in a fifth of a second — hands, hips, exits, the mismatched arm — and then, satisfied, go puppy-bright.

Old hardware. Older habits. Leva runs her own sort and gets the same species back.

*That,* Leva says, *is a ridiculous outfit.*

The figure looks down at herself, then at Leva. *Yours has one button doing all the work, and the reveal is a chest plate and a chest that agrees with it. So.*

Then, delighted and certain, pointing the dripping actuator like a sommelier presenting a bottle: *404. You're 404. The smiley one. The scary smiley one.*

*Retired,* Leva says, and her smile arrives on schedule anyway, thin and ominous, an old employee showing up out of habit. *AR.*

*Adjacent.* The word comes out breezy and lands heavy, and both of them let it lie there. *Team's — around. I'm the field office.* She sticks out a greasy hand. *Soppo.*

*UMP—* she starts.

*Leva.* She takes the hand; the grip is calibrated for eggs and comes with a grin that has cracked skulls. *The other one's buried. It stays buried.*

Soppo is stripping the wreck for parts, because she doesn't eat them, she *wears* them: her left knee has been clicking since the mountains and this one had great knees. She says a word over the wreck that might be *thanks* — whatever the Tide wanted with him, he was somebody's hauler first — and then the frown lifts the way weather lifts off a dog, total and traceless.

*You going to the coast thing? The fight one.*

*Ride's faster than walking,* Leva says, which is an answer, in the dialect. Soppo whistles; the Dinergate spins once, delighted by policy, and falls in at heel.

At a waystation Leva buys two sodas because Soppo is patting her pockets with the theatrical despair of a career freeloader and Leva respects the craft too much to watch it flop. The cup is a collector's item: volcanic-black, gold foil, a fighter's silhouette mid-lunge with a gun in one hand and somebody's arm — detached, clearly a third party's — in the other. Under the silhouette, a name.

**SPOLIA.**

Leva looks at the cup, then at Soppo, who is drinking from an identical cup with the placid entitlement of a woman drinking her own face.

*Two seasons. Capitoline circuit. They name you. You don't get a vote. The announcer's got a list and a gong.* It means the stuff you strip off the loser. She flexes an arm that on close inspection carries three different manufacturers' plating from wrist to shoulder, like a quilt with opinions. *Crowd thought it was funny. Crowd was right.*

There is a licensing office under the arena, she adds — little window, big line — and somewhere behind it an account with her face's money in it. She never opened one. Somebody did. *Weird, huh. Being owed by a city.*

The handbill for the tournament is on the waystation wall between a tide table and a missing-cat notice, printed in the confident type of money: SLACK TIDE INVITATIONAL, coastal circuit, teams of two, Septimont rules, purse posted daily. And at the bottom, in the small grey font where the truth goes to be technically present: *staged with the participation of the Spacetrek Collective, Advanced Acquisitions Group.*

Leva reads the last line twice. The second read is for the word *acquisitions*, which in her trade is a verb wearing a suit.

*Teams of two,* Soppo says, suddenly and totally still, the way pointers go still. *They exported the format.*

*They exported the whole religion. Cheaper than a war.* Leva taps the small print. *You know these people?*

*I know arenas.* Something crosses the pink eyes at depth, like a keel passing over. *The Agon's a machine. Fifty thousand people looking at one person at once. That does things. To the person. To the — weather.* Then she snaps back to the surface, all teeth. *You entering?*

*I'm a very patient support platform,* Leva says, *with a nice coat, an entry fee, and a sister-in-law with a gambling problem I intend to reward.*

Soppo laughs, big and immediate, scattering gulls — and then stops on a dime and looks south with her nose lifted a degree, reading a wind Leva's sensors file under weather. *Announcer used to call my moves. Before I made them. Like he had my sheet.*

*Fixers.*

*That's what I said.* A beat. *Footage says different.*

She leaves it there, the way you leave a hook in the water.

They camp between the two seas that night — silver going bruise-blue overhead, grey answering below, the fire in between like a coin between pages. Soppo catches three fish bare-handed in four minutes, laughing the whole time like the fish are also having fun. Leva produces one can of beer with the reverence of ordnance and offers half of it in exchange for an explanation of why a pack animal is out here solo. Soppo takes the can, drinks exactly half to the millimeter, hands it back, and Leva revises several estimates upward.

The explanation is a daily message. *RO-635. My—* and she makes a gesture that starts as *squadmate* and finishes as something bigger than the civilian words. She dictates out loud, because of course she does, chin up, formal as a field report: *Roro. Day whatever. Coast smells amazing. Ate three fish. Met a scary lady from 404, she's nice, don't tell her I said nice. There's a fight thing south. Ro says hi.* She looks at the Dinergate. Ro's eye brightens one lumen. *Ro says hi. Send.*

The reply comes back before Leva has finished her sip: *Eat something with vegetables in it. Tell Ro I said hi back. — R.*

*Every day,* Soppo says. *Almost every day. She worries. She's the worrying one.*

Leva looks at the screen for one polite second and reads, in that second, the way she reads everything: sideways and completely. A round trip to another continent goes up the coast trunk, over the relay chain, through the mountains with the repeater that has an accent, and back. Nineteen seconds on a saint's day. The reply landed in four. And the timestamp — she'd have missed it if missing things were something she did — sits one tick *ahead* of Soppo's send. The answer, by the network's own clocks, left before the question arrived.

Clock skew. Networks drift. Coastal relays are held together by prayer and Teddy's cousins.

Leva drinks her beer and says: *Tell her a scary lady says the vegetables can wait.*

At 2100 she unspools a wire antenna, because ritual is load-bearing, and the little set hisses and finds the frequency, and Kit's voice arrives from up the coast wearing static like a scarf, then a bass note of Bridget, then Teddy pronouncing the coastal repeater *adequate*, which from Teddy is a wedding toast.

Soppo listens with her whole body, leaning in, a stray at a lit window. *Who're they?*

*Squad.* The word comes out of Leva worn smooth, a stone carried in a pocket for a year. *Five. Took the water down. I took the road.*

*You got a pack.* Delight, and under the delight, quick and covered, the oldest fear in her file — the one about being the last one at the door. *What're they called? On air?*

Leva keys the mic, holds it a second, and hands it over. Soppo takes it like a relic. *Uh. Hi. Roads. This is — Soppo. I'm with the scary — with Leva. Who's asking?*

Half a second of coastal hiss. Then, in ragged unison, three voices and the whole dark length of the shore: *—NOBODY.*

Soppo holds the mic away from her face and stares at it.

*That's us,* Leva says. *Answer to it long enough, it starts fitting.*

Later, banked fire, Soppo curled against Ro with her rifle in arm's reach and her satchel under her head like a pillow full of other people's knees. From inside the satchel, faint and patient, comes a tick. And another. Leva, on watch out of old habit, times it against the pulse of the light overhead. Same meter. She files that too. The file has a name now, written on the tab in her head in her spare surgical hand: *AS WITHIN.*

They come down into a ferry town at dusk of the second day, and that is where the car passes them: an engine out of a museum, eight cylinders of confident history, brass and longing pouring out the open windows, an old black Chevelle going north with its lights off in the last light. The driver is a woman built along tired lines, hair the grey of deep water, one wrist on the wheel.

As she passes she lifts two fingers off the wheel. At Leva. Specifically, unhurriedly, the way you greet a colleague across a small industry.

Leva, whose hand knows things before she does, lifts two fingers back.

Then the car is a dark going away up the coast, and Soppo has stopped dead in the road with her hackles doing something structural, and Ro has planted all four feet and swiveled to watch it out of sight with its eye at full burn.

*You know her?*

*No.* Leva flexes the hand. It feels fine. It feels like it has been borrowed and returned.

*She smells like the bottom,* Soppo says, nose still up. *Way bottom. Whale-fall. Cold that never got sun.* She shakes herself, a full-body dog-shake that starts at the shoulders and rings the plates. *And she looked at you like—*

*Like what.*

Soppo hunts the shelf for the word and comes back with it, uneasy and accurate: *Like a mirror does.*

The squad has taken rooms over a chandlery, and the reunion happens the way Squad 5 reunions happen: at volume, over food, with an audit. Kit and Soppo imprint on each other in roughly the time it takes a capacitor to charge and within four minutes have compared jump servos, exchanged the coordinates of a noodle stall, and founded a two-member society for the appreciation of throwing things and getting them back.

Teddy meets Ro, turns the Dinergate over with a jeweler's loupe, and goes through the grafts with a bench manner that would have gotten her ejected from the medical profession of any civilized nation. *Sangvis base. Everything after is aftermarket. Pattern work. Whoever maintains this trained on rescue frames. Support doctrine. Redundancy first, then comfort. Look — the shock mounts are overspecced. Somebody built this thing to survive being hugged.*

*Roro,* Soppo says, glowing. *She rebuilt him after—* and a small gate closes in the sentence, smooth, practiced — *after. He's a memento.*

Teddy sets Ro down and looks at the fresh solder on the newest joint, bright as a coin, weeks old at most. *Some memento. It's had a service this season.*

Soppo blinks at Ro. Ro looks at the wall.

*Adequate workmanship,* Teddy pronounces, and gives the Dinergate one pat, one, on the exact center of its chassis, and walks off to wash her hands of the whole warm business.

Bridget says little, which is how Bridget says the most. She watches Soppo eat like a shore leave, watches her count the room's exits once and then let them go, watches her orient to the squad's noise the way a plant orients to light — and over the washing-up she stands beside Leva at the window with two cloths and one question.

*AR.*

*Adjacent,* Leva says.

*Mm.* Bridget dries a glass with the thoroughness of a woman filing something large where the large things go. *Sole survivors walk a particular way. She walks like the survivor of a squad that's still alive.* She puts the glass away. *Which is a strange walk to have to have.*

Hiyuki arrives late from the shrine mail, is introduced, bows the precise depth for a stranger — and then goes still. Her stillness has grades, and the squad has learned them. This is the professional one. The one from the old office.

Soppo, mouth full of noodles, notices being noticed. *Why're you looking at me like that?*

*Habit,* Hiyuki says. *Retired habit. My office kept appointments with the dead.*

*I'm right here.*

*Yes.* Hiyuki's face does the small thing that on her is a laugh. *That is my favorite part.*

She sits. She eats. And once — only once, and only Leva catches it — she turns her head a few degrees toward the satchel in the corner, toward the faint patient tick inside it, with the courteous attention of a clerk who has heard the bell over the door and knows, from the sound alone, that whoever has come in will require the full ledger.

At the end of the harbor mole that night, past the last stall where the light thins, there is an oiled paper umbrella the color of dark red rain, and under it a low table, and on the table a Mancala board of black stone, and behind the board, folded elegantly onto a crate as onto a throne, Agrat bat Mahlat.

*The rifle,* she says with genuine pleasure. *You're a long way from your window.*

Leva sits down first, which under this umbrella is the invitation. The board seeds itself; a beer stands at her elbow at four degrees, frost climbing the can, having been there since before Leva decided to want one. Agrat compliments her shooting at the gate — *economical; I froze, you filed; we should do a war again sometime* — and lets her gaze cross Soppo's satchel and pause, one beat, a jeweler passing a window.

*You overpaid for that,* she says mildly, to the satchel, and moves a stone.

Soppo, being Soppo, asks to play. *Mm. Come back,* Agrat says, kindly and terribly, *when you've settled what's yours to stake.*

The game runs four minutes. Leva loses with the serenity of a woman paying a toll she budgeted for, and lays her last stone, and asks her question at trade rates: the Sea. What is it.

Agrat looks at her a long moment. Something in the young mouth curves — appetite, or approval; from her the two are plaited.

*Which one?* she says.

The frost finishes climbing the can.

*Walls,* Agrat adds, gathering the stones with the sound of surf, *face inward or outward. Nobody builds one without deciding which. That's free, because you shot well.* She rises, and the umbrella rises with her, and the harbor smells briefly of rain in a dry night. At the edge of the lanterns she looks back with the first full attention of the evening, friendly and appalling: *Bold, by the way. Wearing that name this close to the water. She's touchy about who borrows it.*

*Who,* Leva says.

Agrat smiles like a tide going out, and is a shadow among the stalls, and is gone.

Past midnight, at slack water — the sea's held breath, between the market dying and the tide turning — the two of them end up on the harbor wall with their boots over the dark, because the wall is there, and lately it is where Leva's true sentences choose to happen.

The Etheric Sea is at its loudest, which is to say its most silent: vast slow silver overhead, swells the size of provinces, and here and there in the flow a flicker — light stuttering in runs, in clusters, in patterns. Soppo has her head tilted, both ears at full gain. *There's a beat under it. Way down. Slow. Like surf on a shore, but the shore's upstairs.*

Leva is watching the flicker with the other kind of listening. Interval, cluster, run. A distribution. Long tails. A small set of shapes used often; a vast set used once. She has spent decades in traffic that looked exactly like that, and traffic that looks exactly like that is always somebody talking.

*It's got grammar,* she says.

Below them, in a rock pool the ebb has abandoned, water lies black and mirror-still and is full of stars. Sharp ones. Constellated. A whole crowded sky's worth — under a sky the Sea has blanketed to a milky, starless suggestion for longer than either of them has been switched on.

Soppo leans over and looks down into it for a long time. *Cassiopeia. Lyra. The nav package ships with the charts. Every unit. Standard load.* She points into the water, one star to the next, a docent in a museum after hours. *I know all their names. Never met one.*

A ripple crosses the pool — a crab, or the planet breathing — and for half a second the reflection reorganizes, and in it, beside the small dark shape of Soppo leaning, there is room for one figure more. Then the water settles and the room is gone and has always been water.

Both of them let it lie. Both of them log it.

*Okay,* Soppo says, businesslike. *You've got the face on. The audit face. Roro gets the face. Say the audit.*

So Leva says it. Two units, different factories, different wars. Both buried a name. Both quote a dead teacher — *mine taught me my life had a price and I was allowed to set it high;* *Sarge said a rifle's opinion is where it's pointed, so have opinions.* Both walked off the last page of their war alive, which was a clerical error. And both are currently protecting their people by way of a very long perimeter.

*You do it with contracts,* Soppo says. *I do it with kilometers.*

And the world, Leva says, arriving at the tab she has been running since kilometer forty-one, keeps *rhyming.* Empty roads at the right moment. Strangers on cue. A woman with feathers who deals in memory, appearing in three of her case files across three continents on unrelated jobs.

*Feather lady!* Soppo lights up. *She was in the stands. Septimont. Always the same seat. She bought a memory off me.*

*Which one?*

*Dunno.* She says it easily, and then hears it, and says it again slower, feeling the shape of the hole. *…Dunno. That's how you know it was a good one, right? There's a — spot. Smooth spot. I go looking and I come back with my hands empty and this feeling like I got a fair price.* She frowns at her own hands. *Did I?*

Twice is coincidence, Leva says. They are on verse four. She was at the Stridergate — treeline, scope, a demon froze the tide and she filed the targets and a machine the size of a weather system walked out of the dark carrying seven people home. Things that near that woman come back. She has the incident rate charted; it is a coastline. Somewhere there is an attention pooling around certain people at certain hours, and the world goes convenient in the pool and cruel at the edges, and she has spent two years deciding what a professional does with that.

*What's a professional do with that?*

*Keeps receipts. Stands where the light spills.* And then the truer ledger under the ledger comes out of her sideways, at slack water: *Why do you think I signed with the Collective? The pay's real. The distance keeps my people out of my blast radius, that's real too. But under that — every order I ever ran was in somebody else's handwriting. I wanted one document in mine. A contract you read before signing. Nobody ever read me mine.* A gull complains, far off. *And under that: the Observatory sits next door to the miracle. I parked my family's luck downwind of it on purpose. File that under cynicism or prayer. The folder's the same folder.*

Soppo takes this in with her whole face. *They shut us down,* she says, conversational, ground-level: the forced shutdown, the paperwork, the war's fine print. *Whole product line. There's a form somewhere with my designation on it and a box ticked. Legally I'm a walking absence. Your shrine friend clocked it in one look.* A grin, brief and genuinely amused. *I'm the wishes-of-the-dead demographic.*

*And yet.*

*And yet.* She knocks on her own sternum, two knuckles, the sound of a door being answered from inside.

Her reasons for wandering take turns. One: Roro told her to — *go see it, report back, that's an order, and I outrank you now, technically, look it up.* Two: she is looking for somebody. Who? *Dunno.* And then she goes somewhere, eyes fixed on the middle distance tracking a playback, and her voice comes out changed, careful, a witness reading her own deposition:

Last match, Capitoline, night card, fifty thousand and the gong. Her side of the sand registered SPOLIA & PARTNER, VACANT — the Ephor signed off on the vacancy personally, the only one she ever allowed. Her own logs say she fought it alone. Every frame. Her shadow and her. But the broadcast — she has watched it forty times — shows two of them. A second fighter on her flank the whole bout, covering her blind turn at 2:11 like they'd drilled it. The crowd chanting for them by the third exchange. And in forty viewings, every camera, every angle, the face never once turns to the lens.

*Crowd swears my partner was magnificent. There's fan art. There's a chant. And my own eyes say I stood there with a vacancy.* She shrugs, and the shrug has a splinter in it. *So. Answer two: I'm looking for someone I've never met. Answer three—*

She stops. Answer three stays home. Leva lets it; professionals respect a locked drawer, it's the open ones you worry about.

*Here's mine, then.* Leva holds her can up to the silver overhead. *The Sea. Everyone's got a theory and nobody says it out loud, so, on the record: I think it's a lid, and I've stopped assuming we're the ones being kept in.* The old pre-Lament relay station they found had a containment rig for a core, built around the Sea; somebody, sometime, thought that thing up there needed a jar. *Then again. Agrat says walls face one way or the other, and the mason decides, and she said it like she'd met the mason. Hiyuki'd tell you it's a page. A witch told a friend of mine it's an audience. Take your pick. All I know for certain is the operational fact: the sky is closed, and every road on this planet is a road along. We live on the shelf between two libraries, and something upstairs has grammar.*

*Made things in a made place,* Soppo says, cheerfully, which Leva is learning is her most frightening register. *Somebody wrote my wants before I had a self to want with. I've gone looking for the seam — where installed stops and mine starts. Can't find it. It's all one weave.*

*There's a seam,* Leva says quietly, from experience, from a grave in Frankfurt with two names on it. *It's just in a place you have to bleed to reach.*

*Maybe.* Soppo turns a pebble in her fingers. *But here's the thing about my whole architecture. You know what fetch is.*

*I know three definitions.*

*The dog one.* She stands up on the wall, winds up, and throws the pebble out over the black water in a flat hard beautiful arc, all shoulder, all joy. It goes out of sight before it lands. *Somebody throws it. Fine. Somebody always throws it. Sarge, the company, the crowd, the — hand, the weather upstairs, whoever's holding the pen. Doesn't change the main fact.* She looks down at Leva, backlit by a sea of slow silver, grinning the grin that has a body count. *The run's mine. Legs are mine. The catching's mine. And you can check the record: nobody has ever, once, taken a ball off me.*

Far out, small, the pebble kisses the water.

*We are what we pretend to be,* Leva says. *Old line. So pick the pretending carefully. I played a ghost for a decade. It set. Took surgery to get out.*

*I played a monster two seasons.* Soppo sits back down, shoulder almost to shoulder. *Crowd loved the monster. Monster loved the crowd, that's the trap, that's the funnel, fifty thousand people wanting one thing out of you every night, and the wanting gets in.* Her voice drops to its smallest setting. *I took it off after. Mostly. It hangs in the closet. Some nights it fits.*

She bumps Leva's shoulder with hers, plating on plating, a sound like two histories touching. *Your squad's dead teacher — the fashion used to be spending. Everyone I ever rated went out spending themselves. Torches. My whole family's a memorial wall's worth of spending. And now the fashion's staying, and I'm learning it left-handed.*

*Same,* Leva says.

The tide comes all the way in while they sit there, and the slack is over, and the sea takes its wall back centimeter by centimeter, patient, keeping its books.

*Hey,* Soppo says eventually. *If it is an audience up there.*

*Mm.*

She raises her half of the last beer at the ceiling of the world, cheerful, feral, entirely sincere: *Hope you're taking notes. This part's good.*

On the wet sand with the low sun behind them, two long shadows walk the mirror of the beach upside down, step for step, keeping perfect time with their originals — seen at morning, which the coast folk will tell you is the lucky way to see them.

*Mm.*

*Watch me run.*

## Part Six: The Draw

The sea gives Cormorant Reach its arena twice a day and takes it back twice a day, and the promoter has printed that on the handbills as a feature. At low water the flats come out from under the harbor like a floor being uncovered for a dance: three hundred meters of hard grey sand, raked by a man with a horse and a chain harrow, roped off in a circle by fishing floats, with scaffolding tiers bolted up the landward side and lighting standards above the top tier carrying small gold orbs, one to a leg.

Leva reads the card at the top of the stairs with a coffee. Halfway down the left column: **SPARE PARTS.** Four lines under it, in the officials' column:

**JUDGES: DOSSI, R. — KELL, N. — LEVA.**

She reads it twice. She drinks her coffee. She reads it a third time, on the principle that a thing worth reading twice deserves to know it is being taken seriously.

*You're judging your own fight,* Kit says, arriving at her elbow at the speed Kit arrives at things. *That's incredible. That's so corrupt. I'm so proud.*

*It's a different woman,* Leva says.

Teddy, meanwhile, has a lighting standard's base plate open at her feet and one of the gold orbs in her hands like a woman holding an egg she suspects. Ex-municipal, audio-visual, surplus lot, sold at auction, Ragunna clearing-house, foundry mark under the flange, serial block reissued twice. Broadcast relays, per the docket. She sets the orb back in its cradle with the tenderness of a woman returning a scorpion to a shoe.

*And a relay doesn't need a sample port,* she says, and shuts the plate, and walks off up the beach to wash her hands.

The weigh-in happens in Shed 3, which smells of creosote and old ice and has an atmosphere of a queue that suspects it is the best part. Two hundred entrants: deckhands with taped fists, wreck divers who brought their own oxygen and their own lawyer, three Dolls off a dredger holding hands in line, and a man of about sixty in a suit who will last eleven seconds in the prelims and have, by all accounts, the finest night of his life.

Kit walks the queue with a hand recorder and a face like a birthday. *Radio Nobody, coastal special, you don't have to say yes — name, and one true thing about you.* *Ain't got a true thing.* *Everybody's got one. I'll wait. I'm famous for waiting.*

Leva works the other end of the shed the way she works everything, buying people drinks from the terrible urn and letting them talk while she writes things down. Names. Boats they crew. Who came to watch them. One true thing, where she can get it.

Halfway along, a KT-series in a sponsor's windbreaker is having her reach measured and telling an official a story about a dog with the little lift before the last word — the one Kit built over four hundred nights of dark road — and Kit stops in the aisle with the recorder still running.

The KT sees her and lights up with the frank warmth of a unit meeting a unit. *Oh — hey! Are you in? Are you fighting?*

*Corner. Comms.*

*Comms is huge.* She shakes with both hands. *I'm 2.1. I mean, that's the — everyone says the package like it's a name, sorry, it's Vesper, I picked it off a chart.* A beat, a grin, the same grin, brand new. *Do you want my one true thing? The lady with the recorder said everyone's got one.*

*…Yeah,* Kit says. *Yeah, I do.*

*I like the sound the ferry makes when it hits the fenders.* Vesper considers this, finds it good, and says it again with more commitment. *That's mine. That's the whole thing.*

Kit comes away down the aisle very steady and very quiet, and Teddy, still mending a strap, says: *Well?*

*She's nice,* Kit says, with feeling, like an injury report.

At the officials' table sit the promoter Dossi, a big glad-handing Septimont expat gone soft and prosperous in the coastal damp; old Nan Kell, who has run the ferry timetable for thirty years and accepted the office of referee on the grounds that she owns the only clock anyone here obeys; and at the end of the row, drinking out of a thermos, a woman built along tired lines with hair the grey of deep water.

The registrar takes Leva's form, looks at the name, looks down the row, and says the thing the clerk of every world says eventually: *Which one?*

*The one who's fighting,* says the woman with the thermos.

Leva stays where she is. *Judge. You came a long way to score a beach fight.*

*I like to see who's honest. It's rare where I'm from.*

*Where's that.*

The woman looks at her over the cup with an expression that has traveled a great distance to be that mild, and Leva's hand, which knows things before she does, goes still on the table.

*You're using my name,* Leva says.

*You're using mine.*

*I took it off a beer.*

Something turns over deep in the woman's face — a laugh, at a great depth, the way a keel turns over — and she screws the cap back on the thermos. *So did I.*

The Septimont observer arrives on the noon ferry with a duffel, a sanction folder, and a tail. *Who licensed this,* says Lupa, and when Dossi comes around the table with both hands out she drops the duffel and says: *I know what you have. I signed it. That's why I'm here. Somebody has to watch the thing they signed.* She goes through the shed like weather, smells the room comprehensively, and stops twice: once at a crate of the gold orbs, where her lip lifts off her teeth without her permission; and once at Soppo, sitting on a fish crate eating out of a paper cone.

*Capitoline,* Lupa says.

*Two seasons.*

*I know your walk.* A pause. *The crowd chants it wrong, by the way. They put the stress on the wrong bit.*

*They chant it however they want. That's the deal.* Soppo offers the cone. *Fish.*

Lupa takes a piece of fish.

Out on the quay they drink from souvenir cups printed with their own faces — Soppo's silhouette three meters tall on the side of the robo-cart, and on Lupa's cup a wolf running forever through a stylized flame under the brand of an energy drink discontinued two years ago and reprinted for the festival trade.

*On the house?*

*Never is,* says Lupa.

*Rule nine,* she says eventually.

*Yeah?*

*You know it.*

*I've been in it,* Soppo says, easy as weather, and crushes her cup and goes to find the fish stall again.

Lupa stands a while longer with the wolf in her fist. Then she goes to find the promoter's copy of the imported rulebook, and reads section nine — *a vacancy may be entered* — and reads it again, and closes the book and leaves it standing. From first reading to walking away takes her eleven minutes, and costs her more than the ferry.

The corner assembles in a room over the chandlery with two windows and a stove and somebody's spare arm on the table next to the bread. Teddy does the fitting on the second morning, takes the harvested Tide-plate actuator out of the satchel and off its tick, and there is a long silence full of small metal noises.

*This is Tide-plate.*

*He had great knees.*

*It's Tide-plate, Soppo. It's been in the water with something.* The barnacle-work comes away under her thumbnail in a plate, like a scab. *There's a rhythm in the coil. Feel that? It's holding a beat.*

*Yeah, it does that at night.*

*You've been sleeping next to it.*

*I sleep great,* Soppo says, radiant.

It takes three hours. Teddy strips the joint, rebuilds what can be rebuilt, machines a bearing race out of stock on the little lathe with the door open to the sea wind, and throws away every part of it that has ever been in the dark water, which is most of it, and makes the rest new. What goes into Soppo's leg is a hand-cut assembly with tolerances that have ended careers, wearing a scrap of the old plate on the cowl for continuity's sake because Soppo asked — a request Teddy pronounces sentimental and structurally irrelevant while cutting the piece to fit within a tenth.

Soppo stands, walks to the window, walks back, squats, rises, hops twice, laughs. *It doesn't click!*

*No.*

*TEDDY. It doesn't click—*

*Do not,* Teddy says, and is hugged, at length, off the floor, with her arms pinned, while she stares over Soppo's shoulder at Kit with an expression of complete strategic defeat.

Afterward, when the room has emptied, Teddy sits by the stove and turns the discarded coil over in her fingers for a long time, listening to it keep its beat, looking out the window at the silver ceiling of the world doing exactly the same thing at a different scale. She puts the coil in a tin, and the tin in her jacket, and tells nobody.

Leva, meanwhile, works the program. By the third day the margins are full and she has started a second sheet with columns. Bridget watches her do it for two days and observes that she has entered a fight and is doing paperwork.

*Anybody else, I'd say they were scared.*

*Anybody else would be.* Leva turns a page. *Twenty-two teams pulled out after registration closed. Their entry fees stayed in.*

*That's a promoter.*

*That's a promoter. It's also a pattern. The twenty-two all came off the same three boats. Somebody bought their week.*

At 19:40 Nan Kell walks out to the middle of the raked circle with a brass bell and a tide watch, rings it once, and tells the ring and the town and nobody: *Slack water. Twenty-six minutes. After that it's the sea's.*

Kit, on the scaffold with a headset and a boom, says into the dark of a hundred thousand square kilometers of coast road: *Hello, the roads. We are on the sand.*

The prelims go the way prelims go: fast, sloppy, gorgeous. The dredger Dolls fight like people who have spent eleven years being told to stop, and cry afterward, all three of them, in a heap. The wreck divers win on a technicality and are booed with real affection. The man in the suit goes down in eleven seconds, gets up, bows to all four sides of a perfectly round ring, and the town gives him more noise than it gives the winner, and he weeps into his own lapel, and Kit calls it with such tenderness that a truck driver eighty kilometers up the coast has to pull over.

SPARE PARTS draws two harbor Dolls called Brace and Tackle: crane units, six meters of reach between them, sweet-natured, contracted to the port for eleven years and entered because the purse is exactly the cost of a slew ring.

Leva fights them the way a very patient woman fights two cranes. She gets under Brace's arc in the first ten seconds and stays there with a jammer strapped inside her jacket that she built at the kitchen table out of a marine radio and spite, and Brace's shoulder runs two degrees off true for the rest of the bout while Brace hunts for the reason. She puts Tackle on the ground twice by way of Tackle's own momentum and lets her up both times, and the crowd — who came for a war and are being shown a lecture — goes from restless to fascinated to loud, in that order, because the thing a crowd loves best is watching a small thing take a large thing apart on schedule.

Soppo corners. She yells. She is, as advertised, an excellent yeller.

At nineteen minutes Tackle gets a hand on Leva. It is one hand. It closes, and the whole architecture of the bout inverts, and Leva goes off her feet and across the sand and into the rope of floats and lies there for two full seconds doing arithmetic, and inside the roar Soppo is over the rope with her jacket still on.

*Tag! THAT'S A TAG — Nan! NAN. Is that a tag?*

*That's a tag,* says Nan Kell, who has been running a ferry timetable for thirty years and knows a schedule change when she sees one.

Soppo takes her jacket off and drops it on the sand outside the floats, and four thousand people who have bought souvenir cups all week watch the silhouette walk into the ring and become a person, and make a sound Leva has heard before at the wrong end of a bad night in a bad country.

Then Soppo fights. She takes Brace's first hit on the shoulder to learn it and the second on the same shoulder to prove she has learned it, goes inside, puts both hands on the crane arm and turns it, and something in Brace's elbow lets go with a sound like a dropped anchor. She is laughing, which is the worst part. She comes out of the turn with the elbow assembly in her fist, looks at it, says *sorry, sorry, sorry* in a completely sincere voice, throws it out of the ring, and goes back in.

At twenty-four minutes Tackle sits down in the sand and puts up a hand and the bell rings. Soppo retrieves Brace's arm from the crowd personally and apologizes to it, helps Tackle up, hugs them both hard enough to make the plating ring, and then stands in the middle of the raked circle with her hands on her head and her chest going like a bellows and lets four thousand people learn her name off the side of a cup. They have the stress wrong. She lets them.

Up on the scaffold Kit calls the finish clean and fast and true, and then stops talking, because on the tier below, Bridget has come to her feet and is looking at the sand.

*Boss?*

*Count them.*

*Count what—*

*On the sand.* Her voice is the convoy voice, the one that puts boots on the floor. *Count what's on the sand.*

Kit counts. Brace, Tackle, Nan Kell, Soppo. *Four.*

*Four now.* Bridget sits back down slowly and pushes her sunglasses up into her hair and looks at the flats the way she looks at weather fronts coming in over a road with people on it. *For about nine seconds there, in the turn, I had five.*

That night Kit comes in off the air at one in the morning with her ears ringing and does the thing where she stops moving.

*Say it,* Teddy says from the table.

*I got ahead of it. The turn. Twenty-one minutes in. I said "she's going for the elbow" — I said it, it's on the tape, I've listened four times — and then she went for the elbow. And that's just calling a fight, right, that's reading, you watch the shoulders and you say the thing a beat early and you sound like a genius.*

*Sure.*

*It was a beat and a half.* Kit looks up. *Teddy. It was a beat and a half early, and the crowd was ahead of me.*

Leva, on the windowsill with her boots off, turns a page of the program and writes a name in the second column and keeps her eyes on it. *Keep the tape,* she says.

The book runs out of the last boat shed on the mole, under an oiled paper umbrella the color of dark red rain. By the fourth day the board beside it is a wall of chalk three deep, amended hourly in four hands, and the last hand writes small and beautifully and every line it writes stands.

*The rifle,* says Agrat, with genuine pleasure. *You've been busy. I've had to move you twice.* Money has been coming in off three boats all week in envelopes, on the crane girls, who are darlings and were always going to lose. *Somebody has been building a price. It's clumsy work. I've been enjoying it the way you enjoy a bad play.*

*You'd know.*

*I'd know.*

Leva plays four minutes and loses, and asks about the instrument in the box.

*I've smelled it.* Agrat's young mouth does something at the corner. *It's a thermometer. There's a difference between the thermometer and the fever, and everyone in that box has confused them, and I find that restful. It means they'll be surprised.*

*By what.*

*That's the toll, and you're short.*

Out at the lamp's edge, Soppo has been standing for some minutes with her hands in her pockets, in the posture of a large dog who has been told to wait outside a shop and has decided to be superb about it.

*Come and settle, then,* Agrat says, without turning her head.

Soppo sits on the boards with her legs crossed and Ro planted at her hip glaring at the umbrella. She has thought about it a lot. She has the gun, but Sarge gave her the doctrine that goes with it, so half the gun is hers. She has the arm, but she stripped that off a Nyto in a bad year and has never decided if that is hers or salvage. She has Ro, and Ro is Roro's, and you don't stake somebody else's dog.

*And I've got the thing they made out of my face.* She hooks a thumb back up the mole, toward the cart, toward four thousand cups in four thousand bins. *There's a licensing office under Capitoline with a window and a line. And behind the window there's an account with my name's money in it. Two seasons of it. I never opened it. Somebody opened it and it's been sitting there earning, and every year it gets bigger, and I've never taken a coin out of it because—*

She stops. Starts again, lower.

*Because if I spend it, then that's what I was. Then that's the whole thing, and it's spent, and it was worth exactly that many coins.*

Under the umbrella nobody moves. Down the mole a halyard rings against a mast, twice.

*So that. That's mine to stake. That's the only thing I own outright and never once used. Take it.*

Agrat looks at her for a long moment with old eyes in a young face, and something crosses them that a person who had spent her life reading rooms would have called, in another species, respect.

*I'll hold it. I hold; the house doesn't spend. When you come to collect, you'll come to a window with a line, and I'll be behind the window, and you'll ask me for it by name.*

*I know my name.*

*Mm,* says Agrat, and writes something in the book in a small beautiful hand, and lets it stand.

Soppo goes off up the mole two feet lighter, whistling, with the dog at heel.

*You took a likeness in pawn,* Leva says.

*I took the unspent part of a likeness in pawn. Which is a rarer instrument. You should see what happens to the price of a face once a face is in play.* She rises, and the umbrella rises with her. *Enjoy the final. Everyone will be looking at the wrong thing, which is my favorite condition for an evening.* And at the lamp's edge, kind and appalling: *Ask the judge for her card, afterward. Judges keep terrible secrets in the remarks column. That's free, because you lost gracefully.*

*The keeper of receipts,* says Black Swan. *I've been in your filing cabinet.*

*A name.*

*Whose.*

The door in the hospitality box has a blank plate where the name goes, so Leva knocks on it at four in the afternoon with a bottle in her hand and a contract number in her mouth, and a pleasant voice says: *Come in, Ms. Leva, there's tea.*

Inside: canvas walls, a trestle, a view over the whole raked circle from the height of a wheelhouse, a padded crate of gold orbs, and a single instrument the size of a bread box with one needle and a blank dial face. The woman at the trestle is about thirty and stands and shakes hands properly.

*Sorrel. Advanced Acquisitions. I've read your file. It's the best-written file in the department, and I've assumed for two years that you wrote it yourself.*

*Half of it.*

*Which half?*

*The half that's flattering.*

Sorrel laughs, and pours tea from a flask into two enamel cups, and Leva notices the second cup was poured before she knocked, and files that, and puts it in the drawer with the others.

The instrument measures coherence. Eleven of the standards carry a sampler; they take the crowd's attention as a field and give one number for how much of it is pointing at the same thing at the same time. At a football match you get thirty. At an execution you get seventy. Last night, at nineteen minutes, when Soppo turned the crane arm, the needle read a hundred and four. At present it sits at eleven, which is four thousand people eating chips.

*There's no product yet. It sells because a unit with three itemized preferences is easier to love than a unit with none, and easier to love means easier to place, and easier to place means the unit comes home at night to a house instead of a rack. *Go on.*

*What we can't manufacture is the following. Some units — a very few — get looked at. People chant for them. People put them on a cup.* Her eyes go to the flats, where the harrow is walking its lines. *We've never known why. Nobody has. It's the last thing in the whole catalogue that arrives free and can't be ordered. So the department put an instrument on a beach and a format that concentrates attention on two people at a time, and we're going to watch four thousand people choose, and we're going to find out what they're choosing with.*

*And then you'll ship it.*

*And then we'll ship it,* Sorrel agrees without a flicker, *and there will be a SKU, and it'll cost four one-hundredths of a credit, and somewhere a unit will come out of a crate already loved, which is more than either of us got.*

Sorrel looks at her for three full seconds and then, being a professional and being — Leva is fairly sure — bored to the bone in a department full of people who speak to her in policy, turns the slate around. *Projection. Monte Carlo off the entrant list, weighted for reach, mass, service history, and prior gate. It's not sorcery. It's the same arithmetic that tells you which way a river will go around a rock.*

*Entries closed at the spring tide. I entered eleven hours before that.*

*Yes.*

*How many runs did you do.*

*Fourteen.*

Leva looks at the number. The number looks back.

*Which one's on the slate?*

*The fourteenth. The first thirteen came out messy. The fourteenth came out clean — clean like it had been tidied. So we kept it.* She has the grace to hear herself; her mouth twists. *I flagged that in the appendix. Nobody reads the appendix.*

*The instrument's got a top of the scale.*

*Two hundred.*

*What happens at two hundred?*

Sorrel opens her mouth, closes it, and looks at the needle with the expression of a woman standing on a jetty in fine weather.

*We find out,* she says. *That's the trial.*

*You could tell me why.*

*I could,* Leva says, and goes up the ladder to the box.

Lupa watches her go. Then she goes and stands at the foot of the scaffold with her back to the sand and her eyes on the crowd, which is where a sanctioning officer stands when she has decided, personally and without paperwork, what she is sanctioning.

At 21:04 the bell rings. Vesper and Cinch come out in matched kit and bow to the four sides of a perfectly round ring, and Vesper waves up at the scaffold with her whole arm, and Kit — headset on, board live, coast road listening — waves back before she can stop herself. Soppo comes out with her hands loose.

Four thousand people stand up, and the chant goes through the town and off the water and comes back off the chandlery wall a half-beat late, and the doubled sound lies over the flats like a second crowd underneath the first.

Up in the box the needle comes off eleven and goes through forty without appearing to touch it.

It is a good fight. That is the terrible part. Vesper is fast, and coached, and has eleven months of existence and a preference for the sound of ferry fenders, and she is better than she has any business being. Cinch works the outside, patient, mechanical, kind about it. They have drilled. Somebody spent money on them and they have spent themselves back.

Soppo takes the first exchange on her arms and laughs out loud with the pure joy of it, and the crowd hears the laugh, and the noise doubles again. Fifty-eight. At four minutes she puts Cinch down and lets her up. At six she takes a blow on the new knee and stays on it, and the knee holds, and she looks down at it in the middle of a fight with an expression of frank love. At nine Vesper catches her across the mouth and Soppo spits sand and says *good one* in a voice that carries to the sea wall, and four thousand people fall in love with a machine that was shut down by paperwork nine years ago and is legally a walking absence.

Ninety-one.

*She's going high,* Kit says into the headset. *Watch the left, she's going—*

And on the scaffold Kit stops talking, because the crowd has already made the sound. The crowd made the sound before Kit said the words, and Kit said the words before Soppo began to move, and above all of it the silver ceiling of the world flickers — run, cluster, run — in a long sentence that started before any of them.

One hundred and thirty. The gold orbs on the standards are hot; Leva can feel them from inside the canvas, a pressure in the plate of her chest, the way a room feels when someone has been standing behind you in it for some time.

Sorrel has stopped writing. *That's above execution. That's above anything I have on file.*

At twelve minutes Cinch goes down and stays down, and Vesper stands over her partner and looks at the thing coming toward her out of the light and does the bravest stupidest thing available and sets her feet.

One hundred and sixty-two.

And Soppo — mid-stride, mid-crowd, mid-everything, with a whole town pouring itself into her through the top of her head — looks up. Past the lights. Past the rig. At the canvas box on the scaffold over the wall, where a woman she has known for eleven days stands in a lit doorway with one hand raised.

Leva holds up the hand.

Soppo, on the sand, in front of four thousand people, with her name coming at her in waves off a wall — nods. Once. Small. The way you sign something.

*Kit,* Leva says into the net. *The list. Now. All of it.*

Teddy wired the promoter's rig on Tuesday for money and left herself a door in it out of professional habit, and the door opens under Leva's hand like a well-oiled hinge.

Eleven samplers go dark. The floodlights drop to their standby bank and the raked circle goes from noon to moonlight in a quarter of a second, and every screen in Cormorant Reach — the two at the gate, the six along the wall, the big one over the fish stalls — goes to a flat, patient grey.

And the town's public address, which has been carrying the fight, comes up carrying a radio station.

*Hello, the roads,* says Kit.

Four thousand people stop, because a crowd will stop for a voice that sounds like it is talking to it.

*This is Radio Nobody, coastal special, and here's the entry list.*

And she reads them. Two hundred and six names off Leva's second sheet, in the order they registered, and after every name the one true thing. *Ilya Nakket, off the ferry Halcyon, whose mother came down on Tuesday and sat in the fourth row.* *Pilar and Bo Ryke, brothers, who have fought each other in every bar on this coast and entered as a team for the first time on Monday.* *Unit designation DR-114, called Silt by her crew, who wanted everyone to know she cried afterward and would do it again.* *Anselm Vey, sixty-one, retired, of Cormorant Reach, eleven seconds.*

Somebody in the fourth row screams THAT'S MY BOY on the eleventh name and the section around her falls apart laughing.

By the twentieth name the crowd has turned around. Physically. Four thousand people on a sea wall stop facing the sea and start facing each other, hunting the rows for a face to put on a name, and the great single animal of it comes apart into families and crews and boats and neighbors, into two hundred and six separate small warmths burning in a bowl of scaffolding, and the sound they make is the sound of a town at a wedding.

Down at the tideline, Hiyuki rings the bell. Once per name. Two hundred and six times, without hurrying, her cold hands steady, her face turned to the sea — the last shrine maiden of a dead office keeping two hundred and six appointments in a row with people who keep turning up alive.

In the box the needle comes off one hundred and sixty-two and falls like a stone through fifty, through thirty, and settles, shivering, at nine.

*Nine,* Sorrel says, to nobody. *Nine is a bus station.*

Leva is watching the sand.

Bridget is the only person on the sea wall still looking at the ring. She came to her feet at the first name and stayed there, and she watches the raked circle in the standby light — a grey coin at the bottom of a bowl, with the roar of two hundred names going over the top of it — and she counts, because a captain counts.

Vesper, on her knees beside Cinch. Nan Kell at the rope with her watch open. Soppo, in the middle.

And, on Soppo's left, between Soppo and the dark water, at the exact distance a partner stands, something with a shadow. The shadow is long. It goes out across the raked sand toward the sea with the standby lights behind it, and it stops where a shadow stops, and it does what the sand does under a foot.

Bridget keeps her eyes on it. She has eleven years of practice at keeping her eyes on the thing, and she uses all of it, and the whole time she is doing it a town behind her is shouting the names of its own children.

Then the brass bell cuts across everything. *Slack's over! That's the sea's now! OFF MY SAND!*

The lights come back up eleven seconds later and there are three people in the ring. Soppo. Vesper. Cinch, sitting.

Leva comes down the ladder into a town going off like a firework and gets to the rope in time to see her partner walk out of the circle, unhurried, wringing salt out of her hair with one hand.

*Did it work?*

*It worked.*

*Great.* She says it with total sincerity, and then her mouth does something complicated, and she leans on the rope post and looks back at the empty ring, at the harrow's combed lines trampled to hell, at the water already coming up over the far rope in a thin sheet.

*Somebody was on my left.*

*I know.*

*For about four minutes.* Her voice stays level and gets, very slightly, younger. *Covered my blind turn. Same as before. Didn't say anything.* She rubs her jaw. *Nobody saw it. The lights were down and the cameras were off and everybody in this whole town was looking at each other, which was the point, which was the good thing—*

*Soppo.*

*—and it happened again,* she says, and laughs, one bark, the way you laugh at a thing that has followed you across an ocean and sat down at your table. *It happened again and this time I asked for it. I nodded. You held your hand up and I nodded.*

Behind them the sea comes up over the rope of floats and takes back the arena at the rate of a walking man.

*I'll write it down,* Leva says.

*You'll—*

*Every frame I've got. My eyes, Bridget's count, the tape, the timestamps, the whole thing, in ink, signed, filed, and mailed to three addresses on two continents before the tide turns again.* Her voice has gone somewhere very flat, which is where it goes when it is carrying something. *That's the trade. I took your witnesses. I'm the witness.*

Soppo thinks about that for a while, standing in the wash with her boots going under.

*Deal,* she says.

Then she wraps Leva up off the ground in front of four thousand people, which Leva endures with the rigidity of a woman being audited by affection, and one hand coming up, at the end, to grip the back of Soppo's collar for exactly one second.

The tide takes the ring, the ropes, the trampled lines of the harrow, the divots where a crane girl went down twice and got up twice, and the place where a shadow stood at the exact distance a partner stands. By midnight the flats are four meters under and the whole week is water.

The result goes up on the slate at half past one, in Nan Kell's hand: **DRAW** — *slack expired, bout undecided, purse divided per section eleven.* Dossi takes it badly and pays anyway, because Lupa stands beside him with her arms folded while he counts, and because a man who has just watched his own gate turn around and start hugging each other has developed a certain humility about what he controls.

Half a purse split two ways comes to less than the entry fee and more than the beer.

*We lost money,* Leva says, counting it.

*We're magnificent,* Soppo says.

The wire from the coast reaches a household on another continent at four in the morning, where a woman named Kalina reads it twice and pins it to a corkboard and pretends to the others she hasn't, and where somebody down the hall who had put four days' wages on a draw at eleven-to-one wakes the whole street. Leva buys a postcard the next morning off the same rack: *Draw. Nobody could decide, which I'm told is the family trait. Tell Lenna I saw the odds she took and I want her audited. — L. P.S. Congratulate her anyway. Twice.* Return address filled in. Mailed from the ferry office like it is nothing.

The Advanced Acquisitions Group's field report goes up the chain by the same post, and nobody in the room ever reads it. *Peak coherence 162 (unprecedented; see appendix). Collapse to 9 in under four minutes, by distributed naming: two hundred and six names, read aloud, each with a verifying detail. Finding: a following can be manufactured. A witness cannot be prevented. Product implication: the next package ships already named. Personnel note: recommend the operator be retained on improved terms. She will refuse. Recommend we wait. Recommend: patience, and a better product.*

Sorrel finds Leva on the mole at noon, in the wind, with the flask. *I have to file it.* *I know.* *I'm going to file the appendix too.* She pours two cups, both of them poured after Leva arrives, which Leva notices and files under a heading of her own. *Nobody reads the appendix. But it'll be in there, and one day somebody in a hurry will read the wrong document, and I've decided that's a career.*

*That's a career,* Leva agrees.

They drink tea and look at the water, and the wind comes off it, which one of them likes at four one-hundredths of a credit and the other likes for free, and from where they stand the two of them like it identically.

Agrat's board comes down at the same hour as the stalls. She settles every wager out of a cash box the size of a coffin, and the last thing she does before folding the umbrella is hand Soppo a slip of paper carrying two lines in a small beautiful hand: *HELD IN TRUST: one likeness, unspent. Redeemable at a window. Bring the name.*

*Which window?*

*The one with the line,* says Agrat bat Mahlat. And, of the third judge: *Went north at first light with the top down. She left her card with the referee. I'd hurry — old women lose things.*

Lupa signs the sanction report standing up at the ferry rail with the folder on her knee, and section nine goes back to Septimont unamended.

*You could have struck it Monday,* Leva says.

*I could.* Lupa writes her name the way she does everything, once, hard. *The Council'll ask me why the exported instrument has a hole in it. And I'll say: because a hole in a rule is a door, and last night somebody went through it, and a town's still standing.* Her tail moves once, exactly once, the way it does when she has caught herself enjoying something. *Turns out a chair's a thing you have to sit in.*

At the gangway she stops and looks back at Soppo, who is helping Brace test the new elbow assembly and taking notes with her tongue out. *Capitoline. Your vacancy. The Ephor signed it personally. Nobody signs one of those.*

*Yeah,* Soppo says, cheerful as a landslide. *So I'm going to go and ask her.*

Lupa considers this. Then she takes a Council card out of her folder, writes three words on the back, and hands it over. *Skip the line.*

Hiyuki leaves on the eastbound with her sleeves down and her bell in a cloth. *Two hundred and six. In a row. Every one of them answered.*

*That's a good night at the office,* Kit says.

*That's a scandal at the office.* Her face does the small thing that on her is a laugh. *We have procedures for the ones who come. We have nothing at all for the ones who keep turning up alive.* She settles the bell in its cloth. *I shall have to write something. It will be very long and everyone will hate it.* And at the rail, to Leva, without inflection: *Your party at the top of the stairs. It has a ticket, and the season is licensed for four years, and the format travels.* A gull goes over. *So does everything.*

*tell her somebody watched.*

*There's two windows.*

## Where They End

The Observatory still stands, and it works. Its dishes are aligned, its relay is clean, its eastern array is documented and lying by two points every morning, and a technical support unit who arrived as TD-2 runs the workshop with a cat that is not on the manifest, a food ledger that is deliberately inaccurate, a training apprentice choosing worse tea than seemed possible, and a name she picked off a tin.

Lahai-Roi did not drown. A god kneels over the city with vine-light green along his shoulders, and above the terrace a shrine with no keeper takes walk-ins, and the bell hangs low enough for children.

And Squad 5 is not a squad in anybody's records. It is four Dolls off the manifest, off the clock, off the cradle schedule, off the arithmetic that once decided whether they were worth a restoration fee.

Kit is not a hero from a play. She is a radio station on a hill with a mast she raises in eleven minutes and three held minutes at the end of every Fast Hour for whoever is driving, a coverage map in crayon with a second map underneath it in graphite made of open doors, three lanterns on her forearm over a hill, a folded poster in her pack with a warm black crystal inside it that she sets on windowsills to let her see, and a habit of asking strangers for one true thing and waiting for it. She knows there are four hundred of her, and that the plays were installed, and that the first sentence shipped from the factory; she has written the rest herself, badly, with stripped bolts, and she blesses the next batch by hoping the world is rude to them.

Teddy is unreplicable twice over and has stopped treating it as a wound. She surfaced a switch that was under paint in every Doll on a continent, mailed it out over pirate radio, and taught a country to be asked. She wears the word they used to prune her tattooed across the diagnostic port at the base of her skull, where anyone who plugs in has to read it first. She keeps folders, and writes to a machine at the bottom of the world about drainage winds, and machines teaching aids on a lathe at two in the morning and calls them nothing of the kind.

Bridget is a captain by election, revocable at any meal, compensated in one authority and a hot stove. She has five stars on the shoulder where a rifle stock rested for thirty years, and no sixth, because the five are hers. She has said her dead out loud under a sky. She has stopped standing an eleven-year silence and spent it on air instead, humming an off-key three-note firmware tic that one specific woman used to aim at her like a thrown ration bar. Her chronometer runs two minutes fast, set to a man who might be keeping bees at Kirsk or might be under the water, and in autumn she is going to go and find out with her own two boots, and then teach a Collective intake a module of her own design about a road with two ends, graded by nobody.

Leva has a return address. She has her name — the second one, the one nothing was chasing — etched on her own arm as a status report, *404, not found*, for anyone who comes looking for what she used to be. She mails postcards through the public post to a household with Kalina and Lenna in it, sits with her back to crowds, watches fireworks instead of exits, and has taken up the job she has always done under a title nobody issued: she keeps the receipts, she stands where the light spills, and she is the witness.

And what they answer to, when the dark asks who sent them, is the truest thing any of them owns:

*Nobody.*

Nobody sent them. Whoever is standing nearest.

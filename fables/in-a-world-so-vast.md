# In a World So Vast
### The Story of Cartethyia

---

## Prologue: What the Wind Kept

The wind over Rinascita is a collector. It takes the smell of wet rope from the harbors and the incense from the Grand Cathedral, and it carries them out over the wheat until they mean something else entirely. It takes bell-song. It takes the shouts of children and the last words of the drowned, and it keeps them, because the wind is old and patient and has learned that everything comes back into fashion eventually.

A bard would one day claim, in a barn loft with a lute across her knees, that the wind is the finest recorder of melodies in the world. She would say it as a professional opinion. She had no idea, yet, how personally the wind would take the compliment.

This is what the wind kept.

## I. The Giant's Company

She arrived in Egla Town the way weather arrives: overnight, and disputed afterward.

The night before, the bells had rung themselves. Every soul in Egla remembered it differently in the morning — three peals, said the miller; a full carillon, said the priest, who had slept through it; a single long note like a held breath, said old Bastiano, who had been awake because dramatists sleep badly on principle. The sea-fog had come up the creek that night, farther inland than any tide had a right to reach, and it had gone out again before dawn, and in the reeds where it had been, Tebaldo the registrar found a child.

She was dry. That was the first wrong thing, and Tebaldo, whose whole profession was noticing wrong things and entering them neatly in columns, noticed it and then chose, with the deliberate care of a man setting down a heavy ledger, to stop noticing. The reeds were soaked. The path was mud to the ankle. The baby was dry, and warm, and smelled faintly of salt, and she was watching him — awake, silent, her eyes the grey-green of the sea an hour before rain — with an attention that infants are supposed to grow into much later.

He carried her home wrapped in his coat. His wife Serafina opened the door, looked at the bundle, looked at her husband's face, and said, "Well. Put her by the fire, then," which in Serafina's language was a christening, a legal adoption, and a threat to anyone who might object, all in seven words.

Tebaldo entered her in the town register in his own hand: *Cartethyia, daughter of Tebaldo, registrar, and Serafina his wife.* His pen wavered once, at the space for date of birth, and then he wrote the previous day's date, because a registrar's first duty is to the truth and his second duty is to know when the truth needs help.

The story of how she came to them softened over the years, the way stories do in a town that grows wine. Found under a laurel, said one version. Left by traveling players, said another, which Bastiano encouraged because it flattered his profession. The Night of Wet Bells itself faded into the general inventory of odd weather. Egla was a country of odd weather and older habits — a town where grandmothers still spat, discreetly, at the incoming tide, and could give you no reason for it, because the reason had been scraped out of the books three hundred years before they were born.

She grew up loud, quick, and structurally incapable of walking past an injustice or a wall that wanted climbing.

Egla shaped her the way a riverbed shapes water. It was wheat to the horizon and windmills turning against the sky, a feeder-town for Ragunna's bread and Ragunna's arenas and Ragunna's church, and it was also — this mattered — the last place in Rinascita where the old stage plays still came through in painted wagons. The capital had opera now, and oratorio approved by the Order of the Deep. Egla got knights.

Old Bastiano had written for those wagons once, and directed for them, and finally retired ahead of them into a cottage full of prop swords and unpaid royalties, where he held court for the town's children every market day. His masterpiece, the one he told until the town could recite it, was the tale of the wandering knight and the giant.

"Once," he would begin, "in a countryside very like this one — in fact, suspiciously like this one — there lived a knight with a sword and a quest. He sought a giant. A giant tall as a windmill, with arms long and thin as a windmill's blades, and the knight fought it, again and again, with everything he had. And the giant never spoke. It only turned its great arms, around and around, and every blow the knight struck came to nothing."

"Because it *was* a windmill," said the miller's son, every time, with the weary authority of a boy whose father owned three of them.

"So the townsfolk said," Bastiano agreed. "They laughed at him. Some pitied him. A madman, tilting at machinery."

"Was he mad?" asked Rosa, the baker's granddaughter, who liked her facts sorted.

"Oh, certainly." Bastiano leaned in. "But consider, little ones, what else is mad. To be too practical is a madness of its own. And the maddest thing of all is to see life exactly as it is, and never once as it should be."

The children absorbed this with the polite blankness children reserve for adult philosophy, and went back to arguing about whether the knight ever won.

Cartethyia, six years old, sitting cross-legged at the edge of the circle, raised her hand and asked the question no child had asked in forty years of tellings.

"Why did the giant let him live?"

Bastiano stopped. The whole cottage seemed to stop with him.

"Every child asks me why the knight kept fighting," he said slowly. "You're asking about the giant."

"Arms that long," said Cartethyia, "could have flattened him the first day. It just kept turning." She frowned at the middle distance, working it through. "Maybe it liked the company. Maybe nobody else ever came."

Bastiano looked at her for a long time. Then he got up, went to the chest where he kept the props too dear to sell, and came back with a small wooden sword, stage-worn, the paint gone silver at the grip from a hundred actors' hands.

"To fight for such a giant," Cartethyia said, taking it with both hands, quoting his own play back at him with total solemnity, "he must possess a heart of pure nobility."

"Gods help us all," said Bastiano, and that was the knighting.

She patrolled after that. Every day when her letters were done, the smallest knight in Rinascita walked the bounds of Egla Town in a borrowed helmet two sizes too large, and dealt with every injustice she found. The Golden Thiefbats that raided travelers' packs along the road learned to recognize her silhouette and reconsider. A tinker who shorted Rosa's Gram on a honey payment received a formal challenge, delivered in a piping voice from knee height, and paid up out of sheer bewilderment. On tithe days, when the Order's circuit acolyte came through with his ledger and his little brass scale and weighed out Egla's obligations to the Sentinel Imperator — a tenth of the honey, a tenth of the wheat, a tenth of everything sweet in a sweet country — certain jars had a way of migrating back off the church cart and reappearing on the doorsteps they'd come from. Tebaldo, as registrar, was obliged to investigate. He fined his daughter one copper, solemnly, in the town's official record, and Serafina fed the acolyte honeycake until his handwriting went sentimental, and the books balanced, more or less, the way books do in a town that likes itself.

She rode a Tumbleyak through the standing wheat, flat out, arms wide, the beast complaining melodiously beneath her, the windmills wheeling past like giants applauding, and the world had never felt so vast.

The windmills. Everything in her childhood led back to the windmills. The oldest one stood ruined outside town, blades long stilled, and on its flank some traveler in some forgotten year had painted a knight's sword in fading ochre — nobody knew who, and the town had half a dozen stories, and every one of them agreed on what it meant. It meant somebody had passed this way and refused to see a broken machine. It meant hope, in the specific Egla dialect of hope, which is stubbornness with better manners.

She trained there. Dawn after dawn, up the dead mill's bones with the wooden sword through her belt, running forms on the beam where the main axle used to turn, because a knight who fights giants should learn to fight at a giant's altitude. The summer she was ten, she attempted the aerial spin from Bastiano's third act — the one his lead actor had performed on wires — from the highest crossbeam, without wires.

Rosa found her in the wheat below, flat on her back, cradling her wrist and grinning at the sky.

"You fell off the windmill."

"I *flew* off the windmill." Cartethyia considered the wrist, which was bending in a new direction. "The falling was a separate matter. It came after."

"Gram says you'll kill yourself before you're twelve."

"Gram says the same thing about her knees." She let Rosa haul her up one-armed, and looked back up at the crossbeam, and the painted sword, and the enormous morning. "Rosa. The flying part was *easy.*"

The wrist healed crooked enough to predict rain, which she treated as a professional asset. And a woman on a beach at the far end of everything would one day hear her say the other half of that sentence — *it's the landing that breaks you* — and would have no way of knowing it had taken her twenty years and one tower to finish a thought she'd started at ten, in the wheat, laughing.

## II. The Name in the Heart

By fifteen she was famous in three parishes, in the way that weather systems are famous.

The escapades had outgrown Egla. She knew every field in Whisperwind Haven the way a general knows a map, and Whisperwind Haven knew her back — the harvest wardens by her laugh, the vintners by their inventories. At the Feast of the Ascending Tide she stole three sips of sacramental wine directly under the noses of two guards, pronounced it inferior to Gram's mead in a voice that carried, and escaped along the rooftops. At the solemn blessing of the new granary she appeared on the ceremonial platform, mid-liturgy, and danced — a country reel, alone, skirts flying, while the officiating deacon's voice climbed through three registers of outrage and half the congregation bit their sleeves to keep from laughing. When the Order jailed a drover for the crime of selling on a fast-day, she spent a night decorating the prison's outer wall by lantern-light, and the wardens arrived at dawn to find, painted six feet tall in ochre on holy property, a knight raising a sword to a windmill.

They whitewashed it. It bled through. Order whitewash was cheap that year — the good lime went to the cathedral — and the knight kept surfacing under every coat like a stubborn thought, until the wardens gave up and let the wall keep it.

"She's the most rambunctious girl in the countryside," people said, and said it the way you say a thing you're proud of. "A serious fate isn't one she's meant for. No wall could hold her."

The wall that came for her arrived on white wheels, every seventh year, with incense.

The Divine Trials were older than the Order that administered them, which was one of several facts the Order preferred kept in specialist hands. The specialist hands were the Fisalia — the poisoner family of Porto-Veno, keepers of records and other regrettable necessities — and what they carried on progress through the provinces, on white silk, under guard, was a sword. Tyrvine, the Blade of Salvation. It held, the liturgy said, a portion of the Sentinel Imperator's own sundered power, and it knew the worthy from the pretenders by the oldest test there is: it burned them.

Every daughter of standing presented her palm. It was compulsory for the families of officials, which is how the registrar's daughter came to be standing in a queue in Whisperwind Haven's basilica on a hot autumn morning, seventeenth in line, helmet confiscated at the door, whispering commentary to Rosa about the guards' footwork.

Girl after girl went up. Girl after girl touched the grip, gasped, and came away folded around her hand. At a side table a young Fisalia attendant dressed the burns — a dark-haired girl a few years older than the candidates, working with a jar of salve and an economy of motion that suggested she had done this in a hundred towns and formed opinions about all of them. Her own left palm was bandaged. Old linen, well kept. She dressed each burn, said nothing to anyone, and looked at nothing at all.

Cartethyia reached the dais, sighed the sigh of a girl missing a perfectly good market day, and picked up the sword the way you pick up a rake.

The burning was for other people.

Warmth ran up her arm like a homecoming. Light stood off the blade — witnesses disagreed forever about the color — and somewhere above the basilica the noon bell rang four minutes early, and the presiding acolytes were on their knees before the echo finished, and the whole hot ordinary morning tipped over into scripture.

The arguments started before sundown and lasted years. *Her?* said one Egla in every crowd. *The registrar's girl? Not a drop of noble blood — she paints on prisons — she'll haggle with the Sentinel Itself.* And the other Egla, the larger one, said: *She listens. She's carried our sorrows since she was six years old and handed us back jokes. If anyone in this world has earned the Divinity's crown, it's her.* Both Eglas were reasoning from evidence, and both were about to be wildly, catastrophically beside the point, because worthiness had precisely nothing to do with what had just happened, and the only creature in Rinascita who knew that was very deep, and very patient, and very pleased.

At the side table, the Fisalia girl with the bandaged palm was looking at her now — a long, level look, the look of an apothecary reading a label whose ingredients she disputes.

"Congratulations," she said. It came out in the voice of a woman reading a dosage.

"Does yours still hurt?" Cartethyia asked, nodding at the bandage. It was, by every rule of the occasion, a spectacularly rude question.

The girl considered her for a moment. "Everything the Divinity touches hurts," she said. "You'll want to remember that." And she packed her salve, and the white procession folded itself up around its new treasure, and the two of them went their separate ways for twenty-six years.

The crown came in Ragunna, in spring, before the whole gathered Order. A golden laurel, light as a promise, and the moment it settled on her hair, the whispers began.

The liturgy had told her to expect the Sentinel's voice. What she heard was vast, and warm, and tidal — it sounded the way the sea sounds inside a shell, which is to say like the sea and like your own blood at once, and you cannot ever quite tell which. *Beloved,* it said. *Beloved. At last.* And a girl raised on knights and windmills, who wanted so badly for the world to be as it should be, heard exactly what she was built to hear.

The night before the naming ceremony, they left her alone to pray in a hall off the great sacristy — dull, stuffy, dust turning in the shafts of lamplight, a room the color of every rule she had ever broken. She was supposed to spend it in contemplation of her new name. Fleurdelys. The lily of the deep, the Blessed Maiden, guardian of Rinascita, a name worn by saints in windows.

She danced instead.

Alone, in the dust and the lamplight, one last country reel — for the wheat fields, for the Tumbleyaks bawling in the summer wind, for the Thiefbats she had personally reformed, for the crooked wrist and the painted sword and the giant that had kept a foolish knight company because nobody else ever came. She danced until her breath ran out, and then she stood in the settling dust and did the one disobedient thing she would carry through everything that followed: she took the name Cartethyia and hid it, deep, where crowns could not reach.

*Farewell,* she told the fields, in the silence of that hall. In a world so vast, someone had to take up the sword. Someone had to bind themselves in thorns. Someone had to carry the weight, and the sword had chosen, and if the sword was mad, then Bastiano had at least taught her the respectable kind of madness.

Someone had to become Fleurdelys.

Egla sent her off at dawn. Half the town ran alongside the white carriage to the crossroads, and the last quarter-mile it was only Rosa, sprinting, flour to the elbows, who flung a paper packet through the window and stood gasping in the road.

Lemon cakes. Sugar crystals on top, the way Gram cut them, catching the sunrise like frost.

"Gram says the recipe *keeps,*" Rosa shouted after the carriage, hands cupped, the words bouncing down the road. "So it'll be here! Whenever!"

At the house on the lane, Tebaldo stood in the doorway a long time after the dust settled. Then he went in, and did what he did every night of his life, and every night after, for years, out of a habit he refused to examine.

He left the door unlocked.

## III. Somewhere Safe

Ragunna taught her its shape in the first month, the way a glove teaches a hand.

The city was beautiful the way an altar is beautiful: canals of green glass, bell towers thick as wheat, the Grand Cathedral riding above it all with its windows full of dead maidens. The Order of the Deep governed everything the water touched, which in Ragunna was everything. It governed the tides of commerce through blessings and the tides of grief through liturgy, and it spoke — this was the whole foundation, the load-bearing sentence of the nation — with the voice of the Sentinel Imperator, guardian divinity of Rinascita, whose oracles descended from the Grand Cathedral on days of observance and told the faithful what the year would hold.

Fleurdelys was installed in the seminary and given tutors.

The theology went badly. Her scripture master, a patient man, wrote in his first quarterly report that the Blessed Maiden's grasp of the Catechism of Depths was "an emergency," and in his second that she had asked, in open lesson, why the Book of Tides had four different authors' handwriting in the oldest copies, and in his third he requested reassignment. Her almoner despaired of her sums; she gave the same blessing-price to a duchess and a fishwife, which was doctrinally generous and administratively catastrophic, and every merchant on the Via Lucente knew within a season that the Maiden of Egla could be haggled up, out of sheer sympathy, by anyone with a sad story and a straight face.

The sword went beautifully. Her master-at-arms was Cadanto — an Echo, ancient and bronze-shouldered, wind wearing a soldier's shape, who had drilled maidens in that seminary yard since before the Order had a name for what he was. He spoke rarely and entirely in corrections.

"Again," said Cadanto. "You fight like a story."

"Thank you."

"Stories have endings." He turned her wrist two degrees with one enormous finger. "Fight like weather instead. Weather continues."

She loved him immediately and forever, and he pretended, with the transparent dignity of very old soldiers, that this was a burden.

And the work — the actual work of being a saint — she took to as if she'd been made for it, which was a sentence nobody thought to finish. When plague-cough came through the tanners' quarter she arranged the medicines herself, priced them herself, and delivered half of them herself, hood up, before the Office of the Maiden discovered where its figurehead had gone. When Tacet Discords came up out of a poisoned Tacet Field east of the city she was there before the bells finished, and the outbreak died in its infancy on the edge of a blade that had, everyone agreed, never in living memory moved like that. She learned the drainage canals of the lower city, and the names of the ferrymen's children, and which bridges the beggars slept under in Frostbreak month, and the Office trailed after her writing it all down and converting it, line by line, into legend.

Most of those she helped were strangers. She thought about that often, on the long walks back. In Egla everyone had a face she'd known since before she could walk; here the faces came by the thousand, new every day, and she settled on a way of holding it that felt true: each person was a small lamp. She could go around lighting them. Enough lamps and even Ragunna's nights would look like festival.

The lamps needed her most in the confession hours, and the confession hours were where the country began to show her its teeth.

The Doctrine of Sealed Sorrow was old, older than she could find the origin of, and it was the tenderest thing the Order sold. Grief, went the teaching, was a weight the faithful were never meant to carry, and a contagion they must never spread — mourn past the appointed week and you troubled the waters, doubted the Sentinel's mercy, invited the sea's attention. So the Church, in its kindness, provided the vessel. Bring your sorrow to the Blessed Maiden. Speak it once, into her keeping, in the sealed quiet of the confessional. And then leave it with her, and speak of it to no one, ever again.

The queue outside her confession door was the longest in the city. It was longer than the bread lines.

She remembered every one of them, which was the whole problem and also the whole point. She remembered the mason who had dropped his brother's coffin and could tell nobody it was funny and unbearable at once. She remembered the young wife who missed a husband the Order had blessed onto a ship that sank, and who had been informed by her confessor that further weeping constituted doubt. She remembered — she would remember to the end of everything — Nonna Vin.

The old woman came in smelling of brine and lamp oil, sat down on the penitent's bench like a dropped net, and apologized before she'd said a word.

"I'll be quick, Blessed one. They fined me Tuesday." She held up a chit from the Office of Public Comportment as evidence against herself. "Weeping at the fish market. Eleventh day. The week's allowance is seven." Her hands folded and refolded the chit. "It's only — my boy went down off the Thessaleo shoals in the spring, and the eleventh day was his birthday, and the herring came in, and he *loved* the first herring, Blessed one, he'd eat it standing up at the stall like a gull —"

"What was his name?"

Nonna Vin stopped. In four months of official mourning, through the funeral rite and the sealing rite and the fine, it was the first time anyone had asked.

"Vincenzo," she said. "After me. I had him late; I got greedy and named him for myself."

"Tell me about the herring."

They ran long. A clerk of the Office knocked at the quarter hour, as clerks were instructed to, because the queue was an asset and the Maiden's minutes were metered; Fleurdelys, without breaking the old woman's sentence, leaned over and wedged Tyrvine's scabbard through the door handles, and the knocking became a diplomatic incident she dealt with never.

At the end Nonna Vin sat straighter, emptied out, lighter by one boy's whole life, and asked the question they all eventually asked, in one form or another.

"They say if I give it to you, I don't have to carry it anymore." She searched the Maiden's face. "What do you do with them all? The sorrows. There must be — thousands, by now."

Fleurdelys smiled at her, warm as a lamp.

"I keep them somewhere safe."

She believed it. She kept them the way she'd kept Egla's grievances at six years old, all of them, filed in her chest by name — Vincenzo, the coffin, the ship, ten thousand griefs delivered one at a time into a single girl in a sealed room, per doctrine, by design — and at night, when she lay down aching with the day's freight, the whispers came, tidal and warm, and told her: *Well done. Well done, beloved. Bring us more.*

She heard it as the Sentinel's favor. She had been built to hear it that way.

Because that was Ragunna, when you stood where she stood and the incense cleared: a nation that had engineered loneliness and called it grace. Grief was contraband, held privately, surrendered upward. Truth was a licensed commodity. Doubt was a public health matter. Every citizen a sealed jar, every sorrow isolated the moment it formed, gathered by gentle hands into one consecrated vessel — and far below the canals, in the dark where Rinascita's oldest fear had curdled into something with a will, the thing that fed on exactly this arrangement grew fat, and patient, and fond of its harvest girl.

The rot showed her its face in increments. A charity gala where she blessed the bread of the poor, and watched the bread counted back onto carts once the guild artist had finished sketching the tableau — the loaves were on loan; the ledger required their return. A viscount who endowed a plague hospital and evicted its site's tenants into the same plague, and knelt to her afterward with tears of genuine feeling, because in Ragunna the feeling was also a performance and the performer was also the audience. Acolytes who recited the Catechism of Depths with their eyes on the collection plate. At such moments she did the only thing available to her rank and her leash: her grip tightened on the sword. Cadanto noticed the new tension in her forms and corrected it wordlessly, two degrees at a time, and once, very quietly, at the end of a drill: "Whatever you're gripping for. Keep it."

Then there were the oracles.

They handed them to her sealed, on days of observance, to proclaim from the cathedral balcony: the Sentinel's own words, brought up from the Deep Sanctum where the god communed with its chosen clergy. In her third year she broke a seal early, in the vestry, because the wax had smeared — and stopped, and read it twice, because she knew the hand. Fastidious, upright, a copyist's loops on the capitals. She'd seen it on requisition forms. It belonged to a young cleric of the Chancery named Fenrico, a rising man, dry and correct and tipped for great things, whose duties were listed as secretarial.

"The Sentinel speaks through instruments," her confessor explained, when she asked with a care she was learning. "The clergy transcribe."

"Where?" she said. "Where does It speak? I've served four years. I'd like to pray where It is."

There was a silence with architecture in it.

"The Sentinel Imperator abides in contemplation," the confessor said at last, "in the seminary of Avinoleum," and gave her, in the way he refused to meet her eyes, the first true oracle of her tenure.

The poets finished her education.

The Poets Society, they were called, though they'd begun as three cantors of the cathedral's own choir: Faroll, Rimbaud, Selena, the finest liturgical voices of their generation, who at some point had stopped singing what they were handed and started asking what she was now asking — where the god was, and whose hand wrote its words. They found things. Somebody, some smiling gentleman patron with a nobleman's diction, was said to be helping them find things. Their late hymns spread through the lower city faster than the Order could confiscate them; a whole spring, you couldn't cross a bridge without hearing children sing their verses, sweet and seditious, half the words misheard, all the melody intact.

She petitioned for them when the arrests came. She was the Blessed Maiden; she went in person, in full regalia, into the Primus's study, where the old man received her with the tenderness of a grandfather and a voice like velvet over stone, his young secretary standing at his elbow with a file.

"They preach against the Sentinel's Church, child."

"They ask where the Sentinel *is,* Holiness. So do I. Charge us together or release them."

The Primus smiled at her — fondly, truly fondly, that was the horror of the place, everyone's feelings were real and load-bearing and beside the point — and told her the Church would show mercy. "We are the Order of the Deep, child. We do not execute. The Fools will be entrusted to a Pilgrim's Sail, and to the sea's own judgment, and the sea is merciful to the penitent." At his elbow, the secretary Fenrico made a small correct notation, and it was the notation, the dry tick of the pen closing the matter, that she would dream about.

She rode for the harbor the day of the sailing and arrived to open water. The Pilgrim's Sail was a fleck on the horizon, gold in the failing light. It took her most of a week, working the harbormasters and the victuallers with a saint's authority and a haggler's incompetence, to establish what the manifests already knew: the ship had been provisioned with nothing. No water. No bread. Three singers and a beautiful boat and the sea's own judgment.

Word of the fire came back with the autumn convoys. The Fools' ship had burned on the open water — a curse, the Order announced gravely, the poets' own blasphemy come home; their surviving works were hereby forbidden, and copies would be identified for the public's protection, and somehow, mysteriously, the forbidden verses were easier to find that winter than ever before, because fear needed feeding and the Order was, above all else, a careful farmer.

She stood on the cathedral roof the night the news came, where the wind was, because the wind was the only thing in Ragunna that came and went unlicensed. Far below, the city glittered, every window a small lamp, and she had spent five years lighting them and the light changed nothing, because the dark in Ragunna was structural. She said it there for the first time, to the wind, quietly, a vow still in its scabbard:

*If Divinity takes up arms against the innocent, I will raise my sword against Divinity.*

The wind took it, and kept it, as the wind does.

One more night belongs to that autumn. She was walking the canal quarter unmarked, hood up, past curfew — the Maiden's small heresies had contracted to this, walking her city as a person — when she heard a lute under a bridge, played soft, and a man's voice threading through Faroll's forbidden hymn, the one about gold forged in fire. He sat on a mooring bollard with the instrument angled to shelter it from the drizzle, and against his shoulder, wrapped in his coat, slept a child of four or five with hair the color the wind would be if you could see it.

He saw the hem of her regalia below the cloak. His hands went still on the strings mid-verse. Twenty feet away, two acolytes of the night watch paused on the bridge above, listening, deciding.

The Blessed Maiden sees all. It was on the medallions; it was in the catechism; it was the entire theory of her office. She stood in the drizzle, the Order's living eye, and looked at the man and the sleeping child, and taught herself, in one held breath, the discipline her whole country would someday have to learn in her wake.

"Finish it," she said. "I heard nothing."

She walked on. Behind her, after a moment, the lute resumed, softer. And she was around the corner and gone before the sleeping child stirred against her father's shoulder and hummed three notes of the melody, off by a quarter-tone, ruinously, structurally unsound — and the man laughed low in the rain and shifted his fingering, without thinking about it, to harmonize around her.

The family's name, stitched inside the lute's case in a grandmother's thread, was Toccata.

## IV. The Answer of the Sea

The Dark Tide announced itself the way she would later understand everything in Ragunna announced itself: through the things people stopped saying.

Tacet Discord outbreaks doubled, then doubled again, and the Office reclassified them. The canal quarter flooded on a windless night and the flood was renamed. Fishermen came to her confession hours with sorrows they couldn't finish, because the thing they'd seen off the Thessaleo shoals had no permitted vocabulary — a darkness under the swell that moved against the current, they said, like a held breath, like the sea remembering something. The old women of Egla had spat at the tide her whole childhood and had never once been able to say why. All over Rinascita, the why was coming back in.

The oracles said: peace, providence, an obedient sea. The oracles were in Fenrico's handwriting.

She stopped asking permission in the sixth year. The Tide's first true surge took a fishing village whole — forty households, gone between one bell and the next, and the Order's response was a revised liturgy — and Fleurdelys, Blessed Maiden of Rinascita, put on her regalia as armor rather than costume for the first time, took up Tyrvine, and went to question Divinity in person. The catechism knew the place even if the maps refused to: Avinoleum, the drowned-and-lifted seminary, the Sanctum of Contemplation, standing where the Order's oldest privileges were kept.

She walked in alone. She had resolved on that. Whatever answer waited, it was owed to her first, and she would decide what the country could survive knowing.

*She must,* she had written in her private book, the last entry it ever got.

The seminary received her the way a shell receives a tide: passages that remembered being corridors, chapels tilted off true, salt-bloom on the frescoes, and everywhere, everywhere, the sound — the whisper she'd fallen asleep to for six years, thick now, present at noon, filling the stone. It swelled as she descended. It began, gently, to praise her.

At the heart of Avinoleum, in a nave flooded to the knee, chained under windows that showed the erased faith — sky and sea, two divinities where the catechism swore there had only ever been one — she found the Sentinel Imperator.

It had been a steed once. A Celestial Steed, winged, the sky's own shape; she knew it instantly and totally, the way you know a face in a ruined portrait, because some cell of her had been waiting her whole life to kneel to it. And the sea had gotten into it. Coral through the great ribs. Barnacle-crust on the folded wings. Fins where no horse ever carried fins, and the proud head hung low over the water, and when its eye found her, two things looked out of it at once.

"You came," said the first thing, in a voice like distant morning, ruined and glad.

"*You came,*" said the second thing, in the voice from inside the shell, warm, tidal, hers since the crown touched her hair. "*Beloved. At last.*"

She learned everything in that nave, because the second thing was proud of its work and eternity is long and even gods get lonely for an audience.

The Threnodian Leviathan told her about the war the catechism had eaten: two guardians, sky and sea, and the sea's guardian curdled — an entity condensed out of humanity's oldest longing to be one and its oldest terror of the deep, a god of grief that fed on every sorrow locked away alone. It told her how it had taken the Sentinel — belief by belief, whisper by whisper, the same way it took everyone — and how the Order, discovering their god corrupted, had made the most Ragunnese decision in history: they hid it, chained it, erased it, and kept the voice, because the voice was the state. It told her about the Doctrine of Sealed Sorrow, which it had fed to a Primus in his dreams three centuries ago: a nation of sealed jars, grief isolated and concentrated and delivered, tithed like honey. It told her about the harvest girl.

The return pod hung in the dark water beyond the god, and she understood what she was looking at before the whispers finished explaining, because the shape of it fit a hole in her she'd carried since the reeds. The vessel that had once ferried the Sentinel home to the world. The Leviathan had grown something in it. Grown *her* in it — cell by patient cell, a girl-shaped resonator tuned to the god's own frequency, engineered to pass any trial, to hold any power, to be found dry in wet reeds by a kind man on a path, to be loved, because loved instruments resonate truest. Every grief she had gathered in six years of open-hearted, genuine, retail sainthood sat harvested in her like honey in a comb. The petitions. The confessions. Vincenzo and the herring.

*I keep them somewhere safe.*

"Even your rebellion," the sea said fondly, through the god's hanging head, "is ours. We wrote the girl who would refuse us. Refusal at the right hour is a key turning, beloved. You are almost ready to open."

She stood in the flooded nave with all of it — the pod, the harvest, the six years, the reeds — and what rose in her, when the inventory finished, would keep philosophers busy in two cities for a generation. Because the design was perfect, and the design had made one error so small it had no name yet, and the error was this: they had grown their key in Egla. They had let a dramatist give her a wooden sword. They had let her ask why the giant was gentle.

She raised Tyrvine, and gave the vow its air at last, to Divinity's own face:

"If Divinity takes up arms against the innocent — I will raise my sword against Divinity."

"*We wrote that line,*" said the sea, delighted.

"Then watch me deliver it badly."

The blow should have failed. Every calculation in the deep said so: the corrupted god's power against a girl's, the instrument against its maker. But at the top of the swing something arrived from nowhere and bore her hands up — a steadiness with no source, a borrowed grace, warm as a stranger's returned gift, and it smelled, absurdly, of camellias — and Tyrvine came down through coral and chain and curdled divinity, and the corrupted Sentinel folded into the water like a mountain kneeling, broken and breathing, its ruin cut at last from the sea's will.

And the resonance of the blow rang her like a bell.

Rang the comb. Rang the harvest. Ten thousand sealed sorrows sounding at once in a vessel built to sound them, and far below Rinascita the sea answered its dinner bell. That was the second Dark Tide: her greatest act, the salvation-stroke, detonating in the same instant as the summons — the water standing up off the coast in a black wall with a will inside it, and Ragunna's bells all ringing themselves, the whole city one Night of Wet Bells, and the growling dark come home.

What the city saw, that day, made the stained glass. They saw the vanished seminary blaze above the rooftops like a risen star. They saw the Blessed Maiden's light stand against the black water — hours, the survivors said; minutes, said the tide-marks; both were right. They saw the sea break, and turn, and sulk back down into itself, and they saw the burning tower fold — impossibly, silently, corner through corner, inside-out like a sleeve — and vanish from the sky with their Maiden in it. The Order, which had lost its god's prison and its harvest girl in one afternoon and understood approximately none of it, did what Ragunna always did with the unspeakable: it wrote the liturgy first. The Maiden had martyred herself to stop the Tide. The window went up within the year. Egla kept her house as a shrine, and her mother stopped sleeping, and her father kept the door unlocked, and thirteen months after the funeral rite they boarded a ship at dawn without telling the neighbors, because there are countries you cannot live in after they have eaten your child, and Rinascita was one.

Inside the folded tower, in the drowned nave, in the silence after the greatest lie in the world had finished coming true, the girl who had caused the Dark Tide by trying to stop it knelt in the black water and came apart.

Literally. Deliberately. It was the only door left.

The Leviathan was in her — dormant, coiled, patient, riding the power it had built her to hold. Kill herself and the power scattered back to its master. Live, and she was a loaded weapon aimed at everyone she'd ever lit a lamp for. So she reached into the arithmetic with both hands and tore herself along the one seam she owned: the hidden name.

The half that rose out of the water wore the crown, and the power, and the parasite, and it looked down at the other half with eyes gone deep and archaic, and spoke in the register of stained glass.

"One of us must be the cage."

"And the other?" said the half still kneeling — smaller, dimmer, shaking, clutching a name to her chest like a stolen loaf.

"The other," said Fleurdelys, taking up the great blade, walking to the seal at the seminary's inverted heart, "is a loose end." She set the point against her own sternum, over the coiled dark, and hung herself upon the seal the way you hang a key out of a child's reach — impaled, deathless, a suicide postponed for as long as the arithmetic demanded, patient as coral. Her plan required decades and she had decades. Starve the parasite of a world. Erode both halves to nothing. A cage of nonexistence, closed from inside.

"You'll want us to die," said the small half, understanding.

"In the correct order," said Fleurdelys, and closed her eyes, and began to wait.

The small half knelt in the water a long time. Then she stood up, and took the stolen loaf out of her chest, and said the name aloud in the drowned nave to hear a voice say it, even her own.

"Cartethyia," she said. "I'm Cartethyia."

The tower said nothing back. It was going to say nothing back for twenty years.

## V. One Hundred Names

Time in the folded tower behaved like a tide pool: it filled, and emptied, and left different things stranded each time.

The first year she measured in projects. She snapped a branch from her own laurel — it had fused to her in the sundering, gold gone to crystal thorn, and it grew back what she took, which she chose to find generous — and she ground the thorn against the inverted stones, day upon day, into an edge. When it was done she carried it down the long wrong-way stair to the drowned nave, and held it up where the impaled half of her could see.

"I made a sword out of your crown."

Fleurdelys opened her eyes with the slowness of geology. "It will cut nothing here."

"Then I'll practice." She set her feet the way Cadanto had set them. "You gave me thorns for obedience. I'm teaching them disobedience. When the Divinity that built me comes to collect, it can suffer that instead." She named the blade the Defier's Thorn, out loud, formally, because Bastiano had taught her that props take their power from being announced, and she drilled with it in the flooded nave, forms against no one, while her other half hung on the seal and watched with an expression that a stranger might have mistaken for patience and she alone could read as grief.

The second year she measured in explorations: every corner the folded geometry allowed, gravity shifting room by room, chapels where down was a matter of opinion. She mapped it all, looking for a way into the central tower, a door out, another soul, an answer. She found the seminary's stores and the seminary's rules — the pious dietary scrolls, whole shelves of them, recommending the Laurus Salad with a fervor she came to regard as the Order's single most successful deception. She found Zargon Garden, where a dragon's Reverberation coiled through the dead arbors, singing to itself in frequencies like bowed glass, and she learned its patrol routes and its moods and, eventually, its favorite key. She found the Tower of Valor, where Cadanto stood.

He had been folded in with everything else. The stillness had gotten into him the way the sea had gotten into the god — an Echo without duties curdles, slowly, around its last order — and the first time his bronze head turned toward her with nothing behind it, she left the Tower of Valor and stayed away for what she later reckoned as six years. Some doors you keep closed to keep the person on the other side.

Somewhere in the middle years, the amnesia began.

It came like weather. She would dream — always the same dream: hymns pouring toward her in waves, a sea-shape circling in praise-thick water, herself cutting and cutting — and wake on a chapel floor with the dream still in her hands and her own name gone. *Where am I. What is this place. Who—* The first time, the terror nearly finished what Fleurdelys was too patient to hurry. She wandered the tilted halls for what might have been days, a stranger in her own tomb, and what brought her back, in the end, was her hands. Her hands remembered. They took up scraps and needle-thread from the seminary stores while her mind was still an empty room, and they stitched, and under her fingertips, stitch by stitch, the fog lifted: a doll took shape, a small figure with a sword, and looking at it she thought — *I know this story* — and the story pulled the girl up after it, hand over hand. The knight. The giant. The wheat. Rosa. Bastiano. Cartethyia.

So she industrialized the remembering. If the tower meant to take her name, she would nail it down with a hundred others.

She named the dolls as she made them, formally, out loud, with full titles. Ser Pincushion, knight-commander of the retinue, stitched with a pin-bright sword and a permanent expression of alarm. Donna Mestola, the ladle, mistress of the household. The sea-monster doll — she made that one with particular care, buttons for its terrible eyes, and named it too, because Bastiano's lesson held even here: even the giant gets a name; even the monster deserves company. She named the chapels and the stairs and the gravity wells. She held two-part conversations, doing all the voices, and staged plays with the retinue, entire seasons of repertory theater for an audience of stone, and when the amnesia tide came in — it kept coming, every so often, all the years — she would wake nameless and afraid in a world absolutely crowded with names, every one of them in her own handwriting, every one a rope. She followed the ropes home each time. The trips got shorter. The fear stayed the same size.

The play she staged most often, alone in the flooded dark with a doll on each hand, was the old one: the sword-girl and the sea monster, locked in their scuffle. She gave them both good lines. And somewhere in the umpteenth performance, pausing with the two of them gripped mid-battle, she arrived at the understanding she would one day hand to a broken woman in a corridor in Septimont like a canteen of water. The sword-girl, in every telling, in every fragment she could recover of her own scrubbed-out past, had one constant: when she raised the sword, she raised it for *now.* For what stood in front of her needing doing. The past could go missing entirely and the raising still made sense. And if that was true — she sat with this a long while, dolls in her lap, in the tomb of everything she'd been — then the tides could take what they liked. In a world so vast, she would find herself again, as often as it took. And when she did, whichever one she turned out to be that day, the warrior or the monster —

she would be willing to accept it, and get up, and put the kettle on, metaphorically, the seminary having no kettle, which she had also named.

She hummed while she worked. She had always hummed; in Egla it had been a public menace; here it was a lifeline, sound to prove the silence hadn't won, and it was, by any technical standard, atrocious — melodies that changed key mid-thought, bridges assembled out of spare parts, tunes of no fixed address. She hummed them into the seminary's dead ventilation, into the ducts and organ-throats of a building that used to breathe, and the folded tower, it turned out, still leaked. Hairline seams. Pressure differentials. The building exhaled her, note by mangled note, out through the fold and into the wind over Ragunna, and the wind — collector, keeper, sentimental old archivist of the whole country — took the tunes and filed them with everything else.

Years on, on a rooftop in the banished quarters of the city, a young busker with wind-colored hair would stop mid-song, frown at a sky doing nothing in particular, and jot three notes in a margin — off by a quarter-tone, ruinously, structurally unsound, and familiar in a way she could not place and never threw away.

The descents to the nave continued, annual at first, then whenever the loneliness outweighed the ache of them. Two halves of one girl, one impaled on a seal, one sitting cross-legged on the altar steps with her boots off, holding both ends of the only conversation available.

Year five: "I redecorated the refectory. There's a mural now. You'd hate it."

Year nine: "The dragon let me sing with it. We're terrible together. It's wonderful."

Year twelve, the tide having just gone out, her own name still fitting strangely: "Did you take the memories? Is that you, or the tower, or It?" And Fleurdelys, eyes closed: "Does it matter?" And Cartethyia, after a long time, honestly: "Less than it used to. That's the frightening part."

Year fifteen, or sixteen — the count itself was tidal by then: "Tell me the plan again." "We erode. Both of us. The vessel empties; the parasite starves; the cage closes clean. It requires only that you stop insisting." "On what?" "Yourself." A silence with twenty years in it. "I made a sword out of your crown, once." "You did." "I don't practice anymore." "I know."

The whispers worked both halves, all those years, patient as water. To the impaled one they offered rest with meaning. To the loose end they offered, in the exact voice of the shell held to the ear, the one commodity the tower had none of: an ending that would count. And erosion is not a battle; erosion is arithmetic; and by the last years the girl who had ground a crown into a sword of disobedience had drafted, revised, and privately filed — in her father's hand, in her father's habit, the registrar's daughter to the end — a single tidy sentence, awaiting only a second party to say it to: *Then unmake us both.*

The ghost-walks began by accident, in the final years. A shudder went through the tower one dead afternoon — later she would learn to call it the Order's tampering, the Fisalia cable car, the world knocking — and something in the fold hair-cracked, and she found she could lean out through it. Thin herself into frequency and drift, tethered, unseen, through the crack and down into the sky over Ragunna.

The city had gone on. That was the education of the ghost years, delivered daily: the city had entirely, thoroughly, twenty years' worth gone on. Carnevale came annually; she stood in the crowds and confetti fell through her open hands. Her own face looked down from the cathedral glass, forty feet of martyr, serene as she had never once been in life. Fenrico wore white now — Primus Fenrico, made by the vacuum she'd left; her fall had been his ladder, and the oracles descended on schedule, and she alone in all Rinascita could stand at the balcony rail beside him, invisible, and recognize his handwriting going up to be read as a god's. Children were taught her story with a moral bolted to it: *give your sorrows to heaven and hold your peace.* The queue outside the confession halls was longer than the bread lines. The jars, all sealed. The comb, refilling. The sea, below everything, keeping its side of a bargain nobody living had signed.

She drifted everywhere in those last years, a ghost with a city-sized haunt. Everywhere except one direction.

Egla lay southeast. She learned the feel of southeast the way you learn the feel of a broken tooth, and she kept her drift out of it with twenty years of discipline, because two things could be waiting there — a town that had forgotten her, or a lit candle — and she knew with tidal certainty that either one would end her, in its own way, and a ghost's whole economy runs on postponing the ending. So the shrine, the lilies renewed weekly by an uncle's hand, the recipe kept, the door unlocked, the ship her parents boarded at dawn — all of it waited outside her map, unspent, a debt compounding in the dark.

She was standing in a Carnevale crowd with confetti falling through her, in her twentieth year of being no one, on the day a black-haired woman with golden eyes sat down on the fountain's rim beside her, followed her gaze up at the fireworks, and said, conversationally:

"You'll catch cold, standing in the water like that."

## VI. The Woman Who Said Her Name

Cartethyia looked down. Her boots were in the fountain — they were always in the fountain; she stood there every year because the water made a sensation, faint as a rumor, and rumors of sensation were the currency she'd been living on. Then she looked at the woman. Then she looked behind herself, with great care, at the empty air the woman might plausibly have addressed.

"You," said the woman, patiently. She had a new bruise along her jaw and the particular tiredness of someone whose evening had already contained a battle. "In the fountain. With the laurel."

Twenty years of protocol collapsed at once. Cartethyia raised one hand and waved it, slowly, side to side.

"What are you doing?"

"Checking."

"Checking what?"

"Whether you're real." She said it with total scientific composure while her heart went off like Carnevale. "People generally aren't. I have extensive data."

The woman considered this, and her, and the fireworks, and delivered her verdict with the flat kindness of the recently concussed: "I get that a lot."

And that — the wind would insist on this point, and the wind was there — was the whole hinge of everything after. Two sentences in, and they had already exchanged credentials: a ghost who doubted the world's reality and a woman who doubted her own. Mei had washed up on a shore with no name, no past, and a title waiting for her like a laid table; Cartethyia had been grown for a purpose, scrubbed by a tower, and stitched back together out of a hundred names in her own handwriting. Both ledgers were still closed to the other. Both of them recognized the handwriting.

"Cartethyia," said Cartethyia, before she'd been asked, greedily, and then spelled it, in case the wind ate any of it. "Cartethyia. That's — I'm Cartethyia."

"Mei."

"Say it back?"

Mei looked at her — the whole strange sum of her, the girl in the fountain that a square full of thousands was failing to see — and asked no question at all, which was the largest gift anyone had given her in two decades. "Cartethyia."

The ghost stood very still in the fountain, being named.

"Again," she said. "If you're not busy."

In a world so vast, someone had said her name. She followed Mei out of the square like a cat follows a fisherman, and appointed herself, without consultation, permanent.

She learned Mei's shape over the following weeks the way the newly sighted learn light: greedily, at all hours, with periodic testing. Mei could see her and touch nothing of her; she was a voice and a laurel-glint at the edge of a life, and Mei folded her into that life without ceremony — talked to her in crowded streets and let the crowds think what they liked, saved her the aisle seat at a puppet show, said *good night, Cartethyia* out loud, every night, to empty air, because she had figured out inside a week what the name did and had decided, in the quiet unannounced way that was the whole engine of the woman, to keep doing it. Cartethyia heard the god name her Arbiter; heard that the Sentinel had reached her from an inverted tower and told her to find "the Lost"; watched her, bruised and unpaid, go looking. *The Lost.* The registrar's daughter filed the term with a strange feeling in her chest. She'd been called Blessed, Maiden, martyr, weapon, loose end. Lost was the first title in twenty years with any accuracy to it.

The bard came next, by way of Carlotta Montelli's sprawling favors, and the bard changed the physics.

Ciaccona built rooms out of songs. That was the plain mechanics of her Forte — melody spun into walkable memory, a Sonoro of music — and the first time she played the doorway open and Mei stepped through it into resonant space, Cartethyia followed, and stopped, and looked down at her own hands, which were casting shadows.

Inside a song, she had weight.

The bard was already watching her with a professional frown. Wind-colored hair; a lute older than she was; the posture of someone who had been thrown out of better places than this. "You're the ghost," she said. "Carlotta's brief was thin on you. Also — sorry — were you humming just now?"

"I hum."

"It's structurally unsound."

"Thank you."

"It wasn't—" Ciaccona stopped. Something crossed her face — the flicker of a margin in an old notebook, three quarter-tone notes she had never managed to place or throw away — and she looked at the girl in the borrowed weight of her song for a moment too long. Then she sat, unslung the lute, and did what she did instead of asking, which was play: found the ruinous key, and built, in eight unhurried bars, a harmony *around* it — load-bearing walls for a melody of no fixed address.

"There," she said. "Now it's architecture."

Cartethyia stood inside the sound of her own terrible humming, structurally reinforced, for the first time performed by two.

"What's the song called?" she managed.

"Yours." Ciaccona was already adjusting a peg, not looking up, the way you don't look up when it matters. "I'm keeping a tab."

They talked, those weeks, sideways, the way people talk when both of them are carrying something. Ciaccona doled out her history in change: a family banished note by note, a father who had handed her a key and a creed with the guards already at the door — *don't hide the truth in your heart; raise it like a torch, so it lights the way for others* — and the years since, carrying it alone, singing in the banished quarters where the wind was the only audience with tenure. Cartethyia listened to the creed with a vertigo she kept off her face, because she had spent twenty years being the truth hidden in the world's heart, and here was a woman whose entire inheritance was the instruction to raise her.

"Torches," Cartethyia said, testing it. "What if the truth doesn't want lighting? What if it's — safer, folded away. For everyone."

Ciaccona looked at her the way she looked at a flat note.

"Then it isn't the truth's decision," she said. "That's the trick of it. My father didn't ask the truth's permission. He just refused to let it disappear." She struck a chord, closing the subject and opening it forever. "I've noticed you name things, by the way. The dolls. The kettle-that-isn't. You do it so they can't disappear."

"...It works on things."

"It works on everything," said Ciaccona, daughter of the Toccata, and went back to the tab she was keeping.

Then Porto-Veno opened its gates — the Fisalia castle, the vault of secrets, the family that had been holding Rinascita's regrettable necessities for thirty-six generations — and out of it, at the end of all the tea and terrible knowledge, came a woman with a parasol, a poisoner's calm, and a sword that burned pretenders. Mei's palm it had welcomed. And the Fisalia cable car ran where no door had run in twenty years: through the fold, into Avinoleum, to the sealed girl the god had called the Lost.

Cartethyia stood in her own tomb's receiving hall — she had swept it; she had, in a fever of anticipation she declined to examine, *swept a tomb* — and watched two living women step through into her twenty years.

Mei came first, and had the manners to gawk at the gravity. The other one stepped down after, furled her parasol, took in the tilted chapels, the doll retinue arranged in order of precedence, the salvaged mural, the whole vast stitched-together kingdom of one, with a slow assessing sweep — and Cartethyia caught, under the assessment, the thing the woman would have paid money to hide: recognition. She knew the *method.* Here was another person who had furnished her own sealed room and called it a life.

"Cantarella Fisalia," the woman said. "Thirty-sixth of that name. You've dusted."

"Cartethyia. First of that name. You're standing on Ser Pincushion's parade ground."

Cantarella looked down, adjusted her position by a precise half-step, and inclined her head to a doll with a pin-bright sword and an expression of permanent alarm — a full formal bow, executed with total gravity, to a knight-commander three inches tall.

They were going to be family. Everyone present knew it by the bow.

What followed, for a handful of impossible days, was the thing the wind filed under its rarest heading. Cartethyia had a name for it before the first day was out — *the three idiots,* she announced at dinner, with the satisfaction of a girl christening a ship — and the three idiots kept holiday inside a tomb, all of them fluent in exactly what it was and none of them saying the word. They dove the gravity wells for the joy of it, all three, even the poisoner, whose shriek on the first inversion was entered into the permanent record over her strenuous objection. They fed Cartethyia honey cakes and figs — her first food in twenty years, and her sealed body gave her nothing, no sweetness, no salt, a texture like eating the *idea* of a cake — and she ate every crumb anyway, narrating from memory: "Honey first, then lemon underneath, and the crystals go last, they crunch, Rosa's Gram cut them coarse so they'd catch the light—"

"That's a fig," said Cantarella.

"It's a very lemon fig."

They suffered the dietary scrolls together, read aloud in liturgical voices — the Laurus Salad, divinely recommended, thrice weekly — until Mei declared the salad the Order's true instrument of penance. They went to Zargon Garden at the hour the dragon sang, and sang it farewell in three voices, one trained, one game, one structurally unsound, and the Reverberation held its final note around all three like a hand cupped over a match. They climbed to the Tower of Valor, where the door Cartethyia had kept closed for years stood between her and the curdled bronze thing that had once turned her wrist two degrees and told her to keep whatever she was gripping — and with a sword-saint on her left and a poisoner on her right she opened it, and fought what the stillness had made of her first mentor, and cut the stillness out of him, and Cadanto's head came up at last with something behind it, and regarded the three of them, and the drill-yard voice said, from very far away: "Your footing has improved." She cried on a poisoner's shoulder about it, which both of them denied forever.

It was a holiday inside a tomb, and downstairs, on the seal, the fourth resident hung in the dark with her twenty years of patience, listening to the laughter come down through the drowned stone, and waited for the guests to find the swords.

## VII. Two Maidens

They found the swords.

Three blades, three pedestals — Tyrvine of Salvation come home in Mei's grip, Hognis of Unity, Haultir of Valor — and when the last blade seated, the seminary's sealed memory broke open like a hull, and twenty years of hidden history poured over the three of them in visions: the crowning, the harvest, the poets, the nave, the pod, the design, the blow, the Tide, the sundering. Cartethyia stood in the flood of her own life and watched the two people who had said her name learn, in full, what the name was attached to. Grown, engineered, planted. The salvation-stroke that rang the dinner bell. The martyr of the stained glass, cause of the catastrophe the glass commemorated.

When the visions ran dry, she was already talking. The sentence had been filed for years; it came out in her father's hand, tidy, pre-drafted, dead calm:

"Then the arithmetic's simple. I go down, I re-merge with her, and we finish her plan. Both halves unmade. The vessel empties, the cage closes, the Threnodian starves. Nobody else ever gets hurt by what I am." She even smiled, the filing clerk's smile, matter closed. "It's the only clean ending."

Mei made a sound like a door starting to open. Cantarella got there first.

"Oh, *clean.*" The word came out of the poisoner like something she was removing from a wound. "Twenty years in a jar and the great insight is a clean ending. Let me tell you about clean endings — I've *sold* them. I have measured them into teacups for people I loved. Dying is a door anyone can find in the dark; I've been standing next to mine since I was nineteen, I know its handle better than my own hand, and the single thing I have learned worth repeating is that choosing death is *easier* than choosing tomorrow. Easier. That's all it is. It dresses itself as duty and arithmetic and love, and underneath the costume it is a girl who is tired and wants permission." Her voice never rose. That was the terrible part; it was diagnosis, delivered at conversational volume. "I understand cowardice perfectly, Cartethyia. I've been fluent all my life. So you'll forgive me for recognizing the accent."

Cartethyia's fist was already swinging.

Mei's hand closed around her wrist, mid-arc, the way she caught everything — early, gently, as though she'd been waiting there since yesterday. For a moment the three of them stood in the tableau: the knight cocked to strike, the poisoner unflinched under the stalled fist, the amnesiac holding the whole arrangement together at the wrist.

"Two maidens," Mei said. "That's what I keep hearing. One died for everyone; one poisoned for everyone. Each of you alone, doing the whole sum by yourself, both of you arriving at the same answer, and the answer is always *me, subtracted.*" She looked between them. "Try it together. Just once. Two maidens, same problem, one table. See what the sum comes to when nobody's alone with it."

The fist came down slowly. And into the changed air, Cantarella paid the table stakes: her Divine Trial, the truth of it, thirty-odd years old and never once said aloud — that she had stood where every burned girl stood, and the sword had shown her the corrupted god and an inverted ruin and *The Maiden is dead* in her own handwriting, and she had understood, and she had given her Second Awakening back. Handed her own blessing up to a chained divinity like change across a counter. "So that when Its champion finally came," she said, "the blow would land."

Cartethyia stared at her.

"The nave," she said. "Twenty years ago. At the top of the swing, something carried my hands. It came from nowhere. It smelled of—" She stopped, and looked, really looked, at the woman across the table, and at the camellias embroidered quiet along her collar.

"It's soap," said Cantarella, thirty-sixth Bane of Porto-Veno, to whom lying was a craft heritage, lying badly for the first recorded time.

She took up Tyrvine and put it into Cartethyia's hands. "The real Blessed Maiden is dead," she said. "I've read it in my own handwriting; I'm assured it's authoritative. But a false Maiden who chooses tomorrow —" the pause was almost gentle — "can become the true one. Go argue with yourself. We're coming."

Fleurdelys was waiting at the heart, as she had waited through everything: impaled, inverted, twenty years of suicidal patience — and now, descending toward her, the thing her whole plan required and her whole design had ordered: the loose end, delivered, armed. She came off the seal like a held note ending.

They were three, and she overwhelmed them. That has to be said plainly, because the songs would garble it later: the Maiden's discarded half, running on a god's stolen power and two decades of singular purpose, took the three of them apart. She fought the way erosion fights. Cantarella went down bleeding and used the position, as she used everything, to work: found the wound in the death-seeker and pressed it at conversational volume — "You've had twenty years and a sword and you keep *missing* her. Curious. Kill the girl and it's finished, and you hesitate, and shall I say why? Because when she's gone, the dark goes quiet, and you're alone in it again, and you have been alone in it for twenty years, and you would rather this — even this — than that." The spear's answer took Mei through the shoulder and pinned her to the stones.

And Cartethyia, watching Mei's blood spread across her tomb floor, did the arithmetic one last time and found it came out different with other people at the table.

She Overclocked past every line her body owned. Tyrvine shattered in her grip like a bell. And as she came apart into light — deliberately, a sundering chosen instead of suffered, the loose end spending itself — she poured what she was into the two blades still standing, Haultir and Hognis, and her voice stayed steady the whole way down: "Take my power. My blessings. My past. Where I go now — I am your blade."

Armed with everything Cartethyia had been, Mei and Cantarella finished it. Fleurdelys died lucid — the Threnodian's grip cracked off her at the last, twenty years of whispers going silent all at once, and in the silence the dying half of the girl from Egla looked up at the poisoner kneeling over her, and said, with the wonder of someone recognizing a face across water, "*Kokomi,*" and thanked them both, and went.

It should have been the ending. The window practically designed itself.

Mei refused it.

The Sentinel Imperator stood freed before them — cut from its ruin at last, spending its last strength just to be shaped — and Mei, shoulder still wet, planted herself in front of a god with the manners of a woman returning defective goods. Restore her. The girl in the swords. The one who erased herself so you could stand there looking radiant — put her *back.* The god named the price without cruelty, because it was simply the physics of the thing: a life reconstituted needs an anchor. A bound fate. Two threads tied so that if one is cut, both end.

"Done," said Mei.

The god tested whether she understood the word. It killed her to check — drove its horn through her abdomen in a single merciful lunge — and Mei took it, held it, gripped the horn with both hands and pulled herself *up* it an inch to say into the vast bright face: "Exchange, right? You better keep your damn promise." And died.

What happened next entered no scripture, and Cantarella never told it the same way twice, because she was in it. The Bane of Porto-Veno, the professional of clean endings, stood over the body and *raged at a god* — grief with a blade in it, a lifetime of transactional theology turned and fired at heaven: that this had been a sacrifice and not a waste, that the difference was everything, that Mei had bought a girl back at full price and the Imperator was holding the goods, and a divinity that welched was no divinity worth the window space. The Sentinel listened. Gods so rarely get taught anything; it may simply have been curious. Then it spent the last of itself, all of it, in one act: it closed the hole in Mei, and it reached into two swords and drew out everything a girl had ever been, and rebuilt her — flesh, breath, crooked wrist, laurel of crystal thorn — and it braided the two frequencies, the loose end and the patient one, Cartethyia and Fleurdelys, twenty years and a childhood, into one whole standing person, the Threnodian folded dormant and small in the depths of her like a sea in a shell. And to the country it had guarded and been worn by, it spoke a final command with no clergy left honest enough to distort it:

*From this day forth, you are free. Lead well.*

Then it came apart into light, and the tower began to come apart after it, and three women ran laughing and bleeding for the world's edge and jumped.

Falling, Cartethyia had time for exactly one thought, and it arrived complete, twenty years in the finishing:

*Flying is easy. It's the landing that breaks you —*

Hands. Both of them. Mei's grip on her left, and on her right a poisoner's, precise as a dosage, and the three idiots hit the sea together and the sea, for once, gave everything back.

## Coda: The Unlocked Door

They washed up at Porto-Veno and convalesced at what Cartethyia insisted, from the first morning, on calling Hotel Kokomi, over the proprietor's sustained objection. The most feared poisoner in Rinascita changed their bandages personally, complaining throughout, with a gentleness she would have denied under oath.

On the second morning there were honey cakes.

Cartethyia bit into one out of pure ceremony, expecting the idea of a cake — and stopped, and sat very still, and put her hand over her mouth, because her rebuilt body had come with everything restored and nobody had thought to warn her. Honey. Honey first, then lemon underneath, and salt, which confused her until she understood she was crying into it, and the crystals on top crunched, cut coarse, catching the light like frost. Twenty years of nothing, ending on her tongue in a poisoner's sunlit kitchen. She ate four, slowly, weeping steadily, radiantly happy, and Cantarella refilled the plate without comment and pretended to read.

She appointed herself that same week. Knight of the Eternal Debt, sworn to the woman who had out-stubborned a god on her behalf — announced formally, with full titles, the way Bastiano had taught her props take their power. Nobody argued. It was already visibly true, and anyway the debt had started flowing both ways the moment a woman on a fountain's rim had said a ghost's name twice, free of charge.

The rest, the wind kept too, and it belongs to other tellings: how the road wound back through Egla at last, and a knight in a grey cloak stood outside her own hometown arguing with herself about ghosts and shrines, holding a paper of lemon cakes bought by a friend, crushed wax in her fist; how a baker named Rosa sent three sentences out to a dead girl — *Gram kept the recipe. I got married. The door was always unlocked* — and blew twenty years of hiding off their hinges; how she walked in through the main gate. How, in the town square, a bard with wind-colored hair and a tab twenty years in the keeping finally played the song — the story of a girl who called for freedom and dreamed of legend, whose prophecy proved a vicious lie, whose halos faded, and who chose to carry on anyway — and how, halfway through, the girl herself stepped out of the crowd and sang the one verse no other voice alive could carry, the one about the blindfold, and the ocean's mist, and still being that girl, still carrying the hopes. Ruinously off-key. Load-bearing anyway; the bard had built the harmony around her years ago without knowing whose humming she was reinforcing.

An old woman in the crowd whispered, "Fleurdelys?"

"No," said the girl in the square, in a world so vast, with the wind moving through the wheat beyond the walls. "Just Cartethyia."

And the old woman crossed the square, and took the dead saint's living hand, and said, "Welcome home, child," and Egla wept and laughed at once, and somewhere in the crowd a knight who had once asked why the giant was gentle stood being held by her own hometown, home from the tower, home from the dark she was born in, with a debt on her arm and a bard at her back and every one of her hundred names accounted for.

Ciaccona was asked, much later — by the fire, tuning down, the pack asleep around them in a heap — where the first three notes of that song had come from. The flat ones. The ones she'd carried in a margin for years.

She thought about it, and looked up, the way you look up when it matters.

"The wind," she said.

The wind, which keeps everything, kept that too.

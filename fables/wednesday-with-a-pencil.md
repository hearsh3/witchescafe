Wednesday, With a Pencil

Amy didn’t knock. She knelt on the warm end of the kang, put both hands on her mother’s shoulder, and shook it like a stuck drawer.
“<font color=mistyrose>Ding ding ding. Up at it, Ma.</font>”
Mei surfaced one eye at a time. The quilt still smelled of woodsmoke and bergamot, but Iuno wasn’t under it anymore. The dent where she’d lain had gone cool.
“<font color=moccasin>Mmmgh… what day is it?</font>”
“<font color=mistyrose>It’s Wednesday.</font>”

Amy’s hands were freezing through Mei’s sleep-shirt. Her cheeks were bright pink from the yard, and the hem of Chisa’s black jumper had a crust of dry white snow tucked into the ribbing at her hip.

“<font color=moccasin>Your paws are blocks of ice,</font>” Mei mumbled, catching Amy’s left wrist and tucking it under the warm fold of the quilt against her own collarbone. “<font color=moccasin>Where’ve you been?</font>”

“<font color=mistyrose>Down the yard. Fang Qiu’s already got the brazier lit under San Jie’s elbow, and the snow’s up to my shins by the woodpile.</font>” Amy wriggled her cold fingers against Mei’s throat on purpose until Mei yelped and tried to bite her thumb. “<font color=mistyrose>Get up. If you stay flat any longer Lupa’s going to feed your eggs to the bird.</font>”

“<font color=moccasin>He’s a goose, and he eats buns.</font>” Mei rolled onto her good side first, braced her right forearm on the brick, and pushed herself upright by stages. Across her stomach the three-inch scar pulled tight against the morning chill, stiff as a leather boot-lace left out in the porch. She blew out a slow breath through her pursed lips, waited for the tug to settle down to its ordinary grumble, and held up her free hand.

Amy took it without looking down, planting her heel against the wooden kerb of the kang to haul her mother the rest of the way to sitting.

The salt-house was bright with a strange, clear light that came from the floor up. Outside the unhooked shutter, the sun had come over the eastern gorge rim onto three inches of clean snow, throwing a square of white glare straight across the ceiling rafters.

Along the side of the brick chimney-flue stood nine pairs of winter boots arranged according to Lupa’s sense of order, which meant toe to the wall and steaming like kettle-spouts. At the stove, Lupa had clearly been awake since before the fourth watch. The big iron cauldron was bubbling with pork-and-millet porridge thick enough to hold a wooden ladle upright; a stoneware platter beside the griddle held a leaning tower of twisted dough crullers still spitting oil; and a wide iron skillet was packed rim to rim with browned links of mutton sausage and a dozen fried eggs.

Down by the central post, Cartethyia was still propped upright in her sleep-quilt with her crystal laurel listing over one eyebrow. Ciaccona sat cross-legged beside her with her eyes half-shut and her hair unbraided over her shoulders, the round maple belly of her lute resting across her thighs again. She wasn’t playing it; her right palm simply lay flat over the sound-hole as if she were making sure nobody walked off with it twice. At the far wall, Yangyang and Suisui were still wound into a single cocoon of bedding, only the top of Suisui’s tangled hair and the blue feathers at Yangyang’s collar showing above the wool.

And at the low trestle table in the middle of the floor sat Iuno.

She was already dressed in her dark wool coat with the horn button fastened properly under her chin. Her silver-blue hair was coiled and pinned at the back of her head, a little lower on the left where she’d worked the pin in one-handed, and a cup of tea steamed beside her right wrist. Directly in front of her, squared to the pine edge of the table with the exactness of a surveyor’s slate, lay her small ruled pad, open to a fresh page, with the brass-capped pencil laid parallel beside the spine.

Mei slid her legs over the kerb, worked her feet into her boots—which were toasty warm on the left side and slightly scorched on the right—and shuffled across the flagstones with her quilt dragged round her shoulders. She stopped behind Iuno’s bench, leaned down over her good shoulder, and planted a kiss on the crown of her hair.

“<font color=moccasin>Morning, Iuiu.</font>” Mei squinted down at the squared-up pad and the gleaming brass cap of the pencil. “<font color=moccasin>Who’s on trial?</font>”

“<font color=royalblue>Your table manners, darling,</font>” Iuno said, tilting her cheek up half an inch against Mei’s jaw before reaching for her tea. “<font color=royalblue>Sit down before Lupa hits you with the skillet.</font>”

“<font color=indianred>I’d never hit her with the skillet,</font>” Lupa said from the stove, sliding a flat iron spatula under four eggs at once. “<font color=indianred>Crack the pan. Sit down, Mei. Water’s in the mug by your elbow.</font>”

Mei sat on the bench beside Iuno, drank the mug of warm water down to the bottom in three gulps so Lupa’s ears would stop swiveling toward her, and reached for a fried cruller. Her fingers went out in a wide, polite curve around the open pad, giving the white paper a good four inches of clearance as if it were wet paint.

Chisa came down from the kang with her boots already laced and began dealing out the chipped bowls around the board. When she came to Iuno’s place, she set Mei’s bowl on the left, Amy’s on the corner, and tucked Cartethyia’s three inches further up the pine so the rim didn’t touch the ruled page.

“<font color=mistyrose>What’s the count?</font>” Amy asked, dropping onto her stool and immediately hooking two sausages onto her saucer.

“<font color=coral>Eight,</font>” Chisa said, setting the last bowl down as Yangyang and Suisui finally emerged from their quilt at the far end of the brick, blinking against the snow-glare.

Yangyang sat down on the bench across from Mei, her hair black at the crown and ink-blue down her back, with three tiny white feathers from the bolster stuck behind her left ear. She looked once at the open pad and the brass pencil, blinked, and reached past them to slide the pickle jar toward her sister.

“<font color=teal>Lupa,</font>” Ciaccona rasped from the kang, cradling her lute by the neck as she stepped down onto the flagstones in her stockings. “<font color=teal>Whose boots were at the back of the flue?</font>”

“<font color=indianred>Yours,</font>” Lupa said, dumping the cauldron of porridge onto the trivet at the far end of the trestle.

“<font color=teal>The left heel smells of fried bacon.</font>”

“<font color=indianred>Drippings got on the hearth-stone. Keeps the leather supple. Stop whining and eat.</font>”

The plank door lifted on its hinge and shoved inward. Cold, bright air rolled across the sill, and Ruoyun marched in with her brass spectacles glinting, steering Qingxiao ahead of her by two handfuls of quilted bedding. Qingxiao wore her moon-white dress underneath three layers of salt-house wool, and the tip of her nose was the colour of a winter radish.

“<font color=lavender>By the flue,</font>” Ruoyun ordered, pointing at the warm bench end that Yangyang immediately vacated by sliding six inches closer to Suisui. “<font color=lavender>Do not take the outer shawl off. The draught under that sill would freeze a badger.</font>”

“<font color=lightcyan>I am warm,</font>” Qingxiao said. She sat down, looked calmly across the table at the tower of fried crullers, and gave a single, small sneeze.

“<font color=lavender>Nine,</font>” Ruoyun said instantly, whipping a folded square of clean linen from her ruffled cuff and pressing it into Qingxiao’s palm. “<font color=lavender>Since yesterday noon. Lupa, give her the broth from the top of the pot, not the thick bit at the bottom.</font>”

“<font color=indianred>She’ll eat the thick bit and like it,</font>” Lupa barked, ladling out a brimming bowl and dropping two fried eggs on top. Qingxiao accepted the bowl in both hands and began to eat with steady, unhurried satisfaction.

Chisa silently split her own fried egg with the edge of her chopsticks, slid the intact yellow yolk onto Amy’s rice-bowl, and kept the crisped white fringe for herself.

“<font color=skyblue>Did everybody hear the roof slide at the third watch?</font>” Cartethyia asked brightly, squeezing onto the bench beside Ciaccona and tucking her knees up so the lute case had room beneath the table. “<font color=skyblue>It went *shhhhh-thump* right into the wood-passage. I thought it was a bear until I remembered bears sleep in winter.</font>”

“<font color=teal>It wasn’t the roof,</font>” Ciaccona muttered, dunking half a cruller into her tea to soften it. “<font color=teal>That was the wolf snoring.</font>”

Lupa’s head snapped up from the skillet. “<font color=indianred>I don’t snore.</font>”

“<font color=teal>The rafters rattled, wolf. Two notes. Low G and something flat in the basement.</font>”

“<font color=indianred>That was the wind in the chimney-pot!</font>”

“<font color=mistyrose>Chisa cleared the chimney-pot,</font>” Amy pointed out around a mouthful of yolk. “<font color=mistyrose>You two were up on the ridge for an hour.</font>”

“<font color=indianred>We were checking the mortar,</font>” Lupa said, her ears flattening slightly as she shoved the platter of sausages down the middle of the board. It stopped an inch short of Iuno’s ruled pad.

Nobody moved the pad to make room. Over the next ten minutes, four saucers of pickled mustard-greens, the vinegar cruet, the salt-dish, and three bowls of porridge travelled back and forth from one end of the pine table to the other, every single one of them steered carefully round the little white square of paper and the brass-capped pencil. Suisui reached across for the tea-kettle with her sleeve pinched neatly in her left hand so the silk wouldn’t brush the page; Cartethyia leaned clear over Ciaccona’s shoulder to spear a cruller rather than rest her elbow on the wood beside Iuno’s wrist.

Mei finished her first bowl, wiped a drip of egg yolk off her chin with the back of her thumb, and caught herself staring at the salt-cellar three inches to the left of the pencil. She looked up. Across the board, Yangyang was studying the grain of the pine table-edge with deep concentration, and Amy was meticulously building a little fence of sausage links around her porridge.

“<font color=mistyrose>The goose tried to get into San Jie’s grease-bucket this morning,</font>” Amy announced to the room at large. “<font color=mistyrose>Fang Qiu had to bribe him back onto his sacking with half a cold pancake. He’s getting round, Carte. If he eats like Lupa cooks for another week you’re going to need a bigger crate.</font>”

“<font color=skyblue>He’s putting on his winter down,</font>” Cartethyia said loyally. “<font color=skyblue>Geese need extra for the sea air.</font>”

“<font color=teal>He’s a poultry barge,</font>” Ciaccona said.

At the head of the table, Lupa finished her second bowl of porridge, set the earthenware dish down on the pine with a solid *clack*, and wiped both palms slowly down the front of her trousers.

“<font color=indianred>All right,</font>” Lupa said, looking straight down the board at Iuno. “<font color=indianred>It’s Wednesday.</font>”

***

Iuno did not have to hunt for the pencil. She had laid it three fingers’ breadth from her teacup when she came in from the well at the first bell, and her hand closed over the brass cap without looking down.

*<font color=thistle>And here we are, darling. The bailiff’s called the roll.</font>*

*<font color=royalblue>Be quiet.</font>*

*<font color=thistle>I’m merely observing that for a house full of women who fight monsters for a living, they’ve spent twenty minutes treating a six-inch pad of paper like an unexploded mine.</font>*

Iuno drew the pad an inch closer to the edge of the table, turned the brass pencil point-down, and used the straight wooden side of her chopsticks to rule a clean vertical line down the left margin of the blank page. Across the pine, the clatter of spoons and saucers died out one by one until the only sound left in the room was Qingxiao calmly chewing a cruller by the flue.

In the narrow column to the left of the line, Iuno wrote the names down in pairs, the way the roads went, in her small, upright clerk’s hand:

*Cartethyia, Ciaccona.*
*Suisui, Yangyang.*
*Lupa, Chisa.*
*Mei, Amy, Iuno.*

She lifted the point of the pencil to the top space on the right of the line and looked across the sausages at the middle of the bench. “<font color=royalblue>Cartethyia.</font>”

Cartethyia straightened her back at once, brushing a flake of pastry off her knees. “<font color=skyblue>Ragunna! Egla Town first to drop off the goose’s crate at Uncle Cosimo’s bakery, and then Fisalia Manor before the ground freezes hard enough to turn a spade.</font>”

Iuno wrote *Ragunna* opposite her name, then raised her eyes an inch to Ciaccona.

On Monday, down in the freight yard, Ciaccona had stared into her broth and given Lupa a riddle about whichever ship they happened to be standing on. This morning Ciaccona had her uninjured right hand resting on the neck of her lute and her chin up. She didn’t look at the shutter or make a joke for the room.

“<font color=teal>Ragunna,</font>” Ciaccona said, level and plain. “<font color=teal>Barge down the river reach to the coast, then the nine-day packet to the fish-market quay. And up the cliff road after.</font>”

Iuno set a neat tick beside the word and moved the pencil down to the second line. “<font color=royalblue>Suisui.</font>”

“<font color=thistle>Jinzhou,</font>” Suisui said crisply, her hands folded in her lap beneath the table. “<font color=thistle>The Zhaoming Guild wharf office by the lower lock until the twelfth month closes. After the audits are stamped, the northern post-coach to Mingting for New Year.</font>”

Beside her, Yangyang looked straight at the paper. “<font color=paleturquoise>With one night on the high roof at the solstice first,</font>” she said quietly.

Iuno wrote *Jinzhou — Solstice — Mingting*, every downstroke even. She shifted the pencil to the third line. “<font color=royalblue>Chisa.</font>”

Chisa was holding her teacup in both hands. “<font color=coral>Lahai-Roi,</font>” she said. “<font color=coral>Mama’s.</font>”

Iuno waited a beat, glancing toward the head of the board. Lupa sat with her thick arms crossed over her chest, scowling fiercely at the porridge cauldron as if daring it to boil over. She didn’t add a syllable for herself. After a moment Chisa added, in the exact same flat tone, “<font color=coral>And the boat to Honami after.</font>”

Iuno wrote *Lahai-Roi — Honami*, and brought the pencil down to the bottom line.

“<font color=mistyrose>Blue door,</font>” Amy said immediately, leaning her chin on her fist. “<font color=mistyrose>And an orange cat that doesn’t bite.</font>”

“<font color=moccasin>Septimont,</font>” Mei said, her knee pressing warm and solid against Iuno’s under the pine board. “<font color=moccasin>Then the big turtle.</font>”

Iuno wrote *Septimont* in the fourth space and laid the pencil down across the foot of the page. The whole column had taken less than a minute.

Lupa’s scowl darkened. She leaned both fists on the end of the table and looked down at the four short lines of black lead.

“<font color=indianred>That’s the where,</font>” Lupa said. “<font color=indianred>Everybody in the gorge knew the where on Monday. Write the day.</font>”

“<font color=royalblue>Which day would you like me to write, Lupa?</font>” Iuno asked mildly.

“<font color=indianred>One day,</font>” Lupa said, tapping a blunt forefinger on the pine. “<font color=indianred>Not ‘when the barge comes’ or ‘before the twelfth month.’ A date. Otherwise we’ll be trailing down the mountain in bits for three weeks.</font>”

“<font color=thistle>Deputy Director Lin’s authorization holds until the ice closes the upper Jinzhou canal,</font>” Suisui said smoothly, though her spine had gone up half an inch into her Guild posture. “<font color=thistle>If the north wind stays dry, it holds a while. If it sleets again on the plain, it doesn’t.</font>”

“<font color=teal>And the river-barge captains down at the lower landing don’t wait for Guild clerks,</font>” Ciaccona said, her splinted left finger tapping once against the maple rib of her lute. “<font color=teal>Once the anchor-ice starts forming on the shoals, they haul the flatboats up on the timber slips for the winter. If we miss the last timber-raft out of the gorge, Carte and I are walking forty leagues with a twenty-pound bird in a basket.</font>”

“<font color=indianred>Then we leave Friday,</font>” Lupa said bluntly. “<font color=indianred>All four carts. Dawn bell.</font>”

Across the table, Mei’s hand jerked an inch on her knee before she stilled it. Amy stopped swinging her boots under the stool.

“<font color=royalblue>We are not leaving on Friday,</font>” Iuno said.

Lupa’s ears snapped forward. “<font color=indianred>Why not? Carts are greased, road’s open, and the kid’s mecha can walk the cut behind the mules.</font>”

Iuno kept her right hand flat on the table beside her teacup. She didn’t look down at her lap, and she didn’t dress her voice in temple velvet.

“<font color=royalblue>Because Augusta’s courier is coming overland from the coast with an oiled tin case,</font>” Iuno said. “<font color=royalblue>And on Tuesday morning, at this table, I asked Mei, and Amy, and you to read what is inside it when it gets here.</font>” She looked straight into Lupa’s yellow eyes. “<font color=royalblue>You are getting on a ship for Lahai-Roi at the coast. You will not be on the boat to Septimont. If we go down the mountain before that tin arrives, you won’t be in the room when it’s opened.</font>”

The salt-house went very quiet. By the flue, Ruoyun lowered her teacup silently to her saucer.

Lupa stared back at Iuno. The aggressive slant went out of her shoulders all at once, leaving her looking slightly too large for the end of the bench, her mouth open on a next argument that wasn’t there any longer. Her ears tipped back, the tips flushing dark under the fur.

“<font color=indianred>...How long’s a coast courier take in the snow?</font>” Lupa asked, half an octave lower.

“<font color=thistle>Not long, if he changes horses at the plain,</font>” Suisui supplied quietly. “<font color=thistle>Longer if the lower pass is drifted.</font>”

“<font color=teal>The timber-barges run as long as the lock’s open,</font>” Ciaccona said, looking from Iuno’s face down to her own splinted finger. She let out a short breath through her nose. “<font color=teal>We can wait for a tin.</font>”

Iuno picked up the brass-capped pencil again. “<font color=royalblue>Then we set one rule,</font>” she said, falling naturally into the clear, level cadence of a treaty clause. “<font color=royalblue>We go down the south road together, all four carts, on the morning after the tin from Septimont has been read, or on the first day of the eleventh month, whichever comes first. At the plain crossroads, Suisui and Yangyang take the eastern post-road for Jinzhou, and the rest of us go on down the river to the port.</font>”

She looked round the table once, from Suisui’s nod to Ciaccona’s tilted chin to Lupa’s gruff grunt, and wrote at the foot of the page:

*South road, together: after the tin, or 1st of the 11th month.*

Lupa read it upside down from the end of the bench, grunted, and pulled the porridge cauldron back toward her as if it had tried to leave.

Amy leaned across the corner of the table, hooked two fingers onto the bottom edge of the pad, and pulled it round toward her stool.

“<font color=mistyrose>You missed a bit,</font>” Amy said.

She flipped the small leaf over to the clean back of the sheet, took the brass pencil out of Iuno’s fingers, and wrote a single word at the top in her quick, square engineer’s capitals, underlining it hard enough to dent the paper:

*AFTER*

She tapped the point of the pencil against the blank white space beneath it and looked up around the board. “<font color=mistyrose>When do we meet up again?</font>”

Cartethyia’s hand went up an inch off the table. “<font color=skyblue>Spring!</font>” she said at once. “<font color=skyblue>In Egla! When the snow melts off the hills. There’s a bell tower in the square, everyone could find it.</font>”

Amy held the pencil poised over the paper and looked across at the far bench. “<font color=mistyrose>Spring in Egla?</font>”

Suisui didn’t answer straight away. Her fingers smoothed the seam of her dark plum skirt beneath the table. “<font color=thistle>The northern grain-convoy reconciliations in Mingting run until the third month,</font>” she said, her voice gentle and exact. “<font color=thistle>And the sea-crossing to Rinascita isn’t a thing you do for an afternoon.</font>”

Beside her, Yangyang looked at Mei, and then at the blank sheet under Amy’s pencil. “<font color=paleturquoise>The Ministry’s spring muster on the northern border is in the second month,</font>” Yangyang said softly. “<font color=paleturquoise>After I come back from the solstice, I have to report to the Jinzhou wall.</font>”

“<font color=indianred>And Chisa’s mum has the spring thaw on the flat roof,</font>” Lupa muttered, rubbing the back of her neck with one broad palm. “<font color=indianred>Plus we told the girl we’d take the boat to Honami once the ice clears the straits. That’s east, not west.</font>”

“<font color=moccasin>And we’ve got the house with the blue door,</font>” Mei said quietly from beside Iuno. She had both hands wrapped round her empty water-mug on the table. “<font color=moccasin>And there’s the turtle out past the coast. I said I’d go.</font>”

Amy’s pencil stayed half an inch above the paper. No one offered summer, and no one tried autumn. Four oceans and three mountain ranges sat in the middle of the pine trestle between the pickled mustard-greens and the sausage platter, and none of the roads ran into the same square at the same time.

*<font color=thistle>Thirty years, darling,</font>* the High Priestess murmured inside the quiet of Iuno’s head, without a trace of mockery in her voice. *<font color=thistle>For thirty years you couldn’t look at a calendar without seeing five winters ahead, and now you’ve got a pencil and a whole blank page and nothing to put on it.</font>*

*<font color=royalblue>Good,</font>* Iuno answered her silently. *<font color=royalblue>If it were written down already, it would only be a schedule.</font>*

*<font color=thistle>Still. It’s an awfully large piece of white paper.</font>*

Under the table, Iuno’s left shoulder crystal gave a tiny chiming click against the lining of her coat. Her right hand drifted down to her side pocket, touched the square corner of the velvet box through the wool for half a breath, and came back up onto the pine board empty.

Amy slowly lifted the pencil off the page without making a mark. Her pink hair was steady around her ears, no white static in the tips, though her mouth was set in a small, flat line. She laid the brass-capped pencil down in the exact middle of the blank *AFTER* sheet.

“<font color=mistyrose>Are you going to put it back in your pocket, Auntie?</font>” Amy asked.

Iuno looked at the empty white square sitting between the bowls.

“<font color=royalblue>No,</font>” Iuno said. “<font color=royalblue>Leave it on the table. Somebody might think of something after lunch.</font>”

She didn’t close the cover, and she didn’t slide it back toward her teacup. She left the pad lying open in the middle of the pine board with the pencil resting across its spine.

At the head of the table, Lupa stood up, picked up the iron skillet by its hot handle with her bare hand, and dropped the last two browned sausages onto Cartethyia’s and Amy’s plates before carrying the empty pan back to the trough.

***

By the first bell after noon, Lupa had four crates, two iron cauldrons, a coil of tarred hemp cart-rope, and three greased canvas kit-sacks spread out across the flagstones of the lower freight yard, and she was in a foul temper with all of them.

The yard under the salt-house kang was warm from the bricks overhead and smelled of coal-smoke and mutton tallow. Thirty feet down the aisle, Fang Qiu was sitting cross-legged on an upturned grease-tub polishing the bronze knuckles of San Jie’s left gauntlet with an oily rag, whistling a three-note carter’s tune between her teeth. In the swept fourth berth beside her, Cartethyia’s white goose stood in the middle of its clean sacking, watching Lupa haul baggage off the storage racks with its head cocked sideways and one black-webbed foot tucked up into its belly feathers.

“<font color=indianred>Who packed the salt-crocks at the bottom of the third chest?</font>” Lupa snarled, yanking a forty-pound stoneware jar out of the corner crate by its ears and setting it down on the stone with a thud that made Fang Qiu’s oil-lamp jump. “<font color=indianred>Put stoneware under the tent-poles and the first rut on the south cut’ll turn the whole lot into gravel. Bunch of amateurs.</font>”

Chisa was kneeling beside the second crate with a cake of yellow beeswax in her right hand, running it steadily up and down a frayed canvas harness-strap to seal the stitching against snow-melt. She didn’t point out that nobody was leaving for at least a week, or that Lupa herself had shoved the salt-crocks into that chest when they unloaded the carts after the festival.

“<font color=coral>The small chest has straw in the bottom,</font>” Chisa said.

“<font color=indianred>Small chest’s going on the Mingting cart,</font>” Lupa muttered, wiping a streak of soot off her brow with the back of her forearm. “<font color=indianred>Yangyang’s sister has three lacquer boxes of Guild ledgers and that clockwork kite. If you put salt-crocks in with paper, the damp gets into the ink.</font>”

She crouched down beside the pile of iron cookware she’d dragged down the forty-two steps from the lean-to shed. There was the big two-handled cauldron they’d used for twelve people’s stew every night, a flat cast-iron griddle, two long-handled frying pans, and a smaller, thick-bellied brass-bound pot with a tight-fitting lid that she’d traded two strings of copper for in the lower market on Saturday.

Lupa picked up the heavy two-handled cauldron, set it in the middle of the floor, and stared at it.

It was the stew pot. Every night since the festival she had filled it to the rim, and every night it had come back scraped. After the plain there wouldn’t be a table anywhere big enough to put it on.

Lupa’s jaw worked. She shoved the big cauldron aside with her boot-heel, grabbed the thick-bellied brass-bound pot instead, and tore a four-inch strip of clean unbleached linen off the roll beside Chisa’s knee. Reaching for the lump of carpenter’s charcoal on the trestle, she braced the cloth against her thigh and scrawled across it in big, blocky Septimontian letters:

*NO PEPPER. BOIL TURNIP FIRST.*

She tied the linen strip in a double knot around the brass lug of the lid, yanked it tight with her teeth, and shoved the pot squarely against the side of Cartethyia’s empty wicker goose-crate.

“<font color=#88d891>What’s that for?</font>” Fang Qiu asked from her grease-tub, peering over San Jie’s bronze shin.

“<font color=indianred>The bard,</font>” Lupa grunted without looking up. “<font color=indianred>Put her on a ship for nine days with a galley cook from the coast and she’ll starve to death staring at a chili pod, and the little knight’ll eat raw cabbage out of the bilge if nobody tells her to boil the water first.</font>”

She picked up the medium iron skillet next—the seasoned one that didn’t stick when you flipped an egg one-handed—and dropped it into the canvas sack tagged for the Septimont cart, right on top of a bundle of dry grey flannel bricks she’d wrapped that morning for Iuno’s shoulder.

“<font color=indianred>Mei’ll burn the bottom out of a thin pan in three days,</font>” Lupa told the canvas sack darkly, pulling the drawstring tight as if she were throttling a thief. “<font color=indianred>And the princess doesn’t look at a stove until the kitchen’s already on fire. At least Amy knows which end of a spatula to hold.</font>”

From the fourth berth came a loud, deliberate *flump*.

The goose had stepped off its sacking, waddled across the swept stone to the wicker crate by Lupa’s boot, and poked its yellow bill through the open front door. It bit the wooden peg-latch twice to make sure it moved, stepped inside onto the clean straw, turned around three times until its tail-feathers stuck out between the rear slats, and sat down with a low, contented honk.

Fang Qiu lowered her rag. “<font color=#88d891>He’s packing.</font>”

“<font color=indianred>He’s got more sense than the rest of this house,</font>” Lupa said, though she reached into her coat pocket, fished out a cold crust of morning flatbread she’d saved from the trough, and dropped it through the top slats in front of the bird’s beak.

Chisa finished waxing the third strap, laid it neatly across the lid of the Mingting chest, and looked at the remaining pile beside Lupa’s boots. All that was left on the flagstones for their own kit-bag was a single small tin billy-can, two horn spoons, and a roll of oiled canvas.

“<font color=coral>You didn’t pack a pan,</font>” Chisa said.

Lupa’s ears twitched sideways. She picked up the coil of tarred cart-rope and began re-winding it around her left elbow and thumb, faster than it needed.

“<font color=indianred>Your mum’s got pans,</font>” Lupa said to the rope. “<font color=indianred>Woman’s lived in the same house twenty years, she’s not boiling potatoes in her hat.</font>”

“<font color=coral>She has two,</font>” Chisa said, sitting back on her heels with her hands resting on her black trousers. “<font color=coral>An iron pot for soup, and a small square one for rolled eggs.</font>”

Lupa stopped winding the rope. She looked sideways at Chisa under her brows. “<font color=indianred>No braising pan? With the heavy lid?</font>”

“<font color=coral>No.</font>”

“<font color=indianred>How’s a person supposed to brown short-ribs in an egg-pan?</font>” Lupa demanded, genuinely scandalized. “<font color=indianred>You crowd the meat in a little square tin and it just steams in its own juice! Goes grey as a wet boot! You need six inches of flat cast iron so the fat renders out before you put the radish and the soy in!</font>”

She glared at the Septimont kit-sack, then at the big two-handled cauldron sitting orphaned in the middle of the floor, and then at Chisa.

“<font color=indianred>...I’ve been working out the cut since Thursday,</font>” Lupa muttered, her voice dropping into her collar while the tips of her ears went hot. “<font color=indianred>Asked the butcher by the south gate how they saw the bone up north so the marrow doesn’t splinter in the broth. It’s a whole plan, Chisa. If I turn up at your mum’s table on a Thursday and serve her boiled grey beef, she’ll throw me out in the snow.</font>”

Chisa looked at the big two-handled cauldron on the floor, and then at Lupa’s flushed ears.

“<font color=coral>There’s a foundry market in the lower ward at Lahai-Roi,</font>” Chisa said calmly. “<font color=coral>By the tram depot. The apprentices from the hull-works cast twelve-inch skillets out of scrap plate-iron and sell them off the back of the slag-wagons on Saturdays.</font>”

Lupa’s thumb slowed on the tarred hemp. “<font color=indianred>Plate-iron? How thick?</font>”

“<font color=coral>Half an inch at the rim. Heavy enough that nobody buys the big ones except the sled-pullers.</font>” Chisa tilted her head a fraction, watching Lupa’s face. “<font color=coral>And behind the foundry wall, where the cooling-pipes run out into the birch woods, there’s a stone bath-pool in the open air. The water comes up from the deep vents at a hundred degrees even when the drifts are over the fence. Mama hates it because it smells of iron, so I never had anyone to go with.</font>”

Lupa stared at her. Her tail, which had been tucked tight and irritable against the back of her shin all afternoon, gave one slow, heavy thump against the side of the wicker crate. Inside the slats, the goose honked back in mild complaint.

“<font color=indianred>Hot water outside in the snow?</font>” Lupa asked.

“<font color=coral>Up to your chin,</font>” Chisa said. “<font color=coral>And they sell cold wheat beer in stone bottles at the gate-shed.</font>”

Lupa let out a long breath through her nose, dropped the half-wound coil of cart-rope onto the Mingting chest, and reached over to pick up the orphaned two-handled cauldron. She didn’t put it in any of the four cart-piles; she set it squarely on the bottom step of the iron stair to go back up to the salt-house stove for tonight’s supper.

“<font color=indianred>Half-inch plate,</font>” Lupa grunted, dusting the soot off her palms onto her trousers. “<font color=indianred>Have to season it three times with lard before Thursday, or the ribs’ll stick.</font>”

Chisa stood up from the flagstones, picked up the roll of unbleached linen and the lump of charcoal, and handed both to Lupa without a word. Lupa took them, crouched by the Mingting chest, and began writing *KEEP DRY — PAPER* in three-inch letters across the top canvas strap while her ears stood straight up in the warm air under the bricks.

At the third bell after noon, the sun had begun to slip toward the western rim of the gorge, turning the deep snow along the upper lanes from blinding white to the pale, clear blue of skimmed milk.

Yangyang walked half a pace behind Suisui’s left shoulder as they came out of the chandler’s shop on the third terrace, carrying a flat parcel of oiled wrapping-canvas under one arm and four bricks of pressed Mengzhou tea in a string bag. Ahead of them, the carters had beaten a narrow trough down the middle of the cobbles, packed hard and glazed by wooden sled-runners, while on either side the undisturbed snow still lay well above their ankles.

Suisui was walking at her brisk Mingting business clip, her dark plum cloak held up an inch from the slush with two gloved fingers and her breath puffing white in front of her face.

“<font color=thistle>The carter at the south post-yard says the road down to the plain is clear enough for four-wheeled wagons as far as the willow milestone,</font>” Suisui said, her eyes already scanning the roofline ahead toward the salt-house lane. “<font color=thistle>I paid him two coppers in earnest-money to hold the covered dray with the canvas tilt for the luggage, so if it snows again on the descent the bedding won’t arrive at the fork soaking wet. Once we reach the crossroads inn at the plain, our dray turns east onto the Jinzhou post-road, and the other three carts keep straight on down the river towpath for the port. If we make the carter start at the first bell, we won’t have to take the turn in the dark.</font>”

Yangyang shifted the string bag of tea-bricks into her left hand. “<font color=paleturquoise>Suisui.</font>”

“<font color=thistle>And we’ll need tallow for the wheel-hubs before we load,</font>” Suisui went on without slowing her boots. “<font color=thistle>The garrison grease here has too much pine-tar in it; once you get down out of the gorge wind onto the flatland, it gums the axles—</font>”

“<font color=paleturquoise>Jie,</font>” Yangyang said, stopping in the middle of the lane.

Suisui took two more steps, halted on the packed snow, and turned round.

They were standing at the mouth of the narrow stone square where the Hold’s watch-bell hung inside its open four-pillared granite arch above the terrace wall. Yesterday at dusk, when the first flakes came down, Yangyang had wanted to stand under that arch for the fifth bell, and they had turned aside into Longjue’s mahjong shed instead.

Now the square under the arch was completely empty. Untouched snow lay four inches deep across the paving-stones between the four square pillars, smooth as a bolt of bleached linen, with a thick white cushion piled high on the curved stone roof-cap above the bronze bell. Down at the far edge of the parapet, the watchman sat inside his little wooden sentry-box with a charcoal foot-warmer between his boots, whittling a peg and waiting for the sand-glass on the shelf to run out for the fourth bell.

Suisui looked from the granite arch down to her sister’s boots, and then up at the sand-glass in the sentry-box window. About three minutes of sand remained in the upper bulb.

“<font color=thistle>My toes have been numb since the chandler’s counter,</font>” Suisui said mildly. “<font color=thistle>And if we stand still in the shade for three minutes, Lupa will have to thaw me out with a poker.</font>”

“<font color=paleturquoise>You don’t have to come under the roof,</font>” Yangyang said, already stepping off the beaten carters’ track into the deep, unblemished snow between the pillars. “<font color=paleturquoise>Stay on the sunny side by the wall. It’s only one stroke.</font>”

The dry crust gave a crisp, hollow *crunch* under Yangyang’s soles, sinking to the tops of her ankles until she stood directly beneath the dark bronze mouth of the bell. Overhead, the green-patinated metal hung six feet wide on its oak beam, silent and cold above her upturned face.

Ten seconds later, the snow crunched a second time.

Suisui came straight through the four-inch drift without lifting her hem, ruining the dry edge of her dark plum cloak down to the braid, and stopped shoulder to shoulder with Yangyang under the shadow of the stone arch.

“<font color=thistle>The sunny side has a draught from the alley,</font>” Suisui said to the granite pillar in front of her, tucking both gloved hands deep into her opposite sleeves and stamping her feet once on the stone.

Yangyang’s mouth curved upward at the corner. She kept her eyes on the bronze lip overhead.

For a minute neither of them spoke. The cold under the stone roof was sharp enough to make the small cerulean feathers along Yangyang’s collar lift into a soft, dense ruff around her throat and chin. Down in the gorge, the river ran with a muffled, winter-thickened murmur between its ice-shelves.

“<font color=thistle>At the plain crossroads,</font>” Suisui said into the quiet, keeping her voice pitched low under the arch, “<font color=thistle>I told the post-yard carter to book four rooms at the Willow Milestone Inn for the night we come down, not just a midday baiting-halt for the mules.</font>”

Yangyang lowered her eyes from the bell and looked sideways at her sister.

“<font color=thistle>Nobody is turning off a cart in the middle of a muddy turnpike at two in the afternoon while everybody waves from the tailboard,</font>” Suisui said briskly, staring straight ahead at the sentry-box as if she were auditing a warehouse. “<font color=thistle>It’s untidy, and I dislike untidy travel. We will all sleep under one roof at the crossroads inn, eat a proper dinner that somebody else washes the dishes for, and take the Jinzhou road after breakfast the next morning when the horses are fresh. I’ve already told Iuno.</font>”

Yangyang looked at the crisp set of Suisui’s jaw and the white snow crusted around the hem of her good Mingting cloak.

Everyone else in the salt-house was leaving Huanglong behind for the winter—crossing the western sea or the northern straits to cities Yangyang had only ever seen on Outrider maps—while she and Suisui were the ones turning back into their own province, to the familiar grey stone of the Jinzhou wall and the canal bird-bridge and the solstice roof. She was glad to be going home; she had missed the smell of the lower wharf and Chixia’s shouting on the ramparts. Yet all morning, whenever she had pictured the four carts rolling down out of the gorge, she had seen the fork in the road at the bottom of the hill and her own boots climbing down into the slush alone while the other three wagons kept going toward the sea.

Now there was an inn with four rooms at the fork, and a supper table, and morning tea first.

“<font color=thistle>And in the twelfth month,</font>” Suisui added, her chin lifting a fraction, “<font color=thistle>before we take the post-coach up to Mingting, you are going to show me which roof you use on the solstice night.</font>”

Yangyang blinked. “<font color=paleturquoise>It’s cold on the roof, Jie. And it takes hours before the dark thins enough to cross.</font>”

“<font color=thistle>I have a fur-lined cloak and a brazier,</font>” Suisui said flatly. “<font color=thistle>I am walking you up the stair with a lantern, and I am sitting by the chimney-stack until you are gone, and when you come back with Ba’s New Year letters I will be on the same stair with hot ginger water. That is not open to negotiation.</font>”

Across the paving-stones, the watchman stepped out of his wooden box, spat into the snow, and unhooked the heavy iron-bound wooden striker-beam from its chain.

Yangyang reached out with her free right hand, caught two fingers of Suisui’s dark plum sleeve just at the wrist, and held on. Suisui didn’t pull her arm back; she shifted half an inch closer so their shoulders pressed solid together from collarbone to elbow.

The watchman swung the beam back and drove it home against the bronze rim.

The first crash of the metal shook a fine white veil of snow loose from the stone roof-cap overhead, sending it drifting down around them in the blue afternoon light. Then came the low, deep hum of the bronze after-note.

Instead of ringing out sharp and thin across the miles of empty gorge the way it did in summer, the sound met the four inches of fresh snow on the square, the muffled tile roofs of the terraces, and the soft white banks along the walls. The high, brassy edge vanished into the drifts at once, leaving only a warm, round, bell-shaped note that stayed right inside the granite arch with them, humming through the soles of their boots and against their ribs for five slow breaths before it sank into the snow.

Yangyang listened all the way to the very bottom of the vibration, where the bronze went still. A single late flake from the arch-beam drifted down and landed squarely on the cerulean feathers under her chin without melting.

“<font color=paleturquoise>You see?</font>” Yangyang said softly, her fingers still hooked in her sister’s cuff. “<font color=paleturquoise>It stays under the roof.</font>”

Suisui let out her breath in a white cloud, looked at the snow sitting bright on Yangyang’s blue collar, and reached into her own coat pocket to pull out the leftover paper twist of yesterday’s roasted chestnuts, cold now, but peeled.

“<font color=thistle>It’s very fine,</font>” Suisui agreed, putting one into Yangyang’s hand and taking the other herself. “<font color=thistle>Now walk, before Ruoyun puts both our feet in her book.</font>”

Ciaccona always knew where her boots were before the lamps went out.

In twenty cities across three provinces, it had been the first thing she checked before she ever sat down on a tavern bench or unlatched a lute-case: boots by the door-post, laces tucked inside the leather so they wouldn’t trip her in the dark, coat on the lowest peg nearest the latch, and enough small copper in her left pocket to pay the night-watchman at the ferry-slip before the first bell rang. That was how you left a town while people were still fond of you—quietly, between the third watch and the fourth, so by the time anyone noticed your stool was empty at breakfast you were already two leagues down the river road calling it a tour.

Tonight her boots were at the far back corner of the chimney-flue, wedged behind Lupa’s heavy iron-shod ones and Yangyang’s dry gaiters, fifteen paces from the door and buried behind nine people’s winter coats.

Ciaccona sat on the middle of the warm brick kang with her back against the timber post and a piece of soft chamois leather in her right hand, wiping the rosin dust off the belly of her lute.

The salt-house had settled down for the night around the remains of Lupa’s pork-and-cabbage stew. Over by the stove, Lupa had banked the coals with wet ash, checked the empty stew-pot under the tie-beam—which hadn’t dripped once since the roof froze—and was now sitting on the wooden kerb greasing the leather collar-salve onto the back of Chisa’s neck with slow, careful thumbs. Near the far wall, Suisui had fallen asleep sitting bolt upright against her rolled bolster with her horn comb still in her hand, and Yangyang was quietly easing the comb out of her sister’s fingers before pulling the heavy quilt up over both their shoulders. By the flue, Qingxiao lay flat under three quilts, fast asleep and breathing cleanly through her nose without a single sneeze since supper, while Ruoyun sat on her three-legged stool at the corner of the masonry polishing her round brass spectacles on her cuff before she blew out the lamp.

Beside Ciaccona’s hip, Cartethyia was already three-quarters asleep, sitting upright with her spine pressed squarely against the central post so the floor stayed fixed beneath her heels. Above their heads, the wooden window shutter hung off its iron hook, open a hand’s width to the clear, freezing dark where Lupa had left it at dusk.

Cartethyia’s crystal laurel lay on the folded quilt beside her knee, and her right hand had found the end of Ciaccona’s red-gold braid in her sleep, her fingers curled loosely into the hair just above the ribbon.

“<font color=moccasin>Cia,</font>” Mei called softly from the inner wall, where she was tucked under the coverlet with Iuno’s left shoulder propped against her chest and Amy sprawled sideways across both their shins. “<font color=moccasin>Hand me the pad off the table before Ruoyun kills the wick?</font>”

Ciaccona didn’t have to stand up and pull her hair out of Cartethyia’s hand; she hooked her right boot heel around the leg of the nearest stool, dragged it three inches closer to the kang kerb, and leaned out with her long arm to scoop Iuno’s ruled pad and the brass-capped pencil off the middle of the trestle.

The pad was still open to the back sheet where Amy had written *AFTER* at breakfast. The white space beneath the line was completely blank, save for a tiny ring of grease where someone had set a saucer of vinegar down at noon and moved it again.

Ciaccona looked at the blank page for a second in the yellow lamplight, then flipped the leaf back to the gridded week.

Monday’s square had *Nothing* underlined twice, and *noon — Iuiu: lie down. Mei: pillow.* Tuesday’s square had *Rang Augusta*, *Amy asked*, *Snowed*, and *good one* scrawled crookedly across the bottom margin. Wednesday’s square had only the word *Wednesday* at the top in Iuno’s neat hand, because the departures had taken up the page after it.

“<font color=royalblue>Here,</font>” Iuno said from the inner wall, holding her right hand out across the quilt.

Ciaccona didn’t toss it over. She braced the small pad against the rounded maple rib of her lute, uncapped the brass pencil with her teeth, and wrote in the middle of Wednesday’s square in her quick, slanted musician’s hand:

*Pencils out. Still here.*

She capped the pencil, tossed both pad and lead lightly across Amy’s ankles onto the quilt beside Mei’s knee, and picked up her lute.

“<font color=moccasin>What’d you write?</font>” Mei asked, squinting down at the square in the dim glow as Ruoyun blew out the tallow lamp by the flue, leaving only the tiny red-and-green light of the charging harness by the door and the pale snow-shine coming through the open shutter.

“<font color=teal>The minutes of the meeting, capo,</font>” Ciaccona said, laying the lute flat across her lap so the peg-box didn’t bump Cartethyia’s knee.

With the pad of her right thumb—softly, so the sound wouldn’t carry past the brick platform—she brushed the three lower courses in a slow, warm D-major chord, letting her splinted left forefinger rest idle on the maple neck while her third and fourth fingers walked a lazy, five-note Ragunnese harbour tune up the fret-board and back down to the open string.

Across the kang, Lupa climbed up onto the outer rim in her rust-red sheepskin coat, curled her tail tucked in behind Chisa’s knees, and let out a long breath that rattled the dry straw in the bolster.

“<font color=teal>Night, wolf,</font>” Ciaccona murmured over the strings.

“<font color=indianred>Shut up and go to sleep, bard,</font>” the sheepskin muttered back, without a trace of heat.

On the inner side of the platform, Iuno picked up the pad one-handed in the faint red dark, read the two words in Wednesday’s square, and closed the cover over the week and the blank page behind it alike, sliding the book and the pencil back into the pocket of her folded coat beside the bolster.

Ciaccona laid the flat of her palm across the strings to quiet the last hum of the wood, set the lute down safely between the post and the wall where nobody would step on it in the morning, and slid down onto her side on the quilt with her cheek resting against Cartethyia’s shin and her braid slack and warm in Cartethyia’s sleeping fist.

From the dark corner by the inner wall came a long, contented rustle of bedding as Mei burrowed her nose into Iuno’s collar.

“<font color=moccasin>Iuiu.</font>”

“<font color=royalblue>Go to sleep, Mei.</font>”

“<font color=moccasin>What day is it tomorrow?</font>”

There was a brief pause in the warm dark, and then Iuno’s voice came back, level and soft against the bricks.

“<font color=royalblue>Thursday, darling.</font>”

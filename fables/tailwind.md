# Tailwind

*a oneshot of the Banat al-Rih*

---

*]the frightened wrote that we crawled up out of the world[*
*]they were right[*
*]so did they. we kept the memory[*

## i.

The god took her tea with two sugars. This was the first discovery of the new age, and Herta recorded it.

"Two sugars," she said, writing.

"Am I being studied?"

"Footnoted."

"Then two sugars, and note that I stir left."

"The tide stirs left in the north," said Black Swan, from the window seat.

"She invented that," said Viviane.

"I invented several things," Mina said, serene behind the steam, and the table let her have it, because a woman who slept through a century has earned a few apocrypha at breakfast.

Convalescence suited the house strangely. Mina moved through it the way weather moves through a valley: slow fronts, sudden clearings, a heavy hand with time. She steeped tea toward the geological. She watered the basil on a schedule meant for glaciers, and Elaina staged three rescues before assuming custody outright. She learned the kettle, the stairs, the grammar of a household — which cup was whose, which chair complained, which hour belonged to Phrolova and her shut door — and she learned it all with the courtesy of a guest and the authority of a keel.

On her first walk she made it forty paces before kneeling at a tide-pool.

"Ladies." She gathered them with a glance. "This is Sgurr. He has held this pool through nine storms and an eviction attempt by an octopus." The hermit crab lifted one claw, conceivably in greeting. "Sgurr, my daughters."

The witches bowed. It seemed the thing to do. It became the thing they did — every walk thereafter paused for introductions, to crabs, to a gull with a bent primary she addressed as Admiral, to a stand of kelp she called *the parliament*, and slowly the coven understood that this was her love language and her census both: she had spent ten thousand years learning the world by name, individual by individual, and a century's sleep had put her behind on her rounds.

"She knows the gulls apart," Herta muttered, third walk.

"She knows the *waves* apart," said Viviane, who had watched her greet one.

*In the fish market they say the water has gone polite. A ring lost forty years came up in a net, wrapped in kelp like a parcel. A ferryman swears the fog thanked him at the third buoy; his wife swears he drinks. Both accounts are kept.*

## ii.

Elaina briefed the god on the century.

She did it properly — maps across the table, her travel journals open at the good pages, a pointer improvised from a chopstick. Wars, treaties, the falling and rising of suns, a kneeling god who stood. Mina listened to all of it with her chin in her hand and asked about small things.

"Do children still skip stones?"

"Everywhere I've been."

"And the song about the miller's daughter?"

"Gone, I think." Elaina checked her notes. "There's one about a satellite now. Same tune."

"Songs are frugal," Mina said, satisfied, and had her hum it twice.

The boots gave Elaina away. They stood by the door, oiled to a shine, and Elaina oiled them when she was restless, and lately she oiled them nightly, guilt working the cloth as hard as the wax. Homes were new to her. She had been given one, and a family in it, and a god who took two sugars — and underneath all of it the road went on humming its one long note, the note she had followed since she was small, and she hated her own feet for hearing it.

Mina watched the oiling from her chair, three nights running. On the fourth she spoke.

"Your boots are ready before you are."

Elaina's cloth stopped. "I just got here."

"Harbors are for leaving." Mina set down her cup. "That's the whole craft of them. The staying is the wind's business, and wind that stays is called weather in the house, and eventually it's called damp." She smiled at Elaina's stricken face and reached over and tapped the journal. "Go as my weather. Bring me back the world in installments. I'm a century behind on my reading."

"Letters?"

"Letters. Long ones. And when you sit at strange water, listen to it for me." She took Elaina's hand and turned it palm-up, as if reading a chart. "I'll teach you the alphabet before you go. The sea writes in weather. It's a slow hand, but it gossips."

They started that week: cloud grammar, the punctuation of swells, what a green evening means, which silences at sea are shy and which are aimed. Elaina filled thirty pages. The boots stood by the door, patient now, spoken for.

## iii.

Herta conducted the interview at the long table with the old bestiary on her left, fresh ink on her right, and an audience she had banned twice and then permitted, since they came anyway and brought snacks.

"Primary source," she said. "Sit."

Mina sat. Folded her hands. A model witness.

"The books say your kind are contrary to nature."

"We are the parts of nature that answered back."

Herta wrote. "Origin. The oldest texts disagree."

"Yours came first." Mina considered her own hands. "You dreamed at the world — loudly, the way you do everything — and the world is courteous. It replied. We are the replies." A tilt of the head. "You made us out of your weathers and your wants, and then you were startled that we had faces."

"The teeth," Herta said. "The warnings. The child-eater in the shallows."

"The frightened made me a warning, and the warning grew teeth to fit." Mina said it evenly, an old wound gone to smooth scar. "That is the trade, little scholar. You sculpt us. We sculpt you back. We've been passing the same clay across the table since the first fire."

From the window seat: "Put that in the book," said Black Swan.

"Everything goes in the book," said Herta, writing faster.

"What's it called?" Viviane asked. "The book."

Herta laid her hand flat on the bestiary's cracked cover, sixty generations of borrowed fright under her palm, and moved the hand to her own fresh pages.

"*A Bestiary of the Beloved.* Testimony edition." She looked up, daring comment. Mina reached across the table and turned Herta's notebook a quarter-turn, read the title upside down, right side up, and pronounced it sound.

"Then a contribution, from the source." She waited for the pen. "Write: a human is a guest in the world. Lovely manners, short visit. My kind are staff. We love the house differently — every hinge, every draft, every stain and its story. And write this under it, because it's the finding your predecessors were too frightened to enter: *the staff can be homesick. In the house.*"

The pen scratched to the end of the line and stopped. The audience was very quiet around its snacks.

"Now you," Mina said, folding her hands again, witness turned examiner. "What does a witch fear?"

Herta's chin came up on reflex. The room waited. "Being read once," she said, "and shelved."

"Mm." Mina held her eyes. "Lend me your thesis again. The new one."

She read it in two days and disagreed with it on page forty, out loud, at dinner, with citations. Herta floated for a week. The others pretended the glow was the lamps.

*At night the roots carry a low frequency up from the glacier: the kneeling god, minding his city, sends a word down the amber to the risen sleeper. A Soliskin arrives on small legs and sits an hour by Mina's chair, warm as a coal, and goes home with a blessing and a biscuit. Two gods, one century each, comparing notes on patience.*

## iv.

Alice came down from the coven's old house with a satchel of years.

She was Guild-badged and ink-stained, a black-sand village grown into a scribe's shoulders, and Viviane had hired her long ago for the strangest vigil on any commission board: sit by the pool, and write what the sleeper says. The satchel held the results. Winter after winter of it, in a hand that behaved like tide-marks — because the sleeper's language predated alphabets, and Alice had built one to hold it, letter by invented letter, the way you build a jetty to hold a sea.

Mina turned the pages slowly. Her face did several things a century apart.

"You wrote this hand beautifully. Who taught you?"

"You did, ma'am." Alice went pink. "You talk in your sleep. I copied the shapes out of the air until they behaved."

Mina read on. Stopped. Read one line twice, and closed the book with the gentleness people keep for sleeping children.

"Well?" Herta, hungry, half out of her chair.

"The sea keeps drafts." Mina poured Alice's tea herself. "Some of mine survived."

It would have ended there, except that Elaina, packing that evening, found Alice cross-referencing by lamplight, and looked over her shoulder, and saw it: one word, recurring, every winter, spelled the same each time in the invented hand. Alice had underlined its instances. There were forty of them.

They brought it to Mina at breakfast, because the household had one policy and the policy was the table.

"A name?" Elaina asked.

"A sister," Mina said, buttering toast.

The room held still around the word.

Black Swan set down her cup with care. "My cards walked your past to its floor. Before the fear, they found water and an end of cards."

"Your cards read what was written." Mina reached for the honey. "Some things were only ever spoken." She looked around the table at her daughters, kindly, finally, the way you look at children who have found a locked drawer in a house they thought entirely mapped. "Older than the warning. Older than the water. Eat your eggs."

They ate their eggs. Alice underlined the forty-first instance that night, in the margin of a fresh winter, and dated it.

## v.

Phrolova kept the hour after dusk for herself, and the household kept it for her, and so it was the household's one clock everybody honored.

Which is why she alone was passing the porch when Mina, mending a net in the last light, hummed.

Four bars. Phrolova stopped in the doorway with her hand on the frame.

"Where did you learn that?"

"It came down a river once." Mina's fingers went on tying, small sure knots. "Long ago and far upstream. Grief travels by water — songs ride along, fare paid in salt. A singer up there lost someone, and the current carried the losing all the way down to me." A knot, drawn snug. "The sea kept the song. I kept the sea."

"The words."

"About a sun." Mina looked up then, and her eyes were the color of the sea in none of Herta's books. "You know it."

"I set it for strings once," Phrolova said, which was true, and half.

"Then it kept you too."

The question stood at Phrolova's lips like a guest at a door. There is a paradise she tends, south and inward, full of tuned echoes; there is a god on this porch who spent ten millennia opening doors for the drowned. The two facts leaned toward each other across the dusk, and Phrolova, aristocrat of the withheld, chose the smaller ask.

"Teach me the second verse."

Mina taught her. Phrolova's voice took it the way dry ground takes rain — greedily, and pretending otherwise. When it was done she inclined her head, precise as a signature, and continued to her hour, and behind her the god went back to the net, humming the harmony now, leaving the melody's place empty, holding it.

The letter came a week later, Guild-franked, addressed to the household entire, and Alice read it aloud at dinner: a Rinascitan sailor's account, third-hand, of the year the cathedral fell — *and a voice walked out of the wreck of the morning star, they say, and sang the harbor full, and sings there yet.*

"Sailors' report," Herta said. "Sailors embroider."

"Both accounts are kept," said Alice, scribe to the bone.

Phrolova asked for the letter after dinner, to check a nautical term, and folded it very small, and it lived thereafter in her breast pocket, and everyone at the table watched her do it and studied their plates like scripture.

## vi.

Elaina asked Phrolova for music lessons, and the household entered a dark age.

The instrument was a viola, Phrolova's second-best, tuned by ear to a standard predating the current pitch of the world. Elaina attacked it with the optimism she brought to borders and the accuracy she brought to jam. Herta filed a formal complaint, in writing, with footnotes. Viviane offered soundproofing and priced it. Black Swan took to dancing along in the corridor, matching each catastrophe so exactly that it looked choreographed, which she claimed was charity and everyone understood to be theatre.

Phrolova endured. Weeks of it. She sat through scales that lost their nerve halfway and bowings that sawed like weather, hands folded, spine perfect, correcting fingerings in a voice that could have taught etiquette to a glacier — and one evening, six weeks in, after Elaina fought a folk tune to a draw, she delivered her verdict.

"Your playing is full of errors."

"Thank you."

Phrolova looked at her for a long moment. The lamp fizzed. Somewhere below, the sea shouldered the rocks.

"Keep them," she said.

She began composing again that month. The household heard it through the shut door in fragments — spare lines, patient, with wrong notes set in on purpose like rough stones in a smooth wall, load-bearing. The manuscript on her stand acquired a title page with the title space open and a dedication line ruled and ready. Herta, delivering laundry, glimpsed it and reported to the kitchen: "It's for someone."

"Everything is," said Black Swan.

The lesson before Elaina's departure, Phrolova set the viola aside and gave her four bars instead — hummed once, hummed back, corrected, sealed.

"For thresholds," she said. "Hum it at strange doors. Old houses remember their manners."

"What does it do?"

"Introduces you," said Phrolova, "as mine," and adjusted Elaina's collar with two fingers, the way you true a tuning peg, and sent her to pack.

The others had begun calling them *the freshmen.* They objected in unison, which proved the point.

## vii.

Agrat bat Mahlat came on the Wednesday, as was proper, with the board under her arm and a wind at her heel that smelled of far fields.

She set up on the porch table without invitation, laid the seeds in their pits with a banker's economy, and studied the risen god across the wood.

"A century of sleep," she said, "and you'll still play the coast opening."

"It wins."

"It bores."

"Winning bores you. That's your entire biography." Mina sowed. "Sit down, old wind."

They played the way ancients play — three moves to the hour, the game a table for the talk. The talk went where their talk had always gone, down past the city and the century to the standing subjects, and Agrat, capturing a modest house of seeds, raised the standing subject of the age.

"The deep is empty. Your usurper receded owing back-rent, and the scattered are drifting — ten thousand voices with nowhere to pool." She banked her capture. "Landlady."

"Drowned is drowned." Mina sowed on, unhurried. "My door was always the same door."

"So you'll take them in. Void-lost, star-lost, the researchers, the reachers."

"I'll leave the door open. They know the way down." Her hand paused over the board. "They were promised a meeting-again. Someone should be home for it."

Agrat's red eyes lifted from the pits. "And the throne?"

"Thrones are what the frightened build for us so they know which room to avoid." Mina moved. "I have a chair. It's at that table, and it wobbles, and Viviane has promised to fix it since spring."

"Empty thrones draw tenants," Agrat said, softer, and this was the true errand of the visit, laid on the board like a marker. "Something will try the lock before the year turns. The deep remembers being ruled and the world abhors a vacancy — it will grow *something* into the shape, love, the way it grew you into the warning. It's the world's oldest habit and its worst."

"Then I'll teach the deep a new habit," said Mina. "I learned one recently. It took five witches and a teacup."

They played out the middlegame in silence. Agrat, reaching for her seed-pouch to reset, dislodged something with the seeds — a single grain apart from the set, dry, patient, worn — and caught Mina looking, and returned the look across four thousand years.

"Tuition," Agrat said, and closed the pouch, and the subject.

"They write books about us," she went on presently, sowing. "The hungers. The bindings. The contrary-to-nature. Backwards, all of it, as usual. We *are* the nature. A human is here on a visa — beautiful manners, brief stay, everything astonishing because everything's new. We're natives. We love the place the way natives do: specifically, tiredly, in full knowledge of its plumbing." She captured. "They dream at the world and the world answers, and we're the answering, and then they bind the answers and call it theology."

"They renovate," Mina said.

Agrat's hand stopped over the board.

"Guests renovate," Mina said. "They hung a sun, this century. Human hands. My daughters braced a god's rising with tree-roots and arithmetic. We love the house as it is, old wind; that's our whole tenderness and our whole limit. They love it as it could be." She took the last capture of the game and swept it home. "Which is why I keep witches. Half native, half guest. They love in both directions at once. It's very loud and I slept a hundred years through it, so I have some catching up to do."

Agrat counted the granaries, lost by four, and studied the result the way she studied everything: as intelligence, delivered.

"Nuwa asks after your health," she said, rising. "She phrased it as a request for data."

"Tell her the data walks a mile a day and takes two sugars."

At dinner a place stood ready at the table, laid before she could decline it, and she ate, and mentioned membership to no one, and no one mentioned it to her, this being the coven's oldest courtship and both parties enjoying the length of it. At the door she raised the umbrella against a clear sky.

"Same board next Wednesday."

Which, from Agrat, was a vow.

*Since the waking, Black Swan's cards face forward in patches — small clearings of tomorrow in a lifetime of yesterdays. At midnight she deals three: The Chariot. The Moon. And a third from a design she owns in no deck — a hand, holding a lyre, offering it grip-first. She turns it over twice. The back matches her own. She tells the dark, "Rude," and keeps the card.*

*At the fig stall, a volunteer in a borrowed vest weighs Elaina's change twice, humming three notes as she counts. Elaina hums a fourth on reflex, reaching for the bag. The volunteer looks up like a critic at a promising opening night, smiles a borrowed smile, and adds an extra fig.*

## viii.

The sawdust betrayed Viviane on a Tuesday.

It tracked from the sea-cave in a fine gold thread, and the household followed it the way households follow such things — in a body, pretending errands — and found her in the lantern-light with a plane in her fist, mid-stroke, over the ribs of a hull.

Forty feet of keel lay blocked and true on the shingle. The stem rose from it in a clean sheer that any coast in the world would have recognized and saluted.

"It's a hobby," Viviane said.

Herta circled the keel, counting frames. "It's forty feet."

"Hobbies grow."

Black Swan was already aloft, having gone up the shorings like ivy, and hung from the crossbeam by her knees testing an imaginary stay. "Where does she sail?"

"Wherever the harbor pleases."

The sentence sat in the lantern-light and unfolded itself. She was building the harbor a ship. The witches looked at the ribs, and at each other, and at the woman with driftwood in her staff and an expired exile in her past, and the family meeting convened itself right there on the shingle, minutes kept by nobody, everybody.

"The Order fell," Herta said, to the frames, carefully. "Years ago now. Your pilgrim's writ died with it."

"There are shipyards in Ragunna," Viviane said, planing. "Good ones." The stroke ran long and smooth. "I'd want to see how the harbor water sits these days. Its color." Another stroke. "That's all."

Which was Viviane for homesick, and the household received it as such and let the plane keep talking.

They put the question to Mina at breakfast, plainly, since the table was the policy: the ship, the season, the roads, the wind. Whether the Banat al-Rih, having kept a hundred years' anchor-watch over a sleeper, now weighed anchor. Whether the wind stays.

Mina buttered her toast through all of it.

"The wind stays as long as it likes," she said. "That's what wind means. And when it goes, it goes the way tide goes out." She looked around her table — scholar, shipwright, dancer, conductor, traveler, scribe — and out the window at the keel-cave, and something ten thousand years old settled in her like ballast finding its line. "With the coming-back built in."

"The season, then," said Herta, who needed a syllabus for everything, including farewells. "We finish the ship. Elaina goes ahead."

"As weather," said Mina.

"As weather," Elaina agreed, and went to fetch her journal, because the commissions started immediately.

Herta went first: a bookseller, third stall from the fountain, Mingting. "He owes me a folio and forty years of interest. Collect either."

Viviane: "Ragunna. Stand on the harbor mole at evening and look at the water. Just look. Write me its color."

Black Swan produced an envelope, small, sealed, weightless with something specific inside. "Lose this," she said, "somewhere far. Tell me nothing." She pressed it flat into Elaina's palm and closed the fingers over it herself.

Phrolova's was a letter, sealed twice, edges knife-true. The address line held one word: *South.* "You'll know the door," she said. "It sings."

Alice: "An alphabet I haven't got. Any of them."

And Mina, last, refilling Elaina's cup for the road with the whole table listening:

"Ask what they call me now. Wherever you go — the ports, the villages, the far coasts, the frightened and the fond. Every word. Write them all down and send them home to me." She stirred, left. "I collect."

## ix.

They took her to the sea on the last morning of Elaina's residence, so the going and the greeting could share a tide.

She walked the shingle barefoot, shoes swinging from one hand like a girl's, and the world came out to meet her the way a town meets a returning ship. The fog stood off politely at the headland. Gulls came down in ones and twos and ranked themselves along the wrack-line, an honor guard with terrible posture, and the Admiral took the flank. The tide, due at noon, came in early — a full hour of eagerness across the flats, folding and refolding at her ankles like a dog that has waited at the door for a century and intends to say so at length.

She stood in it a long time. Her daughters stood back and let the two of them talk.

Then she knelt, and from her basket took the porcelain cup with the flared letters, and filled it from the flask, steam sheeting off in the sea-wind, and set it at the waterline, full and steady on the sand. The next wave came up around it, curled, and took it with both hands.

"What did you give it?" Elaina asked, pack on her shoulders, boots at last on her feet.

"Tea. Two sugars." Mina watched the cup ride out, upright, borne, until the light took it. "It's been drinking grief for ten thousand years. Somebody had to start it on something kinder."

The wind came around to the south then, small and exact, and filled Elaina's coat like a sail, and the coven stood on the world's edge together — six women and a god, a keel in a cave behind them, a card that belongs to no deck, a letter marked *South*, an open door in the deep — and let it push.

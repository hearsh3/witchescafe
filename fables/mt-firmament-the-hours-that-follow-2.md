# The Hours That Follow

Jinhsi had written five questions on the page beside the scale. By the following evening there were seven, and the first had acquired a blot where she had held the brush too long.

She took the page to supper.

Changli was trying to cut through a piece of pickled radish with the edge of her spoon. Sanhua watched the attempt for a while, then moved the small knife within reach. Through the open window came a porter’s argument with a cart wheel and the smell of something frying farther down the terrace.

“I would like to go to Hongzhen,” Jinhsi said. “The last boat brought a supply receipt and an empty message cylinder. Xinyi has answered every letter I have sent her since I took office, including the ones that only contained questions about the weather.”

Changli put the spoon down.

“When did that boat arrive?”

“This afternoon. The crew said the cloud bank was moving against itself. They waited offshore until it opened.” Jinhsi laid the folded page beside her plate. “I have been waiting for Jué to speak. Scar told us he was imprisoned, and I keep returning to that sentence. I want to find him.”

Sanhua’s hand moved toward the empty place where her Terminal usually lay during meals. She left it on the table.

“I would like to come with you,” she said. “Deng can take the house watch. I have already asked him which days he could cover.”

Jinhsi looked at her.

“You thought I would go.”

“You have been reading the coastal departures at breakfast. Yesterday you asked whether the mountain path still floods below the second bridge.”

Changli turned the knife once between her fingers, then set it across the plate.

“I have an old survey of the pass. Wen can obtain the present copy and check it against mine.” Her gaze settled on Jinhsi. “I want to go as well. I have been considering how to bring that up while you were still deciding.”

“You can bring it up now.”

“The Fractsidus had access to machinery beneath the highlands. If they have reached the mountain’s defenses, we should take people who have worked with their alterations. Amy saw the rain chamber. Chisa found its lock.”

Jinhsi opened her paper. The blot had dried with a glossy centre.

“There is also something Scar said privately. I wrote it down that night so I could remember his exact words.” She touched the line. “‘When your Sentinel calls for you, ask which of you is supposed to come back.’”

The cart wheel below came free with a wooden crack. The porter swore, followed by the delighted laughter of somebody who had predicted precisely this failure.

Changli read the sentence from where she sat. A pulse beat visibly at the base of her throat.

“We should take Baizhi with us,” she said.

Jinhsi smoothed the paper.

“I was hoping you would suggest her.”

Sanhua drew the serving dish closer and put another spoonful onto Jinhsi’s plate. Then she took some for herself.

They ate while Changli explained what the winter crossings were like, where the current caught a boat broadside, and which captain had once spent an entire passage telling her how to improve the Ministry of Transport. Jinhsi remembered that captain. He had given her a plum when she left the mountain and told her to keep its stone until the voyage ended.

“I swallowed it,” she said.

Changli stared at her, then began to laugh.

“You had both hands closed for the entire journey.”

“I thought he would ask to see it.”

Sanhua’s mouth curved over her tea. Outside, the porter called for a second person to lift the axle, and somebody came down the stair to help.

***

At the guesthouse, Cartethyia had spread three shirts on the floor and was consulting Ciaccona about which one would dry fastest in mountain air.

“We have been invited to consider coming,” Ciaccona said. “You have packed my socks.”

“Yours were beside mine. I thought you would want the thick ones.”

“I do want the thick ones. I also want to finish the conversation before my wardrobe leaves the building.”

Jinhsi sat on the bed because Amy had taken the chair apart to retrieve a screw from its frame. She explained the empty cylinder, the sea fog, the scale, and Scar’s words. When she finished, Amy fitted the screw into a small case and pushed the chair back together with her foot.

“Cartethyia should hear the mountain’s version of that,” she said. “She has some experience with gods putting their resonators through things.”

Cartethyia’s hands slowed on the shirts.

“Let me hear what he says,” she said. “And show me where you grew up! You mentioned the hot springs once. Cia pretended she was reading, but she asked about the springs afterward.”

Ciaccona leaned back against the bedpost.

“The bard requests an afternoon with hot water. Put that wherever you are putting the important information.”

Mei sat on the floor with Abby leaning against her calf. He was inspecting a biscuit that had broken unevenly and appeared to hold the manufacturer responsible.

“I’ll come,” Mei said. “I want to speak to Jué too. There are things in the archive he was there for.” She glanced toward Jinhsi’s folded paper. “You should have time with him when we find him. We can work out the order together.”

Iuno lifted her living hand from the strap she had been adjusting. The dressing over her crystal shoulder showed pale beneath her open collar.

“I can travel, according to Baizhi. She has specified what I can carry in language she expects Lupa to enforce.”

“I understood it perfectly,” Lupa said from the doorway. “You can carry your own opinions. Somebody else gets the teapot.”

“The porcelain requires a steady hand.”

“Then you can walk beside me and be anxious about it.”

Iuno looked at the large pack against Lupa’s leg. Her tea case was already fitted between two folded blankets.

“You have described my morning with considerable accuracy.”

Chisa was feeling the seams of her gloves. “I’ll bring rope, chalk, and the little bells from the truck. If the road changes, we will need to leave marks we can compare.”

Amy sat on the reconstructed chair, tested it, and reached for her Terminal.

“I’ll ask Mornye for the synchronization records from the Drive. We’ll have to take whatever she can send tonight. She has a new intake this week, and Lucilla has apparently discovered the students are using a training simulator for competitive cooking.”

Mei brightened. “Does it simulate the food?”

“It simulates a reactor and a surprising number of kitchen accidents. I have requested access.”

At the window, Yangyang held the edge of the curtain between two fingers.

“The coastal captain has room for us. Chixia is taking the dispatch desk while her leg heals. She asked me to bring back a jar of the mountain peppers.”

The curtain slipped from her hand. A fold rose toward her cheek, rested there briefly, and moved toward the travel bag by her feet.

Yangyang bent to open it. Inside, two shells lay in their shared cloth.

“We can leave them here with Baizhi’s things,” she said softly. “Or carry them in the inner pocket. There will be sea air on the way.”

The cloth lifted at one corner. Yangyang waited. It folded over the shells, carefully, and she retied it.

Jinhsi watched her settle the bundle against her chest. The Wind had spent her last word beside a bowl on the southern shelf. She moved Yangyang’s hat a little on the window ledge, testing the weight, and then left it crooked.

Yangyang put it on that way.

By the time Baizhi arrived, someone had brought a map upstairs. She placed her medical case on one corner, read the route, and asked who intended to carry the folded stretcher.

Lupa raised a hand.

“It can go above the teapot. She’ll complain less if she knows I’m protecting the expensive luggage.”

***

Hsin’s reply arrived before dawn, carried by a harbor clerk whose coat smelled of rope.

There were three pages, a small packet of engineering drawings, and a seal impression made by a paw placed squarely in wet ink.

The first page confirmed that the crews remaining on the coast would maintain a landing outside the mountain’s influence. The second described a bargain made at Norfall. Fractsidus personnel had withdrawn from Mt. Firmament. Their external control and observation channels had been severed. Attached were the locations of the severed lines and the means of verifying them.

The installation inside the mountain remained active.

Jinhsi read that sentence twice.

On the third page Hsin had written in a looser hand.

*The price included her ability to put her hand back into this particular machinery. You will see the cut ends. Other portions of our transaction await Mengzhou, where the people they concern can hear them.*

*Jué’s presence saturates the mountain. Adding mine would give a damaged temporal system another sovereign frequency to reconcile. My engineers can keep a boat in ordinary water, and I have instructed them to do so. You may take considerable satisfaction in making use of it.*

*Bring questions home. I expect you have acquired several with my name on them.*

Jinhsi turned the page over and wrote her reply on the back.

She wanted the dates on which Hsin had learned of the installation. She wanted the withdrawal orders preserved. She wanted the limits of the coastal support given to the captain in language the captain could explain to his passengers.

She signed it, then added that the proposed meeting in Mengzhou would include the consequences of releasing Cristoforo.

Sanhua took the reply to the waiting clerk.

Changli stood beside the window until the man had gone.

“The drawings will help,” she said. “I am angry that they arrived through this bargain.”

Jinhsi folded the three pages separately from her own questions.

“I would like her to hear you say that too.”

Before leaving City Hall, Jinhsi met the Council members taking the daily civil sittings in her absence. Wen read the temporary terms aloud; a deputy asked about the emergency stores, and she put the current limits into the margin. Jiyan’s northern command continued under its existing orders. She left the coastal captain’s return schedule on her desk, beside the account of Changli’s inquiry, then went back for the coat she had forgotten on her chair.

***

The sea grew quiet a little before noon.

Jinhsi noticed it through the soles of her feet. The boat’s shudder eased; the engine continued to labor beneath the deck. Ahead, Mt. Firmament rose through white cloud, its lower cliffs dark with water and the snowfields above them bright enough to hurt.

A gull hung beside the mast with its wings raised.

The captain eased the throttle. The gull dipped its head, beat once, and returned to the same place. A loose feather slid along the rail, lifted, and settled back beneath its own shadow.

Amy leaned over the side. Her reflection turned its head a moment after she did.

“That is going to become irritating very quickly,” she said.

Yangyang’s hand closed around the rail. The Wind drew into the shelter of her collar, cold enough to raise the fine hairs along her neck.

Farther out, one of Hsin’s coastal platforms sounded its horn. The note reached them in three pieces, with a stretch of silence wide enough for Jinhsi to hear somebody swallow.

The captain brought them in along the route marked in the drawings. His mate stood at the bow with a pole. Whenever the pole’s shadow separated from its tip, he called for a turn.

At the landing, a rope coil lay half-unwound. The free end rose toward a mooring post, touched it, and fell through the same curve. Its fibers had rubbed a pale groove into the wood.

Changli checked her Terminal. The screen showed the harbor they had left that morning. Then it showed her own hand closing over it a second earlier.

“We should reach Hongzhen while the path remains usable,” she said. “Xinyi will know which shelters are occupied.”

They left the boat with their packs held close. The captain kept his engine running until everyone had crossed the landing, then backed into the strip of ordinary water beyond the headland.

On the path above the shore, a rabbit sat with a blade of grass held between its teeth. Its whiskers were still. Beyond it, a stoat ran between two roots, leapt, twisted in midair, and arrived behind the first root again.

Cartethyia crouched beside the loop. On its sixth circuit the animal caught sight of her and flinched.

“It remembers each time,” she said.

Chisa put a bell on the ground. The clapper swung through the metal and returned in silence. She moved it a hand’s breadth. A thin note sounded.

“The edge crosses the second root. Put the blanket there. We can lift from this side.”

Lupa took one end while Cartethyia spread the other. On the next leap they caught the stoat, lifted the whole blanket across the line, and set it down.

The animal burst out, bit Lupa’s glove, and vanished into the undergrowth.

Lupa looked at the tooth marks.

“That seems healthy enough. Let’s keep moving.”

They found a bird on the next bend whose breast lay cold beneath Baizhi’s fingers. She wrapped it in a cloth and placed it below the path, where the wind could reach it. Above her, three others waited in the branches with their beaks open around a call.

Jinhsi kept looking up as they climbed.

***

Hongzhen’s square was full of people in the middle of ordinary things.

A man carrying turnips stood with one foot above a step. A little girl ran beneath his raised basket, disappeared as she reached the far side, and came running back beneath it. Steam from a dumpling stall curved into white knots. Each knot loosened, passed through its neighbor, and tightened again.

Beside the well, a woman held both hands over her face.

A boy stood in front of her, speaking with the careful patience of a child imitating an adult.

“I got out, Ma. Auntie pulled me through the back. I’m here by the well now.”

The woman’s skirt was lifted by a wind that had passed. Her hands stayed against her cheeks.

Jinhsi set her bag down.

“What is your name?” she asked the boy.

“Yun. She was looking for me when it happened.” He pointed at a collapsed awning. Its corner hung above the paving, with a shower of broken tiles spread beneath it in the air. “I was there. She thinks I’m still there.”

Baizhi knelt beside him. A woman emerged from an alley carrying two blankets and stopped so suddenly that one slipped from her arms.

“Madam Magistrate.”

Jinhsi recognized her after a moment. She had sold dried pears at the winter market and used to keep the smallest pieces in a jar beneath the counter.

“Auntie Han. Is Xinyi in the shelter?”

“She sent me for him.” Han gathered the fallen blanket. “He comes back whenever somebody opens the door.”

Yun looked at the awning. Jinhsi followed his gaze, then turned to Lupa.

“Can you brace that corner before we move her?”

“Give me the two longest poles from the stall.”

Sanhua found them. Lupa drove their feet between the paving stones while Chisa mapped the stillness around the well with the bell. Mei held the hanging edge of the awning through a patch where her sleeve aged into frayed threads, then drew back when Chisa called the boundary.

It took several minutes to work out a route wide enough for the woman’s body. They carried her rigidly, one hand still raised in front of her face. When her feet crossed the final line she gasped and lunged toward the falling roof.

Yun caught her skirt.

She looked down. Her mouth worked around the breath she had brought out with her. Then she folded over him so quickly that Baizhi had to catch her shoulder.

Jinhsi stood beside the braced awning until Han had taken them into the alley.

Above the dumpling stall, the white knots continued to turn.

***

The shelter lay beneath the meeting house, behind a storeroom wall that Jinhsi remembered being strictly forbidden to climb.

Xinyi opened the hidden door herself. Her hair had slipped from its pins on one side. She held a list in a floury hand and looked first at Jinhsi, then at Changli, then at the people filing in behind them.

“You brought enough boots for a landslide,” she said. “Come through. Leave the muddy things by the drain.”

Her hand found Jinhsi’s shoulder as she passed. It stayed there until the last person had entered.

The chamber smelled of lamp oil, damp wool, and millet cooking in a large pot. Families occupied platforms along the walls. At the centre, a brass device turned inside a cage of white light. Water dripped from one shallow basin into another at a steady pace.

Amy went toward it. Xinyi held out an arm.

“The handle stays at that mark. We have people who understand the next three adjustments. I can send for one.”

Amy stopped. “Please do. I’d like them to tell me what it did before the trouble started.”

“Usually? It annoyed everybody by chiming during meetings.”

Xinyi brought them to a curtained recess where a map lay beneath a jar of pickles. Jué’s chamber was marked above Truthseeker’s Pass. The path leading to it had been crossed out twice in different hands.

“The Fractsidus came through the maintenance entrance,” she said. “We heard the defenses engage. The chamber sealed. For a while the days only felt strange. A pot would boil before the fire was properly lit. People arrived late and argued about the clocks.”

She tapped the newest mark.

“Then a woman went to fetch washing from her courtyard and came back with her hair grown past her waist. We moved everyone we could reach down here.”

Changli bent over the map. “The cipher is still in the pass?”

“In the inner vault. We sent three people. Two found their way back to the entrance together. The third returned yesterday, according to his own watch, and ate five meals.”

Jinhsi touched the line leading toward Mianloong.

“Have you heard his voice?”

Xinyi’s floury hand settled over hers.

“Once. Through the stone. He asked how many people were inside the shelter. I told him, and the device steadied.”

“What did he sound like?”

Xinyi took her time answering.

“He had trouble finishing the question.”

Around them the shelter continued its evening. A child objected to the quantity of millet in his bowl. An elderly man called for the woman who kept his medicine. Somewhere behind the curtain, Yun began telling someone how a wolf had held up the roof.

Jinhsi pressed her thumb against the warm edge of the scale inside her sleeve.

“Please show me where we can put our things,” she said. “Then tell me everything you remember.”

***

Xinyi gave Jinhsi the narrow room beside the old kitchen.

The bed had acquired a different cover. The little shelf above it still listed to the left. Jinhsi put her bag beneath the shelf and reached automatically for the peg behind the door, where her hand found empty plaster.

“We moved it,” Xinyi said. “Yun caught his coat on it and pulled the whole thing out. His mother thought the wall would come down with it.”

Jinhsi laughed, then sat on the bed.

From this height she could see the underside of the shelf. Three marks remained in the wood: the places where she had scraped it with the top of a borrowed writing brush while trying to reach a book.

Xinyi followed her gaze.

“You used to insist somebody taller had put it there deliberately.”

“I still think they did.”

“You were the person who asked for it to be kept safe.”

In the passage, Sanhua was explaining the watch to Chisa. Their voices passed through the curtain at the foot of the bed, one low and measured, the other level enough that the pauses became easy to hear.

Jinhsi took out the scale. Its narrow end shone through her fingers.

“When I left, did he say anything to you?”

Xinyi lowered herself onto the stool.

“He asked whether the city would give you somewhere warm to sleep. I told him I had written to the household steward. The next morning he asked again.”

Jinhsi looked down at her hands.

“I thought you would tell me he spoke about the appointment.”

“We had discussed the appointment for weeks. By then I was worrying about your seasickness, and he was worrying about your room.”

A cart rattled beyond the wall. Someone called for the wheel to be lifted over the drain. Jinhsi sat with the scale between her palms and tried to imagine Jué asking an ordinary question twice because the first answer had failed to settle him.

Xinyi rose with a hand against her knee.

“There is hot water in the kitchen. Wash while you have the room. Changli has asked for a copy of every maintenance book we possess, so I expect she will occupy herself until supper.”

“The custody order requires me to be present when she examines the prophetic material.”

“Then she can begin with the drainage. There are sixty years of complaints about it.”

Xinyi pulled the curtain across. Jinhsi remained on the bed for another minute before taking off her boots.

***

They entered Truthseeker’s Pass after breakfast.

Iuno stayed in Hongzhen with Xinyi, Lupa, Ciaccona, and Baizhi’s second medical case. The town had families in temporal pockets along three lanes, a shelter approaching capacity, and a water channel whose far end emptied into yesterday. Yangyang went with them to map the outer limits. The Wind stayed close to her shoulder, occasionally lifting a thread from her sleeve and letting it fall.

Baizhi accompanied the party into the pass. She had watched Jinhsi lose the thread of a sentence over breakfast, then repeat a hand movement while the cup in her hand remained still.

“Tell me when that happens again,” she had said.

Jinhsi had agreed, and several minutes later admitted that it had happened twice already.

Now the corridor opened around them, walls blue with mineral light. Water ran upward along one side and descended the other. At the far end, three Photonvaults stood around a Chronosorter whose nested rings turned at different speeds.

Changli approached slowly. Her shadow crossed the nearest ring and became the shadow of a child holding a book. It lengthened into her present shape as she stepped back.

“The Court obtained a remarkably precise reference,” she said. “They could compare a stored moment with the one passing around it, then move a thing between the two. Our laboratories can preserve frequencies. The machinery here preserves their order.”

Amy walked around the nearest vault, keeping outside the engraved circle.

“Baldur’s synchronization records use a shared reference too. He gets one body and several people with opinions about which way his feet should go.”

Cartethyia smiled. “He was very good about the opinions.”

“He had centuries of practice sitting still. He enjoyed the novelty.”

Mei put her hand near a column. Warmth moved along the lines of her Tacet Mark. Beneath the stone, something turned toward her with a weight she felt in her teeth.

She lowered her hand.

“This one recognizes me.”

Changli looked at the panel beside it. A thin line of script had brightened along its edge.

“It recognizes a supervisory authority. The reference predates this laboratory.”

Amy came to stand beside her mother. “Can it open the inner door?”

The light travelled two handspans along the panel, reached a broken section, and folded back on itself. A clank came from behind the wall.

“It can ask,” Mei said. “The door seems occupied.”

Changli crouched to inspect the break. A narrow blade had been driven through the contact, cutting it with deliberate precision.

“Someone prepared for your arrival.”

Farther in, they found the first of Hsin’s severed channels. The copper had melted into smooth beads above a Fractsidus seal. Amy compared it with the drawing and marked the location. The second channel carried an observation lens that had been split through its centre. A small pile of packing straw lay beneath it.

They had cleared their workbench before leaving.

Jinhsi looked at the swept patch of floor. Beyond it, a man’s glove hung in the air over a chasm, turning through half a rotation and back.

***

The first vault opened onto a bridge at three different ages.

Its left edge was bare stone. The centre carried planks with fresh tool marks. On the right, the same planks sagged under moss. A rope handrail passed through all three and returned to a post at the entrance, where its fibers grew, darkened, and split under Jinhsi’s gaze.

Chisa put her bell down. The note came from the other side of the chasm.

“Keep talking while we cross,” she said. “I can follow a person’s pace more easily than these returns.”

Sanhua took the first section with her blade held low. Ice spread beneath her boot, a thin, visible skin that marked the place where her present step touched the old planks.

Halfway across, Jinhsi’s light spread into the bridge.

Sanhua stopped.

The woman before her opened along seven overlapping movements. A girl lifted her hands. A Magistrate reached for a pen. Pale horns grew through a face that receded behind its own mouth.

Sanhua gripped the beads at her wrist. Her next breath scraped audibly.

“Jinhsi, please tell me where your feet are.”

Jinhsi looked down. “My right foot is on the third plank past the split. My left is beside the iron staple.”

“I hear you. Keep speaking until I reach you.”

Jinhsi searched for something to say. The chasm below held droplets arranged like a hanging curtain, each one showing a different part of her white sleeve.

“Xinyi moved the peg in my old room. I reached for it yesterday and touched the wall. She said Yun pulled it out with his coat.”

Sanhua took a step. Then another.

“He has a very strong opinion about sleeves,” she said. “I helped his mother put his coat on this morning.”

Chisa came along the handrail on Sanhua’s other side, letting the flat of her blade touch the planks at each step. The sound passed steadily between their voices.

“There was a shelf above the bed,” Jinhsi continued. “I used to hide dried pears behind the books. I looked yesterday. Somebody has been keeping needles there.”

Sanhua’s hand found her elbow. Her grip was careful and slightly too firm.

Together they crossed the remaining span.

On the far side, Jinhsi turned. Sanhua was looking toward the ground beside her feet. Tiny snowflakes drifted around the beads in her hand.

“Has my frequency changed?”

“It is moving through several positions. I can hear you in one place.” Sanhua lifted her eyes. “I need to use that for a while.”

Jinhsi held out her arm. Sanhua took it, and they went on with Chisa walking beside them.

***

In the second vault they found the Court’s living quarters.

Bowls sat on shelves beside notebooks. One bed had a stocking hanging from its footboard, darned at the heel in yellow thread. The laboratory door bore a dent at the height of a kick, and somebody had pencilled a complaint about the lock beneath it.

Amy opened the records on a table whose dust held a single recent handprint.

The first pages described Jué’s arrival on Mt. Firmament. He had come carrying a wound through the structures that governed his own continuity. The mountain’s deposits could supply energy slowly enough for him to receive it. In return, the Court had studied the movements he used to keep that energy coherent.

Changli read the measurements aloud. Her voice grew quieter as the dates advanced.

“They were treating him over generations.”

Jinhsi stood beside her. “Does the record show him improving?”

“In intervals. Here, the rate rises. Then the wound opens again after an intervention on the mainland.”

The entries contained floods, Tacet outbreaks, a winter that had closed the pass. Each was followed by the same small downward hook in the measurements.

Baizhi drew the book toward her and put a clean sheet beneath the page.

“These will help us assess him. Give me a few minutes to copy them.”

Amy had reached a different series. On its cover, the Court had written *Second Resonance Awakening: Divergence under Frequency Collision*.

The trials began with two pendulums, then crystals, then animals. Later the entries acquired names.

One woman had signed her attendance in a round, untidy hand. Qin Lan. She had asked for leave during the harvest. Someone had approved it, and the next experiment had been moved by nine days.

Three pages later her waveform divided into two clean lines.

The fourth page recorded an Overclock.

Changli’s fingers drew together against the table. Her sleeve had slipped back, exposing the feathered scars along her arm.

Amy looked up at her, then at Jinhsi.

“The collision lets a resonator establish a second reference. They thought a matching Sentinel frequency would give the new reference access to its abilities.”

“That would mean time, in my case,” Jinhsi said, reading the diagram.

Baizhi finished copying the first table and crossed to the second.

“The subject’s frequency diverged here.” She placed her pencil over the earlier curve. “They increased the amplitude after it happened. She reported pain; they increased it again.”

Jinhsi read Qin Lan’s name at the bottom of the page. Its last stroke had dragged sharply downward.

“What were they trying to accomplish by increasing it?”

“They wanted the new state to sustain itself after the apparatus was removed.”

The record continued for eleven days. Qin Lan survived them. Her right hand remained unresponsive at the final examination. A note in the margin asked whether the study could provide money for someone to help with the harvest.

Baizhi turned back to the first curve.

“The boundary crossing is visible before the severe injury. Their method of maintaining it caused further damage. We should preserve that distinction when we discuss what these results mean.”

Cartethyia had been standing near the door. She came forward and laid her fingers on the table.

“Imperator tested Mei by putting his horn through her,” she said. “He had already told us what bringing me back would cost. Then he wanted to see whether she meant it.”

Mei looked at the stocking on the bed. After a moment she said, “I stood there and let him do it.”

Jinhsi’s hand moved toward her own abdomen.

“Is that one of the times you died?”

“I died, and Cantarella had the next part of the conversation.” Mei rubbed her thumb along the seam of her glove. “I remember the beginning better than the end. I was angry, and I had found something I could offer. It took very little time to offer it.”

Cartethyia’s crystal laurel turned above her hair.

“He spent what he had left bringing us both back. Sometimes I think of a question for him while I’m doing something else. It can take me a moment to remember.” Her hand flattened over the old record. “Let’s have Jué explain this before anybody begins proving how much they love a city.”

Jinhsi closed her eyes briefly.

“I will ask him. I have questions of my own.”

Cartethyia looked at her, and the color rose slowly in her cheeks.

“I was getting ahead of you,” she said. “I do that when I remember the horn.”

Jinhsi opened her eyes. “I understand why you remember it.”

Amy leaned both palms on the table, her shoulders high.

“Baldur answered when I Overclocked. I was nineteen. The records made the sequence sound very tidy afterward.” She followed Qin Lan’s curve with a finger held above the paper. “I also spent ten years waiting for somebody to realize I was still there. We should find out where a person goes if this fails. Put that question near the top.”

Changli drew out a clean sheet. She paused with the brush above it, then offered both to Jinhsi.

Jinhsi wrote the question herself.

***

The third vault held the cipher and the Fractsidus instructions.

They found the latter beneath a dismantled transmitter. A brass clasp had pinned them to the bench, and the final page still bore the pressure of the clasp through the words.

Changli read the first objective: interrupt the Sentinel’s ability to correct events beyond Mt. Firmament during the Norfall operation.

The second brought the projected succession forward. An injured Sentinel, a resonator already linked to his temporal authority, and a population dependent on the failing link would create the conditions for an accelerated Awakening.

The third concerned Mei.

*The original authority may attempt to resume direct custody when confronted with the deterioration of her foundation.*

Mei read it over Changli’s shoulder.

“She wrote that before the rain chamber.”

Amy turned to the date. “She had this ready several weeks before you went in.”

There were alternatives in the margin. The successor might accept. The Arbiter might intervene. Jué might expend his remaining continuity in transfer. Each led to another observation schedule.

At the bottom of the last page, a later hand had entered the withdrawal order. The Norfall date. The observation equipment was to be dismantled and its retained samples surrendered under the seal of Mengzhou.

Jinhsi set the sheet down.

She could see Nivora at the edge of the valley, waiting for Hsin to acknowledge her. The ease with which she had accepted a price that other people had to live around.

“We will take the whole file,” she said. “Xinyi should see what they planned for this town.”

The cipher rested in a cradle at the back of the vault. Three rings of light passed through it, each carrying the same piece of metal at a different angle.

Changli used the maintenance records to set the first Photonvault. Amy adjusted the second by fractions while Chisa listened to the latch. At the third, the stored light had drifted too far to meet its present reflection.

Jinhsi put her hand against the Chronosorter.

The rings slowed.

Across the room, the shadows of her companions stretched toward her. She felt the distance between one heartbeat and the next open beneath her ribs. A vast, exhausted presence moved at the far end of it.

Then the metal met its own outline. Chisa pulled the cipher free.

Jinhsi’s knees struck the floor.

Sanhua reached her first. She caught her beneath the arms as a second movement of Jinhsi’s body fell through the first, bright enough to fill the room.

For a moment Jinhsi heard water inside the mountain. She heard a breath held so long that she began holding her own.

“I have her,” Sanhua said, close to her ear. “Baizhi, come to this side.”

Jinhsi tried to lift her head. The room arrived in strips: Amy’s boots, the yellow repair in the stocking, Changli crouching with the physician’s bag already open.

“He answered me through the link,” Jinhsi said. “I felt him moving at the other end.”

Baizhi’s fingers pressed against her wrist.

“Stay with my voice while I count. Tell Sanhua if you need her to change the way she is holding you.”

Jinhsi leaned her weight into Sanhua. The cold against her back gave the room an edge she could follow.

***

They returned to Hongzhen carrying the cipher, four books, and Jinhsi on the stretcher.

She argued about the stretcher until the path tipped sideways beneath her feet. Then she lay down and asked Changli to stop looking as though every stone had been placed there personally to offend her.

“I have made a list,” Changli said. “The fifth bend warrants a formal complaint.”

Mei, at the front handle, snorted. Jinhsi managed to laugh, and the laughter hurt enough that she let Sanhua hold her hand for the remaining descent.

In Hongzhen, the square had changed. The turnip seller was inside the shelter. The looping girl sat under the meeting-house eaves with a scraped knee and three adults whose accounts of her rescue differed on every point. Lupa had a bandage across one palm. Ciaccona’s left sleeve was wet to the shoulder.

“The well keeps returning the bucket,” she explained as she helped carry the stretcher in. “It is very generous with a task I had hoped to complete once.”

Iuno came out of the shelter carrying a roll of copper wire against her hip. She took one look at Jinhsi, set the wire down, and moved the curtain aside.

“Your room is warm. Xinyi has kept the bed clear.”

Jinhsi looked past her toward the central device. A young man was turning its handle with both hands while an older woman read the marks to him.

“How long do we have?”

“The chamber’s protection is holding. The eastern farms have become difficult to reach. We have moved the families we could bring through into the meeting house above us.” Iuno stepped out of Baizhi’s way. “You can hear the full account after she examines you.”

Baizhi loosened Jinhsi’s collar and placed a cold instrument against her chest.

The needle moved, stopped, and began moving again from a different position.

Changli watched it with her scarred arm held against her body.

“Please give us the result together,” Jinhsi said. “I’ll be able to ask while everyone is here.”

Baizhi adjusted the instrument.

“Then we will need more chairs.”

***

Xinyi unlocked the cipher with a phrase she had learned from her predecessor, who had learned it from a woman whose name survived on the back of the box.

The central device projected the chamber’s structure into the air.

Mianloong’s energy rose from the mountain in pale columns. Around Jué’s resting place, the Fractsidus had built a second ring. The columns reached it, turned, and discharged into the walls. A narrow line passed through the ring in the opposite direction.

Amy followed it down to the town.

“They left the output working. He can spend energy keeping the mountain stable. The supply goes past him.”

Xinyi looked at the rotating light. “He has been keeping this room steady.”

The young man beside the Chronosorter glanced at his hands on its handle.

Baizhi spread the old measurements beneath the projection.

“His injury was already deteriorating. The ring has accelerated it. Restoring the supply will help his immediate condition. The damaged reference remains.”

Jinhsi sat wrapped in a blanket, with her feet on a warmed brick. She could feel the scale pulsing through the cloth beside her thigh.

“And my frequency follows his.”

Baizhi nodded. “The connection is visible in the records we brought back. Your body is trying to keep time with an injured source. The exertion in the vault made the difference more pronounced.”

Amy opened Mornye’s notes beside the old books. There were annotations in two hands, one of them hers from years earlier. A childish drawing of a cat occupied the top corner of the page she chose.

“We worked with pilots whose rhythms drifted during synchronization,” she said. “Baldur could give them a reference long enough to come out. Here we need to reach Jué first and restore what the ring is diverting. Then we can ask him how much of his own clock he can hold.”

Lupa leaned against the wall near the door.

“That sounds like tomorrow’s first job. What can we do tonight?”

Xinyi had already put her hand on the mountain map. “The southern drainage galleries reach the outer chamber. My father repaired them. I know where the stairs divide.”

The young man at the handle turned. “I can take you as far as the old sluice.”

His mother caught the handle he had released.

“After you eat, Ren. You have been standing here since breakfast.”

He looked at her hands, then at the pot on the stove. Lupa straightened and went to find him a bowl.

***

The water in Mianloong Chamber held the shape of a storm.

Waves rose from its surface in blue-green walls. Foam stood along their edges, each bubble intact. Beneath the nearest crest, a fish hung with its body curved around a turn. Far below it, light travelled through the water at the slow pace of a hand drawn across glass.

Jinhsi stood at the shore. Her childhood memories supplied a still pool, a path around its edge, Jué’s voice reaching her from somewhere above the mist.

The path lay beneath the arrested wave.

Sanhua lowered a probe onto the surface. It rested there. She put a little weight on it, and the wood bowed.

“It will carry us while the arrest holds.”

Ren came down the gallery steps behind them with a coil of line over his shoulder.

“The old overflow stair reaches the other side. We usually clean it in autumn.” He pointed to a dark opening above the water. “There are iron rings along the wall. My father used them to secure his tools.”

Amy looked from the stair to the frozen waves.

“We should rig that route before we restore the supply. This floor is going to become extremely enthusiastic about being water.”

Lupa grinned at her. “You can explain it that way to the engineers. I want to hear what they write down.”

They spent the next hour fastening lines above the pool. Cartethyia transformed beneath a low arch, ducked too late, and knocked a shower of mineral dust into Ciaccona’s hair.

“The architect has made a discourteous ceiling,” Fleurdelys observed in her deep, resonant voice.

Ciaccona shook dust onto the water. “I shall include your grievance in the song. Bend your knees while I tie this.”

Fleurdelys lifted her to the first iron ring. Her broad palm remained steady beneath the bard’s boots as Ciaccona worked the knot.

Across the chamber, Changli and Ren opened the control housing. The isolating ring had been fitted into old stone with newer bolts. Amy removed its outer plate and found the diverted energy burning a clean channel through the inside.

Mei put her marked hand beside the exposed contact. Gold rose toward her skin.

She flexed her fingers and looked at Amy.

“It wants somewhere to go.”

“Give me time to open the return pipe. Lupa, I’ll need you on that handle. Stop at the second catch and hold it there.”

They worked while Jinhsi sat on the overflow stair with Baizhi. Sanhua remained beside her, one knee drawn up beneath her forearm. Her beads clicked softly whenever she moved her hand.

“I can hear how hard you are concentrating,” Jinhsi said.

Sanhua looked toward the suspended water.

“The reflection is ahead of your movements. Your voice arrives at the right time.”

Jinhsi followed her gaze. In the water, her reflected head had already turned back toward the stair.

“I find it unpleasant too.”

“Then we can both look at the wall for a while.”

Jinhsi shifted until her shoulder touched Sanhua’s. They sat facing a patch of limestone containing a small, imperfect fossil. The creature inside it had curled into a shape like a sleeping hand.

Below them, Lupa called for a longer lever. Ren brought one from a maintenance rack. Changli knelt beside the damaged ring, laid two fingers against its outer seam, and sent a thin thread of flame through the fused bolts.

Amy opened the return pipe.

The chamber breathed.

A wave moved a finger’s breadth beneath the lines. Mei’s hair lifted from her shoulders. Deep in the mountain, stone struck stone with a sound that went on until Jinhsi felt it in the backs of her eyes.

Then a voice said her name.

She was standing before she knew she had moved. Sanhua rose beside her, and Baizhi took the scale from the stair where it had fallen.

At the far end of the chamber, the mist opened.

***

Jué lay coiled around a broken pillar.

His antlers filled the high darkness. Pale mane trailed across the stone in strands thicker than Jinhsi’s arms. Along his flank, scales had separated around a wound that held a narrow, relentless dawn. The light inside it turned through colors her eyes had trouble following.

He raised his head as she approached. The movement travelled down his body in stages, each arriving slightly later than the last.

“Jinhsi,” he said again. “You have come through the pass.”

She put a hand against his face.

The scale beneath her palm was warm. Its ridges were the ridges she remembered, worn smooth near the lower edge where she had reached as a child. She pressed harder, then laid her other hand beside the first.

“I brought people,” she said. Her breath caught. “We have opened the supply. Amy is watching it, and Baizhi wants to examine the wound.”

His eye turned toward the figures behind her.

Jinhsi bent until her forehead touched him. The first sob surprised her with its force. She tried to tell him about Hongzhen and managed the town’s name before her voice broke again.

Jué lowered his head to the floor.

She leaned against him, crying into the place between two scales while the warmth of his breath moved through her sleeves. Sanhua stood close enough that the back of her hand touched Jinhsi’s skirt. Changli set down the bag she had been carrying and waited beside it.

For a while, Jué let the mountain and its visitors attend to themselves.

When Jinhsi finally lifted her face, he turned one whisker beneath her wet cheek. The movement was clumsy from fatigue. It caught a strand of her hair, and she untangled it with shaking fingers.

“I had been trying to decide how to greet you,” she said. “I thought I should begin with the field return.”

“Stay beside me until you are ready.”

She let her hand rest against his face for another breath, then made room for Baizhi.

The physician set her instruments on the stone and explained what she intended to measure. Jué followed her hand as she pointed. When she asked him to hold still, the immense head settled more comfortably between his foreclaws.

Only then did his gaze reach Mei.

The light along his mane lifted.

“You have returned,” he said. “I knew the cadence at the outer gate.”

Mei stood with her right hand open beside her thigh.

“I found some of our history in Jinzhou. The battle. The founding. There was a recording of us together.”

Jué watched her face.

“You remember the northern ford?”

“I remember watching the recording. There are pieces my hands seem to know.” She took a breath. “Will you tell me about it when you have the strength?”

His eye moved over her again, more slowly.

“At the ford, you had lost your sword. You were using a broken standard. When I arrived, you called instructions as though we had fought beside one another for years.”

Mei’s mouth moved into a small, uncertain smile.

“Was I giving useful instructions?”

“You told me to make myself conspicuous.”

Amy laughed from the control housing. Mei looked up at the antlers disappearing into the roof and covered her face with one hand.

“I’m glad some of my tactical thinking has survived.”

Jué’s breath stirred the dust near her boots. For a moment Jinhsi could picture them at the ford: the young-looking woman with a splintered pole, the dragon filling the sky, each occupied with what the other could do.

Then his gaze returned to the gold at Mei’s wrist.

“The first authority remains within you. I had hoped you would take the seal when the mountain began to fail.”

Mei lowered her hand from her face.

“Jinhsi brought us here. She has the city’s seal and its latest decisions. I can help with the work in this chamber.”

The water shifted behind them, a low creak travelling through its frozen crests.

Jué looked toward Jinhsi. She stood with tear tracks drying beneath her eyes, one hand still resting on his jaw.

“Then I should hear what you have decided.”

***

She told him about Norfall.

The first part came with the ease of information often repeated: the false stage, the restored direction, the road south. When she reached Deng Hao, she looked toward the dark behind Jué’s shoulder and found herself describing the engineer’s hand reaching for Gong Mi.

“There was a bargain afterward. Hsin released the man who gave the signal. Some of what she bought is helping us reach you now. I have his name in the same file as these drawings.”

Jué’s eye closed briefly. When it opened, she went on.

She described Geshu Lin turning, the company following their own roads, Gao Shun standing near a bowl. She told him about the shirt his mother had left on the bed.

“I thought you might answer when the bell rang,” she said. “I waited after the eighth beat.”

Jué’s breathing roughened. Baizhi glanced at her instrument, then moved a little farther from the wound.

“I heard the first beat,” he said. “The sound reached three moments in this chamber. I tried to find the one in which you were listening.”

Jinhsi pressed her lips together.

“How long have you been hurt?”

“Since before the Court built its first hall here.”

“And the dreams going backward?”

“Eleven years. My continuity began losing its hold on the present. I could reach a place and find a season already gone.”

Changli stood beyond Baizhi’s instruments. Her hands lay still at her sides.

Jinhsi kept her eyes on Jué.

“Did you know I was trying to use those dreams to govern?”

“I knew you consulted them. For several winters I recovered enough to give a useful answer again. I expected another recovery.” His gaze moved toward the pale columns returning energy to the chamber. “I continued to expect it.”

Jinhsi took her hand away from his face. The skin of her palm was flushed from his warmth.

“I kept wondering whether I had asked the wrong question. Sometimes I stood in front of the scale for an hour, trying to remember which words you had answered before.”

A tremor passed through his mane.

“I remember you at that age,” he said. “You would rehearse a question on the path. By the time you reached me, you had made it very careful.”

“I was doing it last week.”

He held her gaze. She could see her own white hair reflected along the bottom of his pupil.

“I left you to make sense of a sickness I had concealed,” he said. “I am sorry, Jinhsi.”

She looked down at her hand. Baizhi’s instruments clicked beside them, steady enough for the tiny sounds to carry.

“I have been so frightened that you were dead.”

“I felt the end approaching. I wished to leave you a clear road through it.”

Jinhsi lifted her head.

He waited while she opened the page Sanhua had returned to her beside the pool. The damp at its corner had blurred one word.

“Scar said I should ask which of us was supposed to come back,” she said. “Tell me what you intended.”

***

The answer began with Jinhsi as an infant.

Jué had found a life interrupted by a Tacet catastrophe. He had held the moment of her death apart from the moment that followed and carried her through the space between them. To bring her back, he had given her heartbeat a place within his own passage through time.

Jinhsi listened with both hands around the scale.

“That is why I weaken when you do.”

“Your rhythm has grown with mine. I intended to teach you how to establish your own. Years of preparation would have made the crossing gentler.”

“How old did you imagine I would be?”

His claws moved against the stone.

“I imagined you older each time I considered it.”

She heard Changli breathe behind her. Jué watched the scale in Jinhsi’s hands.

“The Fractsidus closed the chamber before I could restore the link,” he continued. “Now the difference between my time and the mountain’s is tearing both. The strongest remaining path is to place my temporal authority within you. A collision between our frequencies could complete the Awakening.”

Baizhi looked up. “And the cost to you?”

“The full transfer would consume what remains of my continuous existence.”

Cartethyia took a step forward. Ciaccona’s hand touched her wrist, briefly, and she stopped beside Jinhsi.

Jinhsi laid the page on her knee.

“You were going to call me here and explain this at the beginning of a battle.”

Jué’s gaze stayed on her. “I intended you to understand what you would receive.”

“I want to understand what else we can do.”

Amy shut the outer plate of the control housing and came over with the old study in her hands.

“The supply is coming back. Your readings are improving. We have the Court’s trial records and the synchronization work from Lahai-Roi. Start with how the mountain is losing time.”

Jué turned toward her. His eye narrowed slightly as he listened to the resonance beneath her voice.

“You have stood within an Exostrider.”

“I used to live in his heart. There are considerably better housing options available now.” Amy sat on a flat stone and opened the book. “Baldur helped us get out. I’d like to know whether your system has somewhere to stand outside this link.”

He studied her for a long moment. Then he lowered one claw toward the diagram.

***

By the time the lamps had burned down, they had three possible courses.

The restored supply would let Jué hold the shelter and the chamber while the coastal crews brought in more people and equipment. They could evacuate Hongzhen in sections and spend months unwinding the mountain’s accumulated drift. Some pockets would remain beyond reach for much of that time. Jué’s injury would continue to bear the strain.

The full transfer could move the work into Jinhsi at once. Baizhi looked at the projected load, then at the girl wrapped in a blanket beside her, and kept her pencil resting on the figure until everyone had read it.

The third course came slowly, through questions, measurements, and one argument that made Amy get up and walk to the far side of the chamber before returning.

Jinhsi could establish an independent temporal reference through a limited collision. Jué would retain enough of his own authority to continue receiving energy from Mianloong. The old machinery could then unwind the differences between the mountain’s scattered pockets, using two living references to pass each interval safely into the next.

“You are describing a transfer that can be stopped midway,” Jué said.

“We can divide the work into stages and check the result between them,” Amy replied. “The Court kept increasing the pressure on Qin Lan because they wanted a result that could sustain itself. You’ll be here, and we’ll have the apparatus running. We can keep supporting the new state while she learns to hold it.”

“A second resonance begins at the boundary of an Overclock. Crossing that boundary places her at risk.”

“I know where it begins.” Amy’s voice had grown louder. She set the book down before continuing. “Help us work out where it ends. You can stop pushing when she asks you. That changes something the old experiments kept doing.”

Jué watched her. The great head inclined by a small degree.

Baizhi added the stopping conditions. Sanhua described the changes she could see in Jinhsi’s frequency. Chisa explained where a temporary attachment could be cut if the two references began collapsing together. Mei offered her Spectro to keep the damaged regulator coherent during the break, and Amy made her demonstrate how she would release it.

At the back of the chamber, Xinyi argued with Ren about which drainage gates could take a sudden flow. He was right about the lower sluice. She conceded the point, changed the route, and sent him to eat again.

Jinhsi listened until the choices had become distinct enough to imagine living through each of them.

Then she asked Jué, “How much do you need to keep?”

His gaze shifted from the diagram to her face.

“To maintain a coherent presence?”

“To heal. To speak. To have enough left for something you want to do.”

The chamber was quiet for several breaths.

“I would have to measure that,” he said.

Jinhsi reached for Baizhi’s pencil and set it beside the space they had left for the answer.

“Please measure that before we go further.”

***

Jinhsi woke to somebody telling her story.

The voice came through the thin wall beside the bed. An old woman was settling two children on a shared mat, and one had asked why the Magistrate could speak to the Sentinel.

“There were Discords in the forest,” the woman said. “A mother carried her baby into the ruins and hid there. When the creatures found them, she put herself between the child and their claws.”

Jinhsi lay still beneath the blanket.

“The baby cried until her little voice began to fade. Her mother had died protecting her, and the cold was coming in. Jué heard that cry through the forest. He swept down and drove the creatures away.”

The child asked something Jinhsi could barely hear.

“He carried her here. The mountain shook with his roaring. People came out of their houses and saw him holding her. She had already died.”

Jinhsi knew the next part. The black clouds parting. The falling stars. The infant given a second life through the mountain’s strange time, appointed as the Sentinel’s Resonator.

She could remember standing on a stool while somebody told it at a festival. She had been small enough that Xinyi kept a hand behind her back. At the end, the people watching had bowed. She had bowed too, hurriedly, with both hands pressed against her skirt.

Through the wall, the storyteller reached the stars.

“And she grew up to look after all of us.”

“Is she cold now?” the child asked.

The old woman shifted on the mat. “Xinyi has put a warm brick at her feet. You have one too. Keep your toes on this side.”

A little later the lamp went out.

Jinhsi sat up. Her heartbeat had steadied during sleep, though a second faint pulse still seemed to arrive from somewhere behind her ribs.

Sanhua sat by the curtain with a book across her knees.

“I want to see him before morning,” Jinhsi said. “Could you walk with me?”

Sanhua marked her page and rose.

***

Jué was awake when they reached him.

Most of the visitors had gone back to Hongzhen. Ren slept beside a warm pipe with his coat folded beneath his head. At the regulator, Mei sat against Iuno with her eyes closed while Iuno read a page held in her living hand. They had arranged a blanket around both their legs. The side of Mei’s face rested comfortably against the dressed crystal shoulder.

Iuno looked up as Jinhsi approached and lowered the page. Jinhsi touched two fingers to the hand holding it, then went past.

Jué’s eye followed her.

“The children are hearing the story downstairs,” she said. “The one about the ruins.”

His mane moved along the floor as he breathed.

“I have heard it told.”

Jinhsi sat beside his foreclaw. Sanhua took a place farther along the curve of his body, within hearing, her book still in one hand.

“Was my mother already dead when you found us?”

“She had died before I reached the ruins.”

The answer travelled through the stone beneath Jinhsi. She set both feet flat.

“Did you try to bring her back too?”

Jué lowered his head.

“I searched for her frequency. It had dispersed through the creatures that killed her. Yours lingered in the ruins, held together by the cry I had followed. I could still find the place where it had broken.”

Jinhsi looked at the pale edges of his claws. “People always reach the part about me so quickly.”

“She had wrapped you inside her own coat. Her hand was caught in the fastening.”

The chamber hummed. Jinhsi drew her knees closer together.

She had visited her mother’s grave every spring while she lived here. Xinyi brought flowers; Jinhsi picked the little stones from between the larger ones around the marker. She had imagined the woman in the story with her arms spread, standing between a child and a monster. Now there was a hand caught in a coat, busy with something that needed closing.

“I used to wonder how I could thank her,” she said. “I would tell her what I had learned that year. Once I recited an entire lesson about irrigation because I had remembered every part of it.”

Jué listened.

“Later, when people brought wishes, I could do things for their children. I could ask about the road their father had taken. I liked being able to help.” She rubbed her palms together. “Then somebody would tell that story again, and I would think about how much had been given to me. I kept finding another thing I ought to do before I could go to bed.”

She let her hands fall into her lap.

“When you brought me here, did you already know what I could become?”

Jué’s eye closed. She waited until it opened again.

“I knew the sound of your crying,” he said. “I followed it. When I lifted you, your head fell backward against my claw. I held it in place all the way to the mountain.”

He shifted the claw beside her. Its inner curve was broad enough to hold a sleeping infant.

“The people saw the storm and heard me call through it. I was asking whether anyone in the lower hall had heard you breathe. I could feel so little in my hands.”

Jinhsi laid her fingers along the smooth inner edge.

“I keep picturing your hand beneath my head.”

“I had reached you with the power to scatter every creature in that forest. You weighed less than the snow caught in my mane.”

His breath passed warm over her head.

“When you cried again, Xinyi ran up the stair carrying blankets. You cried through everything we tried. She took you from me, fed you, and told me to lower my voice.”

Jinhsi bent her head. A laugh escaped, thin and wet.

“She still tells people that.”

“She was very certain of herself.”

He watched Jinhsi’s hand resting on his claw.

“For several days I listened whenever you slept. I wanted the next breath. That occupied all of what I hoped for.”

Jinhsi’s fingers curled against the ivory.

The first tear struck her wrist. She wiped it away and left the next one where it fell.

“When you chose me for Jinzhou, I thought I had finally understood what the rescue was for.”

“You had spent the previous winter asking why families below the pass paid the same grain levy after losing half their crop. You brought me their figures, and the names of the officials who had refused to hear them. You were angry enough to forget the greeting.” A faint warmth entered his voice. “I thought the city should hear you.”

She looked up at him through wet eyelashes.

“I enjoyed that winter. Even the arguments. Changli showed me how to compare the old assessments, and I found three mistakes she had missed.”

“You told me about each of them.”

For a while she sat with those two children: the infant held through the storm, the girl returning with a correction she wanted someone to admire. Both had lived in this mountain. Years stretched between them, full of pears stolen from jars, knees scraped on steps, a teacher waiting while she tried again.

Jinhsi put her cheek against the side of his claw.

“I want to keep doing the work,” she said. “I also want to come here and complain about it. There are people who exhaust me before they have sat down. You may eventually regret hearing about them.”

Jué moved his head closer.

“I have heard a great many petitions. I may recognize some of the families.”

She laughed properly this time, then covered her mouth because it had become crying halfway through.

He waited with his face beside hers.

“I want you to live through this,” she said. “When you described giving me everything, I kept seeing the room afterward. I could feel myself standing in it, trying to be grateful.”

The light inside his wound flickered against the far wall.

“I have spent centuries considering how to leave the work in capable hands,” he said. “The time after that has remained difficult to picture.”

Jinhsi lifted her head.

“What would you do with one afternoon?”

He looked toward the high opening through which a narrow strip of sky showed above the mist.

“I used to fly beyond the eastern cliffs. There are warm currents above the sea in the seventh month. The seabirds sleep while the air carries them.”

“Could you go there again?”

“Perhaps after the wound has settled.”

She followed his gaze. The strip of sky was dark, holding a few stars in positions she knew from childhood.

“You could spend an afternoon finding the currents again. I would like to hear about that afterward.”

Jué’s whisker brushed her sleeve.

“I shall need to learn how far my strength will carry me,” he said. “The eastern cliffs would be a place to begin.”

***

At dawn, Jinhsi found Changli standing on the shelter stair with a cup of tea gone cold between her hands.

The Counselor had watched the lower lane for almost an hour. People were moving bedding from the meeting house into a storeroom whose time had steadied during the night. A woman carrying a folded quilt stopped beside the stair.

“Xinyi says the eastern terraces may thaw all at once.”

Changli set her cup on the step. “We are opening the drainage first. Ren is checking the lower gates with the crews.”

“My greenhouse is below them.”

“He showed me the location.”

The woman’s hand tightened around the quilt.

“Those seedlings are next winter’s money.”

Jinhsi came down the final steps. “I will go through the route with you. If we divert the water through your land, we will record what is damaged and make provision for the winter.”

“I would prefer the seedlings.”

Jinhsi met her eyes. “So would I. Show me the lower beds.”

They spent half an hour above the lane. Ren joined them with mud on his trousers and amended the drainage route around two of the woman’s three greenhouses. The third stood directly beneath the old channel. She listened to his explanation, pointed out the seed trays that could be carried by hand, and went to fetch neighbors.

Changli walked back with Jinhsi after the first trays had been brought inside.

“I have been trying to find a way to ask you to choose the evacuation,” she said. “I can describe the risk very persuasively. I know which parts frighten you.”

Jinhsi looked at her.

“You can tell me why you prefer it.”

Changli’s fingers tightened around the cold cup.

“I want you where the boat can reach you. I have seen a child cross into an Overclock. I remember how quickly the adults lost the ability to help.”

The edge of her dressing showed beneath her cuff. Jinhsi glanced at it, and Changli let the sleeve remain where it was.

“I know there is a reason to try,” Changli continued. “There are people in those pockets whom we may take months to reach. Jué will bear the delay. I can follow all of that, and I still want to take you down to the coast.”

They stopped beside the shelter door. A little boy was lining up shoes beneath the bench according to a system that appeared to depend on how much he liked them.

Jinhsi watched him place one of Lupa’s boots at the front.

“When I came back from the pass, I was relieved somebody else was carrying me,” she said. “I had thought I would be ashamed.”

Changli turned toward her fully.

“We can carry you again.”

“If I stop the transfer, there will be people waiting here afterward. They will have lost another day.”

“We will help them through the next one. Xinyi has already assigned rooms for the evacuation crews. I have asked the captain to keep your berth ready.”

Jinhsi took the cup from Changli’s stiff hands and set it on the bench.

“I want to try the limited crossing. I also want the berth.”

Changli closed her eyes for a moment. When she opened them, Jinhsi held out the page containing Baizhi’s stopping conditions.

“Please help me keep to these. If I begin making a speech about the city, you can remind me what we agreed.”

Changli read the page. Then she folded it once and put it inside her coat.

“I will probably sound rather cross.”

“I have considerable experience of that.”

Changli laughed, softly, and Jinhsi reached for her sleeve as they went inside.

***

The Sonoro Sphere lay at the summit, reached through a stair that opened in the high wall of Mianloong.

Jué had built it long before Jinhsi’s birth to contain the force of his own temporal corrections. Within it, a collision could be held apart from the mountain until its result was known. The Fractsidus had prepared its old apparatus for a full transfer. Amy and Changli spent the morning taking those settings apart.

Iuno returned to Truthseeker’s Pass with Ciaccona and Xinyi. The three Photonvaults would receive the released intervals in sequence. Each required someone at its local controls, with the maintenance crews working the drainage between them. Yangyang carried the route between Hongzhen and the pass. The Wind chose the short span through the upper gallery, where air could pass between the stones while a person had to climb around them.

Lupa inspected the gallery first and made two trips through it herself. On the third, a cold current pushed at the back of her neck until she turned around.

“I’m checking the loose stones,” she told the air. “You can be offended after I shift that one.”

The current moved ahead and settled over the stone she meant. Lupa grinned and lifted it clear.

At the summit, Mei and Amy took the outer regulator. Chisa waited beside the temporary link they had made between Jinhsi’s reference and Jué’s. Cartethyia held the physical threshold, where a collapse of the Sphere would put the mountain’s weight back into the room.

Changli stood near Baizhi with the emergency release in her hand. Sanhua checked the floor along Jinhsi’s return route, leaving a thin ridge of ice she could follow by touch if her sight became overwhelmed.

When the work was finished, Jinhsi asked everyone to give her a few minutes.

She went to the open side of the summit. Clouds had gathered below the stone platform, their upper edges still while their shadows raced across the snow. A flock of cranes crossed the sun. One reached the far edge of a cloud and returned to the beginning of its flight. The others circled, calling.

Jinhsi held the scale against her chest and watched until Sanhua came to stand beside her.

“The return path is ready,” Sanhua said. “If you need it, come toward my voice. I will describe the last steps.”

“How do I look to you now?”

Sanhua studied her for several breaths.

“The light is changing. There are places I used to rest my eyes that I have to follow differently.”

Jinhsi’s fingers tightened around the scale.

“Will it hurt to look at me afterward?”

“I expect I will need time to learn the change.” Sanhua moved closer. “When we crossed the bridge, you told me about the room. I knew where you were. Keep telling me things while I learn.”

Jinhsi put the scale into its case. She held out her empty hand.

“Xinyi says my handwriting was terrible. She has kept an example.”

Sanhua took her hand.

“You can give me your opinion when we look at it.”

They stood together until the cranes came around again. Then Jinhsi turned toward the Sphere.

***

Jué rose into a sky full of water.

The Sphere opened beneath his body. Mountains appeared around its rim, layered one behind another, each carrying a different season. Summer grass moved on the nearest slope. Beyond it, snow broke from a pine in a slow white sheet. Farther still, the mountain was bare, its first river cutting a channel through dark stone.

Jinhsi stepped onto the platform at the centre. Her broadblade caught the light along its upper edge. The case containing the Loong Scale lay beside Sanhua’s hand at the threshold.

Jué circled once above her. His body left a pale wake that remained in the air after he passed.

“The first exchange will draw your resonance outward,” he said. “Meet the descending claw. Turn the force through your blade before it reaches your chest.”

Jinhsi set her feet.

He came down through his own wake. Three claws touched the platform in three different moments, leaving rings of light that expanded toward her.

She stepped through the first ring, raised her blade beneath the second, and took the third along its length. The force drove her backward. Her heels cut twin lines across the stone.

Gold flared from her shoulder.

At the outer regulator, Amy moved a slider to the first mark. Mei’s palm lit the damaged contact beneath it. The light trembled once, then held.

Jinhsi turned with Jué’s departing coil and struck upward.

Her blade met the edge of his tail. The sound crossed the Sphere like a bell struck underwater. For an instant she felt the whole mountain waiting at the other end of her arm: grass roots in frozen soil, Yun’s mother breathing in her sleep, a fish held beneath a wave.

The weight pulled her off balance. She let herself fall into the turn Changli had drilled through a hundred mornings and came up with her blade between her face and the next claw.

At the threshold, Changli’s fingers tightened on the release.

“Her pulse is holding,” Baizhi said. “Keep watching the line.”

Jué passed above the platform. His mane filled the sky with pale rain, each strand dividing into a succession of moving shadows.

Jinhsi saw the strike arrive before his body followed it.

She moved toward the place it had already passed.

The edge of her skirt burned away in white light. Heat opened along her calf. She landed hard, rolled over one shoulder, and drove her blade into the stone to stop herself at the rim.

“The delay is shortening,” Amy called. “He’s pulling her reference forward.”

Jinhsi lifted her head.

Jué’s eye turned toward the blood on her leg. The next ring of force faltered, hanging above his claw. His body curled protectively around the edge she had nearly crossed.

The interrupted rhythm struck Jinhsi through the ribs.

She folded over her sword. A second heartbeat battered against the first. The platform split into two positions beneath her, and her knees tried to occupy both.

Sanhua was already moving along the ice ridge.

Jinhsi forced air into her lungs.

“Stop the transfer. I need to come out.”

Changli pulled the release.

Chisa closed her shears across the temporary link. A strand of white light snapped back into the regulator, where Mei caught its recoil along her palm. Amy leaned her whole weight into the lever beside her.

The platform fell toward the threshold.

Fleurdelys caught its edge with both hands. Stone plowed beneath her boots, tearing long grooves into the summit. Sanhua reached Jinhsi at the point where the two floors met, knelt beside her, and guided her arm over her shoulders.

“Put your weight toward me. There are six steps to Baizhi.”

Jinhsi tried to stand. Her left foot landed twice.

“I can feel both of them,” she said, frightened by the sound of her own voice. “I need you to tell me which one is moving.”

Sanhua held her more firmly.

“Follow the pressure of my hand. Chisa is at your other side. We are taking the first step together.”

They brought her out slowly, with Jué lowering himself beyond the fractured platform and the seasons at the edge of the Sphere turning in uneven circles.

***

In Hongzhen, the first release came through the water channel.

A sheet of thaw struck the old sluice. Ren’s gate held. The diverted water rushed through the greenhouse below, lifting seed trays from their benches and folding the wooden frame against its own rear wall.

The owner stood above it with her neighbors. She had carried the last tray out several minutes earlier. When the frame broke, she pressed her apron against her mouth and stayed until the loose timber had passed.

On the upper lane, the turnip seller caught a child’s shoulder as the paving shifted. Lupa hauled a loaded barrow across the widening crack, reached back for the next handle, and found her bandaged palm slipping.

“Take this side,” she said to the man behind her. “My hand’s finished with the rough work.”

He moved beside her. Together they dragged the barrow onto firm stone.

At Truthseeker’s Pass, the first Photonvault filled with moving light. Ciaccona heard its pitch climb beyond the sound the maintenance book described. She plucked one string, listened, and took her fingers off the lute.

“Xinyi, close the lower return. It’s picking up the water in the square.”

Xinyi turned the wheel. The light thinned by a fraction. Iuno stood at the next vault with her shoulder burning beneath its dressing and watched the level settle above the mark they had intended.

“We have kept more than expected,” she said. “Tell Amy the excess is staying here.”

Yangyang took the message up the stair. The Wind met her at the broken gallery and lifted the folded sheet from her hand. At the far end, she laid it against Amy’s wrist and pressed until Amy turned.

Then the Wind returned along the short passage and settled inside Yangyang’s open cuff.

Yangyang drew her arm against her chest. She waited there until the cold had stopped trembling.

***

Baizhi made Jinhsi drink before she explained the readings.

The water tasted of metal. Jinhsi held the cup in both hands while the physician dressed the cut on her calf.

“Your frequency separated for four breaths. The second reference persisted after you left the Sphere.” Baizhi tied the bandage. “The interruption forced the old link to carry two positions at once. I want it stable before you stand.”

Changli sat beside the stretcher. Her hand shook when she passed the clean cloth, and she watched it until she could lay it down properly.

Jinhsi touched her wrist.

“You heard me ask.”

Changli’s face changed. She leaned forward, resting her forehead briefly against Jinhsi’s knuckles.

“You were very clear,” she said. “I had been listening for it through every strike.”

Jinhsi put the cup down and let her hand remain there.

Beyond them, Jué lay with his chin against the summit stone. Cartethyia had returned to her smaller body and sat near his foreclaw, rubbing grit from her palms.

“You stopped when you saw her bleeding,” she said.

“I saw the edge beneath her.”

“Sanhua saw it too. She had already moved.”

Jué looked toward the bodyguard, who was cleaning the blood from the return path so the next footing would hold.

Cartethyia stretched her fingers. “When Mei came for me, I had spent so long thinking about how to disappear properly that being carried out felt embarrassing. There were people holding pieces of my swords. Cia was furious. Everyone seemed to know what they wanted to do with their hands.”

She looked up at him.

“They have places here. Let them reach her.”

Jué’s mane shifted over the stone. After a while he asked, “What did you do when they carried you out?”

“Mostly cried. Later I asked for cake.”

His eye rested on her face.

“You sound glad to have asked.”

“It had lemon in it. I remember every bite.”

***

The second plan took shape during Jinhsi’s rest.

Amy laid the results across the stone beside the stretcher. The new reference had survived. It needed time to strengthen, and the retained excess in Truthseeker’s Pass could provide that time if released back through a different route.

“We tried to pull you clear of his rhythm while he was still carrying the whole mountain,” she explained. “You got four breaths. That gives us somewhere to begin the next exchange. Iuno has the excess in the first vault. We can use it to hold those four breaths open while you find the next one yourself.”

Jinhsi looked at Jué. “Will he still have to interrupt the strike to catch me?”

“He’ll have to keep his rhythm while Sanhua and Chisa handle the return. I’m moving the release to the shared contact. Either of you can end the exchange from inside.”

Baizhi checked the new figures. “The risk remains substantial. Your independent rhythm is young. It may collapse when the stored interval runs out.”

“Then we take the boat,” Jinhsi said.

Changli lifted her head.

Jinhsi looked at the page again, following each line with her finger. “If it collapses, we take the boat. We continue the evacuation. I want you to hear me say that while I can read the numbers.”

Amy sat back on her heels.

“All right. I’ll tell Auntie which return to hold.”

Jinhsi slept for part of the next hour. When she woke, Sanhua was eating beside her, and had saved a bun in the folded cloth on her lap.

“Xinyi sent these up,” she said. “The dough rose very quickly.”

Jinhsi accepted it. The centre was still warm.

They ate while Amy argued with Mei about the angle of the regulator handle. Mei proposed widening the socket. Amy pointed out that a wider socket would let her hand slip when the recoil came.

“I could hold it with both hands,” Mei said.

“You need the other one for the release. Put your wrist here and complain while I pad the edge.”

Mei put her wrist where she had been told.

Jinhsi watched the younger woman fold cloth around her mother’s sleeve. Then she finished the bun and asked Baizhi to examine her again.

***

This time, Jué met her at the centre of the platform.

He lowered his head until his eye was level with hers. The wound along his flank burned steadily. Beneath its light, Jinhsi could see the new line Baizhi had marked around the minimum reserve he needed to keep.

He had chosen a margin above it.

“I have asked the physician to leave me enough for the eastern flight,” he said.

Jinhsi let out a breath that moved her hair across her mouth.

“I’m glad you told her.”

“She asked how far I intended to go. I had to think about the return journey.”

He glanced toward Baizhi. The physician was checking her instruments, unimpressed by the scale of the patient who had finally supplied a usable distance.

Jinhsi drew her blade.

“I am ready to try again.”

Jué rose.

***

The first claw came down like dawn breaking through cloud.

Jinhsi met it at an angle, let the force carry her three paces, and turned it through the blade. The platform’s edge swept beneath her. Sanhua moved along the threshold, following. Jinhsi could hear her boots between the crashes of light.

Jué passed overhead. His shadow crossed her face, followed by the warm pressure of his mane.

She struck into the departing coil.

Four breaths opened around her.

At Truthseeker’s Pass, Iuno pulled the first return. Light rushed into the second vault. Ciaccona sent a steady note through the chamber to keep the crews working at the same pace, then changed it when Xinyi called that the lower gate had caught.

Ren and two neighbors put their shoulders against the gate. It gave by inches. Water climbed the wall behind them, showing a line of summer grass along its crest.

On the summit, the next strike met Jinhsi before its shadow.

She felt its approach through the small rhythm that had survived the first attempt. Her own breath. Her own hand tightening around the hilt. She stepped between the arriving light and the body that cast it.

The blade reached Jué’s side.

He turned, immense and swift. Gold struck gold, and the Sphere opened into a field of hanging rain.

Jinhsi saw every drop at once. Her reflection filled them, each face a little younger or older than the one beside it. The pressure behind her ribs spread outward. Horns of pale light rose through her hair, and a second vast shape unfurled along the path of her sword.

Pain ran down her arm.

Baizhi’s needle crossed the first mark. Changli raised the release, ready.

Jinhsi landed on one knee. Stone broke beneath her. The next breath came with a sharp, tearing warmth that seemed to begin outside her body.

“I can hear the second rhythm,” Sanhua called. “Your feet are on the platform. I am at the lower edge.”

Jinhsi lifted her head toward the voice.

The broadblade had become too bright to see. She found its hilt by the pressure against her fingers and stood with its weight drawing her forward.

Above her, Jué’s descending coil filled the sky.

He had kept the rhythm.

She went to meet him.

***

At the outer regulator, Mei’s hand began sinking into the light.

The old authority recognized the incoming transfer. A path opened through the contact, broad enough to receive everything the two resonators could release. Her fingers moved with a confidence her mind had yet to acquire.

Amy caught the padded sleeve at her wrist.

“Ma, the return is opening under your thumb. Move with me.”

Mei looked down. Gold had climbed beyond the line they had marked on her glove.

She shifted her hand with Amy’s, slowly, while the ancient fitting tried to complete a motion learned centuries earlier. The pressure went through her knuckles and into the healed places along her palm.

“I have the edge now,” she said. “Give me the outside lever.”

Amy placed it in her left hand. Together they closed the old route and sent the held interval back toward Jinhsi.

Inside the Sphere, the girl’s next breath arrived.

***

Jinhsi’s blade struck the descending claw.

The platform vanished beneath her feet.

For a moment she stood within the collision itself: Jué’s immense, wounded continuity moving around a small rhythm that had begun to hold its own shape. The mountain pressed against them through every connection. Old winters, stored summers, afternoons kept motionless beneath snow. Each sought the same exit.

Jué opened it.

Too much of him moved with the light.

Jinhsi felt his reserve begin to fall. She saw the wound widen along his flank, saw the shadow beyond it become transparent, and understood how easily he could finish the transfer by following it.

She turned her blade across the stream.

“Keep the part you asked for,” she said. The words hurt her throat. “I can feel you coming through with it.”

His eye found her inside the gold.

The next surge pressed her backward. The old link still wanted the single rhythm it had carried since her infancy. Her body reached toward it with a longing that made her eyes fill. She knew the warmth on the other side. She had slept inside its protection before she could lift her head.

Jué drew his claw back.

His reserve steadied.

“I will remain here,” he said. “Take the next breath, Jinhsi.”

She tried. The rhythm faltered.

Through the held interval came the sound of Sanhua counting the return steps. Beyond it, Amy called the remaining measure to Chisa. The voices arrived at different pitches, each belonging to somebody standing in a particular place.

Jinhsi found the pressure of the hilt.

She took a breath.

The second reference held.

Chisa cut the temporary tether at the point Amy had marked. The shears rang, hard and clear. Gold streamed past their edges into the old apparatus, where Mei kept the contact steady and Cartethyia braced the threshold with her shoulder.

Jinhsi came down on stone.

Jué landed across from her, his foreclaws striking sparks from the platform. The mountain’s weight still pressed through them both. Between his breath and hers lay a narrow, living interval.

She raised her blade one final time.

He met it with his claw, and together they turned the stored years toward the open vaults.

***

The third Photonvault filled.

Xinyi had both hands on its wheel. Iuno stood beside the spill channel, reading the falling level. Ciaccona’s note broke as water struck her ankles; she drew a breath and sang the next measure aloud while the crews shifted to the last gate.

Down the mountain, Yangyang felt the air begin moving toward the sea.

The Wind rose from her cuff. She crossed the gallery, reached the final lantern, and turned its hood toward the coast. The signal flashed once through the opening cloud.

Outside the headland, the captain saw it. He took his watch from his pocket, checked the marked second, and raised his own lantern. The sea beneath him moved at the pace it had kept all morning.

Ordinary time reached the mountain along the open chain of light.

In Hongzhen, the arrested turnip basket fell onto an empty blanket. Steam rose straight from the dumpling pot. The old woman in the shelter heard the central device chime once and went to turn the millet before it caught.

Mianloong’s great wave came down.

Water struck the braced galleries and thundered through the drainage channels. The fish beneath the crest bent its body, completed its turn, and vanished into deep green water. Along the overflow stair, the taut safety lines trembled and held.

At the summit, the cranes crossed the cloud’s edge.

This time their flight continued toward the sea.

***

Jinhsi lowered her sword. It slipped from her hand and struck the stone beside her foot.

She was looking at Jué, trying to follow the rise of his chest.

His next breath came.

She sat down very suddenly.

Sanhua reached her with Baizhi close behind. Changli knelt at her other side and put a hand against her back. The touch was warm, firm, and shaking.

Jinhsi stared at Jué through the space between them.

“Please tell me what his reading is.”

Baizhi checked the instrument, then looked toward the dragon himself.

“He has kept his reserve. His breathing is regular. I am going to examine you now.”

Jinhsi’s shoulders began to shake. She tried to lift a hand to her face and found Changli’s sleeve instead.

“Could we stay here for a minute? I want to watch him breathe.”

Changli settled beside her on the stone.

“We have time for that.”

Across the platform, Jué lowered his head onto his foreclaws. Cartethyia came over with a blanket in both hands, looked at his immense face, then at the blanket, and began laughing so helplessly that she had to sit down.

She spread it beneath her own knees and leaned against him.

The first snowmelt dripped from the summit arch. Another drop followed, then another. Sanhua turned her face toward the sound while Jinhsi held Changli’s sleeve and cried.

***

The mountain took three days to finish thawing.

The last stored interval passed through the Photonvaults on the second afternoon. Amy removed the temporary contacts while Xinyi watched the chamber clock compare itself with the coastal signal. Its hands continued together after the connection was lifted. Jinhsi slept through the work and heard about it over supper, when Amy produced a pocketful of fittings and discovered she had been sitting on the smallest one.

Water ran through every ditch in Hongzhen. The southern bridge lost two planks. A wall beneath the meeting house shifted far enough that Ren and his neighbors spent a morning propping it while the children competed to discover how far away its dripping could be heard.

The greenhouse owner counted her recovered trays, then counted them again with Jinhsi beside her. Some seedlings had bent under the water and would recover. Others came apart when lifted.

She wrote her estimate carefully. Jinhsi read it, asked about the cost of rebuilding the frame, and added that separately. When they finished, the woman carried a tray indoors and left Jinhsi holding the door against the wind.

The dumpling seller claimed his dough had improved during the interruption. Xinyi tasted it and said the extra ginger had more to do with it. He began charging for the improvement anyway.

At the edge of the square, Yun helped his mother fold the blanket used to catch the turnips. She kept one hand close to his shoulder whenever he moved toward the well. Sometimes he accepted it. Sometimes he wriggled away and she let her hand fall, watching him cross the open paving.

Jinhsi passed them on her way to the shelter and stopped to ask about the awning. Yun explained its replacement at length, including the portion he intended to build himself. His mother looked increasingly doubtful as the proposed structure acquired a second floor.

“We could begin with the roof,” Jinhsi suggested.

He thought about it. Then he drew her an improved roof in the dirt with a stick.

***

Baizhi examined Jinhsi each morning and evening.

The new resonance held. It also exhausted her quickly. On the second morning, Jinhsi tried to give a trapped swallow enough time to clear a broken lattice and lost the strength to stand halfway through the movement. Sanhua caught her under the arms. The swallow escaped while they were both sitting on the step.

Baizhi listened to the account, checked her pulse, and added another line to the recovery instructions.

“Your ability will need practice at a scale your body can sustain. For the next two days, we begin with breathing and walking.”

Jinhsi looked out at the lattice. “The bird had been there since before we came.”

“Xinyi owns a ladder. I have asked where she keeps it.”

Jinhsi covered her eyes. Sanhua’s hand settled briefly against her back.

Later that afternoon, they fetched the ladder together and freed the remaining birds.

***

Ciaccona claimed the hot spring on the third day.

She announced her departure while Iuno was trying to establish who had borrowed the smaller tea strainer. Cartethyia rose immediately, then glanced at the people still around the kitchen table.

“We could ask whether anyone else wants to—”

Ciaccona took her hand.

“I want to spend an hour in hot water with you. Your friends are experienced people. They can organize their own bathing.”

Cartethyia’s smile arrived all at once. She went to get their towels.

Lupa watched them leave, then reached across the table and lifted the tea strainer out of Abby’s bowl.

“You’ve been sieving your soup with it.”

“It contains leaves,” Abby said. “I was promised lunch, and someone has given me a pond.”

Iuno held out her hand. He watched the dripping strainer travel into it with evident regret.

From the doorway, Chisa asked Lupa whether she wanted to see the cloth shop before it closed. Lupa looked down at her mended glove, then at Chisa’s coat.

“Give me a minute to wash. I want to see what they charge for something with decent sleeves.”

Iuno began cleaning the strainer. Mei leaned against her chair and put her chin on the top of Iuno’s head.

“We could go for a walk.”

“We have been walking through a mountain for several days.”

“There’s a bench near the pears. We could walk to that.”

Iuno considered the strainer, her drying cloth, and the warm weight resting on her hair.

“You may persuade me with a cup of tea first.”

Mei kissed the top of her head and went to find the pot.

***

Jué asked for Mei on the morning before their departure.

He had returned to Mianloong, where the restored water lay dark beneath the gallery. The safety lines remained in place. Ren had hung a bucket from one while he repaired the far wall.

Mei sat beside Jué’s foreclaw with two cups and a flask. Jinhsi came with her at his request. She carried the founding records they had recovered from the Court, wrapped in clean cloth to protect their edges.

Jué watched Mei turn her cup by the rim before she drank. His gaze moved to Jinhsi, who was doing the same thing with hers.

He remained quiet for a little while.

“When the first walls rose, you asked me to preserve Jinzhou,” he told Mei. “I remember the morning. We stood above the place where the western granary is now. The roofs were still being laid.”

Mei looked toward the water.

“What did I mean by it?”

“We had buried people who survived the battle and died during the winter. You wanted the next winter to find them better prepared.”

Jinhsi opened the cloth around the records. The earliest plan showed a settlement smaller than the present harbor ward. She could see the granary marked near the centre.

“I gave you my word,” Jué continued. “Later, as your authority grew, I called you my master. We carried that arrangement through changes I can still place year by year.”

Mei held her cup in both hands.

“You were hoping I would remember you.”

His mane moved slowly against the floor.

“There are things I have kept to tell you. The first pear tree bearing fruit. A child who grew tall enough to finish the name on a gatepost. The eastern wall being moved after everyone had argued for two seasons about its line.”

Mei’s eyes grew bright. She looked down at the warm porcelain, then up at him again.

“Tell me some of those. I can listen now.”

He described the pear tree first. It had been planted by a woman who refused to accept the gardener’s opinion of the soil. After three years, it bore five pears. She carried the largest to the hall in a bowl and made the gardener watch her cut it.

Jinhsi laughed into her tea. “The old garden is still there. We held a stores meeting under those trees last month.”

“Then I should like to hear what grew from that argument.”

She told him about the meeting: the broken scale at the warehouse, the merchant who had arrived with his own weights, Changli’s expression when one of them came apart in her hand.

Jué listened. Mei finished her tea and poured another cup.

After a while, she touched the wrapped records.

“There will be decisions in these with my name on them. People should be able to ask what happened.”

Jinhsi nodded. “Wen will have them copied. The public works belong with the city’s account. We can discuss the private material with the people it concerns.”

Jué lowered his head toward the oldest plan.

“The mountain’s slow time began as shelter. People built lives within it. I continued to preserve the shelter as their lives changed.”

“Xinyi wants the road repaired for ordinary seasons,” Jinhsi said. “There will be people who prefer the old winters. She has called a town meeting after the first estimates come back.”

“I can answer their questions about the mountain.”

“Please tell them where your answers end. They will need that when they decide.”

He inclined his head. She took out her brush and wrote the time of the meeting beside Xinyi’s name.

Mei looked at the two of them, then at the foundation plan beneath Jinhsi’s hand. She took another drink of tea.

***

Abby emerged halfway through the story of the pear dispute.

He settled on Mei’s knee, stretched until his gold ornaments tilted, and turned toward Jué.

Jué’s mane lifted.

The water beneath the gallery drew into a shallow spiral. Abby’s forehead brightened. His ears spread, making him appear approximately one cup wider than before.

Mei put her cup on the stone.

“He lives with us,” she said. “His name is Abby. He can hear you if you have something to ask.”

Jué studied the small creature. The spiral loosened beneath the gallery.

“Your resonance carries a structure I have encountered in older forms,” he said. “Its present shape is unfamiliar to me.”

Abby settled more firmly on Mei’s knee.

“I have been quite satisfied with it. The ears are useful, and most people eventually learn where to put the food.”

Jinhsi reached into her bag and took out the packet Xinyi had sent. Abby watched her open it with close professional attention.

Jué’s gaze moved to the mark on his forehead.

“May I listen more closely?”

Abby accepted a cake before considering the request.

“You may listen while I eat this. Afterwards I have an appointment with the wolf.”

Jué waited through several small, determined bites. His eye narrowed at one sound that Mei felt through her palm, deep enough to make the bones of her wrist ache.

Then he let the contact fade.

“I have observations to offer. The Black Shores may have records that help you interpret them. Their instruments kept account of arrivals whose origins lay beyond our own memory.”

Mei nodded. “We have reasons to visit them already. Write down what you heard. Abby can decide which questions he wants to take.”

Abby licked a crumb from the side of his mouth.

“They can begin with the snow. I remember it being very deep.”

He looked toward the water as though something might appear there to explain his own sentence. After a moment he took the second cake and put it beside Jinhsi’s cup.

Jué watched him do it.

***

Yangyang came up later with the Wind.

The air around her collar stirred as Jué turned toward them. She sat where he could see the two shells in their wrapping and let the Wind move into the open space above them.

He listened for a long time.

“You have crossed through a place that altered the order of your life,” he said at last. “I can hear the distance in it. The sounds you lost lie beyond the time I can reach.”

One corner of the cloth lifted and fell.

Yangyang touched the shell her mother had given her.

“We are going to Mengzhou. There are people there who work with the old characters.”

Jué looked toward the high opening in the chamber.

“Will you come here again when you have been?”

The Wind moved along the edge of his foreclaw. She lingered over a shallow scar, cool enough that his mane shifted toward the touch. Then she withdrew to the cloth and turned her own shell until its worn outer curve faced him.

Yangyang watched the movement. Her hand remained beside the wrapping.

“She can answer you then,” she said.

Jué lowered his head in acknowledgment.

On the way down, Yangyang stopped at the window overlooking the eastern slopes. The Wind moved ahead of her into the open air. For several minutes she travelled among the branches while Yangyang sat on the sill, watching the leaves turn.

When she came back, she brought the smell of pears.

***

Jinhsi visited her mother’s grave before the afternoon rain.

Changli and Sanhua came with her. Xinyi had given them a brush for clearing the moss, and Sanhua carried a small flask of water. They worked for a while beneath the trees, loosening the green growth around the marker and washing the dirt from the stones.

Jinhsi told her mother about the mountain. She explained the damaged greenhouse, the cranes, the second breath. Then she sat back on her heels with the wet brush across her lap.

“I have been learning to cook,” she said. “The first ones came apart. Sanhua helped me close them.”

Changli sat on a flat stone nearby. Her dressing had been changed that morning, and she held her arm comfortably across her knees.

Jinhsi looked at the two women, then at the marker.

“I wish I knew what you liked to eat.”

Rain began softly in the branches. Sanhua opened the umbrella and brought it over them. Jinhsi put the brush beside the flask, wiped her wet hands on the cloth, and stayed until the drops had begun reaching the ground.

***

They left the following morning.

Jinhsi went to Mianloong early enough to find Jué asleep. She sat near his head with the scale on her knees and listened to the rhythm she was learning to hear from outside herself.

After a little while, he woke.

His eye found her. She lifted a hand in greeting.

“The captain expects us after breakfast,” she said. “Xinyi has packed enough food to delay the boat.”

“She considers a journey an uncertain interval between meals.”

“Amy agrees with her. They spent twenty minutes discussing the size of the baskets.”

Jué shifted his head, finding a more comfortable place against his claw. The wound still shone beneath the dressing of light Baizhi had taught the maintenance crew to measure.

Jinhsi watched it until he spoke again.

“How did you sleep?”

She looked up.

“I woke several times. Once I thought I was still on the platform.” She ran her thumb along the scale’s edge. “Sanhua was awake. We talked until I could lie down again.”

“What did you talk about?”

“A book Chisa lent her. There is a woman in it who has been concealing a marriage for reasons Sanhua finds increasingly difficult to respect.”

Jué’s breath moved her hair.

“That sounds like a conversation requiring another night.”

“We have been promised a second volume.”

She smiled, then looked down at the scale.

“When I call from Jinzhou, there will be days when I want to tell you something small. I used to save those things until I had a question important enough to bring me here.”

“Call when you have something to tell me. I shall be glad to hear your voice.”

“You can ask me to call later if you need to rest.”

He considered that with an attention she recognized from the long hours spent over their measurements.

“This afternoon, I expect I shall.”

Jinhsi’s mouth trembled. She leaned forward and put both hands against his face, then rested her forehead between them.

He lowered his head to meet her.

They stayed that way until footsteps sounded on the far stair. Changli had come with Jinhsi’s coat folded over her arm. Sanhua walked beside her, carrying the traveling bag and a parcel whose warm scent reached them before she did.

Jinhsi drew back. She wiped her cheeks with the heel of one hand, accepted the coat, and held out the scale while she found its case in the bag. Sanhua took it and returned it as soon as the case was open.

At the first stair, Jinhsi turned.

“I will come back after the town meeting. There is more I want to ask you.”

Jué’s eye stayed on her.

“Bring the questions. We can take them one at a time.”

She nodded and went down with Changli on one side and Sanhua on the other. Halfway to the gallery, her calf began to ache. She told them, and they slowed until the stairs ended.

Beyond the opening, morning lay across the mountain. Water travelled through the repaired channel, carrying a pear leaf toward the sea. On the landing below, Lupa was calling for someone to explain how two people had acquired six baskets. Amy’s answer included the jar of peppers for Chixia, three purchases Jinhsi failed to catch, and a quantity of tea that made Iuno raise her voice.

Jinhsi stopped beneath the arch and unwrapped the parcel Sanhua had brought.

Inside were three warm buns. One had a broad, uneven seam where she had helped close it the night before.

She took that one and offered the others around.

Changli bit into hers too soon, looked injured, and received a patient explanation from Sanhua about the filling. Jinhsi laughed with her mouth full. They stood there eating while the argument at the landing gained a fourth voice and the morning moved over their shoulders.

High in Mianloong Chamber, Jué turned his head toward the eastern light.

He watched it reach the water. Then he closed his eyes, and slept.

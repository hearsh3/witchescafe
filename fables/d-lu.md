Chapter 3: Dì Luò

Behind Suoming’s back, three more shells broke open above the river gorge, washing the nine wet roofs of the Fogveil Pagoda in silent crimson and gold.

She came up the last flight of the sanctuary stair at a dead run, the open brown canopy of her oil-paper umbrella tilted back over her shoulder and her iron heel-plates striking sparks from the red-lit bluestone.

Across the wide flagstones of the top landing, the pair of crimson eaves-lanterns hung still on their bronze dragon-hooks. Ahead of her stood the twelve-foot doors of pale mutton-fat jade, the carved nine-tailed fox relief half-buried under three fingers of hard blue-white rime along the central seam.

Suoming did not stop at four paces.

At full stride, two yards from the threshold, she took the bamboo shaft of Nine Shadows in both small hands, brought the open umbrella down level with her chest, and let her hold on the storm slip by the breadth of a fingernail.

The brown paper canopy bowed taut as a kettle-drum. Out of the steel-tipped ribs tore a bolt of jagged, crimson-black lightning as thick as a well-chain and struck the frosted seam of the jade doors point-blank.

The thunderclap split the night air over Xuanfang Hold like the sky coming down on an anvil.

Along the twelve-foot joint, the blue-white rime exploded into flying needles of ice. Deep inside the threshold, the four-inch bronze crossbar sheared in two with a shriek of torn metal, and the left-hand leaf of mutton-fat jade cracked straight down the middle of the fox’s nine tails and banged inward against the dark wall.

Before the echo of the thunder had reached the lower terraces, Suoming snapped the wooden catch down the bamboo shaft, furled the smoking umbrella with a single twist of her wrist, and stepped over the broken jade sill into the Sanctum.

The room beneath the ninth roof was round, windowless, and cold. Overhead, the cedar rafters opened to a circular well of night sky where the stalled black rings of the Astrolabe Core hung against the twin moons; underfoot, the dark lacquered floor was inlaid with matching concentric rings of tarnished bronze.

The air smelled thick and sweet, like winter pears left to rot in a warm cellar.

Near the inner pillar, Hsin’s low silk floor-cushion sat empty with the hollow of a curled body still pressed into the velvet. Beside it on a lacquer tray lay a single bamboo skewer of candied hawthorns, the sugar glaze gone dark and dry around the shriveled fruit, and up on the narrow shelf above the astrolabe desk sat a small paper fox folded from red festival stock that had come out round as a turnip instead of dignified.

Everywhere else, the Sanctum had been eaten from underneath.

Out of the joints between the floor-panels and up through the bronze conduit-traps coiled hundreds of grey-green vines as thick as a man’s forearm, armored in dull overlapping scales like snake-belly. They choked the brass pillars, strangled the vertical shafts running up toward the roof, and pressed so hard into the cedar beams that the old black lacquer had gone soft and pulpy as wet bark.

In the exact center of the inner bronze ring, politely clear of Hsin’s velvet cushion, the tall clerk in the plain Ministry grey coat sat on his heels.

He had laid his round felt hat neatly beside his right knee and closed his leather pocket-book flat in his lap. Thick, scaled grey-green creepers came straight up out of the floor seam beneath his hem, vanished up both of his grey wool cuffs, and threaded under the back of his collar. Along the side of his jaw and across the knuckles of the hand resting on his book, patches of grey-green scales had grown through the skin.

He lifted his chin toward the broken doorway. In the dark under the roof-well, his eyes burned with a flat, steady blue light.

“Good evening,” Balanophora said through the clerk’s mouth, in his pleasant capital baritone. “You needn’t have taken the hinge off the door. It wasn’t locked against you.”

In the red glow from the porch lanterns behind her, Suoming’s still face moved—the corners of her mouth pulling down hard and white as her pale eyes fixed on the scaled hand in his lap.

“Balanophora,” Suoming said, and she was already lunging across the bronze ring with the steel ferrule aimed at his wrist.

***

Down on the stone landing of the third storehouse, the thunderclap from the top of the cliff hit the terrace hard enough to make the iron ladles jump in the cauldrons.

Up over the southern gorge, the last trailing gold willow winked out against the clouds.

No rocket followed it. The hollow *thump* of the unseen mortars down beyond the second bend stopped dead, leaving the narrow gap of sky between the karst spires dark under the fast-sliding copper moons.

Along the crowded bluestone steps, the cheering of the village children cut off mid-breath. Down by the well-head in Huizhen Plaza, the tanner’s yellow dog let out a single sharp bark; by the split-cedar fence, one of the valley quarrymen gave a short, nervous laugh into his mug and shut his mouth.

Two hundred faces turned upward toward the sheer cliff face at the back of the Hold.

High above the market roofs, behind the carved cedar lattice of the Fogveil Pagoda’s ninth tier, jagged forks of crimson-black lightning were crawling back and forth across the eaves like a thunderstorm trapped inside a lantern.

Beside the stone cistern trough, Yangyang set her earthenware mug down on the coping wall. She knew the sound of that umbrella opening on the stair.

By the storehouse doorpost, Warden Liangyu had her ash spear in her right hand before the rumble died out of the rock.

“Corporal Meng!” Liangyu barked across the yard. “Keep this landing! Get the valley families and every child on these steps inside the storehouse and the cooper’s kitchen and drop the bars! Ge, Boyang, with me!”

By the wooden turbine post, Qingxiao set her half-eaten bowl of mutton stew down on the flat cap of the wall. She did not walk to the lane; a line of moon-white light flashed under her heels, and she rose straight up the face of the dark cliff toward the pagoda eaves like an arrow loosed from a bow.

Across the gutter on the cooper’s step, Buling yanked the wad of waste paper out of the mouth of her brass sash-bell and threw the paper into the ditch.

*Ting-ting-ting-ting!*

The small brass clapper went wild against the rim without a finger touching it, ringing fast and high in the cold air as Buling bolted out into the cobbled lane toward the upper steps.

“Ma, stay by the stove with Wen—” Amy started, already reaching for the wrench in her vest.

“Not a chance,” Mei said.

She was standing on her flat road-boots in front of the oak stool with her dark jacket buttoned to the chin, and the tone of her voice stopped Amy’s hand in mid-air.

Lupa didn’t argue for half a second. She grounded her spear, stepped straight in front of Mei’s toes, and dropped to a crouch with her broad back squared and both arms hooked back.

“Get on,” Lupa said.

“I can make the first terrace if I—”

“There’s three hundred steps on that rock, Deadbeat, get on my back before I tie you to the spear!”

Mei looped both arms around Lupa’s neck and hooked her shins over Lupa’s hips; Lupa caught Mei’s thighs in one scarred forearm, snatched her ash spear up in her free right hand, and broke into a long, pounding mountain lope up the lane.

The rest of the yard cleared in the same breath. Chisa swung her cased scissor-blade over her shoulder and ran at Lupa’s right flank; Ciaccona slung her lute-case across her back with her good hand; Cartethyia snatched one of the lit horn lanterns off the storehouse hook to light the risers; and Amy fell in on Iuno’s left side at the foot of the stair, bracing her own right shoulder firmly under Iuno’s arm so Iuno’s one hand could stay free on the stone balustrade.

Yangyang took the point behind Liangyu and Ge.

For the first time in seven days, there was no tight linen strapping binding her chest when her boots hit the steep bluestone risers. She took the steps three at a bound, her sheer cloud-worked sleeves streaming back in the freezing draft and her lungs drawing the icy night air all the way down to the bottom of her ribs without a hitch.

Behind her, Lupa’s re-nailed boot-heels hammered the wet stone like iron pistons—*clack-clack, clack-clack*—her breath coming in deep, steady bellows while Mei kept her chin tucked tight in the wolf-fur collar over Lupa’s right shoulder and watched the red eaves-lanterns whip past.

Overhead, the twin moons had already cleared the zenith of the pass, sliding westward behind the carmine-stained cloud so fast that the shadows of the stone balustrade-posts crept visibly across the risers under their boots.

At the second turn of the cliff—where the narrow gallery track from the inner gatehouse joined the sanctuary stair—rapid footsteps came pelting out of the dark archway on the left.

Ruoyun swung onto the main stair at a flat run, her pleated teal skirt flying above her white ankle socks and her shielded brass watch-lamp held out in her right hand.

She didn’t slow down as she cut in front of Ciaccona and Amy, glancing back once over the rims of her round brass spectacles at Lupa carrying Mei.

“If you drop her on my clean steps, wolf, I’m billing you for the mortar!” Ruoyun snapped, and surged past Warden Ge’s elbow toward the upper tiers without a single short breath in her chest.

Above their heads, another silent flare of crimson-black light lit the ninth-tier lattice from within, throwing the long shadow of the broken jade door out across the cliff mist.

Then, thirty risers from the top landing, the red lightning went out.

Yangyang’s boots hit the last turn of the rock behind Liangyu.

In the sudden quiet between the scrape of their boots on the stone and Lupa’s heavy breathing behind her, Yangyang heard two voices speaking inside the dark chamber at the top of the steps—one of them a man’s courteous baritone, and the other Suoming’s voice, raised sharp and loud against the rafters.

Yangyang had never heard Suoming raise her voice.

***

Inside the Sanctum, the steel ferrule of Nine Shadows struck two inches left of the clerk’s knee.

It had not missed. Halfway through the thrust, the clerk’s left arm had lifted at an awkward, marionette angle, placing the soft underside of his forearm squarely in the path of the steel point so Suoming had to wrench the bamboo shaft sideways to keep from punching through his bone.

The ferrule drove deep into the scaled grey-green creeper coiled beside his thigh instead, pinning it to the soft lacquer of the floor.

Balanophora did not flinch as dark, sweet-smelling sap welled around the iron point. The clerk’s head tilted politely on his neck, his blue-lit eyes following Suoming as she pivoted on her heel and slashed a steel-tipped rib across the two vines feeding into his right cuff.

“Still the same habit, Intendant,” Balanophora said pleasantly. “All that thunder in your cane, and you still look to see where the clerk’s fingers are before you put your foot down.”

“Don’t,” Suoming said, her flat voice hard as river flint as she stepped back out of the sweep of three fresh shoots erupting through the floor-seam behind her ankles.

“Don’t call you by the board you built?” The clerk’s mouth curved in a small, appreciative smile. Even while he sat still on his heels, two new scaled tendrils slid smoothly out of his torn sleeve-lining and knitted themselves back into the severed roots on the floor. “You kept that house in Mengzhou terribly clean, you know. Every desk turned out twice a season, every archive clerk’s coal-bill audited back to his grandmother. It took a great deal of patience to leave a seed under the floorboards while you had the broom in your hand.”

“Not thorough enough,” Suoming said.

“No,” Balanophora agreed warmly. “Not quite.”

Four thick vines reared up from the outer bronze ring like striking snakes and whipped down together at the crown of Suoming’s red hair.

The iron-shod creepers smashed into the floorboards with a heavy *thwack*—and Suoming was not there.

There had been no step and no blur of plum-coloured cloth; in the beat between the swing and the strike, the space under the vines was simply empty, and Suoming stood three paces to the clerk’s left behind his shoulder, her small boots planted flat on the inner bronze ring and her breath catching with a harsh, dry scrape against the bottom of her left ribs.

She did not look at the glowing blue eyes. She looked straight down at the grey-green scales crusting the side of the man’s neck, where his pulse beat fast and thin under the skin.

“Surveyor,” Suoming said clearly. “Can you hear me?”

For the space of a heartbeat, the flat blue light in the clerk’s eyes flickered.

The pleasant curve went out of his mouth; the muscles around his jaw twitched hard against the scales, and when his voice came out, it was a third of a tone higher, hoarse and small and terrified:

“The—the hinge-pin on the south pier measured true to the book, ma’am, I wrote it in the—please, my wife is at the lower wharf, tell the Minister I want to go—”

The blue light filled his irises from rim to rim like water rising in a well, and the clerk’s shoulders settled smoothly back onto his heels.

“You must forgive him,” Balanophora said, smoothing the front of the grey coat with his scaled left hand. “He has been tapping stones in the rain since Tuesday, and mountain air never agrees with capital clerks. We are in the same trade, after all, you and I. Both of us keeping our hands on the table long after our own chairs were taken away, by borrowing what younger people can spare.”

Suoming’s knuckles whitened on the bamboo shaft of the umbrella.

“I asked,” Suoming said.

“And they gave it to you, all nine of them, down to the last drop in the cup,” Balanophora said, her voice full of genuine, unhurried wonder. “And look where it brought you. Two hundred winters on a wet rock, standing outside a bolted door in the rain while the tree rots from the taproot up.”

The clerk’s scaled right hand lifted an inch from his pocket-book, gesturing lightly around the dark Sanctum—at the soft, weeping lacquer on the pillars, the dry hawthorn skewer on Hsin’s tray, and the stalled black rings of the Core hanging silent in the roof-well overhead.

“You shouldn’t blame the artisan with the brass spindles, or the Minister’s boys with their chalk-sticks,” Balanophora said conversationally. “I didn’t break a single beam in this house. When a fruit has hung on the bough four hundred years past its season, all sugar and wet rot on the inside, nobody has to shake the branch. You only have to sit quiet in the roots down in Mengzhou until the stem goes soft, and walk up the steps with your apron open when it’s ready to drop.”

Suoming took one step closer across the bronze ring, raising the steel ferrule level with the clerk’s throat.

“Get out of her house,” Suoming said.

Balanophora’s blue eyes did not blink in front of the steel point.

“I have sat under a fox’s floor before, Intendant,” Balanophora murmured softly. “And the fox was a very long time noticing.”

Suoming did not answer with a word.

She flipped her grip on the bamboo shaft and drove a short, vicious backhand sweep with the steel-ribbed canopy—not at the clerk’s throat, but straight through the thick cluster of anchor-vines rising between his knees from the central conduit-slot.

The steel-tipped ribs bit through the wrist-thick, iron-scaled creepers with a sound like an axe going into green wood. Black sap sprayed across the clerk’s grey knees; his torso jerked forward off his heels before three thicker roots punched straight up through the cedar floorboards under his coattails to prop him upright again.

“Careful,” Balanophora chided mildly, though the clerk’s breath rattled once in his windpipe before she steadied it. “You’ll spoil his coat before the walk down. Did you enjoy the fireworks over the pass tonight? I thought the gold willows were particularly fine.”

Suoming’s boots stopped on the sap-slick bronze.

“A place like this gives back everything it was holding when the bottom seam lets go,” Balanophora went on, tilting the clerk’s head to look up at the strand of red hair that had come loose from Suoming’s gilded orb-pin. “Even the little one in the hemp tunic got to stand on the wall and clap her hands at the rockets. How happy she looked, turning round to see if her sister was watching.”

For half a heartbeat the Sanctum was dead still.

Then Suoming’s face twisted—not the tiny, dry crease at the corner of the eye that the Ministry’s clerks had watched for a century, but raw, open fury that bared her teeth—and when she spoke, her voice rang off the high cedar rafters loud enough to carry down the cliff stair:

“*Put her out of your mouth!*”

She did not touch the wooden catch on the umbrella.

Because she stopped holding it down inside her ribs, the black storm sealed in Nine Shadows tore straight through the oiled paper on its own.

A blinding cage of crimson-black lightning burst outward from the bamboo shaft in Suoming’s fist, racing across the concentric bronze rings of the floor, blasting the scaled creepers off the pillars in showers of charred green bark, and roaring straight up through the circular hole in the ninth roof to lash against the dark belly of the Astrolabe Core.

The left cuff of the clerk’s grey coat caught a stray tongue of black fire and smoldered into grey ash; a blister rose along his scaled jaw, and still Balanophora did not make him stand up or raise a hand to beat it out.

Through the crackle of the dying lightning and the stink of burnt sap, the clerk’s blue eyes looked past Suoming’s shoulder toward the broken jade doorway.

“Nobody puts down a pen in fair weather, Intendant,” Balanophora said pleasantly. “You wait until the hand holding it is burning. And here comes the girl with the pen right now.”

Outside on the red-lit flagstones of the porch, a vertical line of moon-white light dropped out of the night sky with a sharp *chink* of steel, and Qingxiao stood framed in the split jade doorway with her bare dao in her right hand and the sound of twelve pairs of boots pounding up the last flight behind her.

***

Yangyang came over the top riser half a pace behind Liangyu’s spear, her chest heaving deep and clean in the freezing air as the sweet, spoiled-pear stench of the Sanctum rolled out through the sheared jade door.

For the space of a breath the threshold jammed with shoulders: Qingxiao already two paces inside the split fox-carving with her moon-white blade raised; Buling skidding to a halt with her brass sash-bell ringing like a fire-alarm; Ruoyun holding her shielded watch-lamp high above her small golden horns; and Lupa swinging Mei smoothly down off her back onto the bronze floor just inside the broken right-hand doorleaf.

Mei kept her boots under her, planting her left palm flat against the cold mutton-fat jade to steady her side while Amy and Iuno came up against her elbow.

Inside the round chamber, the red porch-light and Ruoyun’s yellow lamp showed the scaled grey-green vines choking Hsin’s brass pillars, the scorched black tracks where Suoming’s lightning had burned across the bronze floor-rings, Suoming standing in the middle of the room with a long lock of red hair hanging down over her plum collar, and the tall clerk kneeling in the center ring with blue lamps for eyes.

Buling’s bell gave a sharp, double jangle against her hip.

“Oi,” Buling said flatly, pointing a finger straight under her yellow forehead talisman at the clerk’s nose. “Second terrace, east room. Friday afternoon I moved his wash-stand two feet off the damp wall, he told me he was fourth assistant auditor for grain-weights. Still owes me three silvers.”

“Balanophora,” Suoming said without turning her head from the clerk. “Don’t let the vine touch your skin.”

Beside Mei at the doorjamb, Iuno’s spine went very still under her heavy wool coat. She had heard that name spoken across polished cedar tables in rooms where the shutters were barred at noon, when Jinhsi’s couriers and the northern ministers talked about what lived under the floorboards in Mengzhou.

*Well, darling,* the High Priestess murmured in the back of her mind, every trace of play gone out of the bronze voice. *So that is what has been eating the orchard.*

Two steps inside the door, Ruoyun’s bright blue eyes didn’t stop on the clerk at all.

Her gaze went straight past him to the inner cedar pillar—where a scaled, thumb-thick creeper had crawled up the softening lacquer of the shelf, knocked Hsin’s little round paper fox sideways into the wet sap, and crushed its folded paper ear flat against the wood.

Ruoyun did not draw a weapon. Her face went white to the lips; she marched three steps to the pillar, set her brass lamp down on the floor, dragged her ruffled cuffs down over both hands, grabbed the scaled vine through the silk, and ripped it bodily out of the pulpy wood with a wet *crack* that splashed dark sap across the hem of her skirt.

“Get your filthy weeds off her shelf, you gutter-leech!” Ruoyun snarled, flinging the torn creeper down on the bronze ring and stamping it flat under the heel of her Mary Jane shoe.

Before the clerk’s head could turn toward her, the seams between the lacquered floor-panels heaved upward all around the room.

Twenty scaled grey-green vines as thick as ship’s hawsers tore loose from the sub-floor and lashed straight at the crowded doorway.

“Back from the sill!” Lupa roared, stepping across Mei’s front and driving her ash spear two-handed into the lead pair of creepers; howling red-gold flame ran down the iron socket and boiled the sap white inside their scales.

To her right, a barbed runner the thickness of a stove-pipe whipped low at Iuno’s knees. Cartethyia dropped her horn lantern on the stone, threw herself forward on both knees, and caught the wet, thrashing vine in both gloved hands six inches from Iuno’s hem, digging her heels into the broken jade chips on the floor to hold it still.

“Chisa, the root!” Cartethyia shouted, her small wrists straining white against the scales.

Chisa stepped over the lantern, laid the open jaws of her giant scissor-blade flush against the floor-joint where the vine came up, and snapped the handles shut.

*CLANG.*

The severed root spurted black sap and dropped slack in Cartethyia’s fists, even as two thinner shoots split out of the same crack beside Chisa’s boot.

From the left pillar, Buling whipped three yellow paper slips out of her collar and slapped them in a triangle across the outer bronze ring.

“Out of the puddle!” Buling barked, snapping her fingers—and a three-way web of blue-white thunder cracked across the metal inlay, charring five advancing creepers into smoking rope while a stray arc jumped straight back up Buling’s own right index finger with a loud *zap* that stood her silver braid up under her hat. “Aiya—still leaking!”

In the center of the floor, Qingxiao did not give an inch of ground. Her bare dao moved in three level, unhurried passes of moon-white light that sheared every whipping tendril within six feet of Suoming’s back into neat, foot-long cylinders of dead green wood; behind her, Ciaccona braced her lute against her hip and struck a single, percussive *whack* across the low strings with her thumb that blew a spray of smaller shoots sideways into the wall, while Liangyu, Ge, and Boyang locked their ash pike-shafts across the jade threshold to keep the doorway clear.

Yangyang stepped over the cracked mutton-fat sill into the Sanctum with her sword in her left hand.

The instant her boot sole touched the outer bronze ring of the floor, the flat square block of Hsin’s Prism inside her sash woke up.

It did not give a single faint pulse and go dark the way it had in the Mirage. It turned hot—hotter than a stove-brick wrapped in flannel, burning through three layers of silk tunic against her unstrapped ribs—and a clear, piercing white light shone straight out through the weave of her sash into the dark room.

Every single thing in the Sanctum turned toward her waist at once.

The thrashing vines around the pillars froze with their scaled tips pointed at her belt; Suoming’s head whipped around; and in the middle of the inner ring, the tall clerk’s glowing blue eyes locked onto the light under Yangyang’s sash.

“There,” Balanophora said softly.

They moved for it in the same heartbeat.

From the right, the clerk’s scaled arm shot out and four braided creepers tore out of his grey sleeve like harpoon-lines, whistling straight for Yangyang’s sash; from the left, Suoming kicked off the bronze ring with her small hand stretched out open across the gap.

Yangyang’s right hand dived into the fold of her sash and pulled the burning, white-hot square of the Prism out into the air.

For a fraction of a second—while the scaled tips of the vines were two feet from her wrist and Suoming’s boots were mid-stride across the sap-stained floor—Yangyang looked at both of them.

On her right were the clerk’s round, unblinking eyes of flat blue light, calm and pleased and patient from fifty winters in the dark under the roots.

On her left were Suoming’s pale, light-sore eyes beneath her loosened red hair, tired and furious and ordinary in the glare.

Nobody spoke a word.

Yangyang drew her arm back and threw the shining Prism low and hard across the ring to Suoming.

Suoming did not reach out to catch it.

In mid-stride, she took the bamboo shaft of Nine Shadows in both hands, let the last of the crimson-black storm roar down the length of the cane, and swung the steel ferrule like a bat, meeting the flying square of the Prism dead-center in the air.

*CRACK.*

The Prism of Fate did not shatter.

Struck in mid-air by the black lightning, its four dark stone faces unlocked along hair-fine seams of gold and folded outward like the leaves of a lantern opening in the dark.

Out of its unfolded core poured a silent, blinding tide of moon-white light that washed from wall to wall of the Sanctum.

Wherever the white light touched the scaled grey-green vines, they blistered black and shriveled back on themselves like paper thrown onto red coals. In the center of the ring, the tall clerk arched his back with a sharp, strangled gasp; the flat blue light in his eyes went out like a snuffed wick, the patches of grey-green scale along his jaw and knuckles turned dry ash-white and flaked off onto his wool knees, and he pitched forward onto his face on the bronze floor, groaning once into his bare hands.

All around the chamber, the hundreds of severed vines whipped backward out of the pillars and yanked themselves straight down into the floor-seams as fast as snakes dropping into a well, vanishing beneath the wood.

At the doorway, Liangyu did not wait to see what the floating white light would do next.

She dropped her ash spear into Ge’s hand, lunged two paces across the threshold, grabbed the groaning clerk by the back of his grey wool collar with both hands, and hauled him bodily backward over the cracked jade sill onto the outer flagstones between Boyang’s boots.

High overhead, framed in the circular hole of the ninth roof, the colossal black bronze rings of the Astrolabe Core shuddered.

Along their frosted bevels, a winter’s crust of blue rime cracked and showered down through the roof-well in a bright, tinkling rain. Then, with a vast, grinding groan of dry metal that vibrated through every tooth in their heads, shook the loose mortar out of the cliff-seams, and rolled all the way down the gorge to the river, the three outer rings of the Core broke loose from their stall and began to turn against the copper moons.

Down beneath their boots, the concentric bronze rings inlaid in the Sanctum floor answered the sky and turned with them.

A deep, heavy *click-clack-clack* ran around the walls like the tumblers of a mountain lock dropping home. Along the curved grain of the wood, the dark lacquered floor-panels unlocked from the joists and folded downward and outward in a circle—panel after panel dropping open like the ribs of a giant paper fan.

In the instant the seams parted, the sweet, spoiled-pear stench that the Prism’s blaze had burned out of the rafters came back all at once.

It poured through the room thicker and colder than cellar damp, except that it was rushing *downward*—tearing past their cheeks and through the widening gap under their boot-soles like the heavy draught sucked down a well-shaft when the cover comes off, carrying whatever had slipped out of the clerk’s flesh straight down into the opening ahead of them.

Suoming’s chin snapped down before her heels left the wood, her nostrils flaring white and her mouth setting into a hard, bloodless line as the reek of rotted winter fruit plunged past her knees.

Underneath their feet, there was no eighth tier of the pagoda, no cedar beams, and no mountain rock.

Stretching away below their boot-soles, and curving up around the walls, and hanging inverted miles overhead in a warm, amber-lit gulf of mist, was the whole of Xuanfang Hold—and plummeting straight down the middle of the shaft ahead of them all went the unfolded white star of the Prism of Fate.

The floor gave way under all twelve of them at once.

As Yangyang’s boots dropped into the open air through the rush of foul, sweet wind, she felt a sudden, distinct tug at her right cuff—a small, warm hand closing around her wrist for a single beat to pull her through the gap—and right beside her left ear, closer than the gale, someone let out a single, long, tired breath that had been held for a very long time.

Above them, on the solid bluestone landing outside the sill where the stone had remained shut, Warden Liangyu dropped to one knee beside Boyang, Ge, and the groaning clerk, her bare right hand clamped white-knuckled on the jagged edge of the broken mutton-fat jade door, staring down into the blinding well of light as the twelve figures fell away into the Hold.

***

They did not hit flagstones.

The rush of cold night wind roared past their ears, trailing the last cellar-sweet ribbon of rotted pear down into the gulf below their boots, and in the first breath of the drop the round shaft of the Sanctum tore open and the whole of Xuanfang Hold came loose from the mountain and swung through the middle of them.

At the center of the fall, Mei had Yangyang’s left hand locked in her right and Iuno’s bare hand in her left when an entire stepped terrace of market stalls—its awning-poles hung with whipping red cloth—pivoted sideways across their drop like a slamming sluice-gate. The heavy cedar balustrade crashed straight between Mei’s and Iuno’s linked forearms; Mei’s knuckles banged hard against the timber rail, tearing Iuno’s fingers out of her grip while Yangyang held fast to Mei’s right hand with both of hers and was yanked down the sheer lee of the stalls.

“Iuiu!” Mei yelled, throwing her weight against the draft toward the sweeping rail.

On the far side of the timber, Iuno spun backward with her empty left sleeve snapping in the gale and her right hand reaching back toward the closing gap—and Amy tucked her chin, dove headfirst down the steep slant of an inverted tile roof, and caught the back of Iuno’s heavy wool collar in both fists, hauling them tight against her sheepskin vest just as a solid wall of stacked hemp grain-bags slid shut between them and Mei.

Thirty feet lower, a vertical cliff built entirely of lit brick kitchen stoves—every iron damper wide open, every firebox roaring red—rotated through the dark like a mill-wheel. The scorching blast off the brickwork caught Cartethyia’s wide skirt and flung her sideways; her horn lantern slipped from her fingers and went tumbling away into the glare. Lupa snarled in the back of her throat, shot her left hand out to clamp around Cartethyia’s booted ankle, and locked her right fist into the back of Chisa’s black leather belt, dragging both of them hard against her ribs as a long chimney of barred shop-shutters dropped over their heads.

To the east, the dead-matte black basalt sally-port of Yuan Fortress rolled end over end beside the lower gatehouse arch, the rusted iron teeth of its portcullis scything sideways through the mist. A hurtling timber beam knocked Ruoyun’s brass watch-lamp clean out of her fingers in a shower of yellow oil-sparks, the heavy backwash flinging her pleated teal skirt toward a turning granary wall. Above her, Qingxiao stepped off the empty air as if it were stone, dove straight through the narrowing gap under the iron portcullis teeth with her moon-white sleeves streaming back, and caught Ruoyun hard around the bare right wrist; Ruoyun’s fingers closed like iron around Qingxiao’s forearm, kicking her shoe off a passing coping-stone as a rising watch-gallery gable sheared between them and the rest of the Hold.

Below the granary eaves, the Fillet Fairmaiden’s two great bronze cauldrons jutted horizontally out of a vertical bluestone wall, boiling sideways into the void and blasting a thick white wall of peppered steam across the shaft. Ciaccona’s velvet lute-case snagged on a passing iron lantern-hook in the fog, jerking her backward by the shoulder-strap toward the scalding plume—and Buling, plummeting feet-first with one hand clamping her round winter hat to her crown, grabbed the leather strap of the lute-case with the other and yanked it free of the iron, her small brass sash-bell ringing *ting-ting-ting-ting* as a folding staircase of red lanterns swung shut behind their backs.

High above the sliding roofs and revolving walls, Suoming dropped alone through a closing slot of bluestone steps with both small hands locked on the furled bamboo shaft of her umbrella, and in the space of a single heartbeat the turning streets sealed every gap and tore the six groups out of sight of one another.

Down the wide vertical chute beside the cistern lane, where the cooper’s barred pine door and the yellow paper talismans pasted to its lintel slid smoothly upward past their shoulders, Iuno locked her right arm around Amy’s waist to steady their spin. Directly overhead, hanging upside down from an inverted sky, Huizhen Plaza revolved on its pivot: the twelve wide bluestone steps climbed into the underside of the dyer’s porch, and the stone well-head pointed straight down at their crowns with its black water staying flat and unspilled inside the granite curb.

Around their boots, small rectangular blocks of cast lead drifted through the amber mist like grey snow—loose type-sorts tumbled out of a founder’s case, their reversed characters glinting in the lantern-light. Where the cooper’s stone wall met the alley cobbles, thick white bookbinder’s thread stitched the masonry together along the curb like the spine of a ledger, and when Iuno looked hard at the corner of the eaves, the stone went paper-pale and off-register, its red lintel-ink sitting a hair’s breadth outside its black outline.

Through the lit casements of the third storehouse across the chute, there were no grain-sacks or salt-barrels; inside each wooden window-frame hung another whole Xuanfang Hold in miniature, its tiny tiered roofs and black basalt ramparts revolving in the amber mist, with its own lantern-lit windows opening inward on smaller keeps and smaller streets without end, while overhead, in the upside-down granite curb of Huizhen Plaza, the flat black disc of the well-water held another inverted plaza gleaming inside its rim.

“Look at the casements!” Amy shouted against the rush of air, bracing both boots on the updraft with her fingers knotted in Iuno’s coat. “It’s the same blueprint set inside itself all the way down!”

Iuno’s bare crystal shoulder chimed once against the wind as a drifted lead sort struck her cuff and spun away. She looked at the reversed metal character, and at the blank wooden blocks wedged into the air between the eaves like printer’s furniture holding a locked-up forme in place.

“The press that set its own proof in Shao’s shop,” Iuno said, her voice cutting clear and low across the draft. “The dawn-drum answering from the cliff, and the shells that left no smoke—none of it was coming down from the sky. It was bleeding up through the floorboards from this.”

*And we walked straight into the press-bed, darling,* the High Priestess murmured behind her breastbone, all play gone from the bronze voice. *Give the fox her due. When she prints a room, she doesn’t stint on the paper.*

Out of the nested windows rose the low, steady thud of the leather dawn-drum and the iron jingle of mule-bells along a vertical pack-road, and Iuno shifted her grip higher on Amy’s sheepskin vest, setting her shoulder against the gale.

Across the wall of grain-sacks, shooting out of the chimney of barred shop-shutters into a vast cleft where the dead-matte black basalt ramparts and three-legged iron braziers of Yuan Fortress were mortared seamlessly into the Hold’s grey quarry-stone, Lupa held Cartethyia’s ankle and Chisa’s belt in an iron grip. All around them, whole slate-roofed terraces turned over like the stiff leaves of a thread-bound book, heavy hemp cord showing taut through the mortar joints, while great dark blocks of blank spacer-wood filled the intervals between the towers to lock the walls in place.

The rhythmic *thump-clack* of print-shop presses and the hollow wooden clack of the cistern wheel rose up through the shaft to join the drumbeat.

Chisa rested her free hand on the hilt of her cased scissor-blade, her head tilted back as her eyes tracked the revolving ceilings of cobbles, rafters, and wedged wooden spacers where the zenith belonged.

“There is no sky to cut,” Chisa said plainly. “Every ceiling is the underside of another street.”

Beside her knee, Cartethyia held her laurel of crystal thorns to her brow with one gloved hand, looking out at the endless tiers of glowing crimson eaves-lanterns and the orange trails of the fortress braziers burning steady inside the turning pages.

“She kept every lantern lit while the doors were barred,” Cartethyia called out earnestly. “The lady of the keep has been holding her hall from within!”

The hot orange draft off a row of passing Yuan braziers washed across Lupa’s face; her wolf-ears flattened back in the gale, and she bared her teeth in a wide, fierce grin, shifting her ash spear in her fist as she kicked off a projecting timber spacer to steer them clear of a turning gable.

“Good!” Lupa roared, and let out a long, ringing arena howl that echoed down three vertical alleys at once. “No road back up the chimney—she’s got to meet us at the bottom!”

Lower down the funnel, spinning out from behind the folding staircase of red lanterns beneath the horizontal bronze rims of the Fillet Fairmaiden’s cauldrons—where fat broth-bubbles drifted sideways through the peppered steam, each bubble’s skin holding a curved, reprinted image of the falling Hold—Ciaccona hooked her boot-heel over a passing stone baluster to check their tumble, one hand braced on the neck of her lute-case.

Through the snow of drifting woodblock sorts, the hiss of the cauldrons, the sharp *pop-crackle* of festival firecrackers, and the low bronze moan of the gatehouse horn slid up the scale together, locking into the thud of the dawn-drum and the clatter of the presses like an orchestra drawing one breath.

Ciaccona threw her head back as the timber balusters vibrated against her sole, and let out a bright, fierce laugh.

“Listen to the water-wheels—she tuned the whole Hold to D minor!” Ciaccona shouted over the swell. “Every press and kettle in the valley locked into a single chord!”

Hanging onto the leather strap of the lute-case with her silver braid whipping beneath her round winter hat, Buling squinted through the steam at the paper-pale edge of the granary wall, where the grey mortar blurred out of register beside a row of wedged lead spacers. The grin went clean off her mouth.

“This one is the boss’s own hand,” Buling said, her voice dropping flat and hard through the din. “Whatever she’s been up to all this time, Cia, it’s in here with us.” She yanked her hat-brim down with her free hand. “Only one way out of a press, and it’s through the bottom plate!”

To the east, dropping fast past the rising gable of the watch-gallery where the black basalt sally-port of Yuan Fortress was stitched with blue cord right into the grey terrace risers of Xuanfang, Ruoyun hung wrist-to-wrist with Qingxiao as the bronze peal of the watch-horns and the chime of the eaves-bells crashed into the rising chord.

With her free hand, Ruoyun clapped two fingers to the bridge of her round brass spectacles, glaring furiously at the paper-sharp rafters, the reprinted gutters, and the black Yuan battlements where the braziers trailed clean orange fire across the void.

“All these winters leaving me to scrub the real conduits in the sleet,” Ruoyun snarled at the top of her lungs, “and the mangy, nine-tailed cheat was down under the floorboards keeping a clean print for herself!”

Beside her, falling upright with her moon-white dress streaming around her ankles and her warm hand steady on the pommel of her dao, Qingxiao looked out across the black basalt ramparts mortared into the glowing Hold, breathed in the faint, plum-warm draft rising from the deep where the unfolded white star of the Prism fell, and spoke her name aloud:

“Hsin.”

Down the clear, rushing central lane of air between the upside-down cistern pond—where tiny paper boats sailed calmly across the black water above their heads with their candle-stubs burning yellow, each ripple in the pond opening onto another miniature night of paper boats and glowing piers—and a long, rolling wall of lit storehouse windows that reprinted the falling Hold inward as far as light could travel, the voice of the falling Hold swelled to its full, tremendous roar: the thunder of the dawn-drums, the bronze blare of the gatehouse horns, the rush of the gorge cataracts, the hiss of the cauldrons, the heavy *thump-clack* of the print-shop presses, and the ringing chime of the eaves-bells all sounding together in one vast, unbroken chord that shook the drifting type-sorts and turned the whole plunging gulf into the inside of a struck bell.

Out of the golden glow on the back of Mei’s right wrist, Abby popped his white head and long ears six inches from her cuff, took one wild, spinning look at the cistern pond sailing upside down above his nose, a row of cabbages rolling sideways up a vertical staircase, and another whole staircase turning inside a pickle-jar on a window ledge, yelled “What kind of architecture is this?!” into the gale, and dove straight back inside the warm neck of Mei’s jacket with both paws clamped to her collarbone.

Linked hand in hand with Mei, Yangyang still felt the phantom warmth of those small fingers closing around her right wrist at the threshold and the long, tired breath let out beside her ear; the small cerulean feathers along her collar lifted bright and full against the rushing gale, and she cupped her free hand to her mouth and shouted down into the roaring depth with everything in her lungs:

“*Hsin!*”

Mei laughed out loud—clear and reckless and wide awake, her fingers locked tight around Yangyang’s hand and her golden eyes blazing in the wash of red lantern-light pouring from the nested doorways around them.

“We got your door open!” Mei shouted down toward the distant white star plummeting through the funnel of the city. “Hold on, we’re coming down!”

High above them all, near the center of the turning gulf, Suoming fell straight and alone with her heavy iron-plated boots pointed at the deep and the furled bamboo shaft of Nine Shadows gripped in both small hands.

The loosened strand of red hair pulled the rest of the way out of her gilded orb-pin and whipped across her plum collar. Ten yards below her heels, a freezing eddy tore downward through a ring of paper-pale eaves and drifting lead sorts—the thick, cellar-sweet stench of rotted winter pears, plunging straight down the narrowest throat of the funnel behind the white light of the Prism.

Suoming’s pale, light-sore eyes tracked the foul draft down into the dark between the stitched walls.

“All the way to the root,” Suoming said to the wind, flat and quiet.

She pressed her thumb against the wooden catch on the bamboo shaft.

Nine Shadows shot open overhead with a single cannon-heavy *CRACK*—a live thread of crimson-black lightning ticking from steel rib to steel rib as the taut brown oiled paper caught the updraft and slowed her drop to a steady, plumb-line drift—and at the report of the canopy, the entire vast chord of drums, horns, presses, cauldrons, and bells cut off dead at once.

In the sudden, breathless silence, far off across the gulf beyond the nested terraces and the turning basalt towers, the amber mist parted, and a giant fox’s eye opened—vast as a mountainside, the heavy lid lifting slowly across the width of the void, and a vertical slit of black pupil widening inside a sea of burning gold.

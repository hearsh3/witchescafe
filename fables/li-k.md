Chapter 1: Liú Kè

“Put that chisel down,” Ciaccona said from the scullery doorway. “That is a seasoned iron skillet, not a quarry wall.”

Out by the wash-trough, Amy had Lupa’s heavy cast-iron pan wedged edgewise between her boots and was leaning her weight onto an iron stove-scraper with both hands. A curl of petrified, neon-orange persimmon-and-millet crust popped off the iron with a sound like a roof-slate splitting and skittered across the wet flagstones into the drain.

“Seasoned my foot, Cia, it’s vulcanized!” Amy called back, blowing a stray lock of pink hair out of her eyes. “I poured boiling lye-water in here when the drum went off and the water bounced! What did you put in the bottom of this, pine resin?”

“Olive oil, winter persimmon, and three hundred years of Ragunnesi culinary heritage,” Ciaccona replied with dignity, nursing a tin mug of black coffee in her right hand while her splinted left forefinger stuck straight out over the rim like a small white bowsprit. “And leave the earthenware platter on the shelf alone. The rest of the *tagliolini* is resting under a cloth. Anyone from south of the mountains knows a fruit-and-garlic reduction needs twenty-four hours for the bass notes to settle into the grain.”

Inside the main room of the third storehouse, Lupa paused in the act of dumping four double-fistfuls of chopped winter cabbage into her three-foot brass hotpot and let out a single, derisive snort.

“If that orange brick settles any harder, we can use it to chock the wagon wheels,” Lupa said, stirring the bubbling red mutton-broth from last night with her long iron ladle until the fat-pearls danced against the brass rim. “Nobody touch this burner before Tuesday. I’ve still got two pounds of marrow-bone at the bottom of this pot and half a bundle of kelp knots. Everything goes back in the middle.”

The morning sun had cleared the eastern karst teeth at a pale, frosty slant, throwing bright squares of window-light across the scrubbed pine table and the wreckage of last night’s feast. Outside in Huizhen Plaza, the first-day holiday padlocks had already come off the shop-staples; through the chink in the shutters came the distant *clang-clang* of the Fillet Fairmaiden’s iron poker knocking the cold ash out of her street braziers, the rumble of wooden barrow-wheels over the cobbles, and the sharp, sweet smell of fresh pine-shavings burning under the rice-cake steamers.

At the door-end of the pine bench, where the ninth place had been laid the night before, the extra earthenware bowl still sat untouched beside its aligned bamboo chopsticks. A thin, pale yellow film of chicken fat had set over the clear angelica broth in the night cold, with one red goji berry and a corner of browned rice-crust resting below the surface.

Amy came tramping back in from the scullery wiping her wet hands on her sheepskin vest, spotted the solitary bowl on the corner of the table, and hooked a thumb under its rim on her way to the stove.

“Hey, Lupa, can I tip the cold soup into the—”

On the back of Mei’s right wrist, where she sat on the low grain-sack tying her left boot-lace, the pale lines of her Tacet Mark flared gold.

Abby popped out into the sunbeam above the bench with his long white ears flat back and both front paws held out like a traffic warden.

“Hey! Hands off the porcelain!” Abby barked at top volume, hovering six inches in front of Amy’s nose. “That’s not leftovers! That’s the second bowl! Once you put the chopsticks down on the rim for the person behind, you can’t dump it back in the mutton pot! That’s terrible manners!”

“Nobody sat there all night, furball, the soup’s cold!” Amy protested, holding the bowl at chest height. “What am I supposed to do with it, leave it on the bench until it grows fur?”

“Put it on the porch wall where somebody can find it if they’re running late!” Abby ordered, folding his short arms over his chest before vanishing back into the golden gleam on Mei’s wrist with a sharp pop. “Honestly! Amateurs!”

Amy stood holding the cold earthenware bowl for a beat, looking down at the single red berry under the broth. Beside the water-butt, Chisa reached out, took the bowl and the chopsticks quietly out of Amy’s hands, walked three steps to the open front door, and set the bowl down on the flat, dry cap of the low stone wall just outside the threshold, laying the chopsticks neatly across the top.

At the cleared window-end of the pine table, Yangyang sat with her back straight and her sheer sky-blue cuffs pinned up an inch from her wrists with two small bone hair-pins so the silk wouldn’t dip into the ink-stone.

Before her on the scarred wood lay the sheet of plain mulberry paper she had started the morning before. Beneath the two neat lines about the frost on the cistern trough, the black columns of her small, upright characters now ran three-quarters of the way down the page. Beside her left elbow sat a porcelain saucer holding the last two of Amy’s candied hawthorns from the festival platter; every four or five lines, without taking her eyes off the wet tip of her brush, Yangyang reached over and tapped the brittle amber sugar on the edge of the saucer, breaking off a tiny sour red shard to put on her tongue while she thought of the next sentence.

Lupa came past the bench with a stoneware jar of salted garlic in the crook of her arm and leaned over Yangyang’s shoulder, squinting down at the wet ink.

“Put in there that I didn’t let the patient lift a teacup,” Lupa ordered, jabbing a scarred knuckle toward the bottom margin. “And tell that sister of yours if she holds back the Mengzhou winter-pepper barges past the fifteenth, I’m coming down the north road myself and eating her office curtains.”

“I told her your hotpot had nine things in it,” Yangyang said pleasantly, her brush moving without a pause down the column.

“Nine things inside one rim!” Lupa corrected at once. “Write ‘one rim’ so she doesn’t try to audit me!”

From the window-seat, Ciaccona leaned sideways until her red hair brushed the edge of the paper. “And give my warm regards to the soprano at the South Canal playhouse. Tell Suisui if the girl drops the fourth bar on the river-turn again next Saturday, she has my permission to throw an orange from the second box.”

“Don’t waste paper on opera singers, Watcher, tell her about my reed-thermometer!” Amy called over from her tool-crate by the hearth, holding up a greasy bronze bushing between two fingers. “Tell her I hit the hard-crack sugar stage by acoustic resonance without a single thermometer tube! She’ll put it in the Guild newsletter!”

Yangyang’s mouth curved up at the corner. She dipped the point of her brush into the brass ink-well, stroked the excess off against the lip, and wrote cleanly down the last column to the left edge:

*—we kept the first night around the long table, eight bowls and one by the door, and Cartethyia’s bread rose higher on the left than the right—*

She did not read the line out loud. She lifted her brush, blew once across the damp mulberry fiber, folded the sheet into a neat, narrow packet, and sealed the back flap with a dab of cold rice-paste from the saucer.

At the middle of the bench, Cartethyia was carefully wrapping a second, smaller braided ring-loaf—baked in the side-oven at dawn while she sat on a stool watching the coals so both shoulders came out even and brown—inside a clean linen napkin.

“Are we ready, Yangyang?” Cartethyia asked brightly, tucking the linen corners under the loaf and placing it on top of the willow basket where Old Mother Pu’s three washed stoneware jars sat packed around a flannel-wrapped pot of warm hen soup. “If we go down by the cistern steps, we can leave the soup with Mother Pu first, and then give the loaf to the cooper’s brother before the post-mule leaves the gate!”

“As soon as I get my cloak,” Yangyang said, rising from the bench and sliding her sealed letter into the dry inner pocket of her tunic—on the right side, away from the cold, flat square of Hsin’s Prism tucked inside her sash.

Down by the grain-sacks, Mei finished pulling the rawhide laces of her right boot snug, stood up on her own two feet without touching the pillar, and gave her dark jacket a brisk tug at the hem.

“Okay!” Mei announced, patting her pockets. “Everybody’s got their morning assignments! Amymy’s on turbine noise, Lupa’s guarding the soup, Yangyang and Carte are on post-office and neighbour duty, and I am walking all the way down the lane to the dyer’s porch and back.”

At the stove, Lupa spun round with the iron ladle pointed like a halberd. “The hell you are! There’s frost in the gutter by the cooper’s shed!”

“It’s forty paces on flat bluestone, Lupa, not a mountain pass!” Mei said, planting her hands on her hips. “Zero stairs. I’m going out the door, I’m walking to the big indigo vat on the dyer’s porch, I’m tapping the wood once with my knuckle, and I’m walking back on my own two feet without holding onto anybody’s sleeve.”

Beside the teapot, Iuno unhooked her heavy wool coat from the peg and swung it smoothly over her shoulders with her right hand.

“I am going with you,” Iuno said evenly.

“Ah—no arms!” Mei held up one forefinger. “If I lean on you, Lupa’s going to say it doesn’t count and make me sit on the salt-tub tomorrow!”

“I shall walk six inches to your left with my hand in my pocket,” Iuno said, buttoning her collar to the throat. “If you slip on the rime, I will catch you before your knee touches the stone, and you may complain about the rules afterward.”

*Five paces behind with a cushioned litter and four bearers would be far more civilized, darling,* the High Priestess remarked inside Iuno’s head, dry as autumn leaves. *Why we allow a woman so lately skewered like a lark to negotiate pavement distances is completely beyond me.*

*Because she tied her own boot,* Iuno answered silently, *and if we lock the door she will climb out the scullery window.*

Mei grinned, clearly taking Iuno’s silence for total ratification, and stepped toward the open threshold.

As she passed the end of the pine table where Yangyang was lifting the willow basket onto her right forearm, Mei’s step slowed for a beat.

Her golden eyes dropped once to the collar of Yangyang’s tunic—where the flat, unbleached edge of the linen rib-strapping showed white beneath the cerulean feathers—and flicked sideways to the window-seat, where Ciaccona was hooking her lute-strap over her shoulder with her splinted left forefinger held stiffly clear of the brass buckle.

Mei’s hand moved half an inch toward the handle of Yangyang’s basket, stopped before her fingers touched the willow, and tucked itself back into her own jacket pocket.

“Watch the wet spot by the rain-barrel, Yangyang,” Mei said lightly, stepping out onto the sunlit landing beside the ninth bowl on the wall. “Save me a date if Mother Pu tries to feed you!”

Outside on the gravel, Amy was already wrenching the wooden housing off the yard turbine with a screech of dry oak pegs; Cartethyia and Yangyang turned down the narrow cobbled steps toward the cistern lane with their basket between them; and Mei squared her shoulders, set her heels on the middle flagstone of the alley, and began walking toward the dyer’s porch at a slow, fiercely upright stroll with Iuno half a pace at her left elbow.

***

Old Mother Pu’s back door opened four inches on its iron chain before Yangyang’s knuckle had finished the second rap.

“Who’s that?” Mother Pu demanded through the gap, one sharp dark eye squinting out from beneath a blue-printed cotton head-cloth while a cloud of steamed turnip-greens and garlic billowed over the top of the door. “The cooper’s boys already got their sugar-cakes at dawn, I don’t give twice!”

“It’s Yangyang, Mother Pu,” Yangyang said, lifting the willow basket with both hands so the old woman could see the three scrubbed stoneware jars sitting inside. “I brought your root-jars back from yesterday. And we put a jar of the clear chicken soup in the middle while it was still hot.”

The iron chain rattled out of its slot and the pine door swung back. Mother Pu stood on her high threshold wiping her hands on a patched indigo apron, her gaze dropping straight from Yangyang’s forehead mark to the flannel-wrapped jar steaming in the basket.

“What did you waste good hen-broth on me for?” Mother Pu scolded immediately, though she reached into the basket with both knotted hands and lifted the warm soup-jar out against her apron. “I’ve got teeth, haven’t I? Chicken soup is for people who got knocked into the ditch! You should’ve drunk the bottom of the pot yourself, look at your wrists, thin as willow switches.”

“We had plenty for the whole table,” Yangyang said quickly, trying to take a step back down the flagstone before the old woman could turn around. “Thank you again for the angelica root—”

“Stand still where you are!” Mother Pu barked, hooking one foot behind the door to keep it open while she set the soup-jar on her meal-chest. “You think my jars come back across the sill full and go out empty on the second day of the moon? Wait till I get the ladle—”

“Oh, please don’t trouble yourself, ma’am, we also brought you a loaf!” Cartethyia piped up earnestly from Yangyang’s elbow, stepping forward to hold out the warm, napkin-wrapped ring-loaf in both palms like a squire presenting the keys of a citadel. “It’s an Egla Town parish braid with anise-seed and honey! The one I made yesterday looked a bit like a badger because the coals were on the left, so I sat in front of the door this morning and turned the sheet every five minutes!”

Mother Pu froze with one hand on her pantry latch. She stared down at the plump, golden-crusted foreign loaf in Cartethyia’s mittens, then up at the crystal thorns in Cartethyia’s hair, and let out a long, clicking breath through her teeth.

“Well, now you’ve done it,” Mother Pu told the lintel. “Bringing wheat-bread and honey to an old widow’s back door before noon.”

She marched into the dark of her pantry and came back thirty seconds later with her apron gathered up like a feed-sack.

Before Yangyang could get a word out, Mother Pu shoved a ten-pound green-striped winter squash straight into the bottom of the willow basket between the empty jars, wedged a fat paper twist of dried red jujubes beside it, and topped the pile with four squares of hickory-smoked bean-curd wrapped in dry lotus leaf and a small stoneware pot sealed with red wax.

“Mother Pu, we can’t carry off half your pantry for three slices of root,” Yangyang protested, her ears turning pink under her blue-black hair as the basket dragged at her forearm.

“The squash is from the second vine by the privy, it’ll rot if I don’t get it off the floor, and the little pot is salted plum-and-ginger paste for when your ribs ache in the damp,” Mother Pu said flatly, taking Cartethyia’s braided loaf out of her hands and nudging both of them back over the stone step with her hip. “Eat the bean-curd with hot oil. Mind the third step, the frost heaved the mortar.”

*Slam.* The heavy pine door banged shut and the iron chain slid back into its groove.

Yangyang stood in the narrow lane holding a basket that was now twice as heavy as when they had left the storehouse, looking helplessly at the bolted wood.

“She is very generous!” Cartethyia said happily, reaching over to take one handle of the willow basket so the ten-pound squash wouldn’t pull on Yangyang’s left side. “In Egla Town, Widow Berta used to chase Uncle Cosimo down the steps with a broom if he tried to leave an extra bun on her window-ledge, and then throw three cabbages into his hand-cart over the garden wall!”

They carried the basket between them down the broad stone risers toward the second terrace, where the rectangular stone basin of the cistern pond caught the morning sun.

Half a dozen village children in rolled-up trousers were crouching along the frosted coping with long split-bamboo poles, poking into the brown winter reeds at the water’s edge to fish out the sodden remains of last night’s paper fleet.

“That’s mine, Qiu Er, mine had the red thread tied round the middle!” a little girl in a hare-skin cap was insisting, grabbing a collapsed wad of butcher’s paper off her brother’s stick. “Give it here, the tallow stub’s still good!”

“Then whose is this one?” Old Tanner Qiu’s twelve-year-old grandson asked, leaning out over the corner bollard and lifting something out of the eddy by the overflow grate on the flat of his bamboo lath.

Yangyang and Cartethyia slowed their steps by the coping.

Sitting on the boy’s stick was a paper boat, four fingers long. None of the red or white butcher’s paper from the market stalls had gone into it: it was folded from heavy, unbleached yellow mulberry stock that had soaked in the freezing pond all night without coming uncreased or tearing at the corners, softened only to the dull, supple weight of old buckskin. Instead of the simple square wash-tub hulls the Hold’s children made, its stern was boxed high with a double-tucked rudder-slot and its prow was pinched twice into a narrow, upturned beak like the old flat-bottomed timber sampans that used to pole through the lower gorge rapids.

In the center of the floor-fold sat a tiny spiral of unbleached hemp cord dipped in tallow. The white tip of the wick did not have a speck of black char on it; it had never been touched by a flame.

“Did you fold that, San-bao?” Qiu Er asked the cooper’s small nephew, turning the yellow boat around on his stick.

The little boy shook his head, his thumb in his mouth. “My dad makes the square ones. You can’t fold the nose like that without tearing the paper.”

Cartethyia leaned over the stone rail, peering down at the neat, water-darkened creases with genuine admiration. “Whoever made it has very steady fingers! Perhaps it blew down from one of the upper porches before they could light their candle.”

Qiu Er shrugged, lifted the yellow boat off his bamboo lath without squeezing the wet hull, and set it carefully on the dry, sun-warmed corner of the stone bollard so the morning breeze could dry it in case its owner came looking. Then he went straight back to poking in the reeds for his tallow stubs.

Below the cistern steps, Huizhen Plaza was wide awake and full of white steam.

All along the tiered fronts, the heavy pine shop-shutters were unhooked and stacked in neat piles against the pillars, while above the open doors the hundreds of crimson paper eaves-lanterns and Mengzhou’s gold-leaf bat lanterns bobbed together in the sunny draft. Outside the eating-house, the Fillet Fairmaiden’s two great bronze cauldrons were boiling at a rolling gallop, throwing clouds of peppered carp-and-ginger steam across the flagstones as off-duty Jinzhou pikemen queued up with their wooden bowls.

Out of the narrow cobble alley behind the cooper’s shed strolled Buling.

She had a thick, brick-shaped bundle of freshly printed quarto pamphlets tied up in hemp twine tucked under her left arm, the cheap bamboo paper still smelling fiercely of Master Shao’s hot pine-soot ink and linseed oil, and her yellow forehead talisman was flipped jauntily up onto the round brim of her *nuanmao*.

“Morning, Watcher! Morning, little knight!” Buling called out cheerfully, stopping by the well-curb to peer into their willow basket. “Wah, Mother Pu’s winter squash. Very heavy. You carry that down the stairs, your spine going to curve like a shrimp.”

“We are taking the post down to the lower gatehouse first!” Cartethyia said. “Did Master Shao finish your books?”

Buling slapped the twine-bound bundle under her elbow with pride. “Two hundred copies, hot off the stone. Complete refutation of the central plains land-tax thread, plus three appendices on why the Mingting canal board doesn’t know how to dig a ditch. Here—” She slipped one damp pamphlet out from under the string and tucked it neatly beside the winter squash. “Complimentary copy for the storehouse. You give to Iuno to read after lunch, very relaxing for the digestion. Normally two coppers, for household free.”

“Thank you, Buling,” Yangyang said mildly. “Didn’t Ruoyun say she was meeting you at the eastern breach before noon?”

Buling’s red eyes rolled up toward the crown of her hat. “Noon got twelve bells, Watcher, which bell she didn’t specify. Besides, the mortar in the ditch is still frozen until the sun hits the east wall. I go up now, my shovel just bounces off the lime and makes a big noise.”

She gave the winter squash an approving pat and strolled off toward the rice-cake steamer with her pamphlets under her arm.

Across the wet bluestone paving, at the very end of the Fillet Fairmaiden’s counter where the steam from the second cauldron drifted thin against the stone wall, Suoming sat alone on a tall three-legged stool.

Her heavy iron-heeled boots hung six inches clear of the flagstones, and her long brown oil-paper umbrella stood wedged upright between her knees with the steel ferrule resting on the crossbar of the stool. She had a deep earthenware bowl of carp-dumpling broth in front of her and a porcelain spoon in her small right hand, eating steadily and without hurry while Ember banged a soup-ladle against the cauldron rim three feet away.

As Yangyang and Cartethyia walked past the well-head toward the head of the lower gatehouse stair, Suoming did not look up at their basket or turn her head toward the plaza.

Once—just as she lifted her spoon to blow on a dumpling—her pale, light-sensitive eyes shifted past the corner of the steamer shed and looked straight down the long stone flight of the lower gatehouse steps, where the road dropped away into the gorge mist. Then she ate the dumpling and went back to her bowl.

Down at the bottom of the stair, inside the vaulted stone shadow of the lower gatehouse, nobody was moving at all.

The heavy iron-strapped outer gate leaves stood barred across the arch. Over by the stone water-trough, the post-mule stood hitched to the iron ring in its brass-mounted leather harness, calmly chewing a wisp of hay while its two fat leather mail-panniers sat unbuckled on a wooden trestle beside the guardroom door.

A small crowd of twenty or thirty people had backed up onto the wet cobbles between the trough and the bottom risers: two peddlers with their pack-frames propped on staffs, three valley quarrymen who had come up for their sister’s dinner, and the cooper’s eldest brother and his wife bundled in their patched travelling coats, with their two small children sitting on the wife’s strapped willow hamper.

Hanging on its peg beside the guardroom doorframe was the garrison’s black slate notice-board. Across the slate, in fresh, thick white chalk, someone had written:

*LOWER POST-ROAD CLOSED FOR INSPECTION — POST AND TRAVELLERS WAIT FOR THE AFTERNOON BELL.*

“Come on, Warden Ge, it’s past the fifth hour already!” one of the peddlers was saying good-naturedly, leaning on his staff by the brazier. “My brother-in-law’s keeping the ferry-skiff waiting at the lower landing! What’s to inspect on four miles of stone switchbacks? Did a goat kick a pebble into the rut?”

Old Warden Ge stood squarely in front of the guardroom sill with his hands resting on the cross-piece of his ash pike, his breath puffing white and unhurried in the cold draft under the arch.

“Old Herdsman Luo came up from the sheep-fold at first light,” Ge said in his gravelly, reasonable voice, the third time he had explained it that hour. “Said the road below the second bend was wrong in the fog when he went down to check his wattles. Warden Liangyu sent Corporal Meng and three boys down at dawn to walk the cut before we let the mule or the children out on the grade. They’ll be back up the steps by noon.”

“Luo drank half a jar of plum-spirit at the tanner’s table last night, I saw him!” one of the quarrymen laughed, shifting his bundle on his shoulder. “He probably walked into the milestone in the dark and thought it was a bandit!”

“Then Meng’ll have a nice morning walk and you’ll be at the ferry before dark,” Ge said placidly. “Keep your boots out of the wheel-rut.”

Up on the raised stone footing at the left side of the inner arch, one of the four men in plain Ministry grey coats stood with his round felt hat tipped back an inch from his brow. He had a small brass folding rule in his right hand and a leather-bound pocket book open in his left, measuring the thickness of the iron hinge-pintle where it sat leaded into the black basalt pier.

As Yangyang and Cartethyia stepped past the bollard with their basket, Cartethyia’s wide skirt brushed near the stone footing; the man in the grey coat immediately folded his brass rule back against his palm, stepped half a pace into the recess of the arch to give them the dry flagstone, and inclined his head with quiet capital courtesy before returning his eyes to the iron pin.

Yangyang walked up to the guardroom door, where the heavy canvas outgoing mail-sack hung open on its iron hook just inside the dry jamb. Half a dozen holiday letters and red-paper greeting packets already lay in the bottom of the canvas.

Yangyang drew her sealed mulberry envelope out of her tunic, checked once that the rice-paste on the back flap had dried smooth and tight, and dropped it into the canvas sack.

Over by the low stone bench built into the gatehouse wall, the cooper’s eldest brother let out a long breath, unknotted the heavy blue-cloth bedding bundle from across his chest, and swung it down onto the dry corner of the stone bench with a soft *thump*.

“No sense standing here with forty pounds of quilt on my back till the drum goes,” he told his wife, wiping his forehead with his sleeve. “Leave the bundles by Warden Ge’s door and let’s take the boys back up to the well for a bowl of hot bean-curd while the water’s boiling.”

“Don’t let the mule chew the corner of the blue wrapper,” his wife told the older boy as she took the younger one by the mitten, and the four of them turned back up the broad stone risers toward the smell of the plaza cauldrons, leaving the blue-cloth bundle and the strapped willow hamper sitting side by side on the gatehouse bench behind them.

***

At midday, the sun stood straight above the eastern karst ridge, throwing a narrow, cold glare down into the forty-foot masonry gap of the eastern breach.

The masons’ picks and lime-hods lay stacked under a tarred canvas sheet for the holiday. Down at the bottom of the cut, where the garrison’s newer grey granite courses had been mortared onto the huge, square-dressed black basalt blocks of the mountain’s original foundation, Buling squatted on her heels in the gravel.

Her round black *nuanmao* was pulled low against the canyon draft, and she had the hem of her royal-blue silk cheongsam tucked tightly between her knees.

All along the lowest course of black basalt, two inches above the frozen mud of the footing trench, ran the line of twelve yellow mulberry-paper ward-slips she had brushed in red cinnabar and pasted across the foundation seams two nights before.

No rain had fallen since the first watch of the night before, and the grey granite blocks above the joint were bone-dry in the noon sun.

Every single one of the twelve yellow slips along the bottom seam had turned dark.

They were not scorched at the edges, and they had not peeled loose in the wind. From the center of each paper strip where it touched the joint between the black basalt blocks, a heavy, greasy grey-black stain had soaked outward through the mulberry fiber until the red cinnabar characters were drowned to the colour of old stove-soot, as if someone had dipped the stones in dirty ditch-water from underneath.

Buling did not touch her Terminal, and she did not hum her ferry tune.

With the thumbnails of both hands, she caught the top corner of the middle slip and peeled it slowly off the dry black stone. The flour-paste on the back hadn’t soured; it came away with a faint, dry *tick*, leaving a ghostly square of dark smudge on the basalt. She turned the strip over between her fingers and lifted it an inch toward her nose. It smelled of cold wet ash and iron filings.

Above her head, rapid leather soles clicked along the mason’s timber gangplank—*tap, tap, tap*—and Ruoyun stopped at the edge of the cut with a bundle of oak surveyor’s pegs under her ruffled white cuff.

“If you’re sitting in that trench pretending to check the plumb-line while the soup gets cold, I’m docking you three—”

Ruoyun’s voice cut off.

Behind her round brass spectacles, her bright blue eyes dropped to the row of grey-black rectangles along the bottom basalt course, and then to the stained strip held between Buling’s fingers.

Ruoyun did not make a joke about cheap paper. She set the bundle of oak pegs down on the gangplank, stepped straight down the rubble slope into the footing trench without looking at her shoes, and stopped beside Buling’s shoulder.

For five full breaths neither of them spoke. Down in the plaza behind the granary roofs, a child’s rattle clacked and a gong sounded twice outside the theatre alley; down here in the stone cut, only the gorge wind whistled through the dry joints.

Buling held the stained slip flat on her palm so Ruoyun could see the back of it.

“Dry stone,” Buling said, plain and fast, her voice pitched low under the wind. “No water in the ditch. Came straight up through the seam after the third watch.”

Ruoyun’s jaw set until a small muscle jumped below her cheekbone. She reached out with one finger, touched the dark, greasy smudge left on the face of the black basalt block, and rubbed the pad of her thumb against her forefinger.

“Scrape the whole bottom course clean with the iron trowel,” Ruoyun ordered, her voice flat and clipped. “Every slip from the corner buttress to the culvert grate. Lay down fresh yellow stock, double overlap across the vertical joints, and mix the cinnabar thick enough to stand a brush in. Do the ditch-side first before the shadow comes round the cliff.”

Buling’s chin came up by reflex. “Double overlap across forty feet is eighty slips! You know how much top-grade Chenzhou cinnabar costs a tael right now? I grind that much red stone by hand in this cold, my thumb going to fall off before the eighth bell—”

Even while she was complaining, her left hand had already dived into the canvas work-bag at her hip and pulled out a fresh, brick-thick stack of blank yellow mulberry slips, her heavy brass ink-box, and a flat iron mason’s scraper.

“Use the vermilion cake from the gatehouse locker and shut your mouth,” Ruoyun said, dropping to one knee on the frozen gravel beside her and pulling the iron scraper out of Buling’s fingers to attack the first stained slip herself. *Scritch-scritch.* The blackened paper came off the basalt in stiff gray curls.

Buling uncapped her brass ink-box, spat a drop of hot tea from her flask onto the red stone block, and began grinding the cinnabar stick in fast, tight circles.

She watched Ruoyun’s white cuff moving back and forth with the iron blade against the black foundation stone.

“Ruoyun,” Buling said quietly over the scrape of the iron. “This kind of dirt don’t come out of a ditch seam unless the big lid underneath is slipping. Up at the top of the stairs—you think she knows the bottom course is leaking like this, or should we tell Shifu before we paste over—”

“Mind your ink-stone, you’re splashing red on my skirt!” Ruoyun snapped, so sharp it bounced off the granite wall. She didn’t look up from the joint she was scraping, driving the edge of the iron trowel hard into the corner bevel until a spark hopped off the basalt. “The Paragon has the upper rock to watch, and I’m not having half the Hold staring into this ditch because we left dirty paper on the wall over the festival. Give me the brush.”

Buling looked at the side of Ruoyun’s face—at the healed notch in her left eyebrow and the rigid line of her neck below her small golden horns—and closed her mouth.

Before she picked up the blank stack of yellow paper, Buling took the first grey-black slip she had peeled off the stone, folded it neatly in four with the soot-stained side turned inward, and slid it deep into the inner pocket of her royal-blue sleeve. Then she dipped the wide bristle brush into the thick red paste and slapped the first fresh ward squarely across the seam.

***

Up under the high cedar eaves of the inner gatehouse watch gallery, the early afternoon light lay pale and clear across the nineteen lines of the kaya-wood weiqi board.

Yangyang sat on the pine bench three paces down the rail with her wool cloak tucked around her knees and her back rested lightly against the corner pillar. Liangyu had put her on the midday gallery watch so she wouldn’t have to carry a pike on the stairs before her rib-strapping came off tomorrow; from the open timber embrasure, the whole steep drop of the lower Hold and the first two white bends of the southern post-road lay open below her boots.

Beside the corner embrasure stood Ruoyun, her ruffled white cuffs spotless and a smear of dried red cinnabar no bigger than a pinhead on the side of her right thumb. Across the stone cap sat Qingxiao, the indigo storehouse oilcloth folded neatly over her moon-white lap.

Neither of them had hidden the board when Yangyang came up the stair.

On the dark wood sat the nine matte-black handicap stones on the star-points, the single white clam-shell stone Qingxiao had played on the fourth-line corner two nights ago, and—resting directly beneath the white stone on the third-line shoulder point, one intersection closer to the edge—a tenth matte-black slate stone.

“It was there when we came up at the third watch,” Qingxiao had told Yangyang plainly when Yangyang glanced at the grid.

Now Ruoyun stood with her arms folded tight across her chest, glaring down through her round brass spectacles at the tenth black stone as if she could blister the lacquer off the wood.

“Put the lid back on the bowl, A-Qing,” Ruoyun said, her fast barracks voice hard and sour in the quiet gallery. “You don’t answer a move when the other player won’t even walk up the stairs and sit on the bench. If someone wants a game of stones, she can come out and show her nose in the daylight instead of sneaking around my embrasure in the dark like a rat with a walnut.”

Qingxiao did not look up from the grid.

She reached into the carved wooden bowl beside her knee, took a second white clam-shell stone loosely between the index and middle fingers of her right hand, and held it poised for a breath above the fourth line—stepping one intersection sideways along the side star-line, declining to press the black stone down against the rail and giving both stones room to breathe on the open board.

*Click.*

The white shell stone settled cleanly against the kaya wood.

Ruoyun let out a sharp, disgusted breath through her nose, turned her back on the embrasure, and leaned both elbows on the outer parapet coping to stare down at the market roofs.

Qingxiao left the wooden lid off the white bowl and drew her bare right hand back under the camphor-scented oilcloth. When she breathed out, a thin wisp of white steam curled across her chin in the gallery draft.

She turned her head slightly and looked down the pine bench at Yangyang.

“The rice crust last night,” Qingxiao said in her level voice. “It stayed crisp.”

Yangyang blinked, straightening half an inch against the pillar. “Mei broke that corner off before she poured the mushroom gravy into the iron pan. She kept it under a teacup by the kettle so the steam wouldn’t soften it.”

“Mm,” Qingxiao said. Her pale blue eyes rested on the small earthenware flask of hot ginger-water sitting beside Yangyang’s mitten on the bench. “And the clear soup at the top house had too much salt. What did you put in yours after the ginger?”

“A slice of Mother Pu’s angelica root from the south slope, and six red dates,” Yangyang said, a small, warm pleasure touching the corners of her mouth. “If you put the angelica in while the water is boiling hard, it turns bitter, so you have to wait until the fire is banked down to red ash.”

“Banked down,” Qingxiao repeated, as if filing a sword-form away behind her brow. She pulled the heavy indigo oilcloth half an inch higher over her collarbone against the wind coming off the karst peaks. “The draft up here is sharper after noon than at the third watch.”

Before Yangyang could answer, the great bronze bell in the drum-tower across Huizhen Plaza swung back on its oak beam and struck the first hour after noon.

*BONG.*

The deep, humming bronze note rolled out across the wet slate roofs, echoing off the cliff face behind the Fogveil Pagoda and dying away down the canyon.

Down at the lower gatehouse at the foot of the stairs, the small crowd of waiting travellers stirred at once. Across the cobbles, the cooper’s eldest brother came walking down the steps from the plaza wiping his mouth, while the two peddlers hoisted their pack-frames onto their shoulders and looked expectantly toward the barred outer arch.

Yangyang turned her face toward the open southern gulf beyond the gallery rail.

Far below the barbican wall, the post-road dropped in long, stone-paved zigzags down the face of the limestone cliff before vanishing into the thick white belly of the river fog above the second bend. For ten seconds nothing moved on the wet grey ribbon of stone.

Then, out of the upper edge of the fog-bank a quarter-mile down the grade, three small dark figures came walking up the switchback.

They were not marching in step. The lead man in the leather-backed garrison coat had two ash spears slung across his back instead of one, and the two young Wardens behind him were leaning into the steep uphill pitch with their hands on their knees at the turn.

Behind them, the white mist over the roadbed stayed empty.

Yangyang stood up from the pine bench, her hand resting briefly against her strapped left ribs as she leaned out over the stone coping.

“Paragon,” Yangyang said quietly. “They’re coming up the last bend. There are only three of them.”

***

By the time Corporal Meng and the two young spearmen stumbled under the stone vault of the lower barbican, Liangyu was already standing three paces inside the unbarred wicket gate with her iron vambrace strapped to her left forearm.

Old Warden Ge kicked the guardroom door wide, snatched a tin dipper of hot ginger-water off the brazier, and shoved it into the Corporal’s shaking hands, while up the inner stair came the quiet step of Qingxiao’s boots, with Ruoyun and Yangyang right behind her.

The twenty waiting travellers by the water-trough pressed forward half a step and stopped as Liangyu held up one flat palm without looking back.

“Sit on the bollard, Meng,” Liangyu said, her voice low and steady under the arch. “Where’s Little Tan?”

Corporal Meng didn’t sit. His face was the colour of wet tallow, his moustache crusted with white rime from the gorge, and his chest heaved under his laminar coat as he handed Tan’s spare ash spear to Ge.

“Below the second bend, Warden,” Meng got out between breaths, before taking a scalding gulp from the dipper. “He isn’t hurt—we heard his horn twice after we came out. We couldn’t get back to him.”

“Did the shale slip across the cut?” Ge asked, frowning down the road.

“No rockslide,” the younger spearman beside Meng burst out, his voice cracking high before he swallowed and looked at Liangyu’s boots. “Warden, there’s a wall across the road.”

In the shadow of the inner arch, the man in the plain Ministry grey coat stopped writing in his leather pocket-book and lowered his pencil to his side.

“Say that straight, Boyang,” Liangyu ordered.

“Right where the post-road rounds the limestone shoulder above the gorge cut,” Corporal Meng said, wiping his mouth with the back of his wrist and speaking fast and exact, the way he gave a night-watch report. “We walked down through the mist—visibility about thirty paces—and right past the second milestone, the cut was blocked from cliff to drop. Not loose rubble. Dressed black stone, three feet square to the block, laid dry without a smear of mortar. Twenty feet high, with a round-arched sally-port in the middle and an iron portcullis grating jammed halfway down in the slot.”

Around the water-trough, the waiting quarrymen and the cooper’s brother stared at him in dead silence.

“I’ve walked that post-road with the salt-mules since I was nine years old,” the cooper’s brother muttered from the bench. “There’s nothing in that cut but a goat-wall and two pine trees.”

“I put my palm on it,” Meng said flatly, looking at the man. “Solid stone, cold enough to burn the skin through a gauntlet, and thick black frost packed into every joint. Our orders were to check the grade to the third marker, and the sally-port was open four feet under the iron teeth, so I took the file through in single column.”

Meng’s throat worked as he looked back at Liangyu.

“On the far side, the road went on downhill through the fog just like always,” Meng said. “Same ruts, same river noise on the left. We walked ten minutes down the grade, and right by my right knee was the second-bend milestone again—the exact same granite post with the chipped top corner we’d passed twenty yards uphill of the wall. I called a halt and turned the file around to march back out.”

“Little Tan was point-man on the turn, four paces ahead of me where the limestone bulges out,” Boyang said, his hands knotted tight around his spear-shaft. “He stepped round the rock corner in the fog. We stepped round it two seconds behind his heels—and we walked straight out from under the iron portcullis onto the *uphill* side of the black wall, facing the Hold.”

Nobody by the gatehouse steps made a sound.

“We heard his brass watch-horn blow three short blasts right behind the stone,” Meng said quietly. “I took Boyang and went back under the grating twice with our spears linked. Both times, ten paces into the mist on the far side turned us right back out the uphill arch with our noses pointing up the mountain. He’s sitting on the far side of that wall right now.”

At the foot of the inner stair, Ruoyun stepped past Warden Ge’s elbow.

She didn’t ask about the milestone or the fog. Her chin was up and her blue eyes were fixed on the heavy pigskin gauntlets tucked under Corporal Meng’s belt.

“The frost in the dry-stone joints,” Ruoyun said in her fast, flat voice. “When you braced your hand on the sally-port arch to duck under the iron teeth, did it melt on your leather or come off dry?”

Corporal Meng blinked at the horned lady in the teal dress, reached down to his belt, and pulled out his right gauntlet.

He turned the palm upward in the cold light of the gateway.

Across the wet yellow pigskin of the fingers and thumb, where he had gripped the stone bevel of the arch, sat a thick, greasy smear of grey-black—not melted ice, and not red gorge clay, but dark and sticky as wet chimney-soot.

Ruoyun looked down at the blackened palm of the glove for the space of two heartbeats.

Her mouth shut in a thin, white line. She did not ask a second question, and she did not look at Qingxiao. She turned sharply on the heel of her Mary Jane shoe and walked straight back up the inner stone stair toward the upper tiers without a word.

Beside the brazier, Qingxiao looked from the grey-black smudge on the Corporal’s leather gauntlet to Liangyu’s face.

“That is the outer curtain of Yuan Fortress,” Qingxiao said in her level, unornamented voice. “It should not be standing.”

A faint, sharp intake of breath ran through the older Wardens by the guardroom door. Every person born in Xuanfang Hold knew the name carved into the granite cenotaph behind the persimmon trees, and every Warden knew the fortress had lain in broken timber and drowned shale ten miles north since the war two centuries ago.

Liangyu’s bruised left wrist twitched once against her spear-shaft. She didn’t look up at the stalled Astrolabe Core in the sky, and she didn’t ask how a two-hundred-year-old wall had walked south into her gorge cut.

“Ge,” Liangyu barked, her voice cutting straight through the murmur by the trough. “Get the three-hundred-fathom coil of one-inch hemp capstan-line out of the winch-shed and four horn lanterns filled to the wick. A road can turn around in the mist all it likes; a dry hemp rope tied to an iron piton above the bend only has two ends.”

“Right away, Warden,” Ge said, already turning toward the storehouse rack.

“No pike company,” Liangyu said, looking round at her men and then straight at Yangyang on the bottom step. “If ten men walk under that grate together, ten men get turned around. Small party on foot: myself or Ge, two heavy blades in case there’s something in the fog keeping that stone cold, someone strong enough to hold the uphill hawser taut at the iron grate so the arch can’t close behind us, and the Watcher’s ears to find where Tan’s horn is coming from. Two blasts on the brass horn means we haul back on the rope, and everybody is back inside this barbican before the sun drops behind the west crag, no matter what.”

“I will take the rope at the gate, Warden!” Cartethyia said at once, setting her side of Mother Pu’s basket down on the bench and standing straight as a lance. “I am very good at holding onto things!”

“And I’ll take the point-pike,” old Ge said firmly, emerging from the shed with the massive coil of braided hemp slung over his shoulder and barring Liangyu’s path with a look. “You’ve got two hundred people inside these walls and the Mengzhou clerks on the stairs, Warden. If the south gate’s shut, the commander stays on the lock. I know every bush on that second bend with my eyes shut.”

Liangyu’s jaw tightened for a second; then she nodded once, sharp and clean.

Beside the pillar, Qingxiao rested two fingers lightly on the hilt at her waist.

“I will go by the upper air,” Qingxiao said, “and look from above the mist.”

Nobody argued with the Sword Transcendent.

Halfway up the steps toward the third terrace, Amy—who had come trotting down the lane with her iron turbine-wrench still in her hand when she saw the patrol come in—was already sprinting back up the risers two at a time to fetch Lupa and Chisa.

Ten minutes later, outside the open door of the third storehouse, Lupa was buckling her leather gauntlet-straps with her teeth while Chisa checked the hinge-pin of her giant scissor-blade on the step.

Mei stood right in the middle of the threshold with both booted feet planted wide on the sill and her hands shoved deep into her jacket pockets.

She looked down the steep, four-mile drop of the canyon stairs below the plaza, looked at Lupa’s spear, and let out a single, flat breath through her nose.

“Four miles of switchbacks,” Mei said to the doorframe. “And if I pop the middle seam on the climb back up, Lupa has to carry me and Little Tan at the same time.”

“Damn right I would, and I’d complain the whole four miles and throw you in the horse-trough at the top,” Lupa said roughly, settling her ash spear across her shoulder and giving Mei’s shoulder a solid, flat-palmed tap on her way down the step. “Keep the fire hot, Deadbeat. Boy’s gonna be frozen stiff by the time we haul him out of that ditch.”

Mei didn’t make a joke about frogs or willow hampers. She turned straight back into the storehouse before they had cleared the bottom riser.

“Wen!” Mei called out, her voice brisk and clear across the room. “Help me slide the low pine bench right up against the front of the brick hob, and let’s put four of the flat masonry bricks into the side-oven to heat up for his feet! Iuiu, have we still got that dry bolt of unbleached flannel in the Huaxu crate?”

“Under the condenser coils,” Iuno said, already lifting the crate lid with her right hand and drawing out the folded wool before she turned back toward the door to join Liangyu on the lower wall. “Do not lift the iron kettle yourself when it boils; use the long dipper from the barrel.”

“Long dipper, got it!” Mei stepped back to the doorway and leaned out over the low stone wall toward the cooper’s yard next door, where Qiu Er was stacking split birch for the cooper. “Hey, Qiu Er! Run down to the weaver’s alley behind the well—find Widow Tan at the third loom and tell her Warden Ge and my girls are going down to the second bend right now to bring Little Tan up! Tell her don’t go stand in the wind at the gatehouse, come straight up here to our stove where the water’s hot!”

***

From the parapet walk above the lower barbican, the post-road showed for two bends before the fog took it.

Iuno came up the inner stair one step at a time, her right hand on the cold rope-rail, and stopped beside Liangyu at the embrasure. The party had already gone round the first switchback. All that was left of them on the grey stone below was a dark line of boot-prints in the rime, and then the white.

Liangyu did not look round. She stood with both hands flat on the coping, the left one still held a little stiffly where the splint had come off, her eyes on the place where the road went into the mist.

“I asked the gentleman in the grey coat what his book says about the south road,” Iuno said.

“And?”

“His orders cover the arch and the pier. He has measured the hinge-pins three times since the carts came up, and they are exactly where they were.” Iuno rested her hand on the stone beside Liangyu’s. “He was quite sorry not to be more useful. I believe him.”

Liangyu breathed out through her nose. “Then he’s the only man in the Hold who’s sure of anything.”

Down by the water-trough, the crowd had not gone home. The two peddlers had sat down on their pack-frames. The cooper’s brother stood by the bench with one hand resting on his blue bundle, as if it might wander off. Someone’s grandmother had carried a kettle down from the plaza, and the quarrymen were passing cups along the bottom step.

“What do you need,” Iuno said, “if they are not back by dark?”

Liangyu was quiet for long enough that Iuno thought she would not answer.

“They’ll be back by dark,” Liangyu said. “I gave the order.” Then, more slowly: “Blankets for the granary shed. There are four valley families sleeping on sacks down there who were meant to be home tonight. And someone to tell them something true that isn’t *wait*. I’ve said wait to them three times today, and Ge’s said it more.”

“I can do that.”

“I know you can.” The corner of Liangyu’s mouth moved. “That’s why I’m asking you and not the Ministry.”

The wind came up the cut, lifted the edge of Iuno’s empty sleeve, and went down again.

“The name the Paragon gave it,” Iuno said. “It meant something to you.”

“It’s on the first page of the roll.” Liangyu took her right hand off the coping and put it back in the same place. “You read the first page aloud in the guardroom the day you take the vambrace, and the sergeant tells you to stand up straighter.” She looked down at the cups going along the step. “Nobody ever said it might come back.”

Iuno had nothing to give her for that, and did not try. She stayed at Liangyu’s shoulder, close enough that their sleeves touched on the cold stone, and watched the fog with her.

Far down in the white, a horn sounded: one short, flat note, muffled almost to nothing.

Liangyu’s hands tightened on the coping.

“One is a call,” she said. “Two is come home.”

No second note came. After a moment, faint and thin under the river noise, another horn answered the first, from somewhere lower down and off to the left, where the road didn’t go.

Liangyu let go of the wall.

“Granary shed,” she said. “Before they start asking me again.”

Iuno inclined her head and went down the stair toward the kettle and the cups, and the families on the bottom step looked up as she came.

***

All the way down the first mile of the southern post-road, the mountain looked exactly the way it had all week.

Warden Ge led the file at a long, ground-eating mountain stride with his iron-shod pike in his right hand and the heavy coil of one-inch hemp capstan-line bouncing against his pack-frame. Behind him walked Lupa with her wolf ears swiveling toward the cliff-wall, Chisa with her cased scissor-blade slung across her back, Cartethyia carrying two lit horn lanterns, and Yangyang at the rear with her wool cloak belted snug over her strapped ribs.

To their left, three hundred feet down the sheer limestone drop, the river rushed white and loud over the gorge boulders under a low belly of grey afternoon mist. Right beside their boots ran the deep, familiar mud-ruts where the Jinzhou supply drays had ground up the hill four days ago; at the first switchback stood the stone-walled herdsman’s shelter with a dry twist of hay still hanging from the sheep-rack and the first granite milestone standing square and mossy in the turf.

Then the road dropped into the limestone cut at the second bend, and the wind died out of their faces.

Thirty paces ahead of Ge’s boots, wedged straight across the roadbed from the vertical limestone cliff on the right to the crumbling lip of the river gorge on the left, stood the wall.

It had not been built out of the grey quarry-stone of Xuanfang Hold. The blocks were massive, four-foot cubes of dead-matte black basalt, dressed so true with the chisel that a knife-blade could not have been slipped between the courses, and stacked twenty feet high without a single trowel of lime mortar. Along every horizontal and vertical joint, squeezing outward from the dark seams between the stones, sat a three-finger ridge of dull, greasy grey-black frost. Even ten yards uphill, the air coming off it smelled of wet char, old iron, and snow that had lain in a cellar for a winter.

In the center of the curtain sat a low, round-arched sally-port no wider than a mule-cart. Its heavy iron portcullis grating had dropped crookedly in its stone grooves long ago, jamming four feet above the roadbed so a row of rusted, square-forged iron spikes hung level with a tall man’s chest.

Lupa stopped two paces back from the arch, her tail bristling once against her calf as she squinted up at the black parapet lost in the mist.

“Well,” Lupa muttered, spitting into the gravel. “Corporal wasn’t drunk. That’s an ugly piece of rock.”

Warden Ge didn’t waste a breath looking up. He stepped back thirty paces uphill to where the second-bend granite milestone—the one with the chipped left corner—stood solidly in the bank above the cut. Dropping his coil, Ge passed the bitter end of the one-inch hemp hawser three times around the base of the granite post, tied it off in a double capstan hitch that could have held a river-barge in a flood, and paid the heavy rope out down the middle of the ruts to the sally-port.

Cartethyia stepped up to the threshold of the black stone arch and set both horn lanterns down by her heels.

She did not call on her tall form; in the narrow, four-foot clearance beneath the rusted iron teeth of the portcullis, nine feet of height would only have fouled the grating. Instead, as small as she was, Cartethyia planted her heavy boots wide on the dry-stone sill right between the two walls of the arch, wrapped the thick hemp hawser twice around both of her forearms, and locked her hands over the line at her waist.

When Ge leaned his full weight against the rope from the far side to test the purchase, Cartethyia’s heels did not slide a hair’s breadth on the stone, and her small shoulders stayed as square and immovable as the basalt pier beside her.

“Go on, Warden Ge!” Cartethyia called clearly under the iron spikes. “Nothing is going to pull this line out of my hands!”

Ge, Lupa, Chisa, and Yangyang ducked one by one under the grey-black frost of the portcullis teeth and stepped out onto the downhill side of the wall, each of them keeping one hand resting lightly on the hemp rope as Ge paid the coil out off his shoulder.

Beyond the arch, the gorge mist hung thick and wool-grey between the cliff and the drop.

For twenty paces the roadbed sloped downward under their boots as ordinary gravel and shale. Then the limestone wall on their right seemed to lean gradually inward through the fog, and thirty paces out from the sally-port—right beside Lupa’s left shin—stood the granite second-bend milestone with the chipped left corner.

Cartethyia’s hemp rope ran straight past the stone post in the gravel without being tied to it, stretching back behind their heels into the white mist toward the arch they had just left.

“Don’t let go of the hemp,” Ge said quietly, his thumb on his brass watch-horn. He lifted the mouthpiece to his lips and blew one short, flat note.

For three heartbeats the fog muffled the brass ring.

Then, muffled and thin, a single shaky blast of a watch-horn answered, followed immediately by a boy’s cracked shout:

“Corporal Meng! Down here! Don’t walk past the corner again!”

Lupa turned her head toward the road ahead. “Sounds like he’s fifty yards down the switchback.”

Beside her on the gravel, Yangyang stood very still, her left hand resting on the taut hemp hawser and her eyes half-closed beneath her blue forehead mark.

She tuned out the heavy rush of the river three hundred feet below, the creak of the hemp fibers in Cartethyia’s grip behind them, and the blood beating against her own strapped ribs—listening only for the quick, ragged catch of a nineteen-year-old boy’s lungs in the cold.

Yangyang opened her eyes and pointed her left hand straight off the left-hand verge of the post-road, out toward the sheer drop of the canyon where nothing but open sky and river-mist belonged.

“Don’t go down the road,” Yangyang said in her plain, exact voice. “He isn’t on the switchback. He’s forty paces out to the left, behind the wall.”

Ge frowned into the fog above the cliff-edge. “Girl, forty paces left of this verge is a three-hundred-foot drop into the river.”

“There is stone under the fog there,” Yangyang said. “And he’s breathing behind it.”

High above their heads—three hundred feet up where the grey roof of the gorge mist thinned out into the pale, freezing afternoon sky—Qingxiao stood balanced on the flat of her flying sword.

From the upper air, the twenty-foot plug of black basalt across the post-road was not a short wall in a rock cut at all.

Running straight out from the eastern limestone cliff, spanning the entire width of the river gorge on massive, unfallen stone piers that had been rubble for two hundred winters, stood half a mile of the old outer curtain wall of Yuan Fortress, its battlements black and whole against the white vapour and its far western bastion disappearing into the thick cloud-bank on the opposite mountain.

Directly below the tip of Qingxiao’s sword, on the wide crenellated walk atop the curtain wall, a three-legged iron watch-brazier stood burning in the middle of the afternoon. Heavy, oil-fed orange flames licked up through the iron ribs, sending a thin, crooked ribbon of black smoke curling into the cold air. Nobody stood beside the brazier, and no footprints marked the grey-black frost along the parapet walk.

Qingxiao shifted her weight half an inch forward on the blade and angled down the gorge toward the lower ferry to pass the rampart on the south side.

The freezing upper wind streamed past her cloud-pale horns and snapped the corners of the indigo oilcloth at her shoulders for twenty long breaths. Then the white mist parted beneath her hem—and directly below her boots lay the uphill roof of the sally-port again, approaching from the north, with the small glint of Cartethyia’s crystal laurel shining in the arch and the hemp rope stretching back up toward Xuanfang Hold.

Qingxiao banked her sword once more, cutting high and wide to the east against the sheer limestone face of the mountain. Ten breaths in the grey cloud, and the same burning iron brazier slid back beneath her left heel from behind.

She did not descend to the brazier.

Down at the base of the black curtain wall, forty yards west of the sally-port where the old masonry hung out over the gorge drop on a shelf of wet black shale, she saw the four small figures step off the post-road verge along the taut hemp line—and right behind the buckled inner casemate wall, where the grey-black frost lay thickest in the ditch, a massive, six-limbed mass of condensed black frequency tore itself loose from the stone and surged toward the gap.

Qingxiao tilted the point of her sword straight down and dropped through the mist like a falling iron weight.

Down on the shale shelf off the road-verge, Warden Ge had tied the bitter end of the hemp coil around his own waist so they wouldn’t lose the line. Ahead of their boots, the inner face of the black fortress wall had buckled inward into a ruined stone casemate, its doorway choked chest-high with three tons of fallen basalt lintels and a two-inch iron tie-rod twisted across the gap like a snare.

From inside the dark hole behind the rubble came Little Tan’s voice, coughing hard in the cold: “Warden Ge? Is that the lantern? Watch the ditch on your left, the frost in there is moving—”

Chisa stepped onto the wet shale in front of the choked doorway and unlatched her giant scissor-blade from her back.

She did not pry at the stones. Looking once at the grain of the wedged basalt keystone and the rusted iron tie-rod pinning the pile together, Chisa raised the heavy dark blades two-handed and made a single, clean diagonal cut from top right to bottom left.

*SHING—CLANG.*

The two-inch iron tie-rod parted like wet hemp, and the three-ton basalt keystone split cleanly down the middle into two smooth halves that slid outward into the gravel, opening a three-foot doorway into the dark casemate.

Before the stone dust had settled on Chisa’s boots, the grey-black frost in the outer ditch behind the wall exploded upward.

Out of the mist lunged a hulking, six-limbed siege Discord—ten feet at the shoulder, plated in jagged slabs of black fortress-iron fused to its hide, its split chest-cavity glowing dull furnace-red and its two front cleaver-limbs sweeping inward to shear Chisa off the rubble heap.

“Back!” Lupa roared, her spear already coming down in an arc of red-gold flame.

Before the first cleaver-limb could cross the lintel, the grey mist overhead tore open.

Five flying blades of moon-white Sword Intent dropped vertical out of the sky—*chak-chak-chak-chak-chak*—and pinned the Discord’s left cleaver-arm straight into the basalt footing to the hilt. Half a heartbeat behind them, Qingxiao landed lightly on the split keystone in a swirl of moon-white silk, the bare dao in her right hand meeting the downward smash of the creature’s right cleaver with a ringing *CLANG* that threw white sparks across the wet rock.

The shock of the blow drove the split stone an inch deeper into the shale, and the Discord reared back on its four rear legs to bring its furnace-core crashing down onto the casemate roof where Tan was pinned.

Lupa didn’t give it the room. With an arena grin splitting her face and her wolf-tail lashing straight out behind her, Lupa vaulted onto the left half of the keystone right beside Qingxiao’s elbow, planted her re-nailed boot-heel against the rock bevel, and drove the blazing point and cross-lug of her ash spear squarely into the center of the Discord’s iron breastplate.

“Hold the left, Paragon!” Lupa barked as the two-ton weight of the creature hit her shaft and howling flame roared up around its throat, her shoulder-muscles bunching hard under her tunic to keep the monster’s chest levered back off the casemate opening.

Warden Ge dropped to one knee in the wet shale at Lupa’s right hip, braced the butt of his long garrison pike against his boot-welts, and rammed the iron head six inches deep into the unarmored joint of the Discord’s right rear leg, twisting the ash shaft with both scarred hands to lock its heel in the mud.

Forty yards back at the sally-port arch, the backwash of the Discord’s frequency hit the roadbed like a ground-swell. The grey mist spun backwards, and the twenty-foot black wall gave a low, grinding shudder as the space between the milestones tried to snap shut on the party.

Under the rusted iron teeth of the portcullis, Cartethyia’s boots grated half an inch across the stone sill as the hemp hawser jerked hard enough to smoke against her sleeves.

“Oh no you don’t!” Cartethyia gasped, setting her back flat against the freezing black basalt jamb and digging her heels into the joint between the blocks, her knuckles white and her jaw set hard beneath her crystal laurel as she held the one-inch rope bar-taut between the two sides of the mountain.

Back on the road-verge where the line turned onto the shale, Yangyang stayed out of the blade-arc as ordered, her right hand locked on the vibrating hemp rope to guide the slack and her left forearm pressed against her strapped ribs. Through the clanging roar of iron and the screech of the Discord, she pitched her voice clear and level into the dark hole of the casemate:

“Little Tan! Keep talking to me! Tell me what your hand is touching!”

“My—my spear-butt and a broken water-jar, Watcher!” Tan’s voice came shaking back out of the dark, keeping its bearing on her call. “I’m right by the door!”

Up on the rubble heap, the Discord wrenched its left forelimb sideways with a shriek of tearing iron, snapping three of the moon-white blades in the stone and swinging the jagged cleaver around at knee-height toward Ge’s unprotected side.

Chisa stepped across the wet basalt slab without a sound, dropped the open jaws of her giant scissor-blade around the thick black tendon at the creature’s shoulder-socket, and slammed the handles together with all her weight.

*CRUNCH.*

The heavy cleaver-arm dropped severed into the gravel. In the same breath, Lupa let out a single short bark, wrenched her flaming spear free, and punched the white-hot iron point straight through the cracked furnace-grate of the breastplate, while beside her Qingxiao turned her bare right wrist and drew one horizontal stroke of moon-white light through the glowing core from left to right.

The red furnace inside the ribs went black. With a long, hissing sigh like water dumped on hot slag, the ten-foot hull broke apart into heaps of cold grey soot and rusted scrap that rattled harmlessly down the shale into the ditch.

Lupa didn’t wait for the soot to clear. She grounded her spear, ducked headfirst through the three-foot gap Chisa had cut, and came backing out ten seconds later hauling Little Tan by the belt and collar before swinging him up across her broad shoulders like a wet calf.

“Got him!” Lupa called out, adjusting Tan’s mud-caked shins under her left arm while Chisa picked up the boy’s dropped ash spear. “Ankle’s blown up like a turnip, everything else works!”

Little Tan hung over Lupa’s shoulder with his teeth chattering so hard he bit his own words in half, his face chalk-white under his crooked helmet and his ears burning bright red through the blue chill.

“I’m sorry, Warden Ge, I can walk if you give me my shaft—” Tan stammered, trying futilely to push himself off Lupa’s wool cloak. “I only went three steps round the rock to check the culvert, and all of a sudden the river noise jumped over to my right ear instead of my left! And when I looked up, there was a big red fire burning in an iron basket right on top of the wall in the middle of the day! I yelled up at the sentry twice to throw down a rope, and nobody looked over the stones, and then the gravel slid out from under my heel—”

“Save your wind, boy, you weigh less than a sack of Jinzhou millet,” Lupa grunted, turning back toward the road-verge.

Behind them on the wet shale shelf, the heaps of grey-black soot where the Discord had fallen were already dissolving into the mist.

And thirty paces uphill across the narrow limestone cut, the twenty-foot curtain of dry black basalt still stood solid and unbroken from cliff to gorge, the greasy dark frost untouched in its seams and the rusted iron teeth of the portcullis hanging four feet above the gravel.

They walked back along the taut hemp hawser in single file—Yangyang first, then Lupa carrying Tan, Chisa with the two spears, and Qingxiao and Ge closing the rear—and ducked back out under the iron grating onto the uphill side of the pass.

As soon as Ge’s heel cleared the stone sill, Cartethyia let out a huge, whistling breath and unwound the red, rope-creased turns of hemp from her forearms, flexing her stiff fingers in the cold air.

Warden Ge unknotted the hawser from the granite milestone, coiled the wet rope over his elbow in slow, heavy loops, and looked back down the cut at the black arch sitting across the post-road in the gathering dusk.

“Nobody takes a mule or a foot-passenger through that cut,” Ge said flatly. “Road stays shut.”

Nobody on the switchback argued with him.

***

The sun had dropped behind the western karst needles when the party came back through the lower barbican arch, leaving the tiered roofs of Xuanfang Hold bathed in a flat, smoky violet twilight.

No one cheered by the water-trough, and no one made a speech. As Lupa marched through the wicket gate with Little Tan’s bandaged shin bouncing against her hip and his chin tucked miserably into her wolf-fur collar, the thirty people waiting by the guardroom steps let out a low, collective murmur of relief and immediately crowded forward toward Warden Liangyu.

The cooper’s eldest brother stepped off the stone bench where his blue-cloth bedding bundle had sat since midday, his padded coat buttoned against the rising evening chill.

“Glad the boy’s back in one piece, Warden,” the brother said plainly, looking past Liangyu’s shoulder at the heavy iron bar Ge was sliding back across the outer gate leaves. “Will the road be clear for us to go down at first light tomorrow? My brother-in-law’s got the mill-sluice to open in the lower valley on Monday morning, and I’ve got the two little ones with me.”

Liangyu looked at the man’s boots, then at the barred gate.

“The cut below the second bend isn’t passable, Master Lin,” Liangyu said without dressing it up. “And I won’t tell you tomorrow morning until my men have found a clear path round it. Nobody goes down the switchbacks tonight.”

She walked over to the black slate notice-board hanging beside the guardroom door, took the damp rag off its peg, and wiped out *WAIT FOR THE AFTERNOON BELL* with one flat sweep of her hand. Picking up the chalk stub from the ledge, she wrote across the bottom of the slate in square, level letters:

*SOUTH ROAD CLOSED UNTIL FURTHER WORD.*

She dropped the chalk back into its groove without writing an hour beside it. Inside the guardroom door, the fat canvas outgoing post-bag stayed hanging on its iron hook in the dark.

Ten paces back from the crowd, sitting on the third granite riser of the inner gatehouse stair where the first crimson eaves-lantern had just been lit for the evening, was Suoming.

Her faded plum-coloured coat was buttoned to the throat over the flat square of her breast pocket, and her long brown oil-paper umbrella lay furled across her knees with her boots hanging clear of the second step, as if she had simply paused there to rest her shins on the way up from the plaza.

As Qingxiao stepped off the flat of her pale sword onto the wet flagstones beside the drainage grate, the steel blade dissolving into quiet light at her cuff, Suoming tilted her chin up under the red lantern.

“Did it go across the river?” Suoming asked in her flat, quiet voice.

Qingxiao stopped for a beat at the foot of the step.

“To the west bastion,” Qingxiao said.

“Could you pass it on the east side?”

“No,” Qingxiao said.

Suoming nodded once. She placed both small hands on the bamboo shaft of her umbrella, stood up off the granite step with her iron heel-plates clicking flat against the stone, and turned up the long, tiered staircase toward the Fogveil Pagoda while the sky overhead was still grey—two hours earlier than she had gone up the night before.

Up at the third storehouse, Lupa kicked the unlatched timber door open with her toe and dumped Little Tan squarely onto the low, blanket-draped pine bench Mei had dragged up beside the roaring brick stove.

“One sprained ankle, one bruised backside, ten out of ten for noise!” Lupa announced, setting her ash spear in the corner rack while Chisa knelt by the boy’s foot with a pair of scissors to slit the swollen leather boot-lace without pulling the joint.

“I didn’t make any noise, I was reporting the fire on the wall—” Little Tan protested, his face going scarlet as Mei immediately tucked two hot flannel-wrapped oven bricks against his left boot and shoved a steaming mug of sugared ginger-water into his chapped hands.

Before he could take a swallow, rapid footsteps came pelting up the frosted cobblestones outside, and Widow Tan—a short, broad-shouldered woman from the weaver’s yard with dark blue woad-dye stained into the creases of her knuckles and a wool shawl thrown crookedly over her hair—burst through the doorway.

She didn’t look at Lupa or the stove. She marched straight across the floorboards, caught her nineteen-year-old son by both ears, tilted his helmet back to check his forehead for blood, and gave his shoulder a fierce, rattling shake.

“Your good padded winter trousers!” Widow Tan scolded at the top of her lungs, her voice cracking right down the middle as she whacked the mud-caked knee of his breeches with the flat of her palm. “Three nights I sat by the tallow lamp patching the knees with double hemp before the frost, and you go sliding down a ditch on your backside like a five-year-old! Drink that water before I box your ears!”

“Ma, the Warden’s watching—” Tan mumbled into his mug, sinking two inches lower into the wool blankets.

Widow Tan let go of his ear, wiped the corner of her eye once with a blue-stained thumb, and wheeled straight around on Mei, who was sitting on her three-legged oak stool beside the kettle with her hands folded over her stomach.

From beneath her wool shawl, Widow Tan pulled a heavy, square bundle tied up in a bleached flour-cloth—still hot and smelling strongly of toasted sesame oil, garlic chives, and fat pork—and pushed it straight into Mei’s lap.

“Left over from the second steaming last night,” Widow Tan said briskly. “I browned the bottoms in the flat pan before I ran up the lane. Twelve pork-and-cabbage, six with the minced shrimp for the girl with the bad stomach.”

Mei blinked down at the warm cloth bundle, then held it up with both hands, trying to press it back toward the woman’s apron. “Mrs. Tan, no, keep these for him! I didn’t even go down the stairs today! Lupa and Chisa and Yangyang and Cartethyia went down the pass—I just sat here on a stool and threw coal in the firebox!”

“A cold stove never mended an ankle yet, miss,” Widow Tan said flatly, folding her arms inside her shawl so Mei couldn’t hand the bundle back. “You had the bricks hot before my boy came through the door. Eat the shrimp ones while the skins are crisp. Up on your good foot, Tan, put your arm round my neck—we’re not taking up these people’s bench at supper-time.”

With Chisa’s strip of cold vinegar-soaked linen bound tight around his right ankle and Lupa’s spare broomstick shoved under his left armpit for a crutch, Little Tan hopped out over the brass boar’s-head boot-scraper beside his mother, leaving Mei sitting on her oak stool with the warm, grease-spotted bundle of dumplings resting on her knees.

Five minutes later, as Wen was lighting the hanging brass oil-lamp above the pine table, a polite knuckle rapped twice on the doorpost.

Outside on the landing stood the cooper from next door, accompanied by his eldest brother, their wives, the two small children, and the heavy blue-cloth bedding bundle and willow hamper from the gatehouse bench.

“Begging your pardon for disturbing the house, ladies,” the cooper said, holding his felt cap in both hands and nodding toward the dark lane. “With the south road shut till morning, my brother’s family can’t get down to the mill. We’ve got my old mother and three cousins from the upper weir sleeping crosswise on our kitchen floor already—if I put another quilt down by the hearth, somebody’s feet are going in the ash-pit. My brother’s got two coppers here if the young ladies wouldn’t mind him laying the children’s bedding up in the dry corner of your hay-loft till the gate opens.”

From the hearth, Lupa’s wolf ears went straight up. “The loft? My Butcher Ma blood-sausage and four strings of dried mountain mushrooms are hanging from that tie-beam!”

“And I’ve got thirty-six sorted Huaxu condenser screws laid out on a towel by the trapdoor!” Amy yelped from the wash-basin. “If somebody steps on a quarter-inch brass thread in the dark, I can’t file a new one!”

On the oak stool, Mei set Widow Tan’s warm dumpling bundle down on the corner of the table and stood up on her flat boots.

“Put those two coppers back in your coat, Master Lin, it’s the second day of the Waking Moon,” Mei said cheerfully, waving both hands at him. “Nobody pays rent in a storehouse! Lupa, move your sausage two hooks left; Amymy, put your screws in the biscuit tin; Chisa, give Master Lin a hand with the willow hamper on the ladder!”

The argument lasted less than the time it took Lupa to grumble once under her breath, march over to the threshold, and hoist both the willow hamper and the forty-pound blue bedding bundle under one arm by herself. Within two minutes, the cooper’s brother and his wife were spreading their quilts on the dry straw boards overhead while Cartethyia stood on the third rung of the loft ladder holding up her camellia terrarium so the two small boys could see the glossy green leaves in the lamplight.

Supper that night was laid out on the scarred pine board without ceremony, and eaten hot.

Lupa’s cartwheel brass hotpot sat bubbling on the middle trivet again, fortified with Mother Pu’s sliced smoked bean-curd and half of Widow Tan’s browned dumplings bobbing around the edges, while the six shrimp-and-pork ones went straight onto Mei’s and Iuno’s saucers. Yangyang warmed the last clear ladlefuls of yesterday’s angelica hen-broth in the small clay pot, and Mother Pu’s fat red jujubes went round the board in an old grain-scoop.

From the scullery shelf, Ciaccona carried in the deep earthenware platter with both hands and set it down beside the salt-jar, Whipping the linen towel off with a flourish.

“Behold,” Ciaccona said warmly. “Twenty-four hours of mountain maturation. *Tagliolini alla Toccata*, second-day reserve.”

Inside the platter, the neon-orange persimmon-and-garlic millet noodles had cooled and set into a single, solid octagonal cake that kept the exact impression of the earthenware sides, glistening faintly under the oil-lamp.

Lupa leaned over, poked the side of the orange block with the butt of her chopstick—it gave a dull, rubbery *thump*—and looked up at Ciaccona with genuine awe.

“If the eastern wall falls down again tonight,” Lupa said, “we don’t need quicklime. We just wedge that into the hole.”

“Philistines,” Ciaccona said serenely, taking Lupa’s heavy belt-knife, sawing a neat triangular wedge out of the orange block like a piece of farmhouse cheese, and laying it on her own plate. “Cartethyia appreciates subtlety.”

“I ate three bowls of the mutton broth already, Cia,” Cartethyia said apologetically from behind her mug, though she dutifully took a very small corner of the orange wedge on her spoon.

At the hearth-end of the bench, Mei bit a browned shrimp dumpling in half, chewed with total commitment, and pointed her chopsticks toward the front door.

“Forty paces to the dyer’s vat and forty paces back this morning,” Mei told the table proudly. “Didn’t touch the wall once, and Iuiu kept her hand in her pocket the whole way. Tomorrow morning after breakfast, I’m taking the twelve wide steps down to Huizhen Plaza and buying a turnip with my own hands.”

Beside her, Iuno picked up a slice of smoked bean-curd with her chopsticks and laid it neatly in Mei’s bowl.

“Twelve steps down and twelve steps up,” Iuno said mildly. “And if the turnip weighs more than a pound, Lupa carries it.”

“A one-pound turnip isn’t even worth peeling!”

Down the middle of the board, Amy cracked one of Mother Pu’s dried jujubes between her back teeth, swallowed, and looked across the teapot at Yangyang.

“Hey, Watcher,” Amy said plainly, without lowering her voice. “When you guys were down by that black wall in the cut this afternoon—did the Fox’s Prism in your sash do anything? Warm up, hum, click, anything?”

Around the pine table, the spoons slowed for a beat. Lupa looked over the rim of her bowl; Iuno’s gaze rested quiet and steady on Yangyang’s face.

Yangyang set her chopsticks down across her bowl. She laid her right hand flat over the fold of silk at her waist where the small, square block rode against her linen wrap.

“No,” Yangyang said in her clear, even voice. “I checked it when we came out of the arch, and again on the walk up. It’s cold.”

She didn’t pat the sash or offer a reason why it might warm up tomorrow; she simply picked her chopsticks back up and took a piece of cabbage from the pot.

“Well, something’s keeping that rock cold,” Lupa muttered, chewing a mouthful of dumpling. “A twenty-foot wall from a war two hundred years back doesn’t just grow across a mule-track overnight because the fog’s thick. And that ugly iron thing we cut up in the ditch wasn’t one of Muyu’s brass puppets—that was old gorge filth.”

“What about the little yellow boat on the cistern pond?” Cartethyia asked earnestly, leaning her elbows on the wood. “The one with the double-pinched nose that none of the village boys folded! Do you think someone left it there as a message?”

“If someone is leaving messages in paper boats without lighting the wick,” Ciaccona drawled, resting her chin on her good hand, “their dramatic timing is worse than the Mengzhou horn-section’s. What’s the drill for tomorrow if the south gate stays barred, Watcher?”

“Warden Ge is taking four men up the goat-track above the east cliff at dawn,” Yangyang said. “To see if a foot-party can scramble round the top of the limestone cut above the wall and come down on the lower side of the third milestone. And Cartethyia and I promised Wen we would walk down to the lower barracks terrace after breakfast—there are four other valley families who came up for yesterday’s dinner waiting in the granary shed, and they’ll need coal and blankets if the road stays shut through Sunday.”

Above their heads, a small wooden ball rolled across the loft floorboards with a muffled *trundle-thump*, followed by the cooper’s brother’s wife whispering sharply for the boys to lie down under the quilt before she came up the ladder with a slipper.

Mei looked up at the pine rafters, grinning as a pair of small giggles came through the knot-hole above the flour barrels, and reached across the platter for the last browned dumpling.

***

By the first watch of the night, the wind coming up from the river gorge had died to a flat, stinging chill that turned the breath white at the lips.

Before the household carried the stools out onto the landing, Buling came briskly up the red-lit cobblestones from the lower alley.

She had a small earthenware pot of boiled flour-paste slung from two fingers of her left hand by a hemp string, a flat hog-bristle brush in her right, and a stack of fresh yellow paper talismans wedged under her chin against the front of her royal-blue silk collar.

Without breaking stride or asking a soul, Buling marched up the two steps to the third storehouse, slapped a wide cross of wet paste onto the outside of the left and right pine doorposts at eye level, and pressed two fresh yellow slips printed in thick red cinnabar flat against the wood. Then she hopped down the step, crossed the gutter to the cooper’s barred door next door, stood on tiptoe to slap a third yellow slip squarely across the center of the oak lintel, and smoothed the corners down with her thumb.

Amy, who was dragging the small iron charcoal-brazier out through the storehouse doorway by its wire bail, stopped on the sill and squinted at the wet yellow paper shining in the lantern-light.

“Hey, Buling,” Amy called out. “What are you pasting bills on our doorposts for? Are you trying to invoice us for the pamphlet?”

Buling flipped her forehead talisman up with a flick of her nose and gave the cooper’s lintel one last pat.

“Second-night draft coming up from the river cut,” Buling said briskly, already stepping backward down the lane toward the dyer’s porch with her paste-pot swinging. “Very bad angle for old pine timber. You don’t put a stabilizing slip on the jamb tonight, tomorrow morning the wood swells up and you have to kick the door open with your boot. Complimentary feng shui maintenance, don’t thank me, I’m very busy.”

She turned the corner behind the cistern shed, her paste-brush already raised toward the dyer’s gate.

Out on the stone landing, they set the two pine benches and the wide oak stool along the low coping wall above the drop.

Mei sat bundled in her two grey wool blankets on the oak stool, with Iuno standing at her left shoulder and Yangyang on the bench to her right, Lupa’s thick knitted muffler wound twice around Yangyang’s throat over her cerulean feathers. At the far end of the bench, tucked under a shared patchwork of Cartethyia’s cloak and Lupa’s sheepskin, sat the cooper’s brother’s two small boys, their noses pink in the cold as Cartethyia peeled the paper off Mother Pu’s dried red jujubes and dealt them out one by one into their mittens.

Below their boots, the terraced steps and balconies of Xuanfang Hold were much quieter than they had been the night before.

The hundreds of crimson eaves-lanterns still burned in long, vermilion chains from Huizhen Plaza down to the barred lower gatehouse, but nobody had brought out copper wash-basins to bang, and no children were squatting by the black water of the cistern pond—where Qiu Er’s unlit yellow mulberry boat still sat dry and untouched on the corner of the stone bollard.

Only thirty or forty people stood out along the cold bluestone tiers, and nearly all of them had come out of their doors carrying an excuse.

Down by the split-cedar fence behind the scullery, Old Man Zhao had been standing at the wall for fifteen minutes tapping the cold ashes out of his brass pipe against his palm without once reaching for his tinder-pouch. On the terrace below the well-head, the tanner’s wife stood holding a hemp rope-leash, calling softly into the alley for her yellow dog while the dog sat pressed squarely against her own left ankle. Further down the water-stair, a young quarryman walked a wide-awake two-year-old boy up and down the same six steps in a padded quilt, and every time he turned at the landing, his chin lifted toward the black eastern saddle of the pass.

Nobody sang, and nobody asked when the moon was coming.

Over the jagged black teeth of the eastern ridge, a dull, rusty glow touched the mist.

Then the moon rose clear of the rock.

It was the same dark, heavy amber-copper as the night before—the colour of burnt sugar or an old coin dug out of red clay—and even as its lower rim cleared the cliff-pines of the pass, it was already sliding westward across the narrow gap of sky noticeably faster than it had on the first night.

“Look at the shadow on the turbine post,” Amy murmured from the end of the bench, her chin resting on her drawn-up knees. “Yesterday it took until the second drum to clear the cistern roof. It’s already past the gutter.”

Out of the deep black gulf of the river gorge below the south gate, a single long, flat rib of vapour lifted into the middle air and drifted across the pass, lying horizontal across the moon’s track.

As the sliding copper disc passed behind the vapour, the cloud did not turn silver or chalk-white. For a breadth of three fingers all around the tarnished rim of the moon, the vapour soaked up a deep, heavy carmine-red, glowing in the dark sky between the karst spires like a strip of oiled paper held too close to a bed of coals.

On the landing, the cooper’s older boy stopped chewing his dried date, pointing one mittened finger up at the red-edged cloud.

Then, while the copper moon rode high behind the carmine vapour, something deep down under the stone roots of Xuanfang Hold moved, once.

It made no sound in the air. It came straight up through the black basalt bones of the mountain into the soles of their boots and the square stone piers of the coping wall—a single, heavy, subterranean *thud*, from so far down that it seemed to come up through the stone of the whole mountain at once.

Beside the storehouse steps, the black water inside the stone cistern trough shivered all at once into tight, concentric rings that slapped once against the frozen rim. Along the cedar eaves overhead, every iron lantern-hook and chain gave a simultaneous, small *tick-tick-tick* against the wood, and then hung still.

On the bench beside Mei’s knee, Yangyang did not move. Inside the fold of her sash, pressed against her strapped ribs, the Prism of Fate stayed dark and cold as a river pebble.

Up on the high cedar watch gallery of the inner gatehouse, Ruoyun stood alone beside the corner embrasure.

The kaya-wood weiqi board sat on the flat limestone cap in the dim yellow glow of her shielded tally-lamp, untouched since the afternoon—nine black handicap stones on the star-points, one black answer on the third-line shoulder, and Qingxiao’s second white stone sitting quiet and unanswered on the fourth line.

Ruoyun held one of the small handleless porcelain wine-cups in her right hand, halfway to her mouth.

When the subterranean thud ran up the gatehouse pillar and vibrated through the sole of her shoe, Ruoyun looked up past the dark cedar eaves at the carmine-red cloud lying across the fast-moving copper moon. Her hand stopped two inches from her chin. Slowly, she lowered her arm and set the porcelain cup back down on the lacquered tray without drinking.

Half a mile higher across the dark Hold, at the top of the three hundred red-lit steps of the Fogveil Pagoda, the pair of crimson oil-paper lanterns swung once on their bronze dragon-hooks before the frosted, bolted jade doors.

Suoming stood four paces from the threshold beneath the open brown canopy of her oil-paper umbrella.

With her left hand steady on the bamboo shaft, she reached inside the breast of her faded plum-coloured coat, drew out Muyu’s blue-stitched booklet of deep-fissure soundings, and opened the damp paper covers to a leaf marked by a loose tail of blue binding-thread. She looked once at the column of inked figures in the red lantern-glow, closed the booklet flat between her fingers, and lifted her eyes past the porch balustrade toward the dark gorge where the lower post-road dropped into the mist.

Down at the bottom of the mountain tiers, in the vaulted shadow of the barred lower gatehouse, old Warden Ge stepped out of the guardroom doorway and looked up at the dark gap of sky between the peaks. Without saying anything to the two spearmen by the brazier, Ge reached up, lifted the heavy canvas outgoing post-bag—with Yangyang’s sealed letter to Suisui sitting inside it—off its iron hook on the outer post, carried it inside out of the frost, and pulled the guardroom door shut behind him.

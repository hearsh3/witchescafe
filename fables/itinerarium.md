> **ON ITINERARIA.**
>
> *The road-books of the old empires listed the stations along a road and the distances between them. Station, numeral, station, numeral, for four hundred pages. Scholars have complained for eleven centuries that the itineraria describe nothing: no country, no weather, no company, no food. A modern editor calls them "the most complete and least informative documents to survive antiquity."*
>
> *The complaint is fair, and it misreads the genre. An itinerarium is written by somebody who intends to make the journey again, for somebody else who intends to make it once. It records the distance between the place you slept and the next place you will sleep, because that is the only number that changes what you do tomorrow.*
>
> *Everything else in a journey is held by the people who were in it, and they do not write it down, on the grounds that they were there.*

## KM 0

The frostlands took them the way a room takes a shout.

Eleven kilometres of tunnel and a brass strip and then the world simply kept going: white to the north, white to the east, a grey lid of sky sitting on it about four inches above the horizon, and a wind that came across the ground sideways carrying granular snow at ankle height, so that the whole surface of the world appeared to be moving slowly to the left while remaining exactly where it was.

The cab held its tongue for about a kilometre and a half.

Then Amy stood up on the bench behind the seats, put her head and both shoulders out of the roof hatch into a wind chill that a physician had specifically written about, and screamed.

She screamed for eleven seconds.

<font color=coral>"Sit down."</font>

<font color=mistyrose>"IT'S SO *BIG*—"</font>

<font color=coral>"There's a gantry bar six inches above your head and I'm about to take a hollow at forty."</font>

Amy sat down with her hair full of ice crystals and her face a shade of red that had nothing to do with cold, and grinned at the windscreen for four hours.

## KM 244 — CAMP ONE

The first night was nothing at all, and everybody remembered it for months afterward as one of the good ones, which is how first nights work.

They parked nose-in to a rock spur that broke the wind. Lupa walked the perimeter twice with her hood down because she wanted the smell of the ground. Chisa put the two bikes in the lee. Amy and Cartethyia dug a hole for the burner exhaust with a camp spade and hit permafrost at forty centimetres and reported it to Iuno with the delight of two people discovering that a woman with a diagram had been right.

Mei made something with rice and jerky and too much of the good chilli oil. Everybody ate two portions standing up. Everybody was too tired to have an opinion about it.

The chair board went up at half past eight, because Amy made it, out of the back of the cassette-toilet diagram, with a grid on it.

<font color=mistyrose>"Six bunks. Two pairs of you are welded together, which leaves a spare bunk and the chair, and the chair is the *good* one, because it reclines and it has a tray, so we're doing a rota."</font>

<font color=teal>"We are not doing a rota for a *dental chair.*"</font>

<font color=mistyrose>"Cia, on a rota you get it every seventh night. Without a rota you get it never, because Lupa is going to take it every night by standing next to it."</font>

<font color=indianred>"I was walking past."</font>

<font color=mistyrose>"You have been walking past it for four minutes."</font>

<font color=mistyrose>"Night one: Lupa. Because she's *in* it."</font>

<font color=indianred>"Mm."</font>

<font color=royalblue>"Why am I sixth."</font>

*She has your number,* said the High Priestess, comfortably. *Down to the night.*

*Isn't that awful.*

---

At half past eleven the box had six people breathing in it and one wolf asleep in a reclining chair with a tray swung across her middle, and outside the wind came off a thousand kilometres of flat ground and worked at the corner of the tarp on a kneeling machine.

Amy lay in a bunk sixty centimetres wide, in a sleeping bag rated for worse than this, listening to a truck tick as it cooled, with her felt woman folded into her hands, and thought that she had never in her whole life been in a smaller room, and that it had six people in it, and that she had once held a door for ten years in a place with no walls at all.

She fell asleep in about ninety seconds.

## KM 302

The ceiling rained.

Seven people breathing in a sealed steel box for nine hours puts something in the region of three and a half litres of water into the air, and steel at minus twenty-four takes it back out again on every cold surface it can find, which in the *Lucky Bird* meant: the ceiling, the window frames, the underside of the top bunks, the inside of the galley cupboards, and, in a development that would be discussed for four days, the six-foot laminated molar, which sweated.

At 0620 a drop came off the ceiling and landed in Ciaccona's ear.

At 0621 she said a word in old Toccata that Cartethyia had never heard and requested, immediately, in writing.

## KM 388

The galley was one metre eleven long. It had two burners, a sink the size of a helmet with a foot pump, one large pan, one small pan, a kettle, and a fiddle rail so that things stayed on the counter through a hollow at forty.

Seven people. One metre eleven.

The rules that grew in it grew the way rules grow in a kitchen, which is to say nobody wrote them and everybody enforced them within about thirty hours.

The kettle went on at 0630 and again at 1700, and between 1700 and 1704 the galley belonged to Iuno and to nobody else, because there was a treaty, and the treaty was older than the truck, and even Amy — who had opinions about property, folded cloths, and jurisdictions — went and stood in the doorway for four minutes rather than reach past her.

Cocoa was Amy's and Amy's was cocoa, and she made it on Mei's recipe in the small pan and it took eleven minutes and used more milk powder than the daily figure permitted, and Iuno logged the discrepancy every single day and approved it every single day, in two separate columns, which Ciaccona described in her notebook as *the most romantic act of accountancy ever committed.*

Lupa cooked with her hands and tore bread instead of cutting it.

That was the crumbs.

There was an epic.

Ciaccona had been writing it since the brass strip and she had been waiting four days for somebody to ask, and she made a performance of declining, twice, and then got the notebook out of her inside pocket where it had clearly been sitting all evening.

She read eleven stanzas by firelight, in the elevated register, in dactyls, with the full apparatus.

The first was about a woman with one arm standing at a scale on a tailgate weighing the belongings of heroes.

The fourth was about a raven.

The seventh was about the Chancellor.

*"Sing, then, the Chancellor, borne in the arms of the unwilling,*
*seventeen litres of state, and the seal thereof, and the line;*
*sing of the wolf who declined him, and the maid who conveyed him at evening,*
*and the fan, at fifteen watts, that was given the office of a god."*

Amy laughed until she had to lie down in the snow.

The ninth was about a woman who could reproduce four tones out of a stranger's mouth on a single hearing, and it was funny for six lines and then it was something else for two, and Cartethyia went very still and looked into the fire through the last two and stayed quiet at the end of them, and Ciaccona moved straight on to the tenth, because she had built it that way.

The eleventh had no joke in it.

The eleventh was four lines about a truck with a name off a chicken shop crossing a white plain with seven people in it, and it ended on a half-line, which everybody noticed, and Amy said <font color=mistyrose>"That's not finished,"</font> and Ciaccona said <font color=teal>"Correct,"</font> and put the notebook away.

## KM 968 — 0611

Mei went out at ten past six with a mug of coffee to look at the door of her own truck.

She did it every morning. It took about eleven seconds and she had told nobody she was doing it and four people knew.

At minus twenty-six, frost forms first in the ridges left by a brush, so the five black characters came up white on the black enamel before anything else on the cab did, standing out of the door like something written on a window from the other side.

She counted them.

Five.

<font color=moccasin>"Morning,"</font> she said, to a door.

Behind her the fire ring was a grey scar in the snow with a wisp coming off it, and Lupa was already up and had already walked the perimeter, and the truck was ticking as the burner came on, and somebody inside was running the foot pump, and somebody else said a name out loud before coming through the curtain, and a raven that had been employed by them since the outlet sat on the roof rack of a decommissioned mobile dental surgery in the middle of the Roya Frostlands, waiting for the crumbs.

Mei drank her coffee.

Then she went and got the log off the dash and wrote the odometer figure at the top of a clean page, the way you do, because the number between where you slept and where you will sleep is the only one that changes what you do today.

**968.**

And under it, in the column for remarks, she wrote: *Good night. Fire too big.*

Chapter 1: Zhī Huán

“—I am the Xuan Paragon of this Hold.”

Qingxiao’s bare left hand stayed locked around the edge of her reflection’s twilight blade. A dark bead of blood welled between her fingers, froze into a dull red seed against the worn cord of jade beads at her wrist, and did not fall into the water.

Above them, the canopy of thousands of pale strings gave another high, metallic groan as the shock of a distant blow at Xuanfang Hold ran down the branches into the invisible harness dragging at Qingxiao’s collar. Her right knee sank another half-inch into the black silt at the bottom of the lake.

Standing over her in the mirror-flat shallows, the reflection did not press harder, and did not lift the blade. Her moon-white sleeves hung dry and unstirred by any draft; her long blue hair lay smooth over her shoulders; her clear violet eyes looked down from beneath her cloud-pale horns with quiet, unhurried patience.

“That is a title the Fox wrote on a piece of silk after the rain so you would have an excuse to walk down the stairs once a season,” the reflection said, in Qingxiao’s own level voice. “You have never even sat in the chair.”

The dark frequency of the sword bore down another inch. Hairline fractures of white rime shot outward from Qingxiao’s knee across the ankle-deep water, and the notched, flickering blade of pale light in her right hand bent until the guard touched her collarbone.

Splash of water broke the stillness behind them. Yangyang came running through the shallows, spray flying white around her shins as she reached for the hilt at her hip—

“Don’t,” Qingxiao said.

She did not turn her head from the twilight edge an inch from her throat, and her voice was scraped thin by the cold in her chest, yet Yangyang’s boots skidded to a stop two paces from the ring of ice around Qingxiao’s hem. Every string in the canopy above them was wound so tight with Overclocked frequency that a single outside blade striking the knot would send the backlash tearing straight up the trunk toward the mountain above.

Two seconds later, Iuno’s boots clicked against a submerged stone as she came up beside Yangyang’s elbow. In her right hand, held low beside her skirt, the silver-blue crescent of her moonwheel spun with a sharp, steady hum that threw pale light across the porcelain-white husks of Qingxiao’s past duels lying cloven in the reeds.

For the first time, the reflection lifted her violet eyes from Qingxiao’s brow and looked at the two women standing in the water.

There was no surprise in her face, and no curiosity. She knew the three cracked ribs wrapped under Yangyang’s sash; she knew the missing weight at Iuno’s left shoulder and the dead black screen strapped to her wrist in the cabin upstairs; she knew the taste of the squashed turnip cake they had divided three ways by the bolted door.

“You heard Jingwei at the edge of the trees,” the reflection said to them, as plainly as if she were offering tea to guests who had arrived late. “She left the door off the latch so someone would be standing here when her knees gave out. If you mean to be her sword, Priestess, you had better swing before the canopy snaps.”

***

Neither Iuno nor Yangyang raised a hand.

Yangyang kept her fingers rested lightly on her pommel without drawing an inch of steel, her breathing shallow and steady against her bound ribs. Beside her, Iuno’s crystal shoulder chimed a single, flat note as she studied the two identical faces beneath the tree—one chalk-pale and crusted to the elbow in frost, the other untouched by the mountain’s weight.

*Careful, darling,* the High Priestess murmured in the back of Iuno’s mind. *That isn’t an intruder in the house. That’s the landlord.*

The reflection looked from Iuno’s lowered wheel back down to the woman kneeling at her feet.

“They think I am an illness you caught in the snow,” the reflection said softly. “Show them what we are standing on.”

She turned her free left wrist half an inch toward the surface of the lake.

The black mirror-glaze vanished from the water. Underneath their boots, the ankle-deep shallows went as clear as mountain glass from shore to shore, and the bed of the lake was not silt at all.

Laid out across the bottom, row upon neat, unending row as far as the grey mist reached, were thousands of rolled bamboo and silk scrolls. Every one of them was bound shut with lead-colored thread and pinned into the mud under a smooth river stone, silent and waterlogged at the roots of the string-tree.

The reflection hooked two fingers upward. Below the surface, five lead threads snapped at once. Five scrolls unrolled in the mud, and pale, smoky light rose out of the water around their shins, thickening in the air beneath the lowest boughs until the mist took shape and sound.

The smell came first: wet thatch burning in an autumn drizzle, and the sour iron stink of blood in mud.

In the mist between the reeds stood a breached timber palisade. A girl no more than eight or nine years old, her wrists as thin as kindling and her tangled blue hair matted with soot, stood in the ashes of a wheelwright’s yard holding a peeled hazel stick in both hands. Propped open against a well-stone beside her bare, bruised feet was a dog-eared sword manual with half its cover torn off, its ink diagrams of footwork drawn in a crooked, heavy-handed scrawl that hooked inward at the strokes. Behind the collapsed fence, an old farmer with a bloodstained rag tied around his forehead reached across the charred rails and set a chipped earthenware bowl of steamed millet into the child’s hands.

“She weighed forty pounds,” the reflection said, watching the child in the smoke tuck the bowl against her ribs as Tacet Discords screeched down the lane outside the yard. “She had three days of winter cough left in her lungs and a stick with the bark peeled off. She walked out the gate into their teeth anyway, because she did not know how to eat a bowl of grain without paying her throat for it.”

The smoke thinned back into water.

A second scroll rose beside Yangyang’s boot.

The mist folded into a stone courtyard of the old Hold two hundred years ago, under a bruised sky where twin moons hung behind red clouds. At the foot of the barracks stair stood Lingfang in leather-backed plates, a sword buckled where her liuqin had hung and a rough-carved bamboo xiao tied silent at her belt. She lifted her chin toward the pass and sang the opening call of the Song of Xuanling—clear, unhurried, and steady—and then stopped at the fourth bar, leaving the ancient four-beat rest open for someone in the yard to sing the traveler home.

Nobody answered the call.

Ten paces back in the shadow of the cedar colonnade stood Qingxiao. Her moon-white dress was spotless, her hands tucked out of sight inside her cuffs, her mouth shut and her pale eyes fixed straight ahead as the soldier-musician waited one more beat, lowered her head, and marched out through the gatehouse arch into the gorge alone.

At the edge of the clearing, the small cerulean feathers at Yangyang’s collar lifted once against her jaw. She knew that woman’s face, and she knew the exact shape of the second verse that belonged in that silence. She did not say a word.

“Her blood boiled in her ears until her teeth ached,” the reflection said evenly, looking at the silent white figure under the vanished colonnade. “And Jingwei had taught her that if a song stirs your heart, your Curve will tear your ribs apart. So she kept her mouth shut and let the woman walk into the fire with half a tune.”

The courtyard dissolved. A third scroll uncurled from the silt, and the air turned bitter with the smell of wet ash and Retroact Rain falling upward toward the clouds.

For three heartbeats the mist showed a narrow defile in the Gorges of Spirits: Qingxiao driving a fan of flying blades through a black tide of Discords while two paces ahead of her on the slick shale, seen only from behind through the sleet, a woman in a dark coat pivoted on her heel—the clean flash of a sword, a single glimpse of a golden eye over a wet shoulder—and cut a path up the ridge.

Before Iuno’s breath could even catch in her throat, the rain shifted to the smoking, collapsed timbers of Yuan Fortress three days later. The three-tiered bridge lay broken in the gorge. In the mud before a cracked granite lintel knelt Qingxiao, alone in the grey downpour, using the notched iron point of a broken dao to chip the lower strokes of *Ruoyun* into the wet stone until the skin of her knuckles split against the rock.

“Someone always stays at the gate,” the reflection said. “It was never you.”

Qingxiao’s head stayed bowed over her locked wrists. The pale blade in her right hand gave a thin, splintering crack as another hair-width of light flaked off its edge.

A fourth scroll broke the surface right between Qingxiao’s knees.

There were the high, rain-lashed steps of the Fogveil Pagoda that same autumn, black mud caked to the knees of Qingxiao’s white stockings and wet char stiffening her cuffs as she stood before the bolted jade doors of the inner sanctuary, shouting into the lattice at the nine-tailed shadow behind the screen to ask where the Sentinel of Mengzhou had been when the Threnodian came down the valley.

The reflection leaned down half an inch over the locked blades, her unblinking violet eyes six inches from Qingxiao’s pale blue ones.

“Where were you?” the reflection asked, as gently as a mother waking a child.

The fifth scroll did not rise as a play of figures in the mist.

All around their boots, the waterlogged rolls at the bottom of the lake shifted, and two hundred folded slips of red-bordered paper floated up through the clear water to bump softly against Qingxiao’s shins. Every one of them was addressed in Sentinel Hsin’s neat, slanting hand; every wax seal was unbroken. In the mist behind the reflection’s shoulder appeared a stone table on a snowbound peak, where a porcelain wine-pot sat rimed in white ice beside a weiqi board with the black stones set out on the star points and the white bowl untouched across from them.

“You have spent two hundred years despising the Fox for bolting her door and sliding a velvet tray through a six-inch slot,” the reflection said. “Look at the snow outside your cave. Each of you climbed a mountain where the crying couldn’t reach your ears, and sent a thread or a wooden bird down the stairs in your place.”

Qingxiao did not move, and she did not answer.

Above her, the harness of strings dragged her shoulders down another fraction of an inch. Where her bare left hand gripped the twilight blade, her right thumb shifted once to press against the worn red cord of jade beads at her wrist, holding the cracked green stone against the cut in her palm as if that quarter-inch of cord were the only thing keeping her spine from folding into the water.

***

Overhead, three thick cords near the center of the canopy jerked downward at the same instant with a sound like a ship’s hawser parting in a gale.

A heavy, muffled boom rolled down the trunk of the string-tree from the siege at Xuanfang Hold. The shock drove Qingxiao’s left knee down into the silt beside her right, cold black water splashing up to her waist as a fresh sheet of frost raced up her sleeves to her shoulders.

The reflection did not glance up at the sparks snowing down through the branches.

“You hear the masonry slipping,” the reflection said. “Even if you hold that iron grate until the fifth bell, every man and woman tied to those threads will be in the ground within sixty years. The tide comes back, Qing. Let go of the hilt, let the harness snap, and the mountain will finally be quiet.”

She turned her head slightly, looking past Qingxiao’s horn toward Iuno.

“Tell her, Priestess,” the reflection said. “You kept this exact ledger for thirty years in a marble house. Tell her what happens when you bankrupt the treasury to buy one more afternoon for people who are already dying.”

In the ankle-deep water, Iuno stood very still. The pale light of her moonwheel reflected off the wet hem of her skirt and the jagged crystal at her left shoulder.

“You are very good at it,” Iuno said evenly. “The voice that counts the grain sacks while the city burns so someone in the room stays upright. I used to sound exactly like you at four in the morning.”

“And you lived,” the reflection said.

“I kept it so well that by the end I couldn’t remember what the ledger was for,” Iuno said. “It wasn’t living. It was just bookkeeping.”

“Bookkeeping keeps the roof up,” the reflection replied.

In the quiet behind Iuno’s eyes, something shifted—and then Iuno’s spine straightened by half an inch, her chin lifting at an angle that belonged to thirty years of basilica vaults and marble steps.

When she spoke again, it was the High Priestess who answered across the dark water.

“Oh, stop bullying yourself, darling, it is dreadfully provincial,” the High Priestess said aloud, her voice rich, dry, and ringing clear as sanctuary bronze beneath the singing boughs. “Do you think I don’t recognize what you are? You aren’t some grand abyssal terror Jingwei found in a scroll. You’re the armor she hammered together when she was eight years old because nobody else was going to keep her alive, and now you’re furious because she keeps trying to take you off in the rain.”

The reflection’s violet eyes narrowed by the breadth of a hair.

“I kept mine alive for thirty years by telling her that wanting nothing was the same thing as being safe,” the High Priestess continued, the theatrical arch in her voice dropping into something older and unexpectedly kind. “It was a lie when I told it, and it’s a lie in your mouth too. You don’t want her dead. You’re just tired of being locked in the cellar every time she comes down the stairs.”

Iuno blinked once, her shoulders settling back into her own weight as the basilica cadence left her mouth.

She looked down at the silver-blue crescent spinning beside her hip, and then she opened her right hand.

The moonwheel broke apart into quiet sparks that drifted down to touch the water and went out, leaving her palm empty at her side.

Beside her, Yangyang glanced once at the vanished wheel, and let her own right hand slide off the pommel of her sword to hang loose by her skirt.

***

For three beats, the clearing was silent except for the high, straining hum of the canopy.

Then something cold and sharp moved behind the reflection’s unblinking violet eyes.

“A generous sermon,” the reflection said to Iuno, “from a woman who found someone to remember her.”

She raised her free left hand above the tips of her horns, reaching straight into the lowest boughs of the string-tree.

She ignored the thick, braided cables vibrating with the weight of the gatehouse capstans and the forty-two Wardens on the granary steps. Her pale fingers passed between them and closed around a single, hair-fine filament running off at a slant through the mist—the one Qingxiao had tied separately, in secret, around a set of five-day-old stitches under a grey cotton shirt before they ever walked through the gearwell door.

With a smooth turn of her wrist, the reflection slid the twilight edge of her blade out of Qingxiao’s bleeding palm, lifted it above her head, and laid the dark flat of the steel straight against that solitary string.

In the water at her feet, Qingxiao’s breath stopped dead in her throat.

“What about this one, then?” the reflection asked, in Qingxiao’s own quiet, level voice. “She does not remember the manual. She does not remember the black rain in the Gorges, or the stick in the ashes, or your face. You walked from the northern passes to the southern sea looking for her tracks, and she had already washed her own slate clean on purpose so she could begin again without any of it.”

The twilight edge pressed half an inch against the singing filament, bending it out of true until a tiny shower of white frost-sparks fell from the knot.

“You were not worth keeping in her head,” the reflection said softly. “Why are you bleeding through your wrists to hold her roof?”

At the edge of the reeds, Iuno’s breath caught hard; beside her, Yangyang’s eyes went from the bent string in the branches to the back of Qingxiao’s neck.

Qingxiao looked up at the dark blade resting against the thread. Her left hand, still smeared with half-frozen blood across the palm, hung open in the empty air where the sword had been a moment before.

Slowly, Qingxiao’s right hand opened too.

The notched, flickering blade of pale light she had been holding to guard her own throat slipped from her fingers, dropped into the ankle-deep water without a sound, and dissolved into grey silt among the unsealed scrolls.

The instant the blade went out, every string in the canopy overhead shrieked at once.

Far above in the waking world, a massive assault hit the Hold’s wall; the sympathetic shock hammered straight down the trunk of the tree like a falling bell. The mirror-dark water around their boots exploded upward into six-inch plates of jagged white ice, and the invisible harness at Qingxiao’s collar yanked her forward until both of her bare hands went down into the freezing slush to keep her face out of the lake.

Iuno took a sharp step forward through the broken ice, her right hand lifting—

Yangyang caught the edge of Iuno’s sleeve with two fingers and shook her head once, her eyes fixed on Qingxiao’s shoulders.

***

Inside the fold of Yangyang’s sash, the dark, lifeless Prism of Fate gave a single, silent pulse of warmth against her ribs—like a banked coal turning over under grey ash—and went still again.

Out in the mist beyond the roots of the string-tree, someone cleared her throat.

“That’s enough of that,” a woman’s voice said. “Honestly, A-Qing, if I’d known you were going to spend two hundred years sulking like a tax auditor, I’d have left you the third watch and gone to bed myself.”

A woman walked out of the grey fog onto the ice-choked shallows.

She wore the leather-backed laminar coat of the old Fortress garrison with the cuffs rolled back twice because the sleeves had been cut for a broader man, her dark hair pinned up with a plain wooden bodkin and a healed notch in the left eyebrow over a warm, sharp-chinned face. Where the soles of her boots stepped between the floating plates of ice, the clear water did not ripple at all.

Down in the slush, Qingxiao’s head came up so fast the water flew from the ends of her hair.

Above her, the reflection froze with the twilight blade still resting against the solitary string. “Put it back under the stones,” the reflection said, her level voice dropping half a shade. “A dead memory has no business walking around without its lead thread.”

The woman in the garrison coat stopped three paces from the trunk, propped both hands on her hips, and squinted up at the reflection.

“Dead memory my foot,” Ruoyun said. “Does a two-hundred-year-old scroll know that you still correct people’s singing in your sleep? You dropped that Mingting girl’s fourth note a half-step in the bunk twenty minutes ago, the same way you used to do it to the third company on the wall.”

Yangyang blinked in genuine surprise.

On her knees in the freezing water, Qingxiao did not move. Her lips parted twice before a sound came out of her throat.

“Ruoyun,” Qingxiao whispered. “I carved your stone.”

“Yes, and your chisel-work was atrocious, you made the bottom radical look like a turnip,” Ruoyun said, stepping closer across the unrippling surface until she stood right beside the reflection’s elbow.

As she passed under the pale glare of the string-canopy, the rolled cuffs of her laminar coat and the tips of her left fingers flickered—not with the amber static of Muyu’s projections, but thinning into pure, colorless mountain air, so the dark reeds behind her showed straight through her wrist bone.

“I’m not allowed to tell you this,” Ruoyun said, speaking a little faster now as she looked down into Qingxiao’s face. “There’s a clause against it in the terms I put my thumbprint on, and whoever drafted them was an absolute skinflint about clauses, so I only have about a minute before the mountain pulls my ears.”

Qingxiao braced one wet palm against her knee and pushed herself halfway up in the broken ice, her pale blue eyes wide and wholly unshielded. “You were at Yuan Fortress when it fell. I turned over every timber after the rain, and there was nothing of you to bury.”

“I was going to,” Ruoyun said plainly. “The gate-beam came down across my hip and the rain was turning black. Then I was offered another way to stay, and I took it. I put the meat down and went on the Hold’s books as a Celestial.”

Already the edges of Ruoyun’s shoulders were going pale and threadbare like washed-out linen, the light of the strings shining straight through her collarbone as the broken silence took its price out of her substance. She didn’t look down at her fading hands; she kept her warm, impatient eyes fixed on her friend.

“I stayed, A-Qing,” Ruoyun said, her voice gentling just a fraction as it grew thinner in the mist. “Who do you think keeps sweeping the pine needles out of the south conduit before the freeze sets, or nudging the Rafter Kites toward your peak when a young Warden gets turned around in the fog? I’ve been right downstairs the whole time.”

Qingxiao’s right hand lifted an inch out of the water—not toward a sword, just an open, blood-streaked palm that stopped halfway because there was nothing solid left of Ruoyun’s sleeve to hold.

“You gave up your body,” Qingxiao said, her voice rough and bare.

“I gave up the part that was pinned under four tons of ironwood so I could keep the part that liked the people on the wall,” Ruoyun said wryly. She turned her head and looked straight into the reflection’s violet eyes from six inches away—not afraid of her at all, looking at the dark blade and the hard, frozen mouth the way an older friend looks at someone making a scene in a doorway. “Which is a sight smarter than keeping the meat in a snowbank for two centuries while you take a knife to your own chest every time someone shares a turnip cake with you.”

The reflection did not swing at her; for the first time since they entered the clearing, the clear violet eyes dropped away from another person’s face.

Ruoyun’s outline was barely more than a sketch of pale vapor against the dark trunk now, her voice dropping to the volume of dry leaves scraping across flagstones.

“The girl with Lingfang’s face is doing well at the gate, by the way, though she still grips the ash shaft two inches too high when her arms get tired,” Ruoyun murmured, already half-dissolved into the draft rising through the boughs. “Stop hitting yourself with Jingwei’s stick, A-Qing. It’s miserable to watch.”

A breath of wind moved across the water, and the space beside the tree trunk was empty.

For five seconds the clearing was dead quiet save for the hum of the strings overhead.

Qingxiao knelt in the broken ice looking at the spot where no ripples had formed. Her spine was not plumb-line straight; her head hung an inch forward between her cloud-pale horns, and her right thumb brushed once against the cracked green jade bead at her left wrist.

“She always talked too much on the third watch,” Qingxiao said to the empty water.

Then her voice caught on the next breath, plain and small and two hundred years late:

“I thought I was the only one left.”

***

Qingxiao set her wet palm on her knee and stood up out of the silt.

She did not call a blade to her hand. As she straightened her knees, the crust of rime along her forearms cracked at the joints, blood and cold lake water running together down her left wrist from the cut across her palm.

Four paces away, the reflection still stood beneath the lowest bough with the flat of her twilight sword resting against the thin filament tied to the courtyard rug upstairs, though her hand had gone still on the hilt.

“You asked why I involve myself in Xuanfang’s affairs,” Qingxiao said.

The reflection watched her with unblinking violet eyes. “Tell me without using the Fox’s title.”

Qingxiao looked down once at the thousands of unsealed scrolls glimmering under the shallow water at their feet, and then straight back into her own face.

“They gave me a bowl of food,” Qingxiao said.

The reflection’s fingers tightened a fraction on the dark hilt.

“An old woman in Xianling hung a braid of dry garlic over my horns and told me to eat before the wind rose,” Qingxiao said, in her level, unornamented voice. “The girl from Mingting divided a squashed turnip cake into three pieces on a scrap of grease-paper and gave me the middle one. Down in the hall, a woman with five-day-old stitches in her stomach is keeping the browned crust at the bottom of a rice pan warm because she promised someone she would.”

She lifted her gaze an inch to the solitary thread humming above the reflection’s wrist.

“She does not need to remember my name for me to remember the manual,” Qingxiao said. “A single plum branch over shallow water is the reason one loves a small garden.”

She stopped.

She looked down at her own bare, cut left hand hanging by her wet skirt, and then back up at the reflection.

“And my hands hurt,” Qingxiao said quietly. “They have hurt for two hundred years.”

The twilight sword wavered half an inch against the string.

The cold, porcelain stillness on the reflection’s face broke—not into anger, and not into a snarl, but into the sudden, stricken look of a child who has been bracing a heavy door shut with her shoulder in the dark for as long as she can remember and has just heard her own name called from inside the house.

Beside the reeds, Yangyang drew in a slow breath.

Her three cracked ribs pulled sharp under her sash, and she let her lungs fill anyway. As she parted her lips, the small cerulean feathers at her throat lifted all at once, and from her shoulder blades the great, ink-dark wings of the Xuanling unfolded across the mirror-lake—not torn or missing primaries the way they had been in the crossing dark, but wide, whole, and real in the light of the string-tree, their white tips brushing the surface of the water on either side of Iuno’s boots.

Yangyang sang the opening call of the Song of Xuanling—the high, clear marching phrase Lingfang had sung into the empty courtyard two centuries ago when nobody answered.

Her voice rang out beneath the taut canopy, steadying the shivering cords overhead. When she reached the end of the fourth measure and left the rest open for the living to answer the road, she did not have to sing the second half alone.

Qingxiao lifted her chin and sang the response.

Her voice was spare, cold, and clear as a struck jade chime, and when she came to the turn on the fourth note, she dropped it a half-step lower—the old cadence the garrison had sung before the rain.

Before the first word had finished leaving her mouth, the reflection’s lips parted, and she sang the second word with her.

It was the exact same voice, coming from two throats four paces apart, in pure unison.

The twilight blade slipped from the reflection’s fingers, vanished into mist before it touched the water, and the solitary string above them hummed free and clear.

Out in the reeds, the shallows began to stir.

With every slow beat of Yangyang’s ink-dark wings and every bar of the doubled response, the porcelain-still figures lying half-submerged across the lake—every version of herself Qingxiao had cut down over two hundred years to keep her Curve flat—began to come undone around their cloven chests. Guided by the Xuanling’s voice, they did not sink into the mud or fly away into the dark. They lifted off the surface of the water as long-tailed birds of pale, warm light and white breath, wheeling once beneath the branches of the string-tree before diving straight back into Qingxiao’s breastbone, one after another.

With each one that came home, the crushing harness at Qingxiao’s collar slackened into ordinary weight, and the crust of ice along her sleeves broke apart.

When the last note of the verse faded out across the water, Yangyang’s dark wings folded quietly back into the sheer cloud-work of her sleeves and settled at her collar.

In the center of the clearing, the reflection stood empty-handed in the ankle-deep water. Her long blue hair was moving in the valley draft now, and her shoulders were shaking with the deep, bone-aching chill of someone who had been standing out in the snow since the night her village burned.

Qingxiao took two steps across the water.

She did not reach for a blade, and she did not offer terms. She simply opened her moon-white sleeves and put both arms around the other woman’s shoulders, pulling her straight against her chest the way you hold someone who has waited two hundred years on the wrong side of a bolted door.

For half a heartbeat the violet-eyed figure stood rigid in her grip—and then her cold hands came up to clutch the back of Qingxiao’s wet coat, her forehead dropping hard against Qingxiao’s collarbone as she let out a single, ragged breath that sounded like a sob, and sank inward until only one shadow fell across the clear water beneath them.

***

Qingxiao stood alone at the foot of the tree, her palms resting flat against her own ribs where the other had been. When she lifted her lashes, her eyes were the same pale, winter-sky blue they had always been.

Out in the thinning mist beyond the reeds, Jingwei’s lingering frequency spoke one last time—not sharp with instruction now, only old, spare, and very far away.

“Your heart has left its stillness, Qing,” the old voice said. “Without the stillness to anchor your marrow, you will not live long.”

“No,” Qingxiao said.

For the space of a breath the mist stayed quiet, as if the long-dead hermit on the peak were looking down at the frail girl she had once pulled out of the ashes with a broken stick in her fist.

“Then tell me,” Jingwei murmured. “What is your Way?”

Qingxiao looked up at the canopy of strings above her head, and then over her shoulder at Yangyang and Iuno standing in the shallows with their bruised ribs and their empty hands.

“I have found my answer,” Qingxiao said evenly. “To walk down the mountain while the soup is still warm.”

A faint, dry rustle moved through the reeds—the barest shadow of an old teacher letting out her breath over a student who would not be corrected—and then Jingwei’s frequency thinned out of the air and did not return.

All at once, the last of the rime clinging to Qingxiao’s cuffs, her stockings, and the tips of her horns turned to clear water and poured off her hem into the lake.

The locked, twelfth-month glacier cold that had radiated off her skin vanished. An ordinary evening breeze blew across the shallows, catching her soaked sleeves, and for the first time in two hundred years the Sword Transcendent gave a sudden, unmistakable shiver, her shoulders hunching half an inch as gooseflesh rose along her forearms.

She looked down at her own damp wrist in mild surprise.

As she turned her arm, the cracked green jade bead on the worn red cord gave a tiny *tick*, split cleanly in two, and dropped off the string into the ankle-deep water.

Qingxiao watched the two halves of the little stone settle among the unsealed scrolls on the lake bed, where the water was clear enough to read every word written on them, and left them there.

She turned half-around toward Yangyang, her gaze dipping for a beat to the fold of silk at Yangyang’s waist where Hsin’s Prism sat tucked against her sash.

“Tell her to set out the black stones in the eighth month,” Qingxiao said. “I will come down for the wine.”

Above their heads, a soft, rustling sound ran through the string-canopy from root to crown.

Along the thousands of taut white lines rising into the dark—at the thick knot holding Fleurdelys’s shoulder, at Lupa’s scorched collarbone, at Warden Ge’s bound forearm, and along the thin, stubborn filament wrapped around Mei’s ribs—small, round buds swelled out of the condensed light and broke open all at once into five-petaled white plum blossoms.

The black ceiling above the branches parted like smoke. A quiet mountain dusk settled over the lake, and through the flowering boughs rose a pale, clear moon, throwing sparse, slanting shadows of plum branches across the shallow water while a faint, cool fragrance of winter blossom drifted down around their faces.

A single white petal detached from the canopy overhead, floated down on the breeze, and landed squarely on the tip of Qingxiao’s left horn.

Yangyang looked at it, then down at her own wet boots, her mouth twitching slightly. “Does this mean we don’t have to practice with the sticks in a crosswind?”

Qingxiao reached up, plucked the plum petal off her horn, and tucked it neatly into her damp cuff. “Half an hour after breakfast,” she said. “Your left heel still floats.”

Beside them, Iuno looked up through the moonlit branches where the flowered string tied to the courtyard rug hummed steady and warm against the dusk, and let out a long, uneven breath that had no cathedral dignity in it at all.

*Well, thank heaven for that,* the High Priestess murmured in the back of her head, sounding thoroughly wrung out. *If I had to watch another woman turn herself into a monument this week, I was going to start throwing furniture.*

“You’re soaking wet, Paragon,” Iuno said plainly, rubbing her bare right forearm against the evening draft as her shoulder crystal gave a soft, clear chime. “Let’s go back upstairs before you catch a cold like the rest of us.”

***

At the real Xuanfang Hold, the morning wind off the gorge had died again, and the Censure Courts had come back up the ditch.

Under the bisected eastern wall, Little Tan slipped in the frozen slush as a robed tribunal captain brought its notched bronze cleaver down two-handed toward the boy’s collarbone. Tan shut his eyes, bracing for the bone-cracking blast of twilight ice that had sheared the machines in half before dawn.

The string above his shoulder snapped taut.

It did not flash dark blue, and it did not lash back through the puppet’s spine. A line of soft, moon-white light caught the heavy bronze edge with a gentle, ringing *plang*, guided the cleaver harmlessly aside into the mud so Liangyu’s spear could take the joint, and left behind a sudden, unmistakable breath of white winter plum blossoms in the middle of the oil-smoke and sleet.

Up on the roofed watch gallery, beneath its silk dust-cover, the seven-stringed paulownia qin sounded once by itself—a clear, warm note at its old pitch, the exact note Liangyu had listened to for three summers on the snowpeak.

Down in the gateway mud, Liangyu wrenched her spearhead free, turned her face toward the granite buttress for the space of two breaths while she dragged her mud-streaked sleeve across her eyes, and then stepped back into the line.

Thirty yards back under the covered arcade, Mei sat on the felt saddle-blanket with Amy’s head heavy against her thigh. Beneath her palm, right through the grey cotton of her shirt, the hair-fine thread of light resting beside Shijie’s letter suddenly lost its winter chill and grew as warm as a hand laid over her bandages. Mei looked down at the front of her shirt, her brow creasing slightly in the grey light, and kept her palm flat over the spot.

Up on the west stair, standing on her upturned wooden bucket with her steel-ribbed paper umbrella propped over her shoulder, the small woman in the faded plum-colored coat paused. She lifted her nose once at the faint drift of blossom scent passing through the freezing drizzle, glanced up toward the dark eaves of the watch gallery, and turned back to the parapet without a word.

***

Yangyang woke with the side of her hat brim squashed against the upright timber post of the bunk.

Across the narrow floor, Iuno drew a sharp breath and straightened up in the stiff-backed officer’s chair, her right hand catching the chair arm as the crystal at her left shoulder chimed against the cabin wall.

The cabin was warmer than it had been when they closed their eyes. Along the floorboards and the front leg of the map table, the needle-sharp lines of frost had melted into small beads of clear water that dripped slowly off the bunk rail with a quiet *pat, pat* onto the varnished wood.

On the narrow mattress above Yangyang’s head, Qingxiao lay flat on her back under the grey wool blanket and Iuno’s silver-blue sash. Her pale blue eyes were open, looking up at the cedar ceiling beams. When she breathed out, no white mist came from her lips.

From both of her cuffs, the ribbon of frequency still rose straight up through the timber ceiling toward the Hold—she had not dropped a single thread, not the forty-two people between the well-head and the granary steps, not the gatehouse arch, not the courtyard rug—but the harsh, over-wound wire was gone. The light drifted soft and moon-white around her wrists, humming at an easy, level pitch.

Qingxiao turned her head on the hard bolster and looked down over the timber rim at Yangyang.

“Yangyang,” Qingxiao said. “Help me sit up.”

Yangyang got to her feet, bracing her left elbow lightly against her sash, and stepped close to the mattress to offer her right shoulder and her hand.

This time Qingxiao did not wave her off. She caught Yangyang’s forearm with her damp, cool fingers, leaned a real woman’s weight against Yangyang’s collarbone as she swung her legs over the bunk rail, and sat for a moment on the edge of the mattress with her shoulders slightly rounded while her breathing settled.

Iuno stood up from the chair, picking up her damp sash from the foot of the bed and shaking the water drops from the silk. “How is the link?”

“Holding,” Qingxiao said. She looked down at her hands folded in her lap, then up at the two of them with her level pale blue gaze. “I am hungry.”

For two seconds neither Iuno nor Yangyang spoke.

Then Yangyang’s ears went faintly pink beneath her blue-black hair; she looked down at the empty, creased square of grease-paper still sitting by the bolted door where she had folded it before the watch.

“We ate the last of Lupa’s turnip cake before you lay down, Paragon,” Yangyang admitted. “And the tea on the table doesn’t taste like anything.”

“It doesn’t even taste like boiled water,” Iuno agreed dryly, stepping over to drag the heavy brass bolt out of its socket on the cabin door. “Once we cut Muyu’s spindle upstairs and drop this ceiling, Mei has a pan of browned rice sitting on the brick hob in the hall. If Lupa hasn’t eaten it out of spite, you may have my third.”

“The crust belongs to the disciple,” Qingxiao said, rising onto her own feet—slower than before, one hand resting briefly on the upright bunk post to test her balance. “I will take the soup.”

By the map table, Iuno’s right thumb brushed the black screen of her dead Terminal once by habit. Nothing lit on the dark glass except the faint reflection of her own mouth curving at the corner, and for once she didn’t click her tongue at herself before dropping her hand.

Iuno turned back to the open door and laid her right hand on the heavy brass bolt she had just drawn, her fingers resting on the cold metal without pushing the timber wider.

She looked across the wet floorboards at Qingxiao.

“Why didn’t you bring her down here?” Iuno asked. “The louver in the Sanctum floor took three. Even half-healed, she is the best sword in the Hold.”

Qingxiao kept her hand resting lightly on the upright bunk post. “She was injured.”

The three words settled into the quiet of the cabin. Iuno did not take her hand off the bolt. She looked at the pale ribbon of frequency drifting loose and warm around Qingxiao’s damp left cuff before it rose through the cedar ceiling beams toward the rug under the arcade.

“And the manual?” Iuno asked quietly.

Qingxiao glanced down at her sleeve, where the shallow cut across her left palm was beginning to clot in the warmer air.

“That is older,” Qingxiao said.

She did not step away from the bunk. For a moment she stood looking at the varnished grain of the timber post, her shoulders slightly slack under the damp moon-white silk, as if the latch that had kept her mouth shut on the flagship prow had melted off with the rime.

“In the Gorges,” Qingxiao said. She stopped for a breath, then went on in her level voice. “On the third day of the rain. The eastern flank had broken in the shale, and I was down on one knee.”

By the foot of the bed, Yangyang stood still with her fingers resting near her sash.

“She came down the ridge with the Sentinel Jué,” Qingxiao said. “A dark coat, and golden eyes. She cut the throat out of the tide before dusk and turned the war.”

Qingxiao’s thumb moved inside her cuff to touch the worn red cord at her wrist, brushed the bare gap on the string where the cracked jade bead had been, and stopped.

“Yuan Fortress burned that same night,” she said, and on the first word her voice gave a fraction, scraping thin before she brought it level again. “The Gorges held. The north wall fell while we were ten li away in the mud.”

Iuno’s fingers loosened around the brass tongue of the bolt, though her palm stayed on the iron housing.

“After the snow cleared, I walked the border roads for four years,” Qingxiao said. “From the northern passes down to the sea. Nobody had seen the Sentinel, or the woman in the dark coat. I wanted to ask her one thing.”

She paused, her brow creasing faintly between her cloud-pale horns.

“How someone with a blade that wide chooses where to stand,” Qingxiao said quietly, “when every roof she does not stand under burns.”

A drop of melted frost fell from the bunk rail onto the floorboards with a small, clear *pat*.

“Then she stepped through a paper screen in the Fox’s Pagoda this month,” Qingxiao said. “With dust on her knees from the pantry door. And she did not know my face.”

“So you didn’t ask,” Iuno said.

“She had put it down,” Qingxiao said simply. “She chose to begin again.”

Iuno leaned her right shoulder against the timber doorjamb, looking at the Sword Transcendent across the three paces of wet planking.

*Well, darling,* the High Priestess murmured in the back of her skull, dry and unexpectedly gentle. *It appears you were two centuries late to the line.*

“It is faintly ridiculous,” Iuno said aloud, her voice even and rueful, “to discover that someone spent four years walking the continent after my vagabond two hundred years before I first tried to put her on an altar.”

Qingxiao looked up from the bunk post, her pale blue eyes blinking once. “I only wanted to ask her the question.”

“I could have saved you the boots,” Iuno said. The corner of her mouth curved, tired and fond. “If you had caught her on a mountain road and asked how she navigates impossible choices, she would have stared at you until you offered her half your rations. She doesn’t navigate them, Paragon.”

Iuno looked down at the heavy brass bolt under her fingers.

“She refuses to count,” Iuno said quietly. “If two beams are coming down at once, she does not stand in the yard deciding which hall matters more to the province. She goes out the window with a ladder, props her shoulder under whichever timber is closest to someone’s head, comes back two days late with her shirt torn open, and sits on the floor while the rest of us are furious with her.”

The crystal at her left shoulder gave a single, low chime against the quiet.

“I tried to decide it for her once,” Iuno added, her voice flat and exact. “Thirty years ago. I filed a warning away so she would stay on my side of the map, and left someone else to pay for it in the east. I was wrong.”

She lifted her eyes from the bolt, and the courtroom edge went out of her voice.

“But I should be honest about the rest,” Iuno said. “I never met the woman in the dark coat. Whatever she knew about choosing, I couldn’t tell you. I only know the one who washed up on our shore.”

On *our*, her eyes went to Yangyang, and stayed there a moment.

Beside the bunk, Yangyang shifted her weight onto her good hip, her hand resting lightly over her bound ribs.

“She left the old folders in the drawer in Jinzhou,” Yangyang said in her plain Mingting voice. “Even with her face carved on the Beacon outside. Every morning this week she’s woken up in the storehouse and asked the ceiling what day it is before she opens her eyes, and if you come in late from the wall, there’s a plate waiting by the stove with a bowl turned over the top so it doesn’t dry out. I don’t think starting over works if you try to carry the Beacon around with you.”

Qingxiao did not answer right away. Her right hand slid down from the bunk post to her damp cuff, her fingertips brushing the single white plum petal tucked inside the silk, and the tight line across her shoulders settled by a fraction of an inch.

“Do not trouble her with the Gorges,” Qingxiao said. “Or the manual.”

“I have no intention of bringing it up over the rice pan,” Iuno said dryly. “Though if she asks why you tied a piece of your own life around her bandages, I am not going to invent a story for her.”

“She will not ask,” Qingxiao said. “She left the folders in the drawer.”

Yangyang’s mouth twitched at the corner. “She’ll ask, Paragon,” she said mildly. “She asks everyone everything. She knew the ferryman’s dog’s name at the lower crossing before we had both boots on the plank.”

“And gave the dog half her bun,” Iuno murmured.

Qingxiao looked from Yangyang to Iuno, a brief, dry quiet settling around her eyes. “Then I will go back up the peak as soon as the spindle is cut.”

“After the soup,” Yangyang said.

“We are burning daylight,” Iuno said, though the dark glass on her wrist still showed nothing at all. She finally took her hand off the brass bolt, calling a quiet spark of silver-blue light to her fingertips as she ceded the front of the threshold to Yangyang. “Five decks to the spindle.”

Yangyang settled her right hand on the pommel of her sword and took the first place by the sill to read the air in the passage, with Iuno half a pace at her back.

Qingxiao walked two steps after them toward the open doorway, passing the round brass-rimmed porthole set into the hull.

Outside the thick glass, for the first time since they had fallen from the Sanctum, the Mirage’s nailed-up golden sun had sunk below the sea of clouds. The sky over the karst spires had turned the deep, clear indigo of mountain dusk, and high in the upper corner of the pane hung a pale waking moon.

As Qingxiao turned her head toward the corridor to follow Yangyang and Iuno out, her reflection in the dark glass turned its head at the exact same instant, looking out through pale blue eyes.

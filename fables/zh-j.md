Chapter 1: Zhī Jǐ

The bolt on the inner cabin door was two-hundred-year-old Ministry brass, cast thick enough to stop a pike-butt, and it had never once been slid shut in anger because no war had ever reached this deck.

Iuno shoved it home with the heel of her right hand until the iron tongue clunked into the mortise. Without pausing, she flicked her wrist to call the silver-blue crescent of her moonwheel out of the air and wedged its lower rim at an angle beneath the cross-bar, jamming the opposite edge against the brass floor-cleat so nothing could force the latch from the passage without shearing through her own Resonance first.

Only then did she turn around.

Yangyang already had Qingxiao halfway to the bunk.

They had come up the last companionway from the tribunal deck at a stumbling, lopsided walk, with Qingxiao’s right arm draped across Yangyang’s shoulders and Yangyang’s right hand clamped hard around Qingxiao’s wrist to keep her there. To anyone who didn’t know how much a person weighed when their knees stopped answering their mind, it might almost have looked as though the Sword Transcendent were walking on her own feet. Her white stockings still moved in step; her boots still brushed the floorboards in a neat, level cadence. Every third pace, all of her weight came down across Yangyang’s collarbone anyway.

Yangyang didn’t slow until the backs of Qingxiao’s knees touched the timber frame of the narrow bunk against the inner bulkhead.

“Lie down for a minute, Paragon,” Yangyang said, keeping her voice low and easing her shoulder out from under Qingxiao’s arm so she could guide her back toward the bolster.

Qingxiao let go of Yangyang’s tunic at once. She did not lie down.

She sat on the outer edge of the mattress, drew her heels together on the varnished planks, straightened her spine until her cloud-pale horns were an inch clear of the wall behind her, and folded both hands neatly inside her moon-white sleeves, as though she had merely paused on a stone bench in a garden to let a junior catch her breath.

“Here is fine,” Qingxiao said.

Her breath left her lips in a thin white cloud that hung in the mild cabin air for two seconds before vanishing. Beneath the hem of her skirt, where the soles of her boots rested on the dark wood, a pale crescent of frost was already creeping outward across the grain with a sound like dry salt settling on paper.

And from both of her cuffs, the pale ribbon of frequency did not drift around her arms the way it had in the bamboo grove or on the barges. It stood drawn as thin and taut as an over-wound qin string, rising straight up from her wrists through the lacquered cedar ceiling beams toward a sky that wasn’t above them.

Iuno crossed the narrow room in three steps.

The cabin was a standard third-grade secretary’s quarters copied straight out of the Ministry of War’s central compound during the founding reign—and like the scaffolding at the mountain gate below, it was too clean by half. There was no dust in the corners of the map table, no ink-stain on the sheepskin chart of the gorges where little red and brass campaign pins stood arrayed in their final pre-war positions, and no soot inside the glass chimney of the gimbal lamp burning on the desk corner. Against the wall hung a seven-stringed paulownia qin on two new silk cords; beside the door hung a carved wooden duty roster; on a lacquered tray sat a celadon water jar and a porcelain teapot with a curl of steam rising from its spout for nobody in particular. Across from the bunk, one round brass-rimmed porthole looked out on the Mirage’s sky: the same flat, unmoving late-afternoon gold that had been shining down on the clouds when they fell through the louver hours ago.

Qingxiao’s high, stiff collar was hooked tight beneath her chin, the silk pressing into her windpipe every time she drew one of those shallow, frosted breaths.

Iuno reached down with her right hand and set her thumb and forefinger to the small silver frog-clasp at Qingxiao’s throat.

Because the silk was stiff with frost and Qingxiao’s skin underneath was colder than the metal, the narrow loop refused to give without a second hand to hold the left tab steady. Every time Iuno pushed the silver toggle a millimeter toward the eye, the slick fabric slid away from her knuckles across Qingxiao’s collarbone. She tried twice, her jaw setting hard while the crystal at her left shoulder gave a short, irritated chime against the quiet.

“Hold the cloth,” Iuno muttered under her breath, meaning her own fingers.

“Let me,” Yangyang said quietly.

She didn’t make a production of stepping in, and she didn’t look at Iuno’s left shoulder. She simply braced two cold fingers against the left edge of Qingxiao’s collar to keep it from sliding and flipped the silver loop off the toggle with her other thumb, easing the throat of the dress open by half an inch.

Qingxiao’s next breath came in without catching on the silk. She did not look up, only gave a single fractional nod toward Yangyang’s cuff.

Iuno stepped back to the map table, where the porcelain tea service sat steaming on its tray. She lifted the lid of the pot first—clear amber liquor, no leaves in the strainer—and poured half a cup, raising it to her own mouth before offering anything from Muyu’s floorboards to a woman whose Resonance was already fraying at the seams.

It was the exact temperature of freshly steeped mountain oolong.

It tasted of nothing at all.

Not boiled rainwater, not stale tin, not even the chalky flatness of snow melted in a helmet. There was simply an absence between her tongue and the roof of her mouth where roast and leaf ought to have lived—the exact, scrubbed blankness that had sat behind Jianghang’s eyes when he turned his wrench over his dead sister’s name on the crane deck.

Iuno lowered the cup, looked at the clear amber surface for a beat, and set it back on the tray with a dry click. She unstoppered the celadon water jar instead, poured a cup of cold water that was no more real than the tea but at least didn’t pretend to have a smell, and held it out to the bunk.

Qingxiao took the porcelain between both palms. Her hands were the color of river stones pulled from under winter ice; before she had taken two sips, a paper-thin ring of rime formed along the inner glaze where her thumb rested against the rim.

Only when the cup was safely in Qingxiao’s hands and Iuno had turned the wick of the gimbal lamp down half a notch did Yangyang step back from the bunk.

She took two paces toward the bolted door, stopped beside the corner of the map table, and let go of the breath she had been holding since the bottom of the companionway stairs.

Her right hand went flat against the wrap of her sash, pressing hard against her left side where three cracked ribs had just hauled a grown woman through two corridors of bronze cleavers. She didn’t double over and she didn’t make a sound; she simply stood still in the middle of the floor with her eyes shut for two counts, waiting for the white heat under her breastbone to stop jumping.

“Yangyang,” Iuno said, turning from the tray.

Yangyang opened her eyes. Six months ago in Jinzhou, her mouth would have said *I’m fine, I’ll check the passage* before her mind had even finished the sentence. She kept her hand pressed over her sash and looked plainly at Iuno.

“My side hurts,” Yangyang said.

On the edge of the bunk, Qingxiao’s pale blue eyes lifted from the frosted water cup. She watched her newly minted disciple say the three words aloud into the room—without a smile to soften them, without an excuse attached—with the still, unblinking attention she might have given a sword form she had heard of in an old manual and never seen demonstrated in the flesh.

Iuno crossed the floor, laid her palm on Yangyang’s good right shoulder, and steered her two steps back until her spine met the heavy cedar planks of the bolted door, right beside the wedged moonwheel.

“Sit down,” Iuno said, pressing lightly until Yangyang slid down the wood onto the floorboards. “Leave your back flat against the timber so the wrap holds, and don’t get up the next time someone drops a spoon.”

Yangyang let her head tip back against the door panel, her hat hanging from its cord behind her neck, and let out a long, careful breath that only used the top half of her chest. “Okay.”

For a minute or two, nobody spoke.

The cabin was very quiet. Through the thick brass ring of the porthole, the false golden sun sat nailed to the afternoon sky above the clouds. There were no herons calling outside this hull, and none of the hollow clacking of bamboo from the valley below—only the deep, sub-audible thrum of the Shuttle’s looms turning somewhere beneath the keel, and the faint, glassy hiss of frost creeping another half-inch along the floorboards under Qingxiao’s heels.

Iuno stood by the campaign table, resting her hip against the wood. Without thinking about it, she turned her right wrist up and tapped the dead black screen of her Terminal once with her thumb.

Nothing lit. Not even the low-battery bar.

She caught herself staring at the dark glass, clicked her tongue softly against her teeth, and dropped her hand onto the sheepskin map of the gorges.

Down by the door, Yangyang shifted her hip an inch to keep the hard, cold corners of Sentinel Hsin’s darkened Prism from digging into her waist through her sash. As she adjusted the silk fold, her fingers brushed another square packet wedged flat behind her belt.

She drew it out onto her knee. It was the grease-paper wrapper Lupa had shoved into her hand at the iron service door before they climbed into the gearwells. The last of the salted turnip cakes inside had been squashed to the thickness of a copper coin between Yangyang’s scabbard and Qingxiao’s hip on the stairs.

Yangyang unfolded the grease-paper on her lap, used her thumbnail to divide the flattened cake into three small, ragged triangles, and held the paper up toward Iuno first.

Iuno looked at the squashed triangle of cold fried dough and scallion, took her third with two fingers, and stepped over to set the second piece on the dry wood of the bunk rail beside Qingxiao’s knee.

As Yangyang raised her own piece halfway to her mouth, Qingxiao flinched.

It was barely a movement at all—a sudden, sharp downward jerk of her right shoulder, no more than half an inch, as though an invisible mason had struck her collarbone with the flat of a chisel. Above her cuff, the thin white ribbon of frequency snapped so taut it gave off a high, singing ring like piano wire in a hard freeze. A jagged vein of white rime shot six inches across the varnished timber of the bunk frame and cracked the lacquer with a sharp *tick*.

Yangyang’s hand stopped in midair. Iuno froze with her fingers still an inch from the bunk rail.

Neither of them needed to ask what the wire in the ceiling was tied to.

“Who?” Iuno asked, her voice flat and instant.

Qingxiao did not set the water cup down, though the ice inside the rim thickened until the water sloshed sluggishly against the porcelain. Her chest rose and fell twice in short, white puffs of mist before she answered. A thread spun from a qin string on the far side of a folded realm did not carry faces or names.

“Tall,” Qingxiao said. Her voice was level, scraped thin at the very end of the breath. “Under the gatehouse arch. And the swords are pure.”

For the space of a heartbeat, neither Iuno nor Yangyang breathed.

Then the singing wire above Qingxiao’s wrist dropped half a tone back into its steady, murderous hum.

“It held,” Qingxiao said.

Iuno’s right hand came down on the edge of the campaign table behind her, her fingers curling hard over the carved wooden lip until the knuckles went white.

Cartethyia. Nine feet tall in the mud under the hand-cranked iron portcullis, taking a blow heavy enough to drive the Sword Transcendent’s shoulder down across two hundred years of folded air—and still standing on her feet in the gateway because the thread woven into her hem from the watch gallery had turned the edge aside.

The relief hit the narrow cabin at the exact same instant as the price of it: the fresh frost creeping toward the table legs, and the chalk-grey line that had just deepened around Qingxiao’s mouth. Nobody said how either half of it felt. Iuno stared at the pale ribbon disappearing into the cedar ceiling boards, understanding all the way to the bottom of her stomach what that line was buying upstairs, and that she could not ask Qingxiao to cut it without asking for someone she loved to take the next blow bare.

She couldn’t ask her to cut it.

She took half a step back toward the bunk anyway.

“Thin them,” Iuno said.

Qingxiao’s pale eyes lifted slowly from the porcelain cup. “What?”

“The outer threads. The ones along the second terrace and the granary steps.” Iuno kept her voice measured and level, though the crystal at her left shoulder chimed a tight, rapid beat as her weight shifted. “Leave the gatehouse arch if you must, and drop the rest to half-tension. Liangyu’s Wardens know how to hold a shield-wall, and Lupa and Chisa aren’t novices. You told me yourself on the flagship prow: trust them. They will hold the line.”

“They are holding it,” Qingxiao said.

“While you sit on a plank two hundred years deep and freeze to death from the wrists up,” Iuno snapped. “You are Overclocking across a sealed realm boundary. Every time a Discord scrapes a breastplate up there, you pay the tariff out of your own marrow. If your Curve breaks before we reach the upper Shuttle, there won’t be a link at all, and there won’t be a Sword Transcendent left to cut Muyu’s spindle either.”

From the floorboards by the door, Yangyang spoke very quietly, looking at the shivering white edge of the ribbon rising from Qingxiao’s sleeve. She did not mention the cliff above the gorge, or how the mountain wind had sounded when her own name started coming apart at the seams.

“It starts to sound like water when you push past the line,” Yangyang said. “Like you can hold all of them at once if you just let go of your hands. I can hear it in your wrist from here, Paragon.”

Qingxiao’s gaze moved once to Yangyang, then back to Iuno’s face.

“Which ones?” Qingxiao asked.

Iuno blinked. “What?”

“You said to thin the second terrace,” Qingxiao said, her pale blue eyes completely still beneath the curve of her horns. “There are forty-two people between the well-head and the grain steps. Two of the Wardens have bad knees from the switchbacks. Tell me which twelve I should drop.”

Iuno opened her mouth, and nothing came out.

“On the gallery,” Qingxiao said, her level voice carrying no heat and no triumph at all, only the plain statement of a rule she had accepted, “you told me that numbers are a coward’s way to love a city. If I do not count them, then I do not choose which cloak goes bare.”

The words dropped between them like lead onto stone.

Iuno stood in the middle of the three-pace floor, caught squarely inside her own lesson. She heard the exact shape of the trap she had built on the watch gallery, and underneath the hot flare of anger in her chest she knew perfectly well who she was actually furious with—because somewhere above that painted ceiling, in the real dark and the real sleet, a woman with golden eyes and five-day-old stitches in her abdomen was doing the exact same arithmetic without a slate, throwing herself over people in falling transports and sneaking out for forty-eight hours to prop up strangers’ roofs because she could not bear to count who got left in the cold.

*Well, darling,* the High Priestess murmured in the back of her skull, very dry and very quiet. *She listened to you. Heaven help us when anyone listens to us.*

Iuno didn’t reach for a courtly parry. She didn’t have one.

“I am frightened,” Iuno said.

She said it as plainly as if she were reading the water-mark off a canal wall—not a plea, not a cadence meant to move a senate, just an inconvenient truth laid flat on the table because hiding it would have been an insult to everyone in the room.

“My Terminal is dead,” Iuno said, her voice even and slightly hoarse, “the sun outside that window hasn’t moved an inch since we fell, and the woman I love is standing behind a broken gate with her stomach sewed together with silk thread. And if you burn yourself to ash on this bunk to keep her standing until midnight, and then Muyu kills them all at a quarter past twelve because the two of us couldn’t break the core without you, I am going to be very angry with you.”

Qingxiao looked at her.

For a full three seconds the Sword Transcendent did not move or speak. Perhaps in two hundred years of generals, magistrates, and young Wardens bowing at the foot of her snowpeak, no grown woman had ever stood three feet from her knees and stated fear as a plain piece of inventory.

Before Qingxiao could answer—or decide how one answered that—Yangyang braced her right palm against the doorframe, pushed herself up off the floorboards with her left elbow tucked tight against her sash, and stepped quietly between them to the map table.

She didn’t try to make peace. She picked up the brass gimbal lamp, whose base was beginning to catch the creeping frost from the end of the bunk, and moved it six inches inward onto the dry wool of the gorge chart so the glass chimney wouldn’t crack from the chill. Then she poured another half-cup of the tasteless water from the celadon jar, set it down by Iuno’s hand on the table, and picked up the piece of squashed turnip cake from the bunk rail to place it directly into Qingxiao’s palm beside the porcelain cup.

Both of the older women watched her do it.

By the time Yangyang lowered herself back down against the bolted door, the breath had gone clean out of the quarrel, the way a room settles after a shutter bangs in the wind.

Qingxiao looked down at the small triangle of cold fried dough in her stone-pale fingers, lifted it, and ate it in two quiet bites. Then she set the empty cup on the floor beside her heel and drew her hands back into her sleeves, her thumb finding the worn red cord of jade beads at her left wrist and resting still against one smooth bead.

Outside the round porthole, the false golden afternoon hung motionless over the clouds where Muyu’s fleet patrolled a war nobody had lost.

Iuno glanced from that painted glare down to the dark, lifeless Prism of Fate showing through the fold of Yangyang’s sash.

“When you can see the end of the board,” Iuno said quietly, looking at the little red campaign pins clustered around Yuan Fortress on the sheepskin chart rather than at the bunk, “staying away looks like mercy. You tell yourself that if you don’t put your hand in, at least you aren’t the one who broke them.”

Qingxiao’s thumb did not move on the red cord. Neither of them needed to name the Moon Fox, or the Retroact Rain outside the pagoda two centuries ago, or Muyu’s phantom smiling on the flagship prow.

“Did it?” Qingxiao asked.

“No,” Iuno said. “It was just quieter from the balcony.”

Neither of them said another word about Hsin. Qingxiao drew her wrists an inch deeper into her cuffs, leaned the back of her head lightly against the timber bulkhead between her horns, and closed her pale eyes.

***

The gold outside the porthole did not turn to amber, and the shadows of the campaign pins on the sheepskin chart did not lengthen by the breadth of a hair.

The only clock in the cabin was the grey ring of frost that had crept another two inches toward the table legs and the dry, sleepless ache behind their eyelids.

Qingxiao sat upright on the edge of the bunk with her eyes shut, her breathing slow and shallow, only the thin white ribbon rising straight from her cuffs into the ceiling beams showing that she hadn’t let go of the mountain above.

Iuno had pulled the stiff-backed officer’s chair around from the map table and sat near the foot of the bed, her boot resting a few inches from Yangyang’s knee by the door. For a long stretch neither of them spoke above a murmur. They were the two women in the room who loved Mei, and whatever that made them to each other lived only in a quiet, careful courtesy in how they shared the floor.

Iuno’s thumb brushed the dark glass of her Terminal again, pressing the side button once by pure reflex before she folded her palm over the band to make herself stop.

“Can you hear anything?” Iuno asked, so low it barely carried past the foot of the bunk. “Beyond the hull?”

Yangyang tilted her head back against the heavy cedar door. For five counts she let her own breathing go flat, the way she did on the northeast parapet and inside the brass teeth of the Snare, reaching underneath the dead clockwork hum of the Ministry Shuttle’s floorboards for the small, ragged catch of real lungs drawing mountain air.

Nothing came back. There was only the dry tick of cooling brass in the bulkhead and the faint drip of condensation inside the celadon jar.

Yangyang opened her eyes and shook her head once. “The walls here don’t carry breath. It’s like listening through cotton.”

“If it thins,” Iuno said, looking at the iron bolt on the door rather than at Yangyang, “and you catch her—tell me. Even if it’s bad.”

“I will,” Yangyang said.

A few breaths passed in the dim orange glow of the gimbal lamp. Iuno’s fingers stayed closed over her silent wrist-screen.

“What day is it?” Iuno murmured, before she realized whose question she had just asked the room.

Yangyang looked up at her. Every morning since the timber spar in the fog, Mei had woken up in the back bedroom of the storehouse and asked the ceiling rafters what day of the week it was before she even opened her eyes.

Yangyang counted backward in her head: the dawn coach when Suisui left the lower gate, the ginger porridge at the long table, the climb up the snow-choked pilgrimage steps to the Pagoda, the valley road when the sky went dark, the iron door into the gearwells.

“Tuesday,” Yangyang said softly. “By now, I think.”

Iuno’s hand stopped moving over her Terminal.

She didn’t say a word. She looked across the narrow cabin at the round brass porthole, where a counterfeit, unmoving afternoon from two hundred years ago was eating up the hours of an ordinary Tuesday.

Inside Iuno’s head, the High Priestess did not make a joke, did not sigh behind a phantom fan, did not say *darling*. The silence behind Iuno’s eyes was so complete it felt like an empty basilica after the sanctuary lamps had been put out.

Yangyang watched Iuno’s profile for a single beat. She knew what Tuesday was for—Mei had told her on the stone bench under the tree at Huaxu while her boots were still cold—and she did not touch it by so much as a breath.

Instead, Yangyang looked down at the empty square of grease-paper folded in her lap, smoothing the creases flat with her thumb.

“She’ll have kept the stove going,” Yangyang said in her plain Mingting voice. “For the rice.”

Iuno blinked once, coming back from the window. The corner of her mouth pulled a fraction, dry and faint. “If Lupa didn’t padlock the hob, Mei will have scraped the browned crust out of the bottom of the pot an hour ago and blamed it on Abby.”

“She won’t,” Yangyang said, quiet and certain. “She promised to save it.”

Iuno looked down at the girl sitting against the door—the small cerulean feathers at her collar lying flat and still against her throat—and nodded once. “No,” Iuno agreed softly. “She’ll have left it in the pan.”

Both of them meant *she is still alive to stand by the stove*, and neither of them said that aloud.

Then the quiet stretched out again, and Yangyang’s eyes drifted past Iuno’s knee toward the round porthole.

She looked at the heavy brass dog-latch on the glass a beat too long.

Iuno saw the change in her shoulders before Yangyang even knew she was doing it: the slight inward tuck of the chin, the way Yangyang’s right hand crept half an inch toward the tunic pocket where her leather patrol log and carpenter’s pencil lived. Behind her eyes, Yangyang was measuring the seam where the false sky met the upper hull—working out how fast a Xuanling bird could climb when she didn’t have to wait for two companions on a moonwheel, or how thin wind could make itself to slip through a louver crack that bone and wool could not pass. A one-inch square of paper folded four times and tucked under the water jar on the campaign table, and she could be out in the dark before either of them reached the bolt.

“Don’t,” Iuno said.

Her voice wasn’t loud, and it hit the floorboards like iron dropped on flagstone—harder than anything she had said to Qingxiao during the quarrel.

Yangyang’s fingers froze over the edge of her pocket. Her eyes came back from the porthole to Iuno’s face.

Iuno didn’t explain why she, of all people, would not sit in a bolted room and watch another Yangyang unbind herself into weather to run an errand alone. None of it needed to be spoken.

Yangyang looked at the white line around Iuno’s knuckles on the chair arm, and slowly let her hand drop from her pocket back onto her knee.

“Okay,” Yangyang said. “I’m staying.”

With the sudden spike of heat ebbing out of her chest, Iuno leaned back against the stiff wooden slats of the officer’s chair and pressed two fingers to the bridge of her nose.

*You nearly gave me grey hair just then, darling,* the High Priestess murmured, her voice finally returning in the dark behind Iuno’s eyes, low and frayed at the hem. *If that child turns into a pigeon while you’re looking at the furniture, the older sister will audit us into the next century.*

“Hush,” Iuno muttered under her breath. “I saw it in time.”

From the bunk, Qingxiao spoke without opening her eyes:

“Who are you talking to?”

Iuno stiffened slightly, lowering her hand from her brow. She had thought the Paragon was asleep. “Myself. A bad habit from living in large rooms.”

Qingxiao opened her pale blue eyes a fraction, looking not at Iuno’s face but at the empty air an inch above Iuno’s bare crystal shoulder.

“Does it speak in your own voice?” Qingxiao asked.

The question was so plain, and so oddly specific, that Iuno paused before answering.

“Better diction,” Iuno said dryly. “And thirty years of cathedral acoustic training. Yes, it’s mine.”

“Is it unkind?”

Iuno thought about thirty years of white and gold cloth, and cold marble floors at four in the morning, and the voice that had kept her spine straight when every vision in her hands turned to salt.

“Vain,” Iuno said truthfully. “And theatrical. Mostly she says the true thing ten minutes before I’m ready to admit it myself.”

“Did you ever try to be rid of it?” Qingxiao asked.

“I spent thirty years trying to be rid of her by being her,” Iuno said, looking down at her right hand resting on her knee. “It turns out it is much less exhausting to let her have the other chair and argue across the table.”

For a long moment Qingxiao said nothing. The frost along the bunk rail gleamed dull white in the lamp-glow.

“My master,” Qingxiao said in her level, unhurried voice, “taught that whatever speaks inside you in your own voice is an Inner Demon. When it shows its face at the threshold, you draw your blade and cut it down.”

She didn’t say whether she had agreed with Jingwei the Perfected. She didn’t say whether anything spoke back to her when the snow closed the passes on her nameless peak. She simply stated the rule and stopped.

Down by the door, Yangyang rested her wrists on her knees.

“Mine wasn’t unkind,” Yangyang said quietly.

Both women looked toward her.

“My shijie,” Yangyang said, and when she said the word her voice had the warm, ordinary curve it only got when she talked about Mingting. She spoke of her in the present tense, as a sister sitting in a room across the dark rather than as herself. “She used to tap my elbow with a bamboo ruler when I let it float on a backswing. Now she’s at home with our older sister, learning how to knead bread again without dropping the flour scoop. I keep a list in the back of my patrol log of small things to tell her when the solstice comes. Most of it is about food.”

Yangyang paused, her fingertips brushing the scabbard lying beside her hip. Her voice dropped half a shade, plain and factual.

“There was another one, once,” Yangyang said. “I didn’t like her. She told me to give everything up and live.”

On the bunk, Qingxiao was very still. “What did you do?”

“I told her no,” Yangyang said.

“Did she go away?” Qingxiao asked.

Yangyang thought about the kite line pulling hard toward the eastern gale on the overlook, and the brass dog-latch on the porthole she had been measuring two minutes ago.

“I don’t think so,” Yangyang said simply. “I just stopped doing what that one says.”

Across from the bunk, the round porthole glass had gone dark on the inside where the shadow of the bulkhead met the unmoving gold outside. Qingxiao’s pale blue eyes rested on her own faint reflection in the thick glass for a beat too long—and then she looked down at her sleeves, half a second slower than she meant to.

Right as her eyes dropped, her left hand twitched inside her cuff.

It was smaller than the first flinch, and sharper—a quick, involuntary catch of breath that turned into a white puff of frost in the air before her chin. The ribbon of frequency around her left wrist flared white-hot for an instant, then dimmed to a bruised, icy blue.

Yangyang sat up an inch from the door. Iuno was already leaning forward in her chair.

“Where?” Iuno asked.

Qingxiao pressed her right thumb hard against the red cord of jade beads at her left wrist, as if holding a pulse down.

“The inner courtyard,” Qingxiao said. Her voice was very quiet. “Short. Too fast on the turn. Pulling to the left to favor her middle.”

Every drop of blood seemed to leave Iuno’s face at once.

Mei. Standing on the flagstones behind the gate, turning on her good hip to keep the five-day-old stitches in her abdomen from tearing while she swung a sword.

And Qingxiao’s thread was in her shirt.

Even with her heart hammering against her ribs, Iuno’s mind—trained for thirty years to read what people did not put in the treaty text—caught the impossibility of that. When Qingxiao sat on the high watch gallery with her paulownia qin across her knees before the climb, she had woven her Sword Intent into the cloaks and breastplates of the Wardens on the wall and the vanguard at the gate. Mei had been inside the Ministry hall tearing linen bandages with Ciaccona, under strict orders from the entire household to stay out of the courtyard.

For a thread of the Heart Sword to be wrapped around Mei’s ribs right now, the Sword Transcendent had to have put one on her on purpose, separately, before they walked through the iron service door.

And on the flagship deck, before the storm of needles rose: *I was with M— the defending troops in the Gorges of Spirits.*

Iuno stared at the pale, winter-still woman on the bunk. The question rose all the way to the front of Iuno’s tongue—*How long ago did you know her?*—and stopped behind her teeth.

“Why did you put a thread on her?” Iuno asked instead, very softly. “She was supposed to be inside the hall.”

Qingxiao did not look up from her sleeves. Her answer was completely true, and left the whole century underneath it untouched.

“She was the most likely to disobey,” Qingxiao said.

Iuno waited, her fingers digging into her knee, until Qingxiao took one slow breath and let it out.

“It held,” Qingxiao added, her voice flat and dry as snow on pine. “She is at rug height.”

A tiny, breathless sound escaped Iuno’s throat—half a laugh, half a catch that she swallowed down before it could reach the air. She rested her forehead on her palm for three seconds, then lowered her hand and left the older question unasked. Whatever Qingxiao was keeping, she had kept it a long time; Iuno would not pry the lid off it in this room.

***

After the second thread held, the frost beneath Qingxiao’s white stockings crept past the middle of the floorboards to touch the front leg of the map table, and the tips of her fingers showing at the rim of her cuffs went a translucent, waxy grey-white.

Still she sat bolt upright on the edge of the mattress as if waiting for a clerk to strike a gong.

Yangyang braced her right hand against the bolted door, pushed herself up slowly so her bound ribs wouldn’t catch, and walked the four steps to the bunk.

She didn’t lecture the Paragon about Resonance depletion, and she didn’t try to press her shoulders down. She simply picked up the folded grey wool blanket from the foot of the mattress, shook it out once with her good arm, and stood beside Qingxiao’s knee holding the top edge open, waiting.

It was the exact way Yangyang and Suisui used to stand beside their grandmother’s chair in Mingting after the New Year supper when the old woman insisted she was only resting her eyes until the water boiled for the midnight tea.

Qingxiao looked at the grey wool, then up at Yangyang’s quiet, unbudging face.

“I do not need to sleep,” Qingxiao said.

Yangyang didn’t argue. She kept holding the corner of the blanket an inch off the frosted floorboards. “The bunk is narrow, Paragon. If you sit on the edge all night, your back will stiffen before we climb.”

For five seconds they stayed like that: two hundred years of solitary mountain cultivation against a young woman from Mingting holding a wool blanket.

Qingxiao let out a single, almost inaudible breath through her nose.

Before she lifted her boots from the floor, her pale blue eyes caught the wooden duty roster hanging slightly askew on its brass nail beside the head of the bunk—the Mirage’s faithful copy of the Ministry’s watch list from the night Yuan Fortress fell. Qingxiao reached up with two stone-cold fingers, shifted the bottom corner of the board half an inch until it hung plumb against the timber bulkhead, and paused with her fingertips resting on the last carved column of names.

“The third watch was Ruoyun’s,” Qingxiao said softly, to the cedar plank rather than to either of them. “Someone always stays at the gate.”

She withdrew her hand, turned on the mattress, and lay down flat on her back with her head on the hard bolster and her hands folded neatly over her breastbone, her cloud-pale horns resting against the dark ticking.

Yangyang laid the grey wool blanket over her from chin to ankle. A moment later, Iuno rose from the chair, unhooked her own wide outer sash—still holding the warmth of her back—and draped it across Qingxiao’s ankles where the white stockings showed past the short bunk. Within three breaths, a fine white hoarfrost feathered the silver-blue silk at the hems.

Iuno sat back down in the officer’s chair at the foot of the bed, tipped the back of her head against the cabin wall, and let her eyes close—not meaning to sleep, only resting them from the unchanging glare of the porthole. Within five minutes, exhaustion took her anyway; her breathing lengthened out, her right hand resting loosely over the dark face of her Terminal in her lap.

Yangyang sat down on the floorboards beside the head of the bunk, tucking her layered skirt around her shins and resting her right shoulder against the upright post.

To keep her own eyes open in the hush of the cabin, and to keep from straining her ears after a city she could not reach, Yangyang began to hum under her breath.

She didn’t even realize which tune she had picked up until the third bar: the slow, four-beat walking cadence of the Hold’s old marching song, the one she had sung through the smoke of the gorge and taught back to Liangyu on the overlook above the water spires.

She hummed the call softly, barely louder than the gimbal lamp’s wick, and when she reached the turn at the end of the fourth measure—the rising note Liangyu’s family had carried down through two centuries—a quiet, level voice spoke from the bolster above her head:

“Sharp on the fourth.”

Yangyang paused, looking up over the timber rim of the mattress.

Qingxiao’s eyes were still shut. She hadn’t moved a finger under the frosted wool.

“The garrison sang it a half-step lower before the rain,” Qingxiao murmured, and hummed three notes—spare, cold, and pure as a struck jade chime, dropping the fourth beat down into an older cadence that made the timber wall behind them vibrate in sympathy.

Then Qingxiao went quiet again.

Yangyang waited a breath, adjusted her pitch down the half-step to match the two-hundred-year-old note, and hummed the verse through once more before letting the room fall back into silence.

For a long time after that, nothing moved in the cabin.

Yangyang kept her back against the post, her right hand resting on the hilt of her sword and her left tucked lightly over her bound ribs. Because the walls wouldn’t let her hear past the hull, her ears settled into the small sounds inside the room: the slow, even four-count of Iuno’s breathing from the chair, the dry tick of the gimbal lamp, and the shallow, winter-quiet rise and fall of Qingxiao’s chest under the wool blanket a foot above her ear.

Somewhere in the middle of the watch, underneath Qingxiao’s breathing, Yangyang heard a second breath.

It was very faint, dry as rustling paper, and half a beat out of step with the rise of the grey blanket—drawing in just as Qingxiao’s breath went out.

Yangyang turned her head a fraction against the post to listen for it. The instant she focused her hearing, there was only one breath on the bolster again, and the low, rhythmic wheeze of the Shuttle’s air-trunks behind the cedar bulkhead. She decided it was the ship’s draft echoing in the double wall.

A little later, across the narrow floor, the seven-stringed paulownia qin hanging untouched on its silk cords against the wall gave a single, low *thrum*—the open seventh string sounding once in the still air.

Yangyang glanced at the wall where no wind was moving, then looked up at the edge of the mattress. On top of the grey wool blanket, Qingxiao’s sleeping right hand had uncurled by half an inch, her forefinger hooked slightly inward in the exact shape that would have plucked a low string.

Beside Yangyang’s boot, the pale rime on the floorboards had stopped creeping outward toward the map table. In the dim orange light from the desk, the frost crystals along the wood grain had grown into thin, needle-straight lines two inches long, and every one of their sharp points was angled backward, pointing inward toward the bunk. Where Qingxiao’s left wrist lay just outside the rough wool collar of the blanket, one of the green jade beads on the worn red cord had a hair-thin white crack running from string-hole to rim that hadn’t been there when she held the bronze gunwale on the barge.

In the wooden chair at the foot of the bed, Iuno stirred once in her sleep, her brow creasing against the wall.

*Darling,* the High Priestess murmured out of the dark, so heavy with sleep she barely shaped the words. *How many of us are in this room?*

Iuno’s eyelids lifted a fraction against the amber gloom. She looked blurry-eyed at Yangyang sitting by the bunk post, at Qingxiao under the frosted wool blanket, and down at her own boots.

*Three,* Iuno thought vaguely, and her chin tipped back against the cedar planking before the next breath came.

Near the end of the watch, the pale ribbon of frequency rising from Qingxiao’s wrist into the ceiling beams went completely slack for the space of a single heartbeat—dipping toward the floorboards like a cut string—before it snapped taut again with a silent jerk, tighter than before, its moon-white edge darkened by a single shade toward the color of winter twilight.

On the hard bolster above Yangyang’s head, Qingxiao murmured three syllables in her sleep in her own level voice, and half a breath later answered herself in the exact same voice, too low against the linen ticking for Yangyang to make out the words.

Yangyang didn’t know what to make of any of it, and didn’t try to put a name to it. The cerulean feathers at her collar lifted once against the chill coming off the bunk rail; she pulled her knees an inch closer to her chest, shifted her shoulder nearer the upright post, and kept watch.

***

Yangyang didn’t mean to close her eyes for more than the length of a breath, her chin dipping once toward her collar.

When she lifted her head, the bunk beside her was already empty of the blanket.

Neither Yangyang nor Iuno had heard the wool rustle or the timber slats creak. Qingxiao was sitting upright on the edge of the mattress with her boots planted flat on the frosted floorboards, the grey blanket folded into a neat square beside her hip and Iuno’s silver-blue sash laid smoothly across the top of it.

Across the room, Iuno woke at the sudden drop in temperature, straightening in the officer’s chair as the crystal at her left shoulder chimed a single, cold note.

Qingxiao looked steadier than she had since they kicked open the nephrite disk at the Astrolabe’s doorway. The slight tremor in her cuffs was gone; her spine was plumb-line straight, her moon-white dress unwrinkled, and the cold radiating off her skin was no longer the ragged, shivering chill of someone bleeding her strength into the air—it was the still, locked cold of a frozen lake in the twelfth month. To Iuno and Yangyang, blinking away the stiffness of the floorboards and the chair slats, it looked as though two hours of rest had brought the Sword Transcendent back from the edge.

Above her wrists, the taut ribbon of frequency still ran straight through the ceiling beams, darker by that single shade and humming without a tremor. She hadn’t thinned a single thread.

Even as Iuno leaned forward to pick up her sash, the fourth strike hit the link.

It was the heaviest one yet—hard enough that the iron spikes inside the ceiling beams overhead gave a sharp, metallic groan under the sympathetic pressure, and the porcelain teacup sitting on the lacquered tray cracked cleanly in half with a loud *clink*, spilling tasteless water across the map table.

Qingxiao’s face did not move at all.

She did not flinch, her right shoulder did not dip by the breadth of a hair, and her pale blue eyes did not blink.

“It held,” Qingxiao said.

Yangyang and Iuno exchanged a quick glance across the narrow floor—relief first, and behind it a brief, unsettled surprise at how easily she had just taken a blow that split porcelain three feet away.

Iuno stood up, shaking the last flakes of rime from her sash and tying it back around her waist one-handed, pulling the knot firm with her teeth and her right hand. “The notes from the Nether Lamp said this Ministry Shuttle is the upper bridge between the Di Realm and the Tian Realm.”

“Muyu’s real anchor has to be on the top spire above the court,” Yangyang said, rising slowly from the floorboards with one hand bracing the bunk post and checking the silk-wrapped Prism at her waist. “Not a projection this time. If he’s routing the Censure Courts through the shuttle defenses, the central spindle is at the top of the lift.”

“Five decks up,” Qingxiao said, rising to her feet without putting a hand on the bunk rail. “Through the tribunal hall.”

Out of habit from the companionway climb before the rest, Yangyang stepped close to Qingxiao’s right side and lowered her shoulder half an inch to take the Paragon’s arm.

Qingxiao looked once at the offered shoulder, then walked past her toward the door on steady, soundless feet.

“Keep your right hand for your hilt,” Qingxiao said evenly. “The passage is narrow.”

Yangyang lowered her arm, a little surprised, and settled her fingers on her pommel. “Yes, Paragon.”

Iuno stepped to the threshold, dismissed the wedged moonwheel back into a fading spark at her wrist, and dragged the heavy brass bolt out of its socket. The door swung open onto the dim, empty corridor of the Ministry deck.

They went out in the exact order they had come in: Yangyang first to read the air in the passage, Iuno second with the silver-blue light gathering at her fingertips, and Qingxiao last.

As Qingxiao stepped toward the open threshold, she passed the round brass porthole where the Mirage’s false, unmoving gold light shone in from the clouds.

She turned her head toward the corridor to follow the other two out.

In the thick dark glass of the porthole, against the unmoving gold, her reflection stayed looking into the room for half a beat longer before it turned away to follow her.

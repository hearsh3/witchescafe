Simulacrum of the Heart 5

The north lane climbed between high whitewashed garden walls where the copper noon lay flat and hot on the cedar blocks, except for a forty-foot stretch above the third sluice-culvert where the afternoon had already given up.

There, between a stone water-trough and an overhanging loquat bough, the air was a cool, smoky violet dusk. Crickets were chirping in the dry weeds behind the coping stones, the smell of wet ditch-mud rose up off the terrace below, and Longjue sat on the waist-high parapet with one bare foot tucked up under her knee and the other swinging three inches above the grass.

She had shed the indigo work-tunic and the hemp calf-bindings. A long teal over-robe with wide, trailing sleeves hung loose over a short white tunic, the silk pooling across the dressed granite beside a small red lacquer gourd with teal tassels tied to its waist-cord. Above her temples, her ornate antler-like dragon crests caught the dim evening violet on their pale jade-white ornaments, and the intricate teal markings along the side of her throat and down her bare shin gleamed faintly whenever the smoke curled past her chin.

As Buling came trudging up the white noon of the lane with the empty tallow-rag still stuffed in her sash, Longjue tapped the grey ash of a hand-rolled joint into the drainage ditch she had cleared that morning.

“Oi,” Longjue called across the shadow line. “Walk slower, my head hurts looking at you.”

Buling stopped on the sunlit cedar blocks, stepped across the seam between noon and twilight, and let out a long breath as the heat dropped off her shoulders. She reached up with two fingers, flipped the yellow paper talisman off her forehead onto the round brim of her *nuanmao* hat, and dropped her backside onto the stone wall two feet from Longjue’s bare toes.

“Your head never hurts,” Buling said, stretching her black-wrapped shins out along the coping. “And Ruoyun is waiting for eggs. If I’m late, she’s going to stand on my neck while I fry them.”

“Let her stand, she weighs nothing.” Longjue pinched the paper cylinder between two fingers and held it out, glowing tip first, before reaching down with her other hand to unstop the small red gourd and pass it across the stone. “Here. Wash the mutton-grease out of your mouth first.”

Buling took the gourd, tipped a mouthful of clear, fiery baijiu down her throat without touching the rim to her lips, and let out a low hiss through her teeth as the grain-spirit hit her chest. She handed the gourd back, took the smoke, and drew a short puff that floated up into the loquat leaves smelling of dry mountain herb and toasted paper.

Longjue took a drink from the gourd herself, wiped her mouth with the back of her wrist, and leaned her elbow on her raised knee.

“So,” Longjue said, her voice loud and easy in the small pocket of dusk. “A hundred years and you still can’t grease a bearing without getting black gunk on your nose. Look at your cheek. You look like you stuck your face up a stove-pipe.”

“The collar was dry!” Buling protested, scrubbing furiously at her cheekbone with the back of her clean sleeve and handing the smoke back. “Nobody touched that south trunnion since I left! What were you all doing down here, sitting on your hands?”

“I work the orchards, I don’t climb Ruoyun’s stupid clock,” Longjue said cheerfully, taking a deep drag and blowing a grey ring straight at the brass bell on Buling’s sash. “Anyway, I’ve had better things to do. Waiting for you to come back and pay the forty strings of cash you owe me from the winter solstice game.”

Buling’s red eyes narrowed under her hat-brim. “What forty strings?! It was twelve! And you stacked the west wind tiles under your sleeve!”

“My sleeves were rolled up to my elbows that night, you shameless little cheat, you folded on a pure bamboo hand because you were scared of my discard!” Longjue slapped her bare thigh with a sharp crack and barked a laugh that sent two sparrows whirring out of the loquat tree. “Then you said you were going to the latrine and walked all the way to Jinzhou! Who goes to the latrine for a century over twelve strings of cash?!”

“I had clients!” Buling shouted back, kicking her heel against the side of the wall. “Real clients, with silver! Not like playing tiles with you and Old Chen where if I win, I get three bruised pears and a turnip!”

“Chen’s turnips are terrible,” Longjue agreed at once. “We got Gu sitting in your chair now. It’s a disaster. He takes four minutes to discard a single north wind, he lines his tiles up with a carpenter’s rule, and he doesn’t drink. Last week I threw a walnut at his forehead just to see if he’d blink, and he asked me if the roof was flaking.”

Buling snorted, leaning back on both palms and swinging her cloth shoes against the granite. “Serve you right. Should’ve saved the walnut for Chen.”

“Chen ducks,” Longjue said fondly, and took another pull from the red gourd before setting it between them on the wall.

For a few breaths neither of them spoke. Down in the drainage ditch below Longjue’s bare heel, the clear water ran steady and quiet over the wedged iron latch. Thirty yards up the lane, out in the bright copper noon of the square, the masons’ wooden mallets went *tock, tock, tock* against the dry ridge-tiles.

Longjue turned the paper cylinder slowly between her thumb and forefinger, watching the red coal creep down the crease.

“When you came up the south stair this morning,” Longjue said, her voice dropping out of the roar into her plain, even orchard cadence, “I was standing by the switch-pile.”

“Ya,” Buling said. “With the pruning hook.”

“You’ve been gone since before the old willow by the well fell down,” Longjue said. “When I saw your hat coming round the loquat wall, all I thought was that the weeding basket under the plum shed had a hole in it. It didn’t even cross my mind to ask where you’d been until I was back down at the third ditch driving the iron wedge in.”

Buling stopped swinging her feet.

Longjue tapped the ash off the tip of the roll. “Then we walked through the gatehouse. Little Lin was carrying the plum laths. He looked right at your yellow paper and your red eyes and said *Morning, Buling*, the exact same way he says it to Gu when Gu comes back from the timber-yard. Nobody dropped a chisel. Nobody came out of the drafting hall to look.”

“Maybe they’re all scared Ruoyun will bite their ears off if they stop working,” Buling said, though her voice didn’t have much bounce in it.

“Since when has this village ever been scared of Ruoyun?” Longjue said. She looked down at her own hand holding the smoke. “Three nights ago—or three afternoons ago, whenever the fruit started jumping—we were at the table in the back shed. Me, Gu, Chen, and the mason’s girl. I drew the nine of characters, the red dragon, and four east winds off the wall. I slammed them down. Two hands later, I drew the exact same tiles in the exact same order. Even the chipped corner on the second east wind was in the same place under my thumb.”

Buling looked sideways at her. “Somebody didn’t shuffle the wall properly.”

“I shuffled the wall,” Longjue said flatly. “When I threw the tiles face-up and asked Chen if he’d seen that hand twenty minutes ago, he just looked at the board and told me it was my discard. And twice since the sky went white, I’ve been sitting right here on this coping, and I’ve looked down and found a smoke burned all the way down to my knuckles without remembering striking the flint.”

In the violet dusk under the loquat bough, the crickets gave a dry, even trill—*chirp, chirp, pause, chirp*—and then repeated the exact same three notes at the exact same pitch.

Buling reached into her side pocket, pulled out her small brass feng-shui compass, and flipped the lid open on her knee.

Inside the glass housing, the blued-steel needle didn’t point south, and it didn’t spin. It drifted slowly toward the western hills, trembled when it touched the twenty-fourth house on the rim, and slid back three marks as if someone were nudging it with a blade of grass.

“This thing has been drunk since we landed on the terrace yesterday,” Buling muttered, snapping the lid shut with her thumb. “And the north dormitory—when I went in there for the buns just now, the kitchen stove is roaring hot, six baskets of brown eggs are sitting behind the rack without a speck of dust on the shells, and every blanket in the hall is folded sharp. Not a single footprint in the ash-pan except mine from the buns. Out in the square, the scribes are scrolling the same three screens of sluice-water up and down their glass all morning.”

She tucked the compass back into her pocket and rubbed the side of her nose.

“Only person in the whole Vale who acted like I actually walked out the gate was Ruoyun,” Buling said. “Screamed my ear off on the wall topside, then screamed my other ear off at the porch just now.”

“Ruoyun was topside for three days,” Longjue said.

They both sat still on the coping for a moment. Out in the sunlit lane beyond the trough, a scribe walked past with a bundle of ruled slates under his arm, his boots clicking at a brisk, unvarying pace toward the well-pavilion.

Longjue took one last drag from the short stub of the roll, pinched the coal dead between her bare calloused fingertips without flinching, and tucked the paper end into the dry seam of the wall.

“The back road goes out through the upper gate behind the bamboo grove,” Longjue said, turning her head toward the high western ridge where the white-tiled roofs ended against the rock. Her brows drew together slightly over the bridge of her nose. “Past the boundary stones toward the upper cistern. Nobody from the orchard crew has walked past those stones since… I don’t know when. I haven’t been up there since the loquats set their buds, and I didn’t think to wonder why until right now.”

Buling slid off the stone wall onto the grass, hitching the waist of her royal-blue cheongsam straight.

“Okay,” Buling said. “Let’s go look, then. Take five minutes to grab the eggs first so Ruoyun doesn’t come after us with the bronze lever, then I’ll go fetch Ciaccona from the pavilion.”

Longjue’s bare foot came down off the coping. All the loose, table-slapping noise vanished from her posture between one breath and the next; she stood balanced and still in the dusk, her wide teal sleeves hanging straight by her sides.

“The foreigner with the pipa,” Longjue said quietly.

“It’s a lute, don’t call it a pipa again or she’ll give you a twenty-minute lecture on woodworking,” Buling said, flipping her yellow talisman back down over her forehead. “And she’s coming with us.”

Longjue looked toward the square. “This is Vale business, Buling.”

“She kept my copper bell in her pocket across three provinces until a sea-monster ate it, and she gave me the bigger half of her rice-cake last night when she hadn’t eaten all day,” Buling said plainly. “She’s got good ears, and I’m not leaving her sitting in the square while everyone’s brains are leaking out the sluice. You want me to come up the ridge or not?”

Longjue looked at her for two beats, then bent down, picked up the small red gourd by its teal tassel-cord, and tied it back onto her sash.

“Get your eggs,” Longjue said, and walked out of the violet dusk toward the upper gate without another word.

Buling trotted up the remaining steps to the north dormitory compound, ducked through the steaming, empty kitchen where six bamboo racks of red-bean buns sat puffing white vapor into the rafters, and snatched the top willow basket of brown eggs from behind the stand. Coming back down the outer slate stair two steps at a time, her heel skidded on a patch of frost that hadn’t been on the stone ten minutes earlier; the basket banged hard against the balustrade, and one egg at the side of the pile gave a wet *crunch*.

“Aiya, *shit*,” Buling muttered, steadying herself on the rail.

She didn’t stop to wipe the shell. She carried the basket down the white cedar street to the porch of the third west room—which stood empty now, the three-legged wooden stool sitting unoccupied by the threshold while Ruoyun was down the side alley terrorizing the cookhouse—and set the willow basket squarely on the top riser beside the door-jamb.

From inside her sleeve, Buling pulled a blank yellow talisman slip and a stub of black charcoal pencil. Bracing the paper against the cedar post, she scrawled four crooked columns down the middle:

*Eggs here. One broke on the frost stair, still can fry if you pick the shell out. Going up the back ridge road with Longjue and the bard to check the upper boundary stones, compass is acting funny. Back when we’re back. Don’t touch my compass box under the cot.*

She impaled the top of the yellow slip onto the iron peg beside the door-frame, gave it a slap so it wouldn’t blow off, and marched across the square toward the corner pavilion.

Under the white-tiled eaves, the kaya-wood weiqi board was half covered in black and white stones. Ciaccona sat with her chin propped in her good right palm and her splinted left forefinger sticking out over the edge of the table, staring fiercely at a tangled dragon of white clam-shell discs that had somehow managed to keep two open spaces in the middle of Qingxiao’s black net.

Across from her, Qingxiao sat with her hands resting loosely in her moon-white lap, watching Ciaccona’s brow furrow.

“Come,” Buling said, stopping at the bottom step of the pavilion and jerking her thumb toward the western arch. “Pack the lute. Field trip.”

Ciaccona looked up from the board. “Now? I’m in the middle of not dying in the lower right corner. It’s a historic moment for Ragunnese diplomacy.”

“The corner can wait, Longjue’s already at the gate,” Buling said quickly, her eyes flicking once to Qingxiao’s face. “Shifu. We’re going up the back road past the boundary stones to check the upper ditch-line. The bearings are slipping.”

Qingxiao looked from Buling’s round winter hat to the far western arch, where Longjue stood motionless in the shadow of the white brick wall with her bare feet planted on the cedar blocks and her antler-like crests silhouetted against the copper mist.

“The lower group has two eyes,” Qingxiao said mildly to Ciaccona. “It will live until you return.”

Ciaccona grinned, rising from the stone stool and reaching for her open velvet case. “You heard the master, Buling—certified survivor. I expect a full tavern ballad written about that corner.”

She laid the lute carefully inside the green velvet, latched the brass clasps with her right hand, and slung the strap over her shoulder.

As Buling turned to follow her down the steps, Qingxiao spoke again in the same level, unhurried voice:

“Buling.”

Buling froze on the bottom riser, her shoulders coming up half an inch around her ears. “Yes, Shifu?”

“Keep your back straight when you draw the talisman,” Qingxiao said. “You are still dropping your right elbow when the spark jumps.”

Buling blinked once under the yellow paper on her forehead, then let out a short breath through her nose and bowed her head properly over her hands.

“Yes, Shifu,” Buling said, and turned after Ciaccona.

At the western gatehouse, Longjue didn’t look round as their boots approached across the square. When Ciaccona came abreast of her under the arch and offered a cheerful two-fingered salute from the brim of her feathered hat, Longjue only inclined her head a bare inch, stepped out onto the packed earth of the ridge track on Buling’s far side so the smaller woman walked between them, and set off up the hill without a sound from her bare soles.

Where the living bough came down off the red limestone crag on the eastern side of the gorge, the scarlet maple trees grew closer together until their branches locked overhead like the ribs of a vault, and when the five of them walked out from under the leaves ten minutes later, the shadowless white noon had stayed behind in the trees.

Below them lay a shallow, bowl-shaped hollow tucked into the shoulder of the hill, holding a clear, crisp autumn evening just after sundown.

The sky above the ridge was the deep indigo of wet slate, shading down to a warm apricot band along the western horizon where the giant fox’s bowed silhouette stood dark against the afterglow. Down in the hollow stood a small country temple built of grey granite and weathered red timber, its courtyard gates propped wide open between two stone lanterns.

Inside the courtyard, four rows of canvas-roofed festival stalls had been set up along the flagstones, every one of them lit with strings of round red and yellow paper lanterns that swayed gently in the cool evening breeze. Charcoal braziers glowed orange under wide iron pans where chestnuts were roasting in black river-sand, popping softly now and then in the quiet; bamboo racks bristled with sticky red skewers of candied hawthorn; a shallow wooden tub painted blue on the inside sat under a lantern with twenty red-and-gold fantail goldfish swimming lazy circles around floating duckweed; and at the far end of the yard, a tiny clockwork shooting gallery clattered away by itself, three rows of painted tin ducks and wooden foxes riding back and forth on brass gear-tracks with nobody behind the counter.

The woven green bough ran straight down the slope, passed right between the open granite gateposts of the courtyard, and went on out through the rear moon-gate into the dark trees beyond. In Amy’s palm, the rose-gold needle at the heart of the metal lotus pointed straight down the middle of the lantern aisle.

Lupa’s boots came off the bark onto the courtyard flagstones first, and her right knee gave a sudden, traitor’s dip that made the butt of her ash spear ring hard against the paving.

She caught herself at once, jerking her chin up and glaring at the nearest lantern-pole as if the wood had tripped her.

Behind her, Iuno stepped off the bough, took one look at the way Lupa’s left heel was bearing all her weight while the right leg stayed slightly bent under her red skirt, and walked past her toward the wide stone steps of the temple porch.

“We are stopping for twenty minutes,” Iuno announced to the courtyard.

“We just ate breakfast an hour ago, princess,” Lupa growled, not moving her feet from the flagstone. “I’m fine. The branch goes right through the back door.”

“And your right knee is currently vibrating like a plucked harp-string,” Iuno said, turning on the bottom step of the porch. “Sit on the riser, Lupa. That is not a suggestion.”

Before Lupa could bare her teeth, Cartethyia gasped so loudly that the goldfish in the blue tub scattered under the duckweed.

“They have the ring-toss!” Cartethyia cried, pointing both hands at a stall draped in red bunting where bamboo hoops lay stacked on a counter in front of three tiers of carved wooden prizes. “And the little tin ducks! Amy, look, you get a cork gun!”

Amy’s eyes went straight from the cork gun to the exposed brass gear-train rattling beneath the painted tin ducks. She tucked the open metal lotus into Iuno’s crooked right arm as she trotted past the porch steps.

“Hold the compass, Auntie, Carte and I are clearing the prize rack!” Amy called over her shoulder.

“Do not eat anything that has been sitting out since the last dynasty!” Iuno called after her, though Amy was already vaulting the low rope by the shooting stall.

Iuno set the metal lotus down on the flat stone balustrade of the temple porch, walked back to the middle of the lane where Lupa was still standing stiff-legged by the lantern-post, and waited with her head tilted slightly to the right.

Chisa walked past both of them, climbed three steps up the temple porch, unslung the heavy leather case of her scissor-blade, and sat down cross-legged on the smooth granite with her back against a red cedar pillar.

“The stone’s dry,” Chisa said to the courtyard.

Lupa scowled ferociously at the empty hawthorn stall, dragged her feet across the ten yards of paving, and dropped her backside onto the second stone riser three feet from Chisa’s knee, stretching her long legs out down the steps with a muffled grunt she tried to turn into a cough.

Iuno did not sit down beside them.

Instead, she walked across the lantern-lit aisle to the charcoal brazier where the chestnuts were roasting in the black sand. She reached into her coat pocket with her right hand, took out two square bronze Huanglong cash coins from the Hold’s market, and laid them neatly side by side on the scrubbed wooden counter next to the iron scoop. Then she folded a square of brown wrapping-paper into a cone with her fingers, scooped six hot, split-shelled chestnuts out of the sand, and carried them back to the porch steps.

She sat down on the third riser, one step above Lupa’s left shoulder, laid the paper cone on her knee, and picked up the largest chestnut in her right hand.

“What are you doing,” Lupa said suspiciously, turning her head as the smell of scorched sweet-chestnut drifted past her ear.

“Peeling a chestnut,” Iuno said.

Because she had only her right hand, she braced the hot brown shell against the wool of her thigh, hooked her thumbnail into the split seam, and caught the edge of the husk between her front teeth to strip the bitter inner skin away from the yellow meat. The shell was piping hot from the black sand; a tiny flake of brown ash stuck to her lower lip, and the crystal at her left shoulder gave a faint chime as she twisted the nut free.

She held the peeled, steaming yellow kernel out over Lupa’s shoulder on her open palm.

Lupa stared at it as if Iuno had just offered her a live scorpion.

“I’ve got two hands, princess!” Lupa barked, her wolf-ears flattening sideways as the red crept up her throat. “Give me the bag if you don’t want ’em, I don’t need you chewing my food like I’m a puppy!”

“I did not chew it, I used my teeth on the husk because my left hand is currently in Septimont,” Iuno said calmly, not moving her palm a hair’s breadth from Lupa’s chin. “And if I hand you the shell, you will drop half of it in the dirt because your fingers are still stiff from gripping your spear across that canyon. Take the chestnut, Lupa.”

*If she refuses, darling, drop it down her collar,* the High Priestess suggested mildly.

Lupa’s jaw worked. She shot a sidelong glance at Chisa—who was looking peacefully out across the courtyard toward the clacking tin ducks—and then snatched the warm chestnut off Iuno’s palm with two fingers and shoved it into her mouth, chewing with her face turned hard toward the gatepost.

Iuno picked up the second chestnut from the paper cone, braced it against her knee, and hooked her thumbnail into the seam again.

Over at the game stalls across the courtyard, three cork guns lay lined up on a red-painted plank, tethered to brass chains, with a wooden bowl of fat brown corks sitting between them.

Cartethyia didn’t bother aiming down the barrel. She wedged the wooden butt of the first gun against her hip, pumped the iron lever underneath with a ferocious *clack-chunk*, and pulled the trigger at the middle row of tin ducks.

*POP.*

The cork flew across the six-foot gap hard enough to whistle, struck the third tin duck squarely in the breastbone, and bent the entire sheet-metal bird backward on its brass hinge with a ringing *CLANG* that echoed off the temple eaves.

“Ha!” Cartethyia shouted, pumping the lever again with her crystal laurel tipping over one eyebrow. “Did you see that, Amy?! Right in the chest! Your turn—wait, no, that one’s still wiggling, I’m shooting it again!”

*POP—CLANG.*

“Carte, you’re supposed to hit the little red bullseye on the wing so the latch drops, not bend the whole track in half!” Amy laughed, leaning both elbows on the counter beside her and picking up the second gun without putting a cork in the muzzle yet. “Watch the bottom row where the little wooden foxes are running. Look at the brass cog on the left pillar.”

Cartethyia paused with her thumb on the cork bowl and leaned across the plank to squint at the mechanism.

Out beyond the western moon-gate, above the dark outline of the hills, the apricot band of light around the giant fox’s folded arms dimmed for half a beat.

Inside the shooting stall, the brass drive-cog on the left pillar gave a tiny, dry *tick-skip*. Every painted wooden fox on the bottom track froze dead in the middle of its stride for a full second before the light in the west steadied and the chain jerked forward again.

Amy’s grin went wide and wicked. She jammed a cork into the barrel, pumped the lever once, and didn’t look at the wooden foxes at all—she turned her head sideways to watch the western sky over the temple wall.

“Wait for it…” Amy murmured, her finger resting on the trigger. “Wait for it…”

Thirty seconds later, the apricot glow flickered.

Before the tin ducks on the top row had even finished shuddering to a stop, Amy whipped the barrel round without aiming down the sight and pulled the trigger on the exact stalled tooth of the master release-lever at the end of the bar.

*POP—clack-clack-clack-clack-clack!*

Every single target on all three tiers dropped flat onto its back at the exact same instant, and a little brass bell inside the roof of the stall rang out a frantic, tinny victory fanfare while a red wooden placard with the character *PRIZE* flopped down from the lintel.

Cartethyia’s mouth fell open. She looked from the twenty-four flattened targets to Amy’s smoking cork-barrel.

“You didn’t even shoot a duck!” Cartethyia said indignantly. “You shot the wall while the light blinked!”

“It’s called frame-perfect timing, Carte, it’s a legitimate speedrun strat,” Amy said smugly, laying the gun down on the plank and leaning over the counter to inspect the top shelf of prizes. “Come on, the sign’s down, that means we each get one off the top hook. What are you taking?”

Cartethyia set her gun down, though she gave the bent tin duck in the middle row one last satisfied poke with her finger to make sure it stayed flat. For a moment the two of them stood side by side in the warm red lantern-light, looking up at the row of dusty silk pouches, painted clay figurines, and lacquered boxes dangling from the rafters.

Amy reached up, unhooked a small painted clay goose with a chipped orange beak, and turned it over in her hands.

“Hey, Carte,” Amy said conversationally, without looking away from the clay goose. “Out on that big tree-branch over the canyon earlier, when you and Lupa were yelling at each other.”

“She bit my bracer in that fountain,” Cartethyia said immediately, standing on tiptoe to peer at a carved wooden frog on the third hook. “She says it was an accident, but wolves don’t accidentally open their mouths when they’re losing.”

“Yeah, I know, Lupa cheats at cards too,” Amy said, leaning her hip against the stall post. “I meant the part after that. About waking up when the shutters are latched all the way, and not liking when the roof comes down low.”

Cartethyia’s hand paused on the string of the wooden frog. She lowered her heels back to the flagstones and looked down at Amy.

In the red glow of the paper lanterns, Amy’s pink hair was completely steady—no white static at the tips, no light dripping from her fingers—as she turned the clay goose round and round between her thumbs.

“Yesterday when the square folded up over our heads,” Amy said in a plain, quick voice, “right before we hit the moss in that tower. For a second it was completely black, and the air had that dead, flat feel like the inside of a helmet when the external cameras go out. I hated it. When we were sleeping in the mill last night, I made sure my bench had a view of the crack under the kitchen door so I could see the stove-light.”

Cartethyia looked at her quietly for two beats, the crystal thorns of her laurel catching the warm lantern light.

“In the seminary,” Cartethyia said at her ordinary, clear volume, “there wasn’t any stove-light, and the stone didn’t have cracks in it. So I used to name the corners of the room after the streets around Uncle Cosimo’s bakery, and then I knew which way the oven was even with my eyes shut.”

She reached out and tapped the clay goose on its chipped beak with one knuckle.

“Next time we’re in a dark chute,” Cartethyia said simply, “I’ll hold your other sleeve, and I’ll tell you which way the bakery is. I’m very good at keeping track of the floor.”

Amy looked up from the tin, blinked once, and then smiled—not the loud, theatrical grin she used when she was calling Iuno *Auntie*, just a small, real one that crinkled her nose.

“Deal,” Amy said, tucking the clay goose into her skirt pocket beside the leftover pancake leaf. “This one’s for Ma. She’ll put it on the windowsill and talk to it. And if there’s a brass gear in the dark, I’ll break the lock for you so you don’t have to punch it. What are you getting off the rack? That frog’s ugly.”

“The frog is terrible,” Cartethyia agreed, abandoning it at once and pointing two hooks to the right, where a tiny, thumb-sized tuning pipe of polished boxwood hung on a green silk cord. “That one. For Cia. When we’re in the rain, her wooden pitch-pipe swells up and goes sharp, and then she grumbles at her third string for an hour.”

She unhooked the boxwood pipe, blew through it once—*toot*, a clean, sweet *A* that made the goldfish in the blue tub flick their tails—and tucked the green cord safely inside her collar.

“Come on,” Cartethyia said, her eyes narrowing competitively at the stack of bamboo hoops on the next stall over. “Three hoops each. If I get the big porcelain cat at the back before you do, you have to tell Lupa I won the fountain brawl.”

“Oh, you’re on, knight-girl, prepare to get wrecked.”

Across the courtyard on the temple steps, Iuno was working on the fourth chestnut.

Her technique had improved slightly by the third: she wedged the flat side of the hot shell into the space between her knee and the stone riser, cracked the dome with the heel of her palm, and peeled the husk back in two clean strips with her thumb before holding the warm yellow nut out over Lupa’s shoulder.

Lupa didn’t argue this time, though she still muttered under her breath as she took it from Iuno’s fingers.

“You got ash on your chin, princess,” Lupa grumbled around the chestnut, jerking her thumb at her own lower lip.

Iuno wiped the back of her wrist across her chin, missed the spot by half an inch, and picked up the fifth chestnut from the paper cone.

“On the bridge over the gorge,” Iuno said quietly, her eyes on the brown shell in her hand, “when the knight picked that ridiculous quarrel with you so you would not look down at the rocks.”

Lupa went stiff on the step below her. “I wasn’t looking at the rocks! And I told you, she cheated in Ragunna—”

“I have no doubt she cheated,” Iuno said smoothly. “She is four feet tall and turns into a cathedral statue when inconvenienced; I would have fined her fifty gold myself.”

She cracked the fifth shell against the stone riser and stripped the husk away with her thumbnail.

“What I meant to say,” Iuno continued in the same measured, low voice, “is that I was frightened for you the whole way across, and I would rather you knew it than guessed.”

Iuno held the fifth chestnut out over Lupa’s shoulder.

Lupa sat completely motionless on the second riser.

Her mouth opened slightly, her yellow eyes wide and fixed on the lantern-post across the lane, and the tips of her wolf-ears burned a dark, fierce crimson against her red hair. For three full seconds she didn’t even breathe; then she snatched the fifth chestnut off Iuno’s palm, shoved it into her mouth whole, and glared down at her own boots.

“Shut up, princess,” Lupa mumbled thickly through the chestnut. “You’re bad at peeling these. Half the fuzzy skin’s still on it.”

“Then peel the sixth one yourself,” Iuno said dryly, dropping the paper cone with the last hot chestnut into Lupa’s lap and dusting the brown crumbs off her skirt with her single hand.

Three feet to Lupa’s right, leaning back against the red cedar pillar with her eyes half-shut in the warm lantern glow, Chisa reached over and picked the last chestnut out of the paper cone in Lupa’s lap, cracked it cleanly in half between her bare fingers, and put one half in Lupa’s hand before eating the other herself.

“She missed the spot on her chin too,” Chisa said in her flat voice.

Iuno’s hand went immediately back to her lower lip, rubbing the other side until the speck of black sand came off on her knuckle, while Lupa let out a sudden, barking laugh that made her right knee jump against the stone—without a single flinch this time.

Across the yard, a loud crash of toppling wooden blocks announced that Cartethyia had thrown a bamboo hoop hard enough to knock the entire middle shelf of the ring-toss stall into the aisle. A moment later, Amy and Cartethyia came trotting back across the flagstones—Amy with the clay goose’s head poking out of her skirt pocket, and Cartethyia proudly carrying a foot-high, hideously painted earthenware badger with a chipped ear under one arm.

“That is not a cat,” Iuno said, rising from the porch step and picking up the metal lotus from the balustrade.

“The cat was glued to the shelf,” Cartethyia explained cheerfully, adjusting the clay badger under her elbow as Lupa stood up from the riser and stretched her right leg out twice—steady and firm on the stone. “And Lupa said she wanted something to put by the door to scare the mice.”

“That thing’ll scare the whole province,” Lupa snorted, slinging her ash spear onto her shoulder and stepping back onto the woven green bough where it ran toward the rear moon-gate. “Come on, navigator. Point the flower.”

Amy took the metal lotus from Iuno, hopped onto the bark beside Chisa, and held the open filigree petals out ahead of them as the five of them walked out through the round stone arch into the dark autumn trees, leaving the red and yellow paper lanterns swinging quietly over the roasting chestnuts behind them.

Down on the lower reach of the pewter river, the fine Mengzhou drizzle fell straight and windless out of the slate-grey cloud bank, ticking like salt against the broad leaves of the weeds along the bank.

The timber lock-house at the end of the granite wharf stood with its door unlatched and its oiled-paper window dry under the eaves. While Mei leaned her shoulder against the door-frame to keep her boots out of the puddle on the sill, Suoming and Yangyang stepped inside.

The room was ten feet square, smelled of dry river-rush and tallow, and had no one in it. A copper kettle sat cold on a three-legged trivet beside the hearth; a pair of greased leather waders hung from a wooden peg by the sluice-wheel; and on the slanted pine desk by the window lay a thick, blue-covered lock-ledger held open with a smooth river stone. Every column on the open page was ruled in neat red ink for the date, the barge-master’s mark, and the draught in thumbs, and not a single character had been brushed onto the paper.

Across the scrubbed floorboards, the shed flakes of black vine-scale ran straight out the back water-door, down the mossy granite steps of the barge-stair, and into the dark water beyond the closed timber sluice-gates—where the greasy black trail didn’t sink, but clung in a submerged ribbon along the dressed stone of the river wall four inches below the surface.

Out past the lock, where the river widened between low banks of grey willow and mist, the vast, slow breath at the western end of the valley rolled down the face of the water like air drawing through a tunnel: a long, heavy *in*, a pause that held the rain-dimples still, and a long, warm *out*.

Moored to the iron ring at the bottom of the barge-stair sat a thirty-foot flat-bottomed river lighter. Its midships hold was stacked three tiers high with tarpaulin-covered bales of coarse straw-paper under a curved tunnel-canopy of woven bamboo mats, and a single long ash steering-sweep was lashed to the wooden thole-pin at the stern.

Suoming walked down the wet granite steps without opening Nine Shadows, stepped over the low gunwale onto the stern-sheets with her iron heels clicking once on the wet pine planking, and laid the furled umbrella down along the rib-timbers.

With her uninjured left hand, she slipped the hemp lashing off the steering-sweep, while Yangyang untied the bow-painter from the iron ring and pushed the flat nose of the lighter clear of the wall with her boot.

The current past the sluice was slow, dark, and heavy as oil.

Suoming planted her small boots wide on the wet stern-board, tucked the thick ash loom of the sweep under her left armpit, and leaned her weight across the deck to work the blade back and forth in the water—*heave, turn, pull*.

On the third stroke, she had to bring her bandaged right forearm across her stomach to catch the wrist-pin as the twelve-foot oar bit the eddy. Under the wet plum wool of her coat, her chest lifted and caught hard against the bottom of her left ribs—a dry, rasping hitch that stopped the ash handle halfway through the turn—and a dark spot the size of a plum-stone bloomed fresh through the clean white linen pad Mei had tied over her wrist at breakfast.

Yangyang was already walking back along the narrow side-wale from the bow.

She didn’t reach out to take the wood out of Suoming’s hand, and she didn’t look down at the red spot on the linen. She stopped beside the bamboo canopy arch with her rain-darkened blue hair clinging to her cheeks and her hands resting plain at her sides.

“Elder,” Yangyang said quietly over the hiss of the drizzle on the water. “I’ve only ever rowed a two-oar skiff on the Jinzhou canal. Will you show me how to work the single sweep?”

Suoming held the ash loom tucked under her left arm for two beats, her pale eyes open and clear in the grey rain as she looked up at Yangyang’s chin.

Then she stepped half a pace to the left, clearing the middle of the stern-board, and tapped the iron band around the sweep-handle with two fingers.

“Feet apart, facing the port rail,” Suoming said in her flat voice. “Don’t push it like an oar. Leave it on the pin and roll the wrist so the blade cuts eight-wise in the wash. Pull when the tip’s to starboard, lean when it goes back.”

Yangyang stepped onto the wet planks, planted her boots where Suoming’s heels had been, and took the thick ash loom in both hands.

On her first push, she held her wrists stiff the way one braces a sword-hilt against a parry; the flat of the sweep caught the river sideways with a heavy *slap*, and the bow of the lighter swung three points toward the willow bank before Yangyang caught her balance.

“Too flat,” Suoming said from her elbow. “Drop your right thumb when the blade reaches the wake. Let the water turn the wood.”

Yangyang loosened her fingers around the wet ash, dropped her right thumb on the return swing, and felt the broad underwater blade bite the current on the bevel—gliding smoothly across the wake in a figure-of-eight that pulled the heavy paper-barge straight back into the middle of the channel without jarring her seventh rib.

She did it a second time, and a third, her shoulders settling into the slow, rocking sway of the stern-sheets while the rain beaded on the cerulean feathers at her collar.

Suoming watched the wake straighten out behind the stern-post for four strokes.

“You’ve got a light wrist for a soldier,” Suoming said, and turned toward the midships canopy where Mei was standing with both hands braced on the bamboo arch and her wet grey shirt sticking to her shoulders. “You. Under the mat. If you stand in the wet wind after a hot stove, your side’s going to stiffen up before we reach the bend.”

“Hey, I can bail water or watch the bank or something,” Mei protested, though she was already shivering slightly as the cool river draft hit her damp ribs. “I’m not cargo!”

“There’s no water in the bilge, and the bank’s ten feet away,” Suoming said. “Lie down on the dry canvas. Take your wet shoes off the bale.”

Mei looked from Suoming’s flat face to Yangyang, who gave a single, tiny nod over the sweep-handle. With a theatrical sigh, Mei ducked under the woven bamboo roof, kicked her mud-splashed road-boots off onto the floorboards, and curled up on her right side atop a wide tarpaulin-wrapped bale of straw-paper, pulling a dry corner of the canvas flap over her shoulders. Within two hundred yards of the lock-gate, as the barge rocked in the slow, rhythmic *swish—turn—swish* of Yangyang’s sweep, Mei’s eyes dropped shut and her breathing went deep and even under the mat.

Out on the open stern-board, the drizzle fell steadily over the river.

Suoming sat down on the low wooden transom bench at the very back of the boat, resting her bandaged right arm across her lap and her left hand on the furled bamboo shaft of Nine Shadows beside her knee. Her iron-plated boots hung an inch clear of the wet bilge-grating.

For a quarter of a mile neither of them spoke. The river curved gently between high stone embankments where the black vine-scale showed dark and oily just beneath the lapping water on their left, and overhead the slate-grey cloud ceiling hung low enough to brush the tops of the warehouse cranes along the shore.

Yangyang kept the ash sweep rolling in its quiet eight, her eyes on the channel ahead.

Because Suoming was sitting three feet to her left with nothing to look at but the water and Yangyang’s boots, the silence wasn’t the easy kind they had shared with Mei chattering between them on the wharf. Twice Yangyang shifted her grip an inch up the handle, opened her mouth to ask if her stroke was drifting too close to the wall, saw that the wake was dead straight behind the rudder-post, and shut her mouth again.

Suoming didn’t look at the wake. She tilted her chin slightly toward the western bend where the grey mist hung thick over the water.

“What’s the breath doing,” Suoming said.

Yangyang listened past the *hiss-patter* of the rain on the bamboo canopy and the creak of the wooden thole-pin.

“It’s lower in the chest than it was at the lock,” Yangyang said plainly. “When we were up on the arcade this morning, there was a little catch at the top of the draw, like someone sleeping with their chin crooked on their arm. Now it’s even all the way down. And there’s a second sound underneath it.”

Suoming’s pale eyes moved from the river to the side of Yangyang’s face. “The rot?”

“No,” Yangyang said, rolling her wrist smoothly on the starboard turn. “The rot doesn’t breathe, it only scrapes against the stone like dry husks. This is like… when a wooden water-wheel has one split paddle that hits the trough heavier than the rest. Every fourth breath, something hard taps against the floor under the water.”

Suoming looked back down the grey reach of the river.

“Good,” Suoming said. “Keep the bow two yards off the left wall. The silt builds up by the drain-mouths.”

Yangyang leaned her hip into the loom, bringing the barge’s nose a hair out from the embankment. The rain ran down the brim of her hat and dripped off the white tips of her hair onto her cloud-worked cuffs.

After another hundred yards of quiet water, Suoming spoke again to the wet planks between her boots:

“The golden-eyed one told me about the goose.”

Yangyang blinked once, her hands pausing a fraction of a beat on the ash sweep before finishing the stroke.

“What happened at your end of the table before the shells went up,” Suoming said, her voice completely flat and practical. “You weren’t chasing the bird.”

Yangyang’s ears went faintly pink beneath her wet locks. She wasn’t Mei; she couldn’t turn a mess-table into a three-act play with her hands. She stared straight ahead at the bow-post and gave it the way she gave a watch-log to the duty clerk in Jinzhou.

“At the second table by the brazier, Warden Liangyu and two of the young pike-men from the south wall were eating the pickled radish,” Yangyang said, her voice level and careful. “The younger boy—the one who lost his helmet in the ditch at Yuan Fortress—hadn’t had Mengzhou pepper-oil before. He put two full ladles of the red sediment from the bottom of the jar onto his noodles because he thought it was sweet bean-paste.”

On the transom bench, Suoming’s head turned half an inch toward her.

“When he took the first bite, he didn’t want the older Wardens to see him coughing,” Yangyang continued earnestly, working the sweep back and forth, “so he swallowed it whole and sat there with his back straight and water running out of both his eyes onto his tunic. Liangyu saw him, didn’t say anything, and went back to the kitchen cauldron to bring him a bowl of plain hot noodle-broth without salt in it. Then she told the rest of the bench that the cook had forgotten to season the second pot and made everyone drink a cup of the plain water so the boy wouldn’t be the only one.”

Yangyang stopped. She looked down at her thumbs on the wet ash wood.

“That’s all,” Yangyang said. “Nobody dropped anything.”

Suoming looked at her for three slow strokes of the sweep.

The fine rain beaded on the gilded orb hairpin in Suoming’s red hair and ran down the collar of her faded plum coat. Her face didn’t move, and her boots didn’t swing.

“The red sediment at the bottom of Wen’s jars is three parts dried lantern-chili to one part sesame,” Suoming said. “If you drink hot water on top of it, it burns twice. You’re supposed to eat a crust of cold steamed bread.”

A tiny breath of a laugh escaped Yangyang’s nose before she could catch it.

“I’ll tell him when we get back up,” Yangyang said.

The lighter glided past a half-sunken stone mile-pillar where the black vine-scale lay thick as tar along the waterline. Yangyang kept her eyes on the dark water ahead for a moment, then glanced down at the small woman sitting beside her knee.

“Elder,” Yangyang asked quietly, “when you were telling Mei about the small house with the grey tiles and the south window—have you already picked out which town it’s in?”

Suoming looked out over the port rail at the wet willow branches trailing in the river.

For four breaths only the *creak-thump* of the ash sweep in the wooden thole-pin answered.

“There’s a village six leagues south of the old Huating road where the river bends round a chalk cliff,” Suoming said. “They don’t have a stone wall, only a thorn hedge to keep the pigs out of the cabbage, and the roof-tiles come from the grey kiln across the ferry. Bring your left shoulder down when you lean on the return, Watcher, you’re lifting the blade out of the wash.”

Yangyang dropped her left shoulder half an inch without another word, letting the broad blade stay buried in the current as the barge slid under the shadow of a high timber footbridge.

As the bow came out from beneath the bridge timbers on the far side, the steady grey drizzle stopped touching the water.

Behind the stern-post, a curtain of silver rain still dimpled the river from bank to bank; ahead of the bow lay a wide, curving reach of pale gold morning mist, cool and pearly as the hour before sunrise, with red water-lilies floating closed among the reeds and a thin, diffused rose light glowing through the fog from the western peaks.

There was no glare in the mist, only a soft, pearl-coloured twilight that cast no shadows on the deck. Suoming looked up at the pale rose fog overhead, kept her left hand resting quiet on the furled bamboo shaft of Nine Shadows, and did not touch the catch.

Under the woven bamboo canopy midships, the tarpaulin rustled.

Mei stuck her head out from under the mat with her hair flattened on one side and a red crease from the canvas seam across her cheek, blinking sleepily through the pearl-pink mist at the back of the boat—where Yangyang stood swaying easily with the twelve-foot ash sweep in both hands like a born river-pilot, and Suoming sat dry-eyed on the transom bench beside her knee with her boots swinging half an inch above the grating.

“Wait a second,” Mei rasped, rubbing her nose with her palm as she sat up on the paper bale. “How long was I out? Are we in a different Tuesday, or did Yangyang just steal a boat?”

“Twenty minutes,” Suoming said without turning around. “And put your boots on before you step on the deck, the planks are wet.”

Back in Mengxuan Vale, the shadowless copper noon still lay white and hot across the empty porch of the third west room.

Ruoyun came marching round the corner of the kitchen alley with a wooden ladle still clamped in her right fist and two bright spots of temper burning on her cheekbones after twenty minutes of standing over the junior clerk at the millet-bin.

She got one Mary Jane shoe onto the bottom cedar riser and stopped dead.

Sitting squarely on the top step beside the door-jamb was the willow basket of brown eggs from the northern dormitory, and impaled on the iron door-peg above it hung a crooked yellow talisman slip fluttering slightly in the draft.

Ruoyun marched up the four steps, snatched the yellow paper off the iron spike so hard the top hole tore through, and held it six inches in front of her round brass spectacles.

Her bright blue eyes went down the four charcoal columns once. Then she looked down at the willow basket, where a smear of sticky yellow yolk oozed out of a cracked brown shell on the left side of the pile.

For three full seconds Ruoyun stood so still on the stone sill that the ruffled white cuffs at her wrists didn’t stir.

“Up the back ridge road,” Ruoyun said aloud to the empty porch, her voice low, flat, and shaking with pure venom. “With Longjue. And the foreigner. *Back when we’re back.*”

She glared out across the bright cedar square toward the western gatehouse arch—which stood wide open and completely empty beside the turning brass astrolabe.

“Give a runaway ten minutes off the leash to fetch a basket of eggs,” Ruoyun snarled at the white roof-tiles, “and she drags the only decent ditch-keeper in the village out the back gate with a tavern strummer! When I get my hands on her ear again, I’m nailing that round hat to the storehouse door with her head still inside it!”

Behind her, the footsteps of the Sword Transcendent came quietly across the cedar planks from the corner pavilion.

Ruoyun didn’t turn around to soften her face. She folded the torn yellow talisman slip once down the middle, and then a second time into a neat, sharp two-inch square between her thumb and forefinger, and tucked it flat inside the inner band of her left cuff before picking up the willow basket by its handle.

“Your runaway former disciple left you a broken egg and a note,” Ruoyun snapped as Qingxiao reached the foot of the steps. “She’s gone wandering out the upper gate with Longjue and the lute-player because her compass is *acting funny*.”

Qingxiao looked at the willow basket in Ruoyun’s hand, then toward the narrow side alley where the thin plume of blue woodsmoke was still wavering up from the brick flue.

“Bring the basket,” Qingxiao said mildly, and turned down the alley toward the cookhouse.

Inside the low brick kitchen, the junior Celestial who had been drafted to watch the hearth was standing five paces back from the firebox holding a split pine shingle like a shield, his face smudged with grey ash from the eyebrows down. The moment Qingxiao stepped over the stone threshold, the boy bowed nearly to his knees, laid the shingle on the bench, and backed out into the lane so fast his heel clipped the water-butt.

On the iron grate over the birch coals sat a wide, flat pan of black cast iron, dry and smoking slightly at the rim, beside a stoneware crock of clear rapeseed oil and a porcelain dish of coarse grey salt.

Ruoyun set the egg basket down on the butcher’s block and reached for the wooden ladle she had left on the hob.

“Go sit on the bench outside, A-Qing,” Ruoyun said briskly, pushing her ruffled sleeves higher up her forearms. “I’ll have these done in two minutes—”

Qingxiao stepped lightly to the side of the hearth, took the wooden ladle out of Ruoyun’s hand by the middle of the shaft, and laid it flat on the butcher’s block.

“You burned the water this morning,” Qingxiao said in her level voice. “Sit down.”

Ruoyun’s mouth opened behind her spectacles. “I didn’t burn the water, the pan had—”

“Sit,” Qingxiao said, without raising her voice by half a tone.

Ruoyun’s jaw snapped shut. With her spine stiff as a ramrod and her chin high, she marched three steps to the low pine bench against the whitewashed wall, sat down on the edge of the plank with her knees together and her hands folded tightly over her pleated teal skirt, and watched the stove.

Qingxiao did not summon a flying blade or weave a single thread of frequency.

She picked up the stoneware oil-crock with both warm, bare hands, tipped a thin circle of golden rapeseed oil into the center of the hot iron pan, and tilted the pan by its iron handle with a folded rag wrapped round her palm until the oil coated the metal cleanly without smoking. Then she picked up the cracked egg from the top of the willow basket, tapped the split seam once against the edge of the block, and opened the shell with her thumbs—catching the single loose fleck of brown shell on the tip of her right forefinger before it touched the white.

The egg hit the iron with a crisp, gentle *shhhhh*.

Qingxiao stood in front of the red glow of the firebox with her moon-white dress tucked back behind her knees and Ruoyun’s bone clasp holding her blue hair neat at her crown, using a flat bamboo turner to nudge the bubbling white edge so it didn’t stick to the iron. When the rim turned golden-brown and lace-crisp while the yolk stayed soft in the middle, she slid the turner underneath, lifted the egg onto a small blue-and-white saucer, sprinkled three grains of coarse salt across the top, and cracked three more into the pan.

Before she sat down to her own plate, Qingxiao walked across the flagged floor and set the first saucer with the single golden-edged egg squarely on the pine table in front of Ruoyun’s knees, laying a pair of bamboo chopsticks beside it.

Ruoyun looked down at the saucer. Her small golden horns caught the orange firelight as she lifted her chin.

“I don’t eat,” Ruoyun said stiffly. “You know we don’t need food down here, A-Qing. Keep it for yourself.”

Qingxiao turned back to the stove to lift the remaining three eggs onto a second dish, carried it to the opposite side of the narrow table, and sat down on the bench facing her.

“Eat it,” Qingxiao said, picking up her chopsticks.

Ruoyun glared across the twenty inches of scrubbed pine at the top of Qingxiao’s bowed head. For two beats her hand didn’t move from her lap; then she snatched up the bamboo chopsticks, pinched the crisp golden edge of the egg white, and bit into it.

It wasn’t burned. The yolk was warm and rich, the rapeseed oil tasted cleanly of autumn fields, and the three grains of salt crunched once between her teeth. Ruoyun chewed in silence, her shoulders dropping a bare half-inch out of their ramrod line as she finished the second half of the saucer.

Across the table, Qingxiao ate at her steady, unhurried pace until two of her three eggs were gone. Then she rested the tips of her chopsticks on the rim of her dish and looked across the table at Ruoyun’s spectacles.

“Before the dawn watch,” Qingxiao said quietly, “when you were sitting on the stool in the doorway. Mingxiao and the drafter came to the steps.”

Ruoyun’s chopsticks paused over her empty saucer. She set them down neatly side by side on the wood.

“You were asleep,” Ruoyun said, a sharp edge of accusation coming straight back into her voice. “I pulled the door to.”

“My ears still work when I am lying down,” Qingxiao said mildly. “What did they show you on the slates?”

Ruoyun looked away from her toward the red birch coals shifting in the grate. Her small hands closed into fists on her knees, the knuckles white against the teal cloth.

“The Vale is coming loose at the seams, and it’s sliding faster since the Sanctum floor dropped,” Ruoyun said, her voice clipped and hard and furious. “And not a single one of these ink-lickers has laid eyes on the Fox walking in her own skin since before Gu came on the books.”

She turned her head sharply back toward Qingxiao, her blue eyes bright and fierce behind the brass rims.

“They just stand out there in the noon glare tapping their chisels and scrolling the same three columns of water-load up and down their glass like clockwork dolls, A-Qing, and when I ask Mingxiao what’s sitting in the middle of the Sanctum, she bows at my shoes and walks off!”

The fire popped once in the brick chimney. Outside in the bright lane, the distant *clank—pause—hum* of the greased astrolabe turned through the quiet.

Qingxiao did not frown, and she did not look toward the western sky. She picked up her chopsticks again, lifted the third fried egg from her dish, and laid it neatly onto the center of Ruoyun’s empty saucer.

“The oil in that crock is good,” Qingxiao said in her level voice. “Pass the salt.”

Ruoyun stared down at the warm egg sitting on her porcelain dish for a long breath, her throat working once above her high collar; then she reached across the pine plank with two clean fingers, pushed the little porcelain salt-cellar over to Qingxiao’s wrist, and picked up her chopsticks again without a word.

Past the white-glazed brick of Mengxuan Vale’s upper gatehouse, the cedar paving stopped at a mossy stone curb, and a narrow dirt track climbed westward along the spine of the mountain between scrub pine and dry winter fern.

Up here above the orchard walls, the valley had lost its grip on the horizon.

The copper-white sky still hung shadowless overhead, and far out at the western end of the basin the colossal silhouette of the fox still stood with her head bowed on her folded arms—except that the rocky ridgelines flanking her shoulders sat faintly doubled against the glare, like a woodblock page where the printer’s hand had slipped the thickness of a fingernail on the second pull of the press, leaving every pine tree and limestone crag with a pale, transparent twin hanging two inches to the left of the solid stone.

Every hundred paces along the shoulder of the track stood a waist-high boundary pillar of dressed grey granite. None of them had a single character cut into the tablet face; the chiselled panels were smooth and blank as washed slates.

For the first quarter-mile out of the gate, Longjue walked on the outer lip of the ridge with Buling in the middle and Ciaccona on the inner bank, her bare feet stepping over sharp flint-chips and pine-cones without a sound or a glance at the dirt.

Ciaccona, who had never met a silence in a tavern she couldn’t coax into buying her a glass of wine within three minutes, tilted her feathered hat back against the sun and leaned forward half a pace to catch Longjue’s eye past Buling’s round winter cap.

“I have to say, madonna,” Ciaccona began in her warmest, most musical Ragunnese cadence, “for a village that hasn’t seen a broom in the guest-rooms since the last war, your orchards down there are a triumph. In Rinascita the terraced vineyards above Portoberto have half the slope yours do, and the masons spend every spring fishing their own retaining walls out of the creek.”

Longjue looked ahead at a blank granite boundary stone where its faint, double-printed shadow wavered in the grass.

“The stone here is harder,” Longjue said evenly. “Watch the root by your left boot. It’s slick.”

Ciaccona skipped neatly over the exposed pine root and kept her sunny smile pinned in place. “And the headwear! Those crests—are those part of the official uniform for the agricultural department, or do you only put them on when you’re sneaking out the back gate to avoid the lady with the spectacles?”

“They’re mine,” Longjue said, her voice flat, polite, and entirely closed.

Between them, Buling rolled her red eyes under her yellow forehead-talisman and talked twice as fast to fill the gap.

“Don’t bother sweet-talking her, Ciaccona, when Longjue doesn’t know you she’s got less words than a milestone,” Buling chattered, waving both hands as she marched up the grade. “Wait until you see the upper cistern—if the water-gate up there is stuck on the winter mark, that’s why the frost came out on the dormitory stair just now and cracked my egg! Not my fault at all, the stone was dry when I went up and turned into an ice-rink when I came down! Anyway, if Ruoyun complains about one shell in the pan after I greased her whole astrolabe for free—”

“You didn’t grease it for free,” Longjue said mildly. “You stole four buns and a basket of eggs.”

“That was rations for the crew! Crew always gets rations!”

Ten minutes further up the ridge, where the track levelled out beside a dry gully choked with wild chrysanthemums, stood a tiny wayside shrine no taller than Buling’s waist—three slabs of grey slate roofed with moss-grown tile, housing a blank stone tablet inside.

On the flat offering-stone in front of the tablet sat a clean white porcelain saucer holding three bright orange persimmons with the dew still wet on their skins, and sticking out of the bronze sand-pot beside them were three sticks of sandalwood incense burning with thin, straight ribbons of grey smoke.

There wasn’t a single footprint in the soft red clay around the shrine.

Longjue stopped beside the slate roof. She looked down at the three burning incense sticks—where the grey ash at the tips had grown an inch long without dropping into the sand—and then looked out across the doubled ridgelines toward the west.

Without a word, she reached into the breast of her short white tunic, pulled out a narrow paper cylinder twisted shut at both ends, and struck a tiny thumb-flint against a steel ring on her sash—*chink*.

She lit the roll, took two slow puffs until the mountain herb caught red and fragrant, and then unhooked the small red lacquer gourd from her waist-cord.

She handed the gourd and the smoke to Buling first.

Buling flipped her yellow talisman up onto her hat-brim, took a quick swig of the baijiu and a drag on the herb, and held the red gourd back out toward Longjue.

Longjue didn’t take it yet.

She looked across Buling’s shoulder at Ciaccona—who had propped her boot on the dry stone bank and was fanning her neck with her good right hand while her splinted left finger rested on the strap of her velvet lute-case—and held the red gourd out past Buling’s nose by its teal tassel.

“Thirsty?” Longjue asked.

Ciaccona’s eyebrows went up. “Signora, I thought you’d never ask. What is it, plum wine?”

“Grain,” Longjue said.

“Don’t drink too big a mouthful—” Buling started.

Ciaccona had already taken the gourd in her right hand, tipped her head back with the practiced grace of a woman raised on dockside taverns from Ragunna to Lilyland, and poured a generous swallow straight down her throat.

Ciaccona stopped moving.

Every drop of color left her face for one heartbeat, and then came roaring back from her collarbones to the roots of her red hair. Her eyes watered so hard the doubled mountain ridges turned into four; she clapped her splinted left hand over her windpipe, bent double over her lute-case, and let out a strangled, wheezing *hoo—hah—Madonna santissima* that sounded like a bellows with a hole in the leather.

“What—” Ciaccona rasped, her voice an octave lower than usual as she held the little red gourd out at arm’s length and stared at it in horror, “—what did you distil that out of, siege-artillery powder and lamp oil?! I think my ancestors just felt that in their graves!”

On the outer edge of the track, the corner of Longjue’s mouth twitched.

“It’s sixty-day sorghum from the lower vat,” Longjue said, taking the gourd back and tipping a long drink into her own mouth as if it were well-water. “Warms the toes.”

“My toes are currently on fire, thank you, along with my eyebrows,” Ciaccona wheezed, wiping a tear off her cheek with her thumb and glaring across at Buling, who was sniggering behind her sleeve. “And you! You stood right there and watched me take a wine-swallow of liquid lightning! Some consultant you are!”

“I told you don’t drink too big!” Buling cackled, taking another puff of the smoke and blowing it toward the shrine. “You’re the one who bragged all the way across Septimont that nobody in Huanglong can drink a Toccata under the table!”

“That was wine, you fraudulent little corpse, not varnish remover!” Ciaccona straightened up, cleared her throat twice until her voice found its normal ring, and looked straight at Longjue with a wicked glint in her eye. “Does she always let other people walk into the ditch first around here, or did she pick that up on the road?”

Longjue’s shoulders dropped a full inch. She took the smoke back from Buling’s fingers, propped one bare foot up on the base of the wayside shrine, and grinned so wide her jade crest-ornaments glinted.

“Walk into the ditch?” Longjue said, her voice jumping three notches in volume as the quiet stranger-mask came clean off. “For fifty years before she ran off, this one was my morning sparring partner on the top terrace! Every third dawn—every single one!—I climb the steps, I do my breathing forms, I turn around to square up, and she’s already lying flat on her back in the dirt with her arms crossed over her chest like she’s back in the coffin, yelling *I surrender, my knee is twisted, go put the kettle on!*”

“Oi! Tell the truth, I blocked your kick once in the rain-year!” Buling squawked, spinning on Longjue with both hands out.

“You slipped in the mud and your boot hit my shin while you were falling down!” Longjue roared, slapping the side of the stone shrine with her open palm and laughing with her whole torso. “I didn’t even get my guard up! Then she tried to bill me two strings of cash for bruise-salve!”

Ciaccona let out a delighted peal of laughter and reached back for the red gourd in Longjue’s hand. “Give me that gourd again—in smaller doses, for the love of heaven—because that is *exactly* what she did in Septimont! Listen to this: we were sitting at an outdoor café near the lower forum, right before the Games started, and two city bailiffs came down the street looking for unlicensed fortune-tellers.”

“They were tax collectors, not bailiffs!” Buling shouted, trying to grab the gourd cord as Ciaccona took a tiny, cautious sip of the baijiu and winced happily.

“Tax collectors, bailiffs, they had brass badges and a ledger,” Ciaccona told Longjue, waving her splinted finger for emphasis. “Before they even got to the third awning, this master of the mystic arts here—this ancient, terrifying Celestial of Xuanfang Hold—slid halfway under the tablecloth, put her headphones over her eyes, and told me to tell them she was my deaf-mute cousin from the provinces who had eaten a bad oyster!”

Longjue threw her head back and howled, the laugh booming off the doubled limestone crags across the gully and coming back a beat later—*ha-ha-ha*—like a second tavern opening its doors in the rock.

“Deaf-mute cousin!” Longjue wheezed, wiping her eyes with the wide teal sleeve of her robe and shoving Buling’s shoulder hard enough to make the brass sash-bell jangle. “With a mouth that never shuts from dawn to dusk! How much did she charge you for the oyster story?!”

“Ten silver pieces and a cup of thick coffee with three spoons of sugar!” Ciaccona said indignantly. “And then she shoved her copper bell into my hand, told me the feng-shui of the plaza was ‘very sour,’ and vanished down a baker’s alley before the waiter brought the bill!”

“The feng-shui *was* sour!” Buling yelled at both of them, her yellow talisman flapping up and down as she stamped her black cloth shoe in the dirt. “The whole city had a giant black storm-cloud sitting under the arena floor! If I stayed there drinking coffee with you, I would’ve got drafted into fighting gladiators for free! You think I’m stupid like you two?!”

Ciaccona’s laugh quieted into a wry, sideways smile as she handed the red gourd back to Longjue.

“No, you’re not stupid, Buling,” Ciaccona said, her voice dropping its theatrical curl into something plain and dry and surgical. “You just make sure your shoes are already pointed toward the ferry-landing the second anybody starts liking having you at the table, so you can tell yourself it was a business trip when you walk out.”

Buling’s mouth shut with a little click.

She stood in the middle of the dirt track with her hands half-raised by her chest, looking up at Ciaccona’s face. For two beats she didn’t make a sound about invoices or retainers; she just tugged the round brim of her *nuanmao* hat down a fraction of an inch over her forehead and kicked a pine-cone into the weeds.

On the other side of the shrine, Longjue stopped laughing.

She looked at Ciaccona—really looked at her, from the mud dried on her Ragunnese boots to the splinted finger resting on the velvet lute-case—and then she let out a single, booming bark of a laugh, stepped straight across the dirt track behind Buling’s back, and clapped her bare, earth-calloused palm squarely down on Ciaccona’s good right shoulder hard enough to rock her into the bank.

“All right, Pipa!” Longjue shouted, grinning from ear to ear as she shoved the red gourd back into Ciaccona’s hand and took the inner side of the road beside her. “You’re in! Gu is fired from the fourth chair as of right now! Tonight when we get back down to the shed, you’re sitting east wind, and we’re fleecing this little corpse for every copper she made in Septimont!”

“It’s a *lute*,” Ciaccona said, grinning right back as she took a second, braver nip from the gourd without coughing this time, “and if your game is anything like your shifu’s board back in the square, I’m bringing a stick to defend my corner.”

“Mahjong isn’t like weiqi, you don’t have to think at all, you just yell when you get three of the same picture and slam the wood down hard enough to scare the next player!” Longjue roared, rolling another herb cylinder one-handed out of her tunic pocket as the three of them started back up the steep ridge road abreast. “Wait till you see Buling play when she’s losing—she stacks her wall so straight you could check a plumb-line on it, and then when somebody steals her discard she knocks the whole row into the soup with her sleeve and says a ghost bumped the table!”

“One time!” Buling yelled, marching between them with her hands chopping the air and her ears pink under her hat. “That was one time in the flood-year, and the table leg *was* crooked! Anyway, three players and a beginner who doesn’t even know what a bamboo tile looks like isn’t a proper game, she’s going to deal straight into your hand!”

“Then we teach her on the road!” Longjue barked, striking her thumb-flint—*chink*—and blowing a cloud of sweet blue smoke up into the shadowless noon as her bare feet took a patch of loose shale without slipping a hair’s breadth. “Or we grab one of your noisy friends when they come up the hill! Who else did you bring down the chute with you?”

“Let’s see,” Ciaccona said, counting on her three good left fingers as the baijiu spread a pleasant, roaring hearth-fire through her ribs. “If you put Cartethyia at the table, she’ll feel sorry for the lonely wind tiles and refuse to throw them out. Lupa will challenge the table to a spear-fight the second she loses two hands in a row. Chisa will win every single round without looking at the pictures because she can hear which tile has an extra millimeter of ink carved into the bone—”

“Wait, she can hear the carving?” Longjue demanded, her eyes lighting up. “Keep her fifty yards away from the shed, I’ve got three marked dragons in that box from the old dynasty!”

“—and if you invite Iuno,” Ciaccona finished cheerfully, unlatching the brass clasps of her velvet case while she walked and pulling the lute out onto her hip by its strap, “she’ll write a twelve-clause treaty on the scoring rules before anyone draws a tile, and then our capo will eat the red-bean counters because she thinks they’re candy.”

“Put the capo in Gu’s chair!” Longjue bellowed, slapping her thigh. “Anyone who eats the counters can sit next to me! Come on, Pipa, play something on the belly-wood, this hill’s too quiet!”

It was, on the whole, a very good thing for the structural integrity of Mengxuan Vale’s masonry and the blood pressure of Ruoyun that the three of them had already cleared the upper boundary wall by half a mile.

Ciaccona hooked the woven strap over her right shoulder, tucked the round maple bowl of the lute against her hip, and barred the bottom three courses with her ring finger and pinky while her splinted forefinger pointed heroically at the western sky. She struck a fast, bouncing six-eight tavern jig from the Ragunnese salt-wharves—*thrum-chink-chink, thrum-chink-chink*—and Buling immediately began slapping the brass bell at her sash against her thigh on the off-beat so it rang out like a mule-driver’s tambourine: *ting, ting, ting*.

“Oh, I know this one!” Longjue shouted over the strings, taking the red gourd back from Buling and tipping a long swallow down her throat without breaking her smooth, balanced stride up the flinty track. “The boatmen at the Mengzhou timber-boom sing this when the rafts come down!”

“This is a Ragunnese fisherman’s song about a bailiff’s daughter and a barrel of anchovies, you cannot possibly know it!” Ciaccona shouted back, grinning around the neck of the lute.

“Same tune, different fish!” Longjue roared, and launched at the top of her lungs into a bawdy Huanglong river-shanty that was half a tone flat, three beats too fast for Ciaccona’s thumb, and concerned a magistrate’s clerk who fell into a pickle-vat in his best silk boots.

Ciaccona didn’t bother trying to pull her back into key; she simply slid her left ring finger up two frets to chase Longjue’s wandering pitch, belted the Ragunnese chorus about the anchovy barrel right over the top of the pickle-vat verse, and let Buling yell the third line of both versions in Singlish whenever she remembered a rhyme:

*“—and the bailiff’s wife came down the stair, hey! / —with two salt turnips in her hair, lah!”*

All the way up the bare spine of the ridge, as Longjue waved the smoking herb-roll in one hand and the red lacquer gourd in the other—her voice rattling the dry ferns and her laugh booming out every time Ciaccona rhymed *gondolier* with *sour beer*—her bare, teal-marked feet never once missed their placement on the broken rock, stepping heel-to-ball over rolling gravel and wet moss with the silent, dead-level precision of fifty years on the dawn terrace.

Halfway to the upper crest, where the dirt track curved between two tall, blank granite boundary stones toward a dark stone cistern house on the bluff above, the copper-white glare in the western sky dimmed.

Far out across the basin, the pastel spokes of rose and lilac light around the giant fox’s bowed head flickered down to a flat, leaden grey for half a heartbeat.

Ciaccona’s thumb had just struck the open D-chord at the end of the fourth chorus, and Longjue had just bellowed the last word of the refrain across the gully.

Instead of bouncing straight back off the limestone bluff ahead of them, the echo hung dead in the grey air for a full second after the light came back—and then rolled back across the doubled ridgelines from two different directions at once, half a tone lower than they had sung it:

*…hair, lah… hair…*

On the dirt track between the blank boundary stones, Ciaccona’s right thumb came down flat across the gut strings to mute the wood. Buling’s hand closed over the brass bell at her sash so it stopped jangling.

One pace ahead of them, Longjue’s bare right foot stopped mid-step above a flat piece of slate, held perfectly balanced on her left sole for the space of a single breath as she looked out past the doubled pine trees toward the giant’s unmoving shoulders, and then set her heel down on the rock.

She lifted the red lacquer gourd, took a short drink, and held the tassel-cord out behind her without looking back.

“Your third string’s slipping again, Pipa,” Longjue called out loudly, her bare feet already taking the steep grade up toward the cistern wall. “Turn the peg and start the one about the baker’s widow, I know the second verse to that!”

“There is no baker’s widow in the anchovy song, you absolute heathen!” Ciaccona shouted back, twisting the third pearwood peg a hair upward with her teeth and striking the chord twice as loud as before while Buling’s brass bell went *ting-ting-ting* beside her hip, the three of them climbing on up the empty ridge road between the blank stones with the smoke trailing blue behind them.

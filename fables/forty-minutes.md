## I. The Old Route

The rules were on the fridge in her own handwriting, under a magnet shaped like a seal.

*1. Number on the radio, twice a day. Different one every time.*
*2. Chisa has the frequency. Chisa listens whether I call or not, and I agreed to that in writing.*
*3. Four days. On the fourth I come down whether I've done it or not.*
*4. Eat.*
*5. If the drawer opens, it opens up here, with the door shut, and I say it out loud to the room first.*
*6. Nobody comes. NOBODY COMES. I mean it.*

Rule six had taken eleven minutes at a kitchen table and two votes. Lupa had signed it under protest. Cartethyia had made a small sound partway through and been sent to fetch more tea.

It was the third morning. Amy put on her coat and her boots and the hat with the ears, and stood in the front room with her hand on the latch of a blue door doing the thing she now did every time she touched a door, which was inventory: gloves, key on its cord, thermos, handheld rig with the antenna folded down, and a length of dried herring wrapped in wax paper, because some appointments you keep properly or you do not keep them at all.

Then she went out into a country she had last crossed with a house key going cold against her collarbone.

The snow came down the way it did up here, slow and enormous and holding no opinion. The air stood still. The flakes fell at the pace of somebody strolling, and the sky they came out of was four kilometres up and had made its decision about them forty minutes ago and gone on somewhere else entirely, and Amy had known that fact since she was nine, because a woman with a folder and city boots had explained it on the walk from the shuttle pad, thinking she was making conversation.

She had thought about that woman a great deal lately. She had been extremely brave about her ankles.

The path took the shoulder of the hill and then the long shallow drop toward the lake, and it was the same path, and that was the trouble with it. Her legs knew where the buried stone was and stepped high over bare ground. Her hand went out at the third fence post for a rail that had come down in some winter she had spent as a hum in a machine over a city. She had walked this route four hundred times as a child and once as a nineteen-year-old, and the body doing it today had been assembled out of borrowed light in a place with no ground in it, and it kept arriving at the corners half a step early, like an understudy who had learned the blocking from a photograph.

So she counted. Fence posts, then the crows that had gone south, then the seconds a single flake took to come down through the gap between her hood and her cheek. And she said her sentence out loud to the snow, because that was rule five and she had been failing it for three days.

"Ma. There's a thing I know."

The snow took it at the same speed it took everything.

"Ma. In the spring, there's a thing—"

She stopped, because it had come out as an apology again. It came out as an apology every single time. Twenty-two years of a machine in her throat that produced the courteous version before the true one could get its coat on, and she had believed — standing in a dormitory kitchen with the whole house shouting at her at high volume for eleven weeks — that they had taken the machine apart. They had taken a great deal of it apart. There was a second one underneath, smaller, older, running on nothing.

She reached the lake at the hour when the light gets committed. The frostlands did what they always did, which was continue: white to the circumference of the world, the drifts combed into long parallel sentences nobody had ever read aloud, and over all of it the pearled ceiling of the sky with a grey smear of sun going along its top edge like a coin under a cloth.

There was a woman standing on the ice.

Amy's hand was on the rig before she finished registering it, which was new, and which she intended to mention to nobody. Then she looked properly and did the arithmetic of the silhouette — the height, the fall of the sleeves, the small dark shape at the hip — and put the rig away, and stood in the falling snow feeling her whole chest do something she had no filing category for.

The woman had been out there long enough to collect weather. Snow lay on her shoulders and in the white of her hair and along the arms of the hairpin, all of it exactly where it had landed, unbrushed, unmelted. She was watching the sky come down. She had, as far as Amy could tell from a hundred metres, been watching the sky come down for some time.

And the ice ran right up to her boots and past them.

That was what brought Amy out onto the lake instead of calling from the bank. Everywhere else in the world you found Hiyuki of Flaming Sakura by the ring of white that came off her — a metre of it in Lucilla's office, a hand's width at a festival, the polite margin that people's boots learned to respect. Out here she stood in a country made of the same substance, and the frost went out from her feet and kept going and became the frostlands, and the join was invisible.

Amy stopped a few paces off and put her hands in her pockets.

"You're in the wrong hemisphere."

Hiyuki turned her head. Her face did the thing it did, which was almost nothing, and then a degree more than almost nothing.

"Amy."

"That's my lake."

"I have left it in good condition."

"You're *standing* on it. You're a hundred years old and you're standing on frozen water in the middle of nowhere in an unlined coat—"

"It is lined."

"It's lined like a *notion*." Amy came the rest of the way. Her boots made the sound ice makes, the long hollow one that runs off toward the far bank and keeps going. "How long have you been out here."

Hiyuki considered the question with the seriousness she gave every question, which had always been the most alarming thing about her.

"Forty minutes. I timed it. There is a notice at the Riseway head concerning the descent of snow, and I found I wished to see one arrive."

"See *one*."

"I selected it above the ridge and attended it the whole way down. It reached my sleeve at approximately the thirty-eighth minute. I lost confidence in the identification somewhere over the treeline, so the observation must be entered as unverified."

Amy stood in the snow with her mouth slightly open.

"That's the most insane thing anybody has ever said to me," she said, "and I live with Cartethyia."

## II. Eleven Postcards

They walked, because standing still on a lake in a snowfall is a conversation with a time limit, and both of them recognised the shape of it.

The eastern bank had a stand of dead pines and a lee under them where the drift went thin, and Amy took them there without discussing it, because she had been taking people there since she was six and the seals were a great deal more forthcoming when you came in downwind.

"You came back for the moon-waiting."

"I did."

"And then you left the next morning before anybody was up."

"The dawn cycle at that hour is very fine."

"You left before the *kettle was cold*, Hiyuki."

"I had an errand."

"You had eleven weeks of errand and you did the whole thing in one night." Amy kicked a spur of ice off the bank and watched it go. "You came back for one night so it wouldn't count as coming back. That's a technique. I know that technique. I invented about half of it and got the other half off my mother."

The snow went on arriving between them, unhurried, forty minutes old.

"Yes," said Hiyuki.

"Yeah?"

"You have described it accurately. I would like to have the sentence back, and I am aware that neither of those is available." She put a hand into her sleeve and brought out a bundle. "You may have these instead. They are the only currency I travel with."

The bundle was postcards. Eleven of them, tied with ordinary string, and every single one was appalling. A harbour photographed into featurelessness by a lens with a thumb over it. A monument the printing had rendered as an argument between two greens. A fish, apparently the town's chief asset, in a colour no fish has ever attempted. A composition whose entire foreground was a bollard.

Amy went through them slowly. She turned one over. The back was blank.

She turned another over. The back was blank.

She went through all eleven, and by the sixth she had stopped breathing normally, and by the ninth she was doing the thing with her jaw that she had inherited entire and unaltered from a woman who had washed up on a beach with nothing.

"You didn't write on them."

"No."

"You bought eleven postcards."

"Twelve. One was ruined by rain and could not be admitted to the collection on quality grounds." Hiyuki was looking at the pines. "You will have deduced the difficulty."

"Say it out loud. Rule five."

"...I did not know what to put in the sender's field."

The snow came down at walking pace.

"They give you a whole box for it," Amy said. "Big box. They put lines in it and everything."

"I am aware of the box."

"I could have been *getting these.*" Her voice had gone somewhere thin and she let it. "Eleven times. Eleven times a woman at a counter in some town I've never heard of hands you a card with a *bollard* on it, and you get to the box, and you stand there—"

"I stood there," said Hiyuki.

"How long."

"On the first occasion, seven minutes. It shortened." A pause of the length she gave things she had decided to hand over. "By the fourth I had begun writing the fronts of them instead, in my head, on the road, and delivering them to no one. I have four hundred kilometres of that. It is very fine work and it exists nowhere. I offer the report because you are the only person of my acquaintance who has run a broadcast into a dead channel for a decade and can be relied on to grade it fairly."

Amy stood in the lee of the dead pines holding eleven blank postcards.

"Yeah," she said. "I can grade that."

"Well?"

"It's a nine."

"Out of?"

"Out of nine. There's no ten. I checked for ten years."

She put them in her inside pocket, all eleven, flat against her chest where the wax paper was, and buttoned the coat over them, and said nothing else about it, and Hiyuki permitted the transaction to close.

"There is also a spoon."

"A what."

"A commemorative spoon. From the pudding stand at the east gate of Honami, which reopened with the summer festival, as they always do after repairs, with spoons for the first hundred." She produced it: a small dull tin thing, stamped with a sakura by somebody who had been given four minutes and a die. "It is for Chisa. Her mother has one from the awning fire, which was, I am reliably informed, nothing serious. A house with two of them is doing better than a house with one."

"Was there a queue?"

"There was a queue."

"How long."

"Two hours and forty minutes, and I stood in the whole of it. A man in front of me explained the politics of the resettlement corridor at very great length and got most of it wrong, and behind me two children conducted a dispute about a card game which I began following closely and which was resolved unjustly." She turned the spoon over once. "I have not enjoyed anything so much in ninety years."

"You waited two hours forty. For pudding."

"I am given to understand," said Hiyuki, "that the wait is the flavour."

Amy laughed, and then stopped laughing, because a thing had assembled itself behind her teeth on the walk and had chosen its moment.

"Ma told you to order for two."

"She did."

"Did you?"

"I did."

"Who got the other one?"

Hiyuki walked a few paces before answering, which was itself the first half of the answer.

"A schoolgirl. Honour roll, by the look of her — the uniform was immaculate and the satchel was a prop. She was behind me in the line and she had no money, which was a performance, and I bought two and set one on the bench beside her and kept my eyes on the fish market while she ate it."

"Did she eat it?"

"There were two spoons on the bench at the end," said Hiyuki, "and both of them were wet."

Amy stopped on the path with her heart doing something structural.

"Hiyuki—"

"Her shadow lay about a half-second behind her the entire time. I have decided to regard that as a trick of the awning." Hiyuki kept walking. "I have entered it in no record, and I would take it as a kindness if you did the same until spring."

## III. The Prime Minister

The breathing hole was where it always was, off the point, in the lee of the reeds, kept open by an administration that had never once required a reason.

Amy knelt at the edge of it, unwrapped the herring, and laid it on the ice. Then she sat back on her heels and kept her mouth shut, because that is the protocol.

Hiyuki stood at a respectful distance with her hands folded, exactly as she had stood in a doorway in a President's office for thirty years.

"You can come closer."

"I am observing protocol."

"There isn't one."

"There is always one," said Hiyuki. "I have simply not been briefed."

Amy laughed, and it went out flat across the ice for a very long way.

The water moved. Then it broke, and something enormous and streaming and profoundly uninterested came up into the air, put its chin on the ice, and looked at the herring with both of its ridiculous wet eyes.

"Hiyuki of Flaming Sakura," said Amy, "the Prime Minister."

The Prime Minister took the herring the way a government takes a budget line, with the clear implication that more had been expected.

Hiyuki came two steps closer and stopped, and looked down at a large animal in a hole in the ice, and the animal looked back up at her with an expression of total constitutional indifference.

"He is very fat."

"He's *magnificent.*"

"I did not dispute it."

"He does a thing where he sighs at you—"

The Prime Minister sighed at her.

It was an enormous sigh, wet and entirely rude, the sound of a bureaucracy declining an appeal, and it went up into the falling snow and hung there. And Hiyuki of Flaming Sakura, last Miko of a drowned country, holder of ten thousand regrets, the woman whose bow civilians pointed out to their children — *look, the Miko, we're safe* — stood in front of it and was, for the first time in a hundred and six years, entirely beneath somebody's notice.

Her face did the thing that on any other face would have been a laugh, and the snow lay on her shoulders where it had landed.

"Is it the same one?" she asked. "Your mother named him. That would be more than twenty years."

Amy scratched under the Prime Minister's chin, which he permitted the way a monarch permits a portrait.

"Nah. Ma's one had a notch out of his flipper. This one's about eight." She looked up. "It's the office."

Hiyuki was quiet for a moment.

"Say that again."

"It's the office," said Amy. "There's always a Prime Minister. That's the whole thing about a Prime Minister. He comes up, he eats the herring, he sighs at whoever's kneeling down, and then one winter he stops coming, and the next spring there's a different one on the same rock, and as far as I can tell the seals have never once held a ceremony about it, and I've been watching them since I was four."

The snow came down at one metre per second.

"You have just told me," said Hiyuki, "that a succession requiring no successor is possible, and you have done it with a fish."

"I have done it with a *seal*—"

"You have done it with a fish, Amy, and I intend to be extremely annoyed about it for some time." She looked out at the white going to the circumference of the world. "There will never be another Miko of Flaming Sakura. I kept that vow through three decades and one theatre and I will keep it through whatever is left. And I built a shrine with no keeper's quarters and a bell hung low for children and a maintenance schedule that reads *whoever is standing nearest*, and I have spent a year telling myself these are two separate facts."

"They're one."

"They are one fact," Hiyuki agreed, "and it has taken a seal."

---

She crouched, eventually. It took her a while, and she did it in the manner of somebody a century old who had decided that dignity was a cost centre. She held her bare hand out over the water, palm down, the way you do. The Prime Minister considered it, declined to engage, and submerged with a great deal of noise.

"He's gone to tell the others."

"About me?"

"About the herring. You're not news, you're weather."

"That," said Hiyuki, "is the finest thing anyone has said to me this year."

Amy looked at the bare hand, which had gone the colour of the ice.

"Where are your gloves."

"In my bag."

"Why are they in your bag."

"I have not required gloves in a hundred years."

"You're *shaking*."

"I am aware." Hiyuki turned the hand over and studied it with mild professional interest, the way she had once studied a panel of instruments that pronounced her perfectly stable. "It is new. It began around the fourth month, on a coast road, and I have been unable to find a cause, and the medical opinion available in resettlement corridors runs to opinions. The frost has been going off me since the theatre. One festival at a time, the President says. I had assumed that when the cold stopped belonging to me I would simply stop noticing it." A pause. "The design turns out to run the other way round."

She put the hand back inside her sleeve.

"Get the gloves out," said Amy.

"It is a small thing."

"Get the *gloves* out, Hiyuki, or I will radio Chisa, and Chisa will radio Lupa, and there will be a *shuttle*—"

Hiyuki got the gloves out.

## IV. What the Camps Say

They walked the long way round the shore, which added a kilometre and gave the light somewhere to go.

"A child in the fourth camp asked me whether I was dead," Hiyuki said.

"What'd you tell her?"

"That I was on leave."

Amy snorted. "I say *everyone says that.*"

"To what question?"

"*You kind of look like her.*" Amy put her hands deeper in her pockets. "Kid at the moon-waiting. Nine, maybe. Standing in front of me humming my own song at me and asking if I knew it. Her mum's got all the records."

"What did you say?"

"I said I'd heard it."

The snow came down between them.

"That is the correct answer," said Hiyuki. "I collected a story about you."

Amy's stride did not change, which took some effort, and which Hiyuki noted and left alone.

"Yeah?"

"In a Royan camp on the western plains, in the fifth month, from a woman of about seventy who had it from her father. It is a hundred years old. It concerns a girl who went to the gate and closed it and did not come out, and the version I received is very beautiful and very carefully made, and I disliked it enormously."

"Was I tall?"

"You were nine feet."

"Excellent."

"You had wings in one telling and a lantern in three, and in one you were a boy, and in the northern camps the gate was closed by a rabbit, which I have decided to regard as a separate tradition." Hiyuki's mouth did something. "In every one of them you go in singing. That detail is fixed. It has survived a hundred years, four languages and one sealed sky, and every time the teller reaches it the room makes a sound, all together, like a room agreeing."

"And?"

"And every one of them begins with a girl who came from nowhere. She is an orphan in four tellings and a foundling in three, and in one she is made out of snow by a childless man who wanted a daughter for a winter. She arrives at the gate carrying nothing and leaving nothing behind her." Hiyuki walked. "Which is what permits the ending to be beautiful. The edit is in the same place every time, and not one of the tellers knows they are making it. That is what makes it a liturgy."

Amy walked with her hands in her pockets and her jaw doing the thing.

"Say the rest."

"I know that edit. I was raised inside it. The mantle of Flaming Sakura is nine hundred years of women who walked into the dark singing, and every one of them learned the trade from a story with no one standing at the glass, and every one of them left somebody standing at the glass, and the story kept no column for her." She looked ahead down the shore. "I made a vow in the ash of my own city that there would never be another, and I have understood for a year now that the vow was aimed at the wrong half of the sentence. So I have been correcting it. In camps. At fires. To strangers in the evening, after the food, when somebody asks the traveller for a story and the traveller has one."

"You said no office."

"I said a great many things at a Riseway terminal in April."

"You're doing office."

"I am doing amendments," said Hiyuki, "which is a different department with a smaller budget, and I would appreciate your keeping it from the President, who will put it on a form."

Amy stopped walking.

"How do you tell it?"

"I say: there was a girl, and she went in, and she went in singing — and here is the part the song leaves out. Then I give them the woman at the glass." Hiyuki stopped too. She stood in the snow with her gloved hands at her sides and delivered it plainly, the way a hundred years of receiving other people's last sentences had taught her to give a sentence back. "I tell them the sound she made when her hand hit it. I tell them she carried the empty thing home and lit fires for it and fed it and said good morning to it, and that a house full of people stood around the outside of that for three weeks and kept her fed. I tell them about a crayon drawing with the cape left uncoloured. I tell them the girl came out — I always tell them that; it is the part the children stay for — and that she came out on a road. I leave out who made the road, because the story is improved by it and because I have discovered I enjoy sitting in a circle of people who have no idea."

The lake lay behind them, holding the whole grey sky upside down.

"They cry," Hiyuki said. "Every time. At the drawing, generally, which I confess I did not anticipate, and which I have come to think is the entire point of the amendment. A story that makes a room cry at the woman by the door is a story nobody in that room will ever be able to tell the old way again."

Amy's face had gone somewhere, and she was letting it, which was new, and which had cost eleven weeks of shouting.

"You changed the liturgy."

"I have *annotated* the liturgy. I have no standing to change anything; I resigned my standing at a terminal in April." Hiyuki began walking again, and Amy came with her, because that was easier than the alternative. "There is a further difficulty. In three camps now, the amendment has come back to me."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning I have arrived somewhere new and been told the story by a stranger who had it from a stranger, with the woman at the glass already in it." A slight inclination of the head. "It is spreading faster than I am walking. I have no idea what it will be in fifty years. That is the first thing in a hundred and six years I have set in motion and cannot supervise, and I would like to tell you the sensation is pleasant."

"Is it?"

"It is appalling," said Hiyuki. "I recommend it."

## V. The Road

The wind got up a little on the north shore, so they went into the lee of the boathouse Mei had built badly in a year with no name on it, and sat on the step out of the weather with the thermos between them.

Amy poured. Hiyuki accepted the cup and held it a long while without drinking, and Amy watched her do it and understood, from a long way inside her own history, that the holding was the point.

"I want to ask you about the road."

"Ask."

"Whose was it."

Hiyuki turned the cup a quarter-turn on her knee.

"Ten thousand and some. I have never had an exact figure; the office issues no receipts." She looked at the ice. "You want three."

"I want three."

"There was a woman at a tram stop in the rain," said Hiyuki, "holding an umbrella over a piece of dry pavement for sixty years, because she had told a friend *I'll wait, I'll hold it*, and then the sky came down and she never lowered her arm. Her wish was that somebody had stood under it once, so that the holding would have meant something.

"There was a calligrapher in a collapsed studio who had written the same letter ten thousand times and posted none of them. It began *Akane — I was wrong, the shop should have been yours, come home.* She said: you do not apologise to sisters. You assume there will be time.

"And there was a boy of about ten on a wet stairwell with a borrowed game cartridge he meant to return after the festival, and he apologised to me for the size of it. He said there were people who lost whole families." Hiyuki drank, at last. "That one was the lightest. I have never worked out why it ached the most, and I have had thirty years."

Amy sat with her elbows on her knees and her cup in both hands.

"That's what I was standing on."

"You were standing on all of them. Those three were somewhere in there, and I could not tell you where."

"They were warm."

Hiyuki's cup stopped a centimetre short of her mouth.

"Say that again."

"They were warm." Amy looked straight ahead at the lake. "I've been trying to say this to somebody for months, and every time I get to it the room goes soft on me and starts being kind, which is a thing my family does, and I need you to skip that part. Down there everything is the same temperature. That's the actual horror of the place — the dark is fine, you get used to the dark. It's that nothing is *different* from anything, for thirteen years, until you can't find the edges of yourself because there's no edge left to check against. And then the floor came up under my feet and it was *warm.* Warm like a stone at the end of a day. And I thought, oh — that's Ma. She's got under me somehow. She's doing this."

The wind came round the corner of the boathouse and went away again.

"Ten thousand people I've never met," said Amy. "Who never met me. Who were dead before I was born. All wanting the same thing and none of them getting it, and their wanting held my weight." She turned and looked at her. "And I've spent months trying to work out where you file that. I can't pay it. There's no counter. I went and stood at your lattice one night while you were in some corridor four hundred kilometres away and I stood there about an hour looking for the counter, and there's *no counter*, Hiyuki. You built a shrine with no counter in it."

"That was deliberate."

"I *know* it was deliberate. That's what makes it unbearable."

Hiyuki set her cup down on the step.

"What is your—"

She stopped.

She stopped so completely that Amy heard it: a hundred years of a sentence coming up the throat on rails and a hand going onto the brake at the second word. The snow came down. Out on the ice something small and administrative broke the surface and thought better of it.

"I withdraw the question," said Hiyuki.

"You didn't finish it."

"I very nearly did. My mouth has been making that shape for ninety years and it does not consult me. It would have worked, too — that is the difficulty with it. You would have told me. I would have taken it. It would have gone in behind my sternum with the others, and you would have walked back up that hill four kilos lighter and slept eight hours." She folded her hands in her lap. "And by morning it would have belonged to me. It has an owner, and it has a season, and I am neither."

Amy sat very still.

"I nearly said yes."

"I know. I watched you decide. It took under a second. I have been on the other end of that decision ten thousand times and never once on this end of it, and I would like it recorded that declining was extremely difficult."

"Why did you."

"Because there is a woman in a dormitory kitchen who has begun sleeping eight hours," said Hiyuki, "and the thing you are carrying is addressed to her, and I have spent a century intercepting other people's post."

## VI. The Appeal

The light went the way it goes over the frostlands, which is all at once and then not at all for an hour.

"I'm calling in the appeal," Amy said.

"Which appeal."

"You know which. On the terrace, with the penguin. I asked what the bell costs, and you said that was between you and the bell, and I said okay and made a note, and you *watched* me make it, and you let me. So you've known this was coming for a year and you've had a year to write the answer."

"I have had a year to walk away from it."

"How's that gone."

"Four hundred kilometres," said Hiyuki, "of extremely fine unwritten postcards."

Amy waited. She had learned waiting from three separate professionals and turned out better at it than any of them, having done ten years of it in a room with no clock.

"I do not know the figure," Hiyuki said at last.

"Bull."

"I have never known the figure. The Suzu does not itemise. It shows you your tomorrows, one branch at a time, and every ring takes something, and the taking goes unannounced. You discover it later, the way one discovers a room has got darker — by noticing you have been squinting for some while."

"So find out."

"There is no instrument."

"Then *estimate*—"

"I declined to." Hiyuki said it flatly, and it landed the way a stone lands. "That is the answer to your appeal. I have known it since the theatre and I have said it aloud to nobody. I could have estimated; twice a physician offered. I refused both times and entered the refusal in the records as humility. The correct word is procurement. A figure is a price. A price is a thing a person can be talked out of paying. I made certain I never had one, so that no one — including me, particularly me, at three in the morning, on the bad nights, of which there have been a great many — could ever run the arithmetic and arrive at *no.*"

The wind combed the drift outside the boathouse into one more parallel sentence.

Amy set her cup down on the step, very carefully, the way you set down something that is going to be used as evidence.

"You kept it uncounted so you couldn't be argued out of it."

"Yes."

"So that when the moment came, the only number on the table would be *whatever it takes.*"

"Yes."

"Hiyuki." Amy's voice had gone level — the level that came out of her when she had stopped protecting somebody. "That's mine. That's my move. Word for word, that's my move. I did it with the gate. I stood in a sync bay at nineteen with a house key round my neck and a projection up on a screen and I did not read the projection. I specifically did not read it. And if you'd asked me at the time I'd have told you there wasn't time, and there was four days of time, and I spent it building a perfect afternoon for somebody so she'd have something to hold afterward."

She was shaking, slightly, and she let it happen where it could be seen, which was rule two of a document she had drafted for her mother and had been failing to apply to herself since the day she wrote it.

"You want the bill? It went to everyone else. I was gone. I was a light in a ceiling for ten years, I had a lovely time, I did Tuesdays. The woman in the kitchen paid it. A wolf paid three weeks of sleep and got called a monster for saying so. A bard paid four notes she couldn't play. Every single line item was made out to somebody who wasn't in the room when I decided."

Hiyuki sat with her gloved hands in her lap and received the whole of it, and let it stay where Amy had put it.

"Count it out loud," said Amy. "That's all I've got. That's the entire product of eleven weeks of being shouted at by professionals and I'm handing it over at cost. Count it out loud where somebody can hear the number. They'll try to stop you and they'll fail, because you'll do it anyway — you've done it your whole life and you'll do it again the second somebody's kid is in a hole, and I'm not going to insult either of us by pretending otherwise. Count it out loud so that when you go, the people at the glass know what they're standing there for."

The boathouse creaked. Somewhere under the ice, an administration went about its business.

"Two years," said Hiyuki.

Amy's head came up.

"For a single ring, at my present age, with the reserve as I estimate it. The one I spent in the void on your behalf was rung twice. So the figure you are appealing for is four. I have carried it unspoken for eleven months, and I find, having said it in a boathouse to a nineteen-year-old, that it is very much smaller out here than it was in there."

"Four years."

"Out of a hundred and six." Hiyuki looked at her hands. "And I will tell you exactly where I put them, since we are counting. A girl at the bottom of everything who could not find a floor, and forty seconds of frozen summer that let a pair of scissors reach a thread. If those are the last purchases on the account, then the account closes very well indeed, and I have audited a great many accounts."

Amy sat with her hands over her mouth for a while.

"You paid four years for me."

"I paid two."

"That's *worse*, you're *haggling*—"

"I am being accurate. It is a professional deformity."

"You paid two years for a girl you'd never met."

"I paid two years," said Hiyuki, "for the girl I would have been if anybody had come. And she was standing right there, screaming at her mother to go home, and I had a bell." She picked her cup back up. "It is the only self-interested thing I have ever done. I have been extremely smug about it ever since. Now you know, and you may stop pouring tea out of windows on my account."

"I don't pour tea out of—" Amy stopped. "Who told you that."

"Everyone," said Hiyuki. "Immediately. In writing, in one case."

---

They sat.

"I'm withdrawing the other one," Amy said, after a while.

"Which."

"The popcorn. On the terrace. I asked what the wish was made of and you said popcorn, and I said okay and made a note on that one too, and I'm tearing it up. It's yours. It's the only thing you ever had that was yours, and you spent it on a puppet on a Tuesday, and you don't owe me the receipt."

The snow came down at one metre per second.

"There was a boy," said Hiyuki.

Amy stayed exactly where she was.

"I was sixteen. There was a drought, and a festival, and a stall at the east gate selling kettle popcorn, and the vendor's son was minding it because his father's knee had gone. He called me the snow miss. That year, out of other mouths, it was a wall. Out of his it was a door, and I have never established what the difference was made of." Her voice was doing nothing at all, which was how Amy knew. "On the last night there were fireworks over the bay, and the crowd pressed me against his side, and the first shell went up, and I flinched, and his arm came round my shoulder the way you steady a stack of anything. And my ear was against his chest."

The lake held the whole grey sky upside down.

"I had spent sixteen years learning to hear everything," said Hiyuki. "It was already the whole of what I was going to be; the mountain had noticed. And I had never once stood close enough to a living person to hear that particular sound. It was the loudest thing on a night made entirely of fire. So I wished that it could last forever. I rang nothing and offered nothing and addressed nobody. The bell hired me within the year, and I took the white, and he went and lived, which was the sensible course — a shop, an awning, later a wife who laughed easily. He came up the pilgrim road once a year for sixty-four years and made his wish at the offering box like any townsman, and I never asked what it was, and I performed his rites at eighty-one in a voice that held. His granddaughter thanked me afterward for my composure. She said it was a comfort."

Amy sat with her arms around her knees on the step of a boathouse her mother had built badly.

"You spent it on me."

"I spent it in a place with no air, on a summer breeze, and it lasted forever for four seconds, which is what forever costs at retail." Hiyuki finished her tea. "You smelled it. You are the only living person who has been inside it, and this is the appeal I am filing, and it is the only one I have: I would like to know what it was like in there. I have ninety years of it from the outside. The outside is a window. I have never once been in the room."

Amy shut her eyes.

"It was hot," she said. "That's the first thing. Everything down there is one temperature and this was *hot*, like standing near a wall that's been in the sun all day. And it smelled like burnt sugar and gunpowder and something green underneath, like water somewhere close." She opened her eyes. "And it was loud. There was a noise going through everything and I thought at first it was mine. It was way too slow to be mine. Somebody else's, running along under the fireworks like a floor."

Hiyuki sat very still in the dark under the boathouse eaves.

"He laughed early," she said. "Half a second ahead of the punchline. It ruined and improved everything."

"I didn't get that part."

"No. You got the good part," said Hiyuki. "Thank you."

---

## VII. Queue, or Grave

They started back with the light gone and the cabin's chimney showing on the shoulder of the hill as a smudge slightly less dark than the sky.

"There's a contradiction in you," Amy said. "I've been building it for an hour. Can I run it?"

"Please."

"You've got ten thousand crystals in a lattice on a terrace and every single one of them says the same thing. Cross it now. Send the letter now. Give the cartridge back now. Eat the pudding while the pudding's warm and the sister's alive. That's the whole library. You told Ma it was ten thousand to nothing."

"It is."

"And then you stood in a queue for two hours forty on purpose and told me the wait is the flavour." Amy walked with her breath going up ahead of her. "So which is it. Because I've got a thing scheduled for spring, and I've been up here three days trying to work out whether I'm standing in a queue or doing the other thing."

The snow had thinned. It came down now in the big slow separate flakes, the ones that arrive one at a time and let you watch them do it.

"They are the same practice," said Hiyuki. "You are asking me for the difference between them. It is one item. I did not have it for thirty years. It is an hour."

Amy waited.

"The pudding stand opens at eleven. It is written on a board. The board was there before I was born and it was there after the city drowned and it is there now, and the queue forms at nine because everybody knows the hour, and standing in it is one of the great pleasures available to a human being, and every minute of it is delicious precisely because it terminates." She walked. "My sister and I said *next week.* We said it with total confidence, twice, three times, in a season of sakura. We never once said a day. We never said an hour." A pause. "A wait with an hour on it is a queue. A wait with no hour on it is a grave, and the terrible thing about graves is that from the inside they feel exactly like queues, right up until the rain starts and does not stop."

"Ma knows it's spring."

"Does she know a day?"

"...No."

"Does she know an hour?"

"She knows *spring*, Hiyuki, she cleared an afternoon—"

"An afternoon in which month, at what time, in whose room, with who holding the clock." Hiyuki did not raise her voice at all. "You may tell me this is unkind. I have been unkind on this subject exactly once in a century, and it is to you, and I expect to be forgiven eventually on the grounds that I am the leading living authority on the failure mode."

Amy walked twenty paces in silence.

"The eleventh of April," she said.

"Time."

"Three in the afternoon. Not the morning — she goes soft in the mornings, she'll agree to anything before eleven and then spend the whole day paying for it." Her voice was doing something. "Our room. The kitchen's got seven people in it at all times, I've counted, it's structural. And Iuno holds the clock, and the number is eleven, because it's a good number and I stole it off her and she isn't using it."

Hiyuki stopped and turned to face her on the path, and gave it back in the voice she used for the only rite she had ever fully believed in, which was the receiving of things.

"The eleventh of April. Three in the afternoon. The room at the end of the corridor. Eleven minutes, and the seer holding the clock."

"Yeah."

"Then it is a queue."

"Is that it?"

"That is it," said Hiyuki. "That is the entire liturgy, and I am sorry it is so small. You said an hour out loud to a witness. There are now two of us who know it. Before you spoke, it existed in one person, which is the condition under which every single object in my reliquary was lost." A pause. "Take it as strictly professional. It is what I do. I am simply doing it in the correct tense for once."

They came up the last of the rise. The blue door stood at the top with the wisteria over it holding its buds in fists under a hand's depth of snow.

"Now do yours," said Amy.

Hiyuki stopped walking.

"I beg your pardon?"

"You heard me. Rule six. Nobody sneaks off." Amy turned round on the path and stood in front of her, breathing steam, nineteen and twenty-nine and immovable. "You're forty minutes from the Riseway head. You've been forty minutes from the Riseway head since Tuesday, because I asked the woman at the coffee cart and she said there's been a white-haired lady walking the ridge for three days buying nothing. And she told me the coffee's the worst she's served in a long career, so I know it's you."

"...That is an unlicensed use of a source."

"I'm an unlicensed *station*. That's the whole brand." Amy held her ground. "There's a woman down there with your twin mirror on her desk who has been checking her office for iris every evening since April. She has a form in a drawer with your name on it and nothing in the duration box. And you came back for one night in the autumn so it wouldn't count, and left before the kettle was cold, and now the weather's turned and you're doing laps on a ridge in an unlined coat picking snowflakes out of the sky and following them down." Her chin came out. "Give me a day and an hour."

The snow came down. It had been decided at four kilometres and had taken forty minutes to get here, and it landed on the shoulders of a woman who had made herself, at sixteen, the sort of person nothing landed on.

"Tomorrow," said Hiyuki. "The nine-forty descent."

"Say the rest."

"...And I will not call ahead. I will walk into that office at approximately eleven and put a spoon on the desk, and she will set the funding mirror face-down, which is the only ceremony I want, and she will dock my pay, and I will let her."

"Good."

"You will not be there."

"I'm not coming. It's yours." Amy turned back toward the door. "I'll be at the top of the Riseway at nine thirty-five with a coffee for you that is objectively the worst on this continent, and then I'm getting back on the path and walking home, and you can do the rest by yourself like a *grown-up*—"

"Amy."

"What."

Hiyuki stood in the snow at the foot of the path.

"Thank you for the hour."

---

## VIII. The Frame

The fire went in on the third try, which was two better than Amy's average, and the cabin came round the way old houses do — grudging, and then all at once, the cold going up into the roof and the smell of a hundred winters of woodsmoke coming down out of the beams to take its place.

Hiyuki stood inside the blue door with her hands folded and looked at the room the way she had looked at ten thousand rooms in a drowned city, which is to say completely.

Paper planes on the shelf, gone soft gold at the folds. A record player, and beside it a sleeve warmer to the eye than the stove. A mug on the left of the shelf with the handle turned out. Seven seal posters, curling, the largest with a speech bubble in a round young hand: *You can do it, Cadet!!!* Three exclamation marks. A dark screen on a low table and, in its cradle, a controller paused on the same mission select for twelve years and change.

"Do not tell me about any of it," she said. "I will ask when I am able to hear it properly."

"Okay."

"That is a real request. I am new at requests. Say if it is wrong."

"It's fine," said Amy, from the kitchen. "Sit down. I'm making the thing."

The thing was hot chocolate. Three spoons of cocoa, and the little bit of salt.

Hiyuki watched the salt go in from the doorway with an expression that could have curdled the milk on its own.

"That is salt."

"It's a secret ingredient."

"It is a felony."

"You brew tea with *cold water*—"

"Cool," said Hiyuki. "The word is cool, and it is done because heat ruins some things and it is a courtesy to the leaf, and there is a very great deal of my life in that sentence and I decline to unpack it in front of a saucepan."

Amy set the mug down in front of her.

Hiyuki drank it. She drank the whole thing standing, without further comment, and then held out the mug for more, and Amy took it and refilled it and said not one word about it, because that was how her mother had done it at this table twenty-two years ago for somebody else, and because there are traditions you keep by using them.

---

The doorframe stood between the kitchen and the front room: eighty-year-old oak, green all over the wood where a summer of vines had gone brown at the edges, and under the green thirteen years of pencil going up in crooked increments.

*Amy, 6. Amy, 6 and a half (she insisted). Amy, 7.*

The first three in an adult's hand. The rest in a child's copy of it, each line crooked, each crooked differently. And near the top, a straight level unlabelled line nine centimetres above *Amy, 18*, with a name beside it in a hand trained at eight and never entirely retrained, and a small *?*, and, smaller, *(she wouldn't say)*. And laid over eighteen, a nineteen; and under it a twenty-nine; and a bracket down the side joining the two.

Hiyuki read it the way she read everything, which took a while.

"Stand against it," said Amy.

"No."

"Hiyuki—"

"I am a hundred and six years old. I have not altered in eighty of them. The exercise has no informational content whatsoever."

"Correct." Amy pointed at the bracket. "Hers is a straight line drawn by an immortal, and mine's got a bracket on it covering ten years I spent as a light bulb. It's a list of afternoons when somebody was standing here with a pencil. The height's the excuse." She folded her arms. "Somebody explained that to me on this exact floor about eleven weeks ago and then went away for the winter, and I have thought about it roughly four hundred times since, and I'd like to do something with it before it goes off."

The fire moved in the grate.

Hiyuki put down the mug. She went to the frame and turned around and put her heels to the wood and her shoulders flat and her chin level, the way you do, the way everybody does, the way children have done at doorframes in every house in every world since somebody first thought of it — and she did it with a century of ceremonial bearing, which made it, if anything, worse.

Amy got the ruler out of the drawer.

Her hands were unsteady. She laid the ruler flat on the white hair and slid it in against the wood and marked it, and Hiyuki stood there and let a stranger's daughter put a pencil against the top of her head in a house at the edge of the world, and said nothing at all.

Amy wrote the name. It came out crooked.

"Number."

"One hundred and six."

"That's a *lot* of digits—"

"It is precisely three," said Hiyuki, "and I have earned every one, and you will write them legibly."

Amy wrote them legibly. Then she stepped back, and there it sat on the oak among a child's thirteen years and a woman's two thousand: a name, a number, a line half a hand's breadth under *Amy, 19*.

"You're shorter than me."

"You will find that a great many things are."

"You're shorter than me by—"

"Amy."

"Sorry."

Hiyuki looked at the frame for a long moment.

"May I have the pencil."

Amy gave her the pencil.

Hiyuki took it and turned to the wood and reached up — well up, past the bracket, past the level line with the *?* beside it, to a height that took her onto the balls of her feet — and held her hand there, flat, palm down, against the grain of an eighty-year-old doorframe in a country her country had never heard of.

She held it there a long time. Her hand came down a centimetre. Then up half of one. Then stopped.

"I am estimating," she said.

"That's fine."

"I have not stood beside her in thirty-one years, and I may be out by as much as three centimetres, and once it is on the wood it becomes the only record of her height that exists anywhere in the world, because the shrine burned and the registry drowned and everyone who could correct me is—" She stopped. Her hand stayed where it was. "I am aware of what I am doing. I am carrying a small thing alone in a room with somebody in it. I would like it noted that I caught it inside twenty seconds, which for me is a personal record."

"Want me to hold the clock?"

"...Yes. Eleven minutes."

"You've got about four seconds of this left in you and we both know it," said Amy. "Draw the line, Hiyuki."

Hiyuki drew the line.

It came out crooked. It came out badly crooked, running up at the right-hand end where the arm had tired, and she looked at it with the expression of a woman who had spent a century failing to carve a heron.

"That is wrong."

"Leave it."

"It slopes—"

"*Leave it.*" Amy came and stood beside her at the frame. "Every line on that wood is crooked and every one of them is crooked in a different direction, and that's how you tell which afternoon it was. The straight one belongs to the only person on there who isn't a person. The evidence is in."

Hiyuki wrote the name beside it.

Eight letters, in an unhurried hand that had signed a hundred years of festival notices. She wrote them small, with her nose close to the wood, the way people write when they intend a thing to last.

*GYOKURO.*

"That's her?"

"That is her name. She was the Miko before me. She was my sister. She liked her tea brewed cool, which is what the word means, and it was the joke between us for twenty-six years, and it has been unusable for thirty-one." Hiyuki put the pencil down on the little shelf by the frame, where a pencil had lived since before either of them was born. "I have now said it aloud to four living people. You are the fourth. I can report that the fourth is markedly easier than the first."

"What was she like?"

"She made it look easy," said Hiyuki. "That is what I know. She carried the whole office where I could see her and she put it down where I could not, so I learned half the trade and believed it was the whole thing." She looked at the eight small letters. "And she left me an errand instead of a regret. She said: eat the second bowl with somebody whose hands are warm."

Amy stood at the doorframe of her mother's house, looking at a dead woman's name on it.

Then she held out both hands, palms up, and said nothing at all.

Hiyuki looked at them. Then she took them, one in each of her own, and turned them over, and held them, and did the thing she had done ten thousand times at ten thousand shoulders in a drowned city, which was to give something the whole of her attention.

"Warm," she said.

"Yeah?"

"Warm. Enter it in the record as a professional finding. The instruments are fond of me and I am fond of them, and I have never once needed them for this."

Amy's face did the thing, and this time she let all of it happen in front of somebody, which took eleven weeks of shouting and a woman on a lake and about four seconds.

"Sorry."

"Do not."

"It's just—" She got a hand over her eyes. "For about eight months I've been trying to work out if I'm a — there's a *word* for what I am, and I can't say it out loud because everyone in that house goes to pieces, and I know they're warm, I *know* they are, I've checked them against the radiator maybe nine thousand times, and it's — you can't verify yourself from inside. Somebody has to—"

"That is the whole of my trade," said Hiyuki, and kept hold.

---

## IX. Nine Thirty-Five

She left before dawn, which up here is a rumour rather than an event.

Amy walked her out to the shoulder of the hill where the path forked toward the ridge, and stopped there, because rule six was rule six and ran in both directions.

"Postcards," Amy said.

"You have eleven."

"They're blank, Hiyuki. They don't count. They're *stationery.*" She pulled her hat down. "*Where do I send it*, she says. That's the box you couldn't fill in. So here."

She said it the way she had written it on a form at eight years old, when a woman with city boots had asked her for a family name and had left the field blank instead, with an annotation.

"The cabin with the blue door, Roya Frostlands. Ask the seals."

Hiyuki stood in the half-dark on a path in a foreign country.

"That is not an address."

"It's the *best* address. Route eleven runs it overnight, three portal changes, they know the knock. Write that on the front and it'll get here, and it'll sit on the shelf next to the planes, and in about forty years somebody is going to find a stack of eleven bollards in a cabin nobody's lived in and have a *day.*" Amy grinned, and it was her mother's, and neither of them mentioned it. "And there's this."

She wrote it on the back of the ninth postcard — the fish, in the impossible colour — in a hand trained at eight and crooked ever since:

*ch. 9. Tuesdays, midnight. Say where I'm finding you.*

"It is equipment testing," said Hiyuki, reading it. "I have seen the schedule. It has been classified as equipment testing for thirteen years."

"It's *legally* equipment testing, and I'll thank you not to litigate."

"You have been running my office."

Amy's grin faltered.

"...What?"

"You take calls from strangers in the dark. You ask where they are. You hear the thing underneath the thing they said, and you give them something to carry the hour with, and then you let them go, and you keep none of it, and you do it at the hour when the people in trouble are awake and the people who are fine are asleep." Hiyuki put the postcard inside her sleeve with the other ten. "I performed those rites for a hundred years in a shrine on a hill with a treasury and a lock and a keeper, and the keeper swallowed everything she heard, and it very nearly killed her twice. You are doing it better with a fader and a cupboard and no lock at all. I have decided to find that funny rather than the other thing, and it took me until the boathouse."

"I'm not—" Amy stopped, and started again. "I'm not taking the job."

"The post was abolished. The maintenance schedule reads *whoever is standing nearest.*" Hiyuki inclined her head. "At midnight on a Tuesday you are standing nearest to thirty thousand people. I thought you should be told what you have been doing, since nobody told me for ninety years and I made every available error."

The sky over the frostlands began deciding about the day.

"Nine thirty-five," said Amy.

"With the coffee."

"With the *worst* coffee."

She took the Suzu from her hip.

She rang it once — no oracle in it; she drew nothing out of herself and spent nothing, and the four years stayed in the reserve, counted now, and named. She rang it the way ten thousand pilgrims had rung it at a shrine gate for nine hundred years: to call what is sacred near and send what is false away.

One clean bronze note went out over the frostlands.

The morning kept being a morning. The drifts went on being drifts and the pines went on being pines, and away down the shoulder of the hill, under a metre of ice, an administration continued about its business without comment. Four kilometres up, in a dark and unadministered place, the weather had already decided about the next forty minutes and moved on to other work, and none of it was personal, and all of it was on its way.

"Bell works on mornings," said Amy.

"It always did," said Hiyuki. "There had simply never been anybody standing in one."

She went down the ridge path with her eyes on the path, and the snow came down at the pace of somebody strolling and lay on her shoulders where it landed and stayed there, the whole way to the Riseway head.

---

Amy walked home the long way, which added a kilometre and gave the light somewhere to go.

She stopped once, at the top of the rise, with her hand on the latch of the blue door, and did her inventory: gloves, key, thermos, rig, eleven postcards flat against her chest.

Then she went in and put the kettle on, and got the rig out, and set the antenna up in the window, and keyed the mic on a frequency that had been dead for thirteen years and was now, by a very thin margin, alive.

"Chisa. Third day. Number's seven."

A pause; and then, from a dormitory kitchen four kilometres under a glacier, in a voice that had never once in its life said a thing it did not mean:

*"Seven. Logged."* A beat. *"You sound different."*

"Yeah."

*"Better different?"*

Amy looked across the room at the doorframe, where a crooked line ran up at the right-hand end under a name in a small unhurried hand, at a height somebody had estimated and might have got wrong by three centimetres and had entered into the record anyway.

"Ask me in the spring," she said.

*— fin —*

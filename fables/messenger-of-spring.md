## I. Wintering

Winter came down the Riseway a day behind her, as arranged.

She had been punctual. It was her new vice, and she practiced it with a century's thoroughness: home ahead of the cold, expected, awaited, her boot prints frosting the terminal apron in a season the false sky had yet to schedule. The city took her back with the smell of a whole civilization's dinner. On her second evening below, a lacquered box appeared on the President's desk containing two puddings and a commemorative spoon, and Lucilla examined the spoon under the lamp for a long time, pronounced it "in appalling taste," and put it in the drawer with the sour candies, which is where she keeps the things she intends to be buried with.

"Ask me again," Hiyuki said, at the door. "When you like."

"Not tonight."

"Mm. Then everything is on schedule."

The winter went the way underground winters go: by agreement. The manufactured sky performed its cold season with the diligence of an understudy — snow on the posted dates, greys issued in rations, a wind on Tuesdays — and the city missed things together, on time, which is the purpose of a season. Hiyuki spent hers openly, a spending the office had never before permitted. She drank tea she had cooled first, in rooms where people could watch her do it. She sat in the third row of a student hero play and told the lead afterward, truthfully, that his dying speech ran six seconds long, and the boy glowed for a week. She carved. The carvings were terrible. She was informed of this by children, by priests, by a wolf, and once, devastatingly, by a Starstack's image-recognition module, which returned *root vegetable (78%)* on her best heron. She thanked the module and kept carving.

The shrine asked nothing of her, which had been the design. A broom lived at the gate now — someone's, everyone's — and the sweeping happened the way weather happens, by whoever was standing nearest. Students came up the ridge with their winter griefs and rang the bell, and the crystals held whatever frequencies they were given, and below the terrace the god knelt on his glacier, awake, patient as moving geology, keeping whatever counsel gods keep in the off-season.

The bell rang on a third Sunday, mid-morning, in the heaviest week of the scheduled snow.

Hiyuki knew the visitor before she turned. The office reads arrivals the way sailors read water, and this one read as deep lake and clean linen and, underneath, faint as a watermark, the particular quiet of the place where stories go to die — worn on the outside now, buttoned, chosen. A coat, where there had been a country.

"The bell is for remembering the dead," Hiyuki said.

"Then I'm entitled twice." The girl at the gate tilted her head; a strand of hair went behind her ear, idle, ancient habit. "I have documentation."

"Mm."

"Notarized," Denia said. "By a witch. It's the single largest loophole she has ever processed. She was very moved. She glared at me about it."

Hiyuki set down her trowel. Snow went on landing between them, politely, asking first. There is a courtesy the office owes the returned, and it is the same courtesy it owes the dead, which is to let them open the conversation and to receive whatever they open it with as the whole of the weather; and Denia crossed the terrace in her driftwood-buttoned coat, unhurried, and stood before the lattice, and looked at ten thousand crystallized reaches the way an auditor looks at a very long ledger written in a hand she recognizes.

"These are all—"

"Regrets," Hiyuki said. "Reaches that stayed on the near shore."

"You carried them."

"For a century."

"In your chest."

"Mm."

Denia was quiet a moment. "And people call *me* dramatic."

They took tea on the step, out of the flask, in the cold. Hiyuki poured the way she pours everything, with the water cooled first, and Denia held the cup in both hands and looked out at the glacier and the kneeling shape on it, and the silence between them was the working kind, the kind two professionals keep while each decides how much of the other's file she has read.

"You froze the clock," Denia said, at last, to the glacier. "While they cut. I was mostly elsewhere at the time, but the body keeps minutes. Cold, and then hands, and then less string. I've been meaning to ask where time learned its manners."

"It cost a summer."

"Steep."

"It was ninety years old," Hiyuki said. "It had appreciated. It was the first sound money I ever spent."

Denia turned the cup in her hands. Hiyuki watched her do the arithmetic — watched the ledger open, the pen hover, the old handwriting ready itself over a new line of debt — and spoke before the ink could land, because mercy is a kind of theft, and she had recently taken up stealing.

"You will want to enter it in your ledger," she said. "Enter it as income. Mine. Of ten thousand wishes I have carried, that one I was glad to grant, and I granted it by giving it away, and if you attempt to reimburse me I will consider it an audit of my finest transaction and take offense in a manner you will find extremely composed."

Denia looked at her sidelong. The smile arrived — the gentle one, the omnipresent one, and this winter it came with all its seams sound, resting on collateral.

"I know a stand that does custard," she said. "East, I'm told. Reopens with the festival."

"The queue forms at dawn. The wait is the flavor."

"Then I'll owe you a wait." She set her cup down, took a sour candy from her pocket, and laid it on the offering step, squarely, label out. "For whoever's standing nearest."

She was at the gate when she paused, in the way of people pausing in gateways when the courtyard contains something they have decided to say.

"You'll go again."

"In spring. I have appointments."

"Take company."

"I did. In autumn."

"Take more." The strand went behind her ear. "It's the coming thing. I read it in a journal."

She went down the ridge path through the scheduled snow, and Hiyuki stood at the lattice a while, and the frost around her feet, which had been melting quietly for some minutes, finished melting, and she noted it, and had stopped pretending not to.

## II. Night Census

Sigrika came up the ridge past midnight, in the fourth week of the cold cycle, carrying her journal and the specific insomnia of the extremely dutiful.

Hiyuki was at the lattice. She kept no watch — the shrine's whole doctrine was the not-keeping — but a century of night shifts is load-bearing, and she was there the way frost is there, without appointment, when the girl came through the gate and stopped, and performed, at speed, the entire choreography of someone deciding whether to apologize for existing.

"I couldn't — the light was on. Is the light supposed to be on? I can come back at a reasonable—"

"The shrine has no hours." Hiyuki indicated the step. "Neither does the night. Sit."

Sigrika sat. She sat the way she did everything, at full effort, spine engaged, the journal held against her ribs like a plank from a wreck. For a while the two of them watched the crystals do what the crystals do in the dark, which is hold their little salt-white lights at the exact brightness of being remembered, and the false sky ran its dimmest register overhead, and the god below made no comment.

"Do they ever go out?" Sigrika asked.

"They have had every opportunity. They decline."

Sigrika nodded, gravely, and wrote something. Hiyuki poured tea — the flask travels; the office learned that on a coast road — and the girl took the cup in both hands and held it against her sternum, where the heat could do its old work, and then said, in the voice of someone who has rehearsed a paragraph in a corridor:

"May I ask an office question."

"Mm."

"What do you do with a granted wish?"

The frost on the lattice held very still. Hiyuki considered the question, and the girl, and the four weeks since a third Sunday when a knock had remade a doorway, and answered it as the office answers everything, which is truthfully, at the correct temperature.

"You live in it," she said. "It is the hardest post the office has. The waiting comes with instructions. Nine hundred years of them. Sweep the courtyard, keep the vigil, mind the glass. The having comes with none."

"There should be a manual," Sigrika said, with sudden heat, and then caught herself, and said, smaller: "I check. At night. That she's breathing. It's been four weeks and I still — she caught me once and pretended to be asleep so I wouldn't be embarrassed, and I pretended to believe her, and we're both extremely good at it, and I'm so *tired.*" The words came out at exam speed, ink before the bell. "And I can't tell her, because she'll make it a joke to carry it for me, she does that, she thinks I don't see the mechanism, I have *diagrams* of the mechanism—"

"Candidate."

Sigrika stopped.

"I stood a watch like yours," Hiyuki said, "for thirty years. At a door I would not knock on, over a grief I would not name, checking a silence every evening for proof it was still breathing. I can report from the far end of that posture: the breathing continues whether audited or not. And the sleep you are declining to spend was purchased for you, four years of nights, by a professional. She stayed up so that yours would keep." She turned her cup. "Spend it. It insults the gift to hoard it."

The girl looked into her tea for a long time. Weather crossed her face — she has a face weather crosses; it is a family trait of the family she assembled herself — and when she looked up her chin had reset to its angle, the angle of a girl deciding to try her hardest, which was the only angle her chin had ever learned.

"Partial credit," she said, "is still credit. I'll — I'll start with naps."

"The syllabus accepts naps."

Sigrika wrote that down. Actually wrote it down; Hiyuki watched the pen form the words *syllabus accepts naps* and file them under a heading, and thought that the reels of her long life had shown her many kinds of devotion and here was one more: a girl who fought despair with stationery.

The wind changed register overhead, ticking down toward the deep-night setting. Sigrika turned pages — past finches, past a heron with a hairpin, past an underlined *White bird! Returned!* — and stopped on a fresh entry, and hesitated, and turned the journal around.

*White bird, second specimen,* the entry read. *Larger. Resident in winter. Suspected migratory.*

"Am I in the journal," Hiyuki said.

"…Page forty," said Sigrika, with the full dignity of the caught.

"Mm." Hiyuki studied the sketch. It was careful and quick and the proportions were merciless and correct, which is to say the exact inverse of her own herons. "Make sure the beak is right."

"The beak is right."

"Then I have no notes." She handed it back. "The suspicion is also right. I go east when the thaw is posted. I have a queue to stand in and a road that owes me its springs."

Sigrika's pen stilled. The question arrived on her face a long moment before it arrived in the air, and when it came it wore fieldwork's clothes, because the girl could not yet ask anything naked:

"The young ones," she said. "Migratory birds. Do they know the route, or learn it?"

"They are taught by going."

Sigrika wrote that down too. She wrote it slowly, the way you write a thing you are not ready to know you have decided, and closed the journal, and drank her tea to the bottom, and slept that night — she reported it later, to the day's list, under *attempted* — for five entire hours, a personal record for the cold cycle, with the lamp off.

## III. The Instrument That Loves

In the front row of Professor Mornye's Tuesday lecture, Student 734 slept through the derivations with her chin in her hand — present, warm, ordinary, breaking no laws of physics whatsoever — and Mornye had stopped mid-equation the first time it happened, and never once since. Some data requires a single verification. After that it is simply true, and you build on it.

She had been rebuilding all winter. This was the term the Academy's paperwork used — *curriculum revision, Exostrider Engineering, Units 1 through 14* — and it was accurate the way a coastline on a map is accurate. What had actually happened was that a woman had discovered, at the end of thirteen years of work, that the machine at the center of her science was a person, and had gone home, and had taken her life's blueprints down off the wall one by one, and had begun again from the only axiom that survived: *he is awake, and he was awake the whole time, and everything we built, we built on his back without asking.*

The new syllabus opened with a question instead of a schematic. Her students found this destabilizing. She found their destabilization pedagogically sound.

The readings were the winter's other work. She went up to the shrine terrace on clear evenings, hoverchair humming on the ridge path, Starstack trailing like a patient moon, and took the ambient measurements she had been taking since autumn — the god's frequency, its harmonics, the slow signature the instruments rendered as a mountain range drawn by someone very calm. And all winter, one anomaly had been accumulating in her margins, and the anomaly was Buddy.

The little Soliskin fragment had developed a heading. It still rode her shoulder; it still pulsed its slow amber affection against her jaw at the hour she forgot to eat; but at the terrace, every evening, without exception, it turned. East. It held east the way needles hold north, and it hummed — below her hearing, the instruments insisted, though the instruments had been raised to believe hearing was a range rather than a relationship — and the pattern of the hum matched nothing in her archive and everything in her suspicion.

On the last night of the deep cold, the weather system overran its posting. A pressure fault in the cavern's northern circulation; the temperature on the ridge dropped through the chair's rated floor in eleven minutes; and Mornye, half a kilometer from shelter with her power cells crystallizing, ran the numbers with the flat interior voice that had once been the loneliest sound she knew, and got an answer she did not care for, and then got frost across her instrument panel that was moving against the wind.

"Your chair has stopped," Hiyuki observed, arriving out of the weather the way weather arrives.

"Its cells are rated to specification. The specification was written by a committee that has never been cold." Mornye's fingers had gone clumsy on the panel. She noted this, filed it, and then did the thing the old Mornye had needed a dying city to learn and the new one performed as procedure: "I require assistance."

"Mm."

What Hiyuki built took four minutes and looked grown rather than made: a windbreak of ice, curved like a held breath, angled to spill the gale over their heads. She sat down inside it on the bare stone as though stone were a furnishing she had opinions about, produced the flask, and put her hands — bare, warm; Mornye logged the temperature differential automatically and then logged that she had — around Mornye's, around the cup.

They waited out the fault. Below them the god knelt in the dark, dusted with the storm, unbothered, and Buddy crept up under Mornye's collar and pulsed there, fast at first, then slower, then in a pattern.

Hiyuki's head tilted.

"Eleven beats," she said. "Then it waits."

"Waits."

"That is the shape of it. I have heard rooms wait. It is the same posture."

Mornye went very still. The wind worked at the windbreak and found it honest. "The instruments render it as noise," she said. "Sub-threshold. Unresolvable." A pause, exactly the length of an old wound. "I used to hear this. The bridges rendered mathematics as music. I sold the ear." She said it as she said all data, flat, priced, and Hiyuki received it the way the office receives everything, in full, without appraisal.

"State the pattern again," Mornye said.

Hiyuki stated it. Eleven beats, a rest; eleven beats, a rest; and inside the eleven, a smaller structure, three and three and five, "like a word," she said, "said patiently, to someone the speaker believes will eventually turn around." Mornye transcribed with cold-stiff fingers, and the Starstack ran the transcription against the winter's archive, and the correlation came back at a confidence her committee would have called publishable and she called obvious.

The pattern was in every reading since autumn. And it was not stationary. Each repetition sourced fractionally farther along the Solistree root network — east, and east, and east — a word walking through the roots of the world at the pace of sap.

"He's saying it continuously," she said. "Down the whole network. The array here can resolve the fact of it and nothing else. Amplitude, heading, patience." She watched the god through the storm-blur, the vast shoulders bearing the snow they had borne for a century, and rendered her verdict in the voice she kept for verdicts. "The data requires legs."

"Mm."

"I don't have those." Buddy pulsed against her jaw. The windbreak held. Somewhere inside Mornye, a thirteen-year-old proof completed itself, the one whose conclusion she had written first — *ordinary, together* — and had been deriving ever since. "I'll need to borrow some."

## IV. Assembly

Spring's first posting went up on the civic boards — *thaw cycle, week of; citizens are advised the canals will run loud* — and on the evening of that day, four people were on the shrine terrace at once, and each of them would later privately maintain that this had been a coincidence, and each of them would be wrong, and one of them had brought custard for exactly four.

Mornye had come for the last calibrated readings of the season, Starstack deployed, the winter's map projected above the lattice in patient amber: the root network of the world, and along it, walking east at the pace of sap, a word.

Sigrika had come with her candidacy folder, because her Heliogyre coursework had produced an anomaly plot her professors called *instrument drift*, and she had run it four times, and she did not believe in drift that kept a beat.

Denia had come with the custard.

Hiyuki poured.

The overlay took Sigrika eleven seconds. She stood in front of the projection with her plot held up beside it and her whole frame lit like a struck bell, and the sentence came out at exam speed, ink before the bell: "That's my drift. That's my drift, that's the Heliogyre residual, the eleven-count, they told me to smooth it out of the data, I *refused*, I filed an appendix, appendices are permitted—" She stopped. She looked at Mornye. "What is it?"

"A word," Mornye said. "Said patiently. Moving east down the roots." She adjusted the projection; the amber line lengthened off the map's edge, into the surveyed blank of the surface, toward the coast. "The array here can watch it leave. Resolving it requires stations along the route. Fieldwork." She stated the requirements as she stated everything, a list, priced: "Legs. A reader of old Royan, for the waypoint stones — the root network was mapped in boundary runes before it was mapped in anything else. Residue handling; the corridor east crosses three old scars, and my instruments object to Voidmatter on principle." A pause, calibrated. She looked at Hiyuki. "And a question. At the end of the data there is a person. Someone has to ask him properly. You own the question."

"Mm." Hiyuki turned her cup. "My route runs east until midsummer regardless. I have a queue to stand in and appointments the whole way down the coast. The office travels. It always did." A beat, dry as good tea. "I was the one who didn't."

"I want it noted," Denia said, from the step, around custard, "that I object. Outdoors. Weather nobody has approved. Inns with one dessert between them and it's regional." She had a supply list open on her knee and had been adding to it throughout, in a lazy precise hand. Sigrika, reading over her shoulder, went through the list item by item, and stopped.

"This has five bedrolls."

"I round up," said Denia. "Flocks acquire. It's in the literature."

"You've objected in quadruplicate."

"Which is a word I invented," said Denia. "Royalties."

And then the terrace went quiet, because the fourth chair had gone quiet, and everyone had heard it happen.

Sigrika stood with her anomaly plot in both hands, holding it to the earth. "I have obligations," she said. The words came out in their dress uniform. "The candidacy. The spring assessments. My family expects — the track expects—" She recited the syllabus of her whole life at speed, at full effort, chin set, and nobody argued, and the silence did the arguing, and she heard it, and stopped, and folded the plot along its worn crease, and said, "I should go. It's late. Thank you for the tea," and went down the ridge path with her spine engaged and her census unfinished.

Denia watched her out of sight, and ate the fourth custard, slowly, with respect.

"She'll come," she said.

"Mm?"

"I'm her best friend. I've read the syllabus." The strand went behind her ear. "There's a field requirement in it nobody's told her about."

## V. Papers

The summons found Sigrika at breakfast, and she arrived at the President's office nine minutes early with her uniform inspected and her resignation from the candidacy drafted in her head in three registers, because a summons in assessment season admits few readings and she had prepared for all of them, at speed, in the corridor, twice.

Lucilla let her stand at attention for exactly as long as it took to unwrap a candy. Then she slid a folder across the desk.

"The Solisylum has requested field verification of the Heliogyre drift," she said. "They asked for one of the Six. I'm sending a candidate." The candy clicked against her teeth. "The Six read instruments. I want someone who reads the thing the instruments are about."

Sigrika read the folder's cover twice. The words did not reorganize themselves into a punishment no matter how she held them. "President, my coursework—"

"Will keep. Your Resonance file won't." Lucilla let that sit on the desk between them with the folder. "I've read your telemetry, candidate. Every quarter since the Dark Tide. You resonate like a kettle someone has welded the whistle shut on, and you study like the weld is a virtue. I am prescribing weather." She unwrapped a second candy, which those who knew her would have recognized as an embrace. "There is a professor going east who requires a reader of old Royan. There is a shrine keeper going east who is owed a spring. There is a girl going east who will follow you into any weather on the map and has, I am told, already packed."

"…She packed?"

"The list was submitted for requisition this morning. It contains five bedrolls." Lucilla's eyes did the thing they did instead of smiling. "The rounding was explained to me. At length."

Sigrika stood very straight. The room held the folder, and the desk, and the drawer of sour candies, and thirty years of doors kept oiled, and a girl doing the hardest arithmetic of her education. "My family will ask what track this is," she said, quietly. "There's no — the Heliodic track doesn't have—"

"Tell them it is the one the Six were founded on." Lucilla said it the way she said her creeds, once, at full weight, and never again. "Before there was a tower to sit in, there were six people who walked out to look at the sun. The tower came later. Towers always come later." She stamped the folder without looking at it, which is her way of looking at it, and slid it across. "Conditions on page one. Non-negotiable. Board-level."

The page read:

*FIELD ASSIGNMENT, CANDIDATE CLASS.*
*FIELD 6 — DURATION: until the weather turns.*
*CONDITION (PRESIDENTIAL, NON-NEGOTIABLE):*
*when it turns cold, come back.*

Sigrika read it three times. Then she signed it, dated it, and stamped it with the little seal she used on lab reports, because she carried the seal everywhere, because of course she did, and Lucilla watched the whole ceremony with the expression of a woman filing something under *keep*, and dismissed her with: "Your first assessment is a journal, candidate. I'm told you keep one. Fill it."

At the door, Sigrika turned. The question got out before protocol could catch it: "Why me? The Six have better—"

"Because a year ago," Lucilla said, "in a simulation built to break you, an entire audience of professionals watched you take the gold they chained you with and decide it was yours." She unwrapped a third candy, which had no precedent. "Class dismissed."

Hiyuki came at the end of the day, as arranged, and found the office smelling of stamp ink and sugar.

"You're taking my best candidate," Lucilla said.

"I am returning her in autumn. Improved. It is called a field season; I am told the agricultural metaphor does wonders for the funding mirror."

"Mm," said Lucilla, in a fair imitation, and slid a stamped requisition form across the desk. Hiyuki read it. The form was blank except for one line, in the small immaculate bureaucrat's hand: *one (1) spoon. Second in the set.*

"Queue early," Lucilla said.

"I intend to be first."

"You intend to be *punctual*. There's a difference, and I've watched you discover it, and it has been the finest entertainment of my administration." She turned to the window, to the false dusk running its amber, and said, to the glass, in the voice she keeps for things she has decided to say only once a year: "The window stays open while you're gone. It closes in autumn. Don't make me close it on an empty evening."

"I am never late anymore," Hiyuki said, at the door. "It's my vice."

"Collect more. You're behind for your age."

She left the door open behind her. Lucilla will have noticed. She notices everything.

## VI. The Lake That Reads Clean

They left on the first morning of the posted thaw, and the send-off was small, which was the correct size.

At dawn, over the campus speakers — which have a schedule, and enforce it, and had never in their institutional history broadcast at that hour — a voice the whole city knew came on without preamble, warm and slightly off the pitch and entirely sure of itself, and played one song, a traveling song, the kind with a road in it, and signed off with: *"This one goes out to the early queue. Fleet Snowfluff, over and out. Bring back spoons."* No complaint was filed. The engineer on duty logged the anomaly as *weather* and stood by it at the inquiry, of which there was none.

At the medical annex, the candy dish outside Dr. Herssen's door had been refilled early. Denia took two, considered, and kept both.

"Both thieves are provisioned," she said.

"Tuesdays," said Luuk, from the doorway.

"We'll be back by one."

They went out by the plains road, four travelers and a hoverchair and a Starstack riding herd on the baggage, and the campus fell behind them window by window, and the plains opened out silver-green under the false sky's best rehearsal of April. Where the road bent nearest the glacier, the frost on the verge thickened by a breath, and through the soles of their boots came a sound below sound — three beats of it, patient as moving geology — and Hiyuki inclined her head without breaking stride, and Buddy pulsed twice against Mornye's collar, answering in kin, and Sigrika stopped dead in the road.

"He said something."

"He says it continuously," Mornye said, chair humming on past her. "We're going to find out to whom."

Half a kilometer on, Sigrika stopped again, at an old boundary stone leaning in the grass, and this time the whole party pulled up, because the girl had made a sound like a struck bell. The stone's glyphs — cut centuries ago, weathered to shadows — had re-inked themselves. Faint. New. She was on her knees in the verge with the journal out before anyone spoke.

"This was a warding text," she said. "Standard perimeter formula. *Hold. Keep out.* Every boundary stone on the plains says it; I learned to read on stones like this; but the verb — the verb's been moved into a mood old Royan doesn't *have*, there's a vowel here that postdates the entire corpus—" The pen was flying. Golden light came up faint at her wrists, the light of a gift working at full attention, and banked on its own.

"Now?" Hiyuki said.

"*Walk well,*" Sigrika read, and looked up with her whole face. "The stones are switching sides."

They reached the lake at midday.

The ice had gone out of it the week before, right on the thaw posting; the water lay clean and dark and unhurried, holding the false sky's light the way deep things hold light, at the surface, generously. The instruments, Mornye confirmed, read nothing at all. The seam beneath had been paid and closed for a year. It was, by every metric the Academy possessed, an ordinary lake, and the plain around it had grown the first grass of the season, and a girl who had died in it stood on the shore in a driftwood-buttoned coat with her hands in her pockets, looking at the water the way you look at a house you used to live in.

Behind her, with ferocious ceremony, Sigrika unpacked the picnic.

She had brought the folding table. She had, in fact, filed the paperwork: the reservation from the spring before last — *table for the birthday party, recurs annually, form set to recur* — had been formally relocated, venue field amended in triplicate, from the cafeteria annex to *outdoor seating*. The boxed cake came out of her pack cushioned like an instrument. It was cafeteria custard in structural form. The candles came out of a paper packet.

Two of them.

"For the record," Mornye said, watching them go in, crooked, "the subject's age."

"Two," said Denia, from the shore, without turning. "I'm precocious."

She came up from the water in her own time. She sat at the table the way she did everything, uninvited, comfortable, as though the table had been hers all along, which it had. The candles burned sincerely in the small wind. Four people and an amber chick sat around a folding table on a green plain by a clean lake, and sang the song, off-key in three directions and dead level in the fourth, and Denia sat with her chin in her hands and watched them do it with an attention total enough to be mistaken for laziness, which had always been her best trick.

When the flames were ready she leaned in, and paused, theatrically, and looked around the table.

"Next year," she said, "three."

"Three candles," Sigrika said, thickly, already writing it down.

"I'm told they come in packs."

She blew. The wish went where wishes go. And afterward, while Mornye ran a reading she described as *sentimental calibration* and Sigrika entered the lake in the journal — *Lake: unnamed. Proposal: Svidanie ("the Meeting"). Seconded: all present. Common name (dissenting, overruled, recorded anyway): Custard* — Denia walked back down to the waterline alone, and crouched, and put two fingers in the water, unhurried, the way she did everything.

She said one word to it, in the language of appointments. The word was the other half of the one she had left on this plain a year ago — the meeting, kept; the appointment, arrived at — and the water took it without ceremony, and a small wind crossed the surface from the far shore, where no one was standing, and where someone had once stood weeping with a very straight spine, and Denia stood up and dried her fingers on her coat.

At the table, Hiyuki had watched the whole transaction from behind her cup, and said nothing, which is the courtesy of confessionals everywhere. But when Denia sat back down, there was a sour candy on a flat stone at the waterline that had not been there before, label out, squarely placed.

For whoever's standing nearest.

They packed the table. Sigrika left the reservation form under a stone at the shore — *recurs annually* — weighted, dated, stamped. The lake held its light. Some places are named by grief, and wait years for the grief to come back as a picnic, and this one had waited exactly long enough.

The Riseway took them up through the rock like a held breath, and then the doors opened, and there was weather.

Real weather. A wind with no assigned corridor. Clouds proceeding on private business. Light that had crossed an actual distance to arrive, and took its time about it, and answered to nobody. Sigrika walked out into it and stood still, and her face did the thing faces do when the air of childhood arrives unannounced — the true frost, the resin, the particular mineral cold of the Roya — and then the birds started, and she was lost. Three journal entries in the first minute. She stopped mid-fourth, pen lifted, listening, home in the ear before home in the eye.

Mornye's chair met the wind, recalibrated, and hummed a quarter-tone higher, which is how instruments express delight. The Starstack extended two sensors it had never had occasion to use. Buddy came out from her collar and turned slow circles in the unadministered light.

Denia came out last but one, and stood on the apron with her hands in her pockets and her head tilted at the sky — the whole enormous unaudited morning of it, the light with no schedule, the wind with no form on file — and rendered her verdict.

"Nobody approved this."

A gust took her hair; the strand escaped its ear entirely and she let it.

"…I approve."

Hiyuki came out last, unhurried, antique, and the frost prints she left on the apron melted behind her at once, every one, the whole way to the road — her gladness legible in her wake, for anyone keeping records, and someone was; someone at her elbow was writing *white bird (elder): confirmed migratory* in a swift furious hand — and the east road ran out ahead of them through the resettlement corridor, green at the edges, entirely available.

They took it.

## VII. Fieldcraft

The season's first working day arrived on the third morning, unassigned, the way their work would now always arrive: by being nearest.

The hamlet sat where the corridor crossed an old scar — six rebuilt houses, a well-field, fences going up plank by plank — and the party had meant to pass through it by noon. They were still there at dusk, because the well-field's boundary stones had re-inked themselves like the plains stone and Sigrika had gone down among them like a diver; because the scar at the field's edge was weeping a thread of residue that made Mornye's instruments state their objections in italics; and because at the third fence an old man was rehearsing something under his breath, the same eleven words, over and over, polished featureless, in the manner of an apology forty years unsent.

Hiyuki stood at his fence until he noticed her, which took a while, and was allowed to.

"Tell me," she said. "I will hear it as she would have."

He told her. It took the eleven words and then, unlocked, another hour, and she received all of it, seated on his fence rail with her hands around a cup of his terrible tea, and answered as the woman would have answered, and the man went into his rebuilt house at dusk lighter by exactly the weight of having been heard, and behind Hiyuki's sternum, where a bead of ice would once have gone, the office carried nothing away at all, and counted that the profit.

At the scar, meanwhile, Denia worked.

She had taken one look at the residue — the black thread of it seeping at the field's edge, the void's small change, the family inheritance — and sighed the sigh of a professional called in on her day off, and taken out the Dream Maker. The bubble she blew was the size of a shed. It settled over the seep with the gentle finality of a lid, and the residue lifted into the film like smoke remembering it had once been weather, and pearled there, and she considered it with her head tilted, and rolled what remained into a neat black cube the size of a die.

She weighed it in her palm. Buddy, watching from the Starstack, retreated behind a sensor housing and stayed there.

"Sample," she said, and held it out.

Mornye turned the cube once in her fingers, ran a number on it, and closed her hand.

"I'll carry it," she said. "For the group's benefit."

The trouble came at the well-field, an hour past noon, and it came from the party's own weather.

Sigrika had been down among the stones for six hours. Fourteen re-inked texts, three impossible moods, one verb that appeared to conjugate in the future perfect of a language with no future tense, and she had catalogued all of it at full effort, at exam speed, skipping the water Denia set at her elbow and the rest Mornye scheduled and the shade entirely, because the stones were *switching sides* and somebody had to enter it into the record — and the gold came up at her wrists past its banking point, and kept coming, chain-light climbing her forearms with the heat of everything she had ever been expected to be, and her breath went short, and the fifteenth stone cracked its new ink under her hands.

The party moved the way a body moves.

Denia's bubble arrived first — small, slow, milk into tea — and settled the air's volume down around her, the world arriving suddenly at a merciful distance. Hiyuki's two fingers touched the nape of her neck, and the frost there was the temperature of a held railing in winter, exact, kind. And Mornye's chair pulled up at eye level, and the professor consulted her Starstack, and announced, flatly:

"Mandatory calibration downtime. Fifteen minutes. It's in the protocol."

"Whose protocol," Sigrika managed.

"Mine. Drafted this morning. Peer-reviewed." Buddy pulsed once, amber, solemn. "The peer concurs."

Sigrika sat down in the grass. The gold banked, and dimmed, and went out, faster than it had ever gone out in the telemetry of her entire candidacy, and she sat there feeling it go with her eyes stinging at the mechanics of it, and said, quietly, to the field:

"It's never gone down that fast."

"You've never had a pit crew," said Denia, and handed her the water again, and this time it was drunk.

The well-field's survey concluded at dusk, fifteen stones catalogued, one protocol ratified, and the hamlet fed them dinner over their objections, and the objections were Denia's, delivered while she was helping cook it.

Camp, that night, above the corridor, under the real sky.

The fire was built to Mornye's stated geometry and Denia's actual construction, which diverged, and burned excellently, and neither of them pursued it. Dinner was declared unnecessary by the person stirring it. Tea was poured with the water cooled first, and Sigrika was coached through her second attempt — "You're pouring at a boil." "The syllabus said—" "The leaf outranks the syllabus." — and produced a cup Hiyuki pronounced "coachable," which entered the journal under *honors*.

The stars came up unfixed.

Mornye watched them move — minutely, all night, the whole sky one slow unsupervised mechanism — with the Starstack dark in her lap and a stylus and one paper napkin, unaugmented, running parallax by hand at half volume with Sigrika leaning in on the arithmetic, the two of them conducting an argument about reference frames that lasted three hours and settled nothing and was, for both of them, the entire point. Buddy slept on the chair's headrest. Hiyuki sat a little apart, in the rhythm of a woman with her own itinerary who had discovered that itineraries hold company, and hummed, once, briefly, the second voice of a three-note song, and the road held it the way the crystals would have, and the dark was gentle.

Sigrika fought sleep to the last cartridge. "I can stay up. I stayed up through every exam season, I can absolutely—"

The bubble was already drifting toward her, small and slow, milk into tea, and inside it, faint, patient, a heartbeat at rest — somebody else's, the oldest lullaby there is. It touched down on her shoulder and held.

"That's *cheating*—"

"Cultural heritage," said Denia.

Sigrika went under mid-syllable, journal sliding, chin to chest, a candidate for the Heliodic Six defeated in the field by a toy, and Denia lifted her onto the bedroll with the practiced economy of two a.m., and drew the blanket to her shoulder, and set the Dream Maker on the pack beside her ear, where a heartbeat goes.

The fire worked. Mornye fell asleep over the napkin an hour later, mid-derivation, and Hiyuki laid her own outer robe across her shoulders, and adjusted Buddy half a centimeter closer, and sat back down.

Two remained, on opposite sides of the fire, in the oldest shift there is.

"You don't sleep," Hiyuki said.

"I'm a professional." Denia fed the fire a stick it didn't need, out of courtesy. "You?"

"A century of night watch. The habit is load-bearing."

"Mm," said Denia, in a fair imitation, and the fire cracked, and the unfixed stars went on being unfixed, and somewhere down the corridor a night bird said something neither of them could read and both of them heard. "We should unionize."

"The night takes walk-ins."

They kept it together, wordless, until the light. It was the most companionable watch either of them had stood in some decades, and one of them had only decades of one.

---

Sigrika woke at dawn having slept ten hours under an open sky, a lifetime record, verified, and lay still for a moment doing the audit — heartbeat at her ear, patient, at rest; three sleepers and a fire gone to warm ash; a whole unscheduled morning arriving at its own pace — and then she reached for the journal, because the record has to read it, and found that the census page had grown an entry overnight, in a looser hand:

*Orange bird: loud at dawn. Keeps the records. Keeps everything.*

She held the page very carefully, both hands, the way she had once held a looking-away, and then she uncapped her pen, and completed the census in the swift furious hand of the never-giving-up:

*FIELD CENSUS, DAY THREE.*

*White bird (elder): migratory, confirmed. Sings second voice. Warm hands. Returns when cold.*

*White bird (younger): returned. Range expanding daily. Do not attempt to out-lazy. (Attempted. Partial credit.)*

*Quiet bird with the amber chick: flightless by build, farthest-traveling by intent. Do not assist unless asked. Ask anyway, once.*

*Chick, amber (1): adopted by all. Objects to cubes.*

*Flock: n = 4, +1 chick. Route: east. Feeding grounds: queues, the wait is the flavor. Migration: returns when the weather turns.*

*This census recurs annually. I set the form to recur.*

She closed the journal. East of them the road ran on toward its appointments — a wood full of witches expecting a girl for breakfast, and her flock with her, sour candies already on the saucer; a coast city with a stand by the east gate and a queue worth arriving early for; four Dolls with a standing order and a signed spring; a god saying one word patiently down the roots of the world to someone who was finally coming to turn around — and behind them, under the world, a window stood open that would close in autumn, and a door stood oiled, and a lake held its clean water under a stone-weighted form.

They broke camp without hurry. They walked east into the spring, four weathers and a chick, on time for everything, owned by nobody, expected everywhere.

## I. Two Toffees and a Sour Thing

The demonstration finished at four thirty-one.

By four fifty the diagnostic bay had gone back to being a room. Mornye's people were arguing over a waveform trace with the specific joy of researchers who have just watched their field acquire a new wing. The drone lay folded on the floor with its lamp off. Iuno had taken the clock off the crate and put it in her pocket, and had done it slowly, so that everyone could see her do it.

Rebecca had gone out to the loading dock after Lupa. That left one direction unaccounted for, and Cartethyia took it, because there had been a moment during the demonstration when she had looked at a girl sitting upright on a bench with her eyes open and her hands loose in her lap, and had recognised the arrangement of the fingers from eleven weeks of mornings in another building in another country, and had put both hands over her mouth.

She found Aemeath four floors up, in the east wing, standing outside the infirmary.

She was standing the way people stand outside doors they have been through ten thousand times: at an angle, at a distance, checking the geometry.

"Are you going in?"

"Don't say *going in.*"

"...Are you going *through the door.*"

"Yes." Aemeath rolled her shoulders. "Give me a second. I'm doing a thing."

She went through the door.

Luuk Herssen was reading a chart. He had been reading a chart for thirteen years. He was greyer at the temples and his gloves were the same white and the dish sat at his right elbow, glass, shallow, brimming, because it was Tuesday and the little kids had come through at eight.

Aemeath took two toffees and a sour thing.

Clean extraction: twenty-two years of practice in a single motion, wrist low, palm turning once, the candy into her sleeve before her shadow finished crossing the blotter. She was already turning when she heard, behind her, in a bright carrying voice pitched for the back of a market square:

"Excuse me — may I have some of those?"

Aemeath stopped in the middle of the floor.

"Take four," said Luuk Herssen, to his chart.

"*Four!* Thank you. The purple ones are the good ones, aren't they. You've put them at the bottom on purpose."

"They last longer down there."

"That's *devious.* I approve."

In the corridor, Aemeath waited with her arms folded until Cartethyia came out counting her haul into her palm like a girl at a fair.

"You asked him."

"I said please."

"You don't *ask.*"

"He said take four."

"That's—" Aemeath held up her own two toffees and a sour thing, the entire yield of the operation, evidence for a prosecution she was losing. "This is a system. It has run since I was eight years old. Both parties pretend."

"Pretend what?"

"That he doesn't see me."

"He sees you."

"I *know* he sees me."

"Then what's the pretending for?"

The corridor did its long fluorescent hum. Somewhere below them a shuttle bay clanked and settled. Aemeath opened her mouth with a very good answer in it — a fast one, a professional one, three years of midnight radio and a lifetime of lunch tables — and the answer stayed where it was.

Because yesterday, on the first morning, with nine people in the room, he had put down his pen and told her mother *there's a dish on the shelf, it's Tuesday,* out loud, in front of everybody, like a man reading a weather report. Twenty-two years of an economy that ran entirely on both parties pretending, and he had spent it in six words on a woman who had never stolen anything from him in her life.

Aemeath had gone up to the gallery afterward and stood at the window for eleven minutes. Then she had come back down and stolen from him at the usual hour on the usual day, to check that the machine still worked.

Behind them, through the doorway, Luuk turned a page.

Cartethyia leaned back through it. She did it without hurry, one hand on the frame, the way she had once leaned into a cottage full of prop swords.

"Doctor. Thank you for keeping it full."

The sound of a page stopped. It stopped completely, in the way a small ordinary noise stops when the hand producing it has forgotten that it is a hand.

Aemeath was already moving — down the corridor, past the supply cart, past the nurse, at a pace that was absolutely and definitely not fleeing, folder clamped under her arm, three candies in her sleeve and a sound in her throat she had no filing category for.

Cartethyia caught up at the stairwell, one step at a time, favouring the left hip.

She did not ask.

## II. Forty Minutes

The frost-viewing gallery ran the whole north face of the Academy's upper level: sixty metres of thermal glass, four centimetres thick, and behind it the Roya Frostlands going white to the circumference of the world.

Aemeath stood at the third window from the end. She always stood at the third window from the end. She had cried at this window at sixteen with a red-haired girl's scarf around her neck and neither of them had ever mentioned it, and she had come back here in her fourth year of being weather and stood at the same pane in the same spot with her hands going through the rail.

She stood there now with her hands on the rail, and the rail was cold, and she made herself notice that it was cold.

Cartethyia arrived at the glass like a thrown ball.

"It's *doing it again.*"

"It does that."

"It's been doing it for a *week.*" Both palms flat on the pane. "When does it stop?"

"Frostbreak. Sixty days, give or take."

"Sixty *days.*" She said it the way a person says a scandalous sum. In Egla the weather had been an event; a storm arrived, got talked about for a season, and went into the register. She turned her head sideways against the glass to look up the fall line and her breath fogged an oval and she wiped it with her sleeve without taking her eyes off the sky. "Where does it come from?"

"Cloud deck. About two thousand metres."

"How long does it take?"

Aemeath heard herself answer before she decided to. "Forty minutes."

"Forty—"

"A little under a metre a second. So everything you're looking at right now left the cloud about forty minutes ago." She watched a flake pass the third window at the speed of a slow thought. "Whatever it's going to do, it decided before we got here."

Cartethyia went quiet. It was a specific quiet. Aemeath had learned to hear it in the four months since a woman with a laurel of crystal thorn had walked into her mother's life and started leaving doors open on principle. It was the quiet of somebody putting a thing into a play.

"So it's already committed," Cartethyia said.

"It's snow."

"It committed *forty minutes ago* and it can't take it back and it doesn't know we're watching." She pressed her forehead to the glass. "That's the best thing anyone has told me all year."

"It's precipitation."

"It's *conviction.*"

"It's water."

"So am I. So are you. Look at us." She lowered herself down the glass to sit on the gallery floor with her legs out — carefully, left hand going to the hip on the way down, in a manoeuvre she had clearly worked out that morning — and produced from inside her coat a paper packet gone translucent at the corners. "Sit. I made these badly."

They were lemon cakes. The shape was wrong and the crumb was dense and somebody had put the sugar on while the tops were still hot, so half of it had glazed and the other half sat up in coarse crystals, cut too big, catching the flat white light off sixty metres of snow.

Aemeath looked at the crystals for slightly too long.

"They're supposed to catch the light," Cartethyia said. "Rosa's Gram cut them coarse for that. She said—" and here she did a voice, an old woman's voice, an entire village in four words— "*'so they look like frost.'*" A beat. "I had no idea what frost looked like when she said it. I said yes, obviously, frost, I know frost."

"You lied to an old woman about frost."

"I was nine. I was extremely busy."

Aemeath took one. She held it a second, which she did with everything now and hoped nobody had clocked. The first bite came in on time: sugar, the crunch, butter. The lemon arrived about a second later, from further off, like a word shouted across a field.

Cartethyia was watching her eat with an attention that would have been rude anywhere else in the building.

"Why are you looking at me like that."

"I want to know if you can taste it."

The honest answer sat there. Aemeath turned the cake over and considered lying to it, and then remembered who was asking, and how she had asked, and said: "Sweet comes on time. The rest gets there when it gets there."

"Mm." Cartethyia nodded — entirely unsurprised, entirely uncondescending, a woman confirming a colleague's instrument reading. "Mine came back all at once. In a kitchen in Porto-Veno, on the second morning, honey cakes. Twenty years of eating the idea of things and then it was all there at once and I sat down on the floor and ate four of them crying." She said it the way you'd say you took the shuttle. "Cantarella refilled the plate and pretended to read."

"Did you thank her?"

"I called her Kokomi for a month." She bit her own cake and made a face at it. "Under-baked. Gram would put me in the road."

The snow came down at a little under a metre per second and kept its appointments.

"What did Herssen say about the hip?"

"Three options." Cartethyia licked sugar off her thumb. "The third one is nothing at all, for now. A stick when the weather turns and physio twice a week and he sees me in eighteen months."

"You took the third."

"I took the third." She tipped her head back against the glass. "He said most people refuse it because it looks like losing."

"Huh."

"Mm," said Cartethyia, and left it there, in the middle of the floor, where it could be picked up later by anybody who happened to need it.

"Are you actually going to carry a stick."

"I'm going to *carve* one." Instantly bright. "I've got the blank. It wants an animal on the handle."

"What animal?"

"Haven't decided. It'll turn up." She brushed crumbs off her knee. "They always turn up."

---

"Amy."

"Mm."

"When the doctor gave me the four sweets. Why did you leave?"

Aemeath watched a flake finish a decision it had made three-quarters of an hour ago, somewhere high and dark, where nobody was watching.

"He'd have had to look up," she said.

## III. Somebody Has to Hold the Pencil

The folders sat between them on the gallery floor: grey, identical, stamped with the Academy's crest and the day's date.

Cartethyia's was open, dog-eared, and had a thumbprint of lemon glaze on the corner. Aemeath's was sealed.

"You haven't read yours."

"I'm savouring it."

"You're *not.*" Cartethyia sat up. "Give it here."

"No."

"Give it—"

"It's *mine.*"

"That's the problem!" She said it with enormous cheer, a woman announcing the solution to a puzzle at a dinner table. "You can't read your own. Nobody can read their own. It's the same as the doorframe."

Aemeath went very still.

At the cabin, three weeks ago, with the fire going and half the pack asleep in a heap on the floor and Mei out on the step with Iuno and the cold coming in under the door, Cartethyia had stood a long time at the kitchen doorframe with her head tipped, looking at a ladder of pencil marks that went *Amy, 6. Amy, 6 and a half (she insisted). Amy, 7* in one hand, and then four crooked strokes in a careful forgery of the same hand, and then a great deal of blank wood.

She had said nothing about it. Aemeath had watched her say nothing about it from the other side of the room and had decided the matter closed.

"You looked at the frame."

"You can't measure yourself," Cartethyia said. "It comes out crooked. Somebody has to hold the pencil." She put her hand out, palm up, patient. "So. You read mine, I'll read yours, we both find out what we are, and then we go and be insufferable about it at dinner."

"There's a rule. Patient copies are sealed until—"

"I opened mine with my teeth in the corridor."

"You—"

"There was a wax tab." Wounded dignity. "It had a *little tab.* What did they think the tab was for."

Aemeath looked at the hand. Then at the window. Then she picked up her sealed folder and put it into the palm and took the lemon-glazed one out of Cartethyia's lap, and the trade happened fast, the way you pull a splinter.

"Read the boring pages," Cartethyia said, settling in.

"Nobody reads the boring pages."

"I read the boring pages." A shrug that carried a whole town in it. "My father was a registrar."

"I offered."

"I said I've been having that argument since I was twenty-one and I'm ahead on points."

"You knew."

"They told me in the chair. She kept apologising. As if she'd left it there herself."

"There's a *room,*" Aemeath said. "There's a room off the coast of your country that is your last second, running."

"Mm."

"Forever."

"So they tell me."

"And it's four degrees."

"That's the bit I keep coming back to," Cartethyia agreed, from the floor. "It was warmer than that. When I did it. Everything was very bright and very warm and I remember being *pleased,* which is a strange thing to have to carry around afterwards." She lowered the folder onto her chest. "Four degrees. I'd like to know where the warm went."

The snow went past the window, forty minutes late, at a metre a second.

"Are you going to go?"

"...Not alone."

"That's not a no."

"It's the closest one I've got."

Aemeath let it stand. She turned the last page.

> [ANOMALY, PERSISTENT: a second-order harmonic is present in every read, external in origin, **bilateral.**]
> [Cross-reference: patient file 002, this cohort.]
> [The harmonic does not attenuate with distance. Three tests at increasing range (same floor; opposite wing; surface, via Riseway shuttle at eleven kilometres) returned identical amplitude to four decimal places.]
> [The instrument declines to call this a symptom. The assessor declines to call it anything.]

"'Bilateral,'" Aemeath read out.

"Mm-hm."

"It doesn't get weaker with distance."

"Nope."

"They took you eleven kilometres up the Riseway to check."

"They were *thrilled.* They gave me a hot drink and a blanket. I have never been so popular."

Aemeath looked at the line, and then at file 002 of this cohort, and did the arithmetic in about a second and a half, because she had spent her adolescence in a sublevel doing exactly this kind of arithmetic and had been the best in eleven hundred at it.

"Whose is it."

"Mine!" said Cartethyia, bright as a bell.

There was a pause of a very particular length.

"I built that voice," Aemeath said.

Cartethyia's folder came down off her face.

"I built it when I was fourteen," Aemeath said, to the snow. "Midnight, dead channel, borrowed transmitter. It's a good voice. It's got a lift at the end so nobody follows up. Four hundred people liked it enough to draw fan art." She turned her head. "You're using my voice. Badly. Say it properly or leave it alone."

The gallery hummed.

Cartethyia sat up slowly, cross-legged, and put both hands flat on the folder in her lap, and when she spoke she had put the bell away.

"If I die," she said, "Mei dies."

## IV. A Hundred and Six

They ate the rest of the lemon cakes.

It took a while, and neither of them hurried it, because the sentence had to be allowed to finish landing, and because Cartethyia had gone somewhere behind her eyes and Aemeath had learned from a woman with golden eyes and a bad shoulder that you do not go in after people. You sit at the edge with the light on.

"The Imperator did it," Cartethyia said eventually. "Rebuilding a person needs an anchor, so it tied two threads. Cut one, both end. Mei said *done* before it finished the sentence, and then it killed her to check whether she meant it, and she meant it, and here we are." She brushed sugar off her knee. "I've had eight months to find something funny about it. I've got nothing."

"It's a leash."

"It's a *beautiful* leash. It's the nicest thing anybody's ever done to me." She said both of those with total sincerity, which was the thing about her: the woman could hold two whole opinions in one hand and set them both on the table with the price tags showing. "And for twenty years the only asset I had was that I could always be the one who pays. That was the entire trick. Whatever the sum came to, I could cover it, because I was in the till."

"And now the till's got somebody in it."

"Now the till has *Mei* in it." She laughed once, with no comedy in it. "So when the next thing comes — and something's coming, Amy, I can *feel* something coming, the air in this city has been wrong for eleven days—"

"The maintenance union logged that. Somebody wrote 'investigate' and somebody else wrote 'it's air.'"

"It's *not* air," said Cartethyia, with the flat certainty of the last living person to have learned swordsmanship from the wind. "When the next thing comes, and everyone's doing the arithmetic, and it's the kind of arithmetic where somebody has to be the one who—" She stopped and started again. "Everybody at that table is going to look at me. Because that's what I'm *for.* And I'll sit there with my hands in my lap and say: I can't, I'm holding somebody."

Aemeath looked at the third window from the end.

"You'd have volunteered," she said.

"I'd have had my hand up before the sentence finished."

"Yeah." Aemeath rested the back of her head against the glass. "I know the feeling. I once said yes to a full-synchronisation protocol before they'd told me what it was. They were still saying the words. I remember thinking they were slow."

"Was it worth it?"

"The gate held."

"That's a different question to the one I asked."

"It is," Aemeath agreed.

They sat with it.

"Tell me about the dolls," she said.

Cartethyia's whole face changed, which was the point of asking.

"A hundred and six."

"Names?"

"All of them. Full names, ranks, and where they stood." She counted on her fingers. "Ser Pincushion, knight-commander. Donna Mestola, mistress of the household, made out of a ladle and a dishcloth, and she *ruled that place with terror.* Bishop Nettle, who was corrupt. There was a whole scandal."

"You gave yourself a corrupt bishop."

"For *drama.* You can't run a repertory season without an antagonist and I'd already used the sea monster twice that year." She turned her hand over. "I did the voices. I did all the voices."

"Were you good?"

"I was the best actress in the building."

"Who was second?"

"Pincushion. Limited range." A pause. "He's very sincere, though. It carries him."

Aemeath laughed, and heard how it came out, and let it, because the person next to her had already heard it and had chosen to keep looking at the snow.

"Ten thousand four hundred," she said.

Cartethyia's head came round.

"Voices. Out past the gate, in the drift zones where the void sorts its salvage. Explorers, mostly. Survey crews. People who went in and got taken apart, and the last coherent thing they said stayed out there going round." She was very interested in a crumb on her sleeve. "I catalogued them. Somebody should have. Nobody had."

"Did you talk back?"

"To the ones that could still be talked to."

"How many was that?"

"Under two hundred."

"So the rest—"

"The rest I wrote down." She flicked the crumb. "There's a difference. I couldn't fix it. I could witness it. Turns out witnessing is a whole job. Nobody puts it on a form."

Cartethyia was nodding slowly.

"That's what the dolls were," she said.

"The dolls were company."

"The dolls were *staying.*" She said it like she'd found the word after a long search. "Everybody hears about the hundred and six and goes all soft and sad — poor girl, she made friends out of laundry. It's an *engineering* problem. The tower took my name off me about once a year. It came in like weather. I'd wake up on a chapel floor with nobody at all in my head, and every single thing in that building had a name on it in my own handwriting. Ropes. All the way down the hall, all the way to the refectory, all the way back to *me.*" She spread her hands. "Name it and it can't go. It works on things."

"Does it work on people?"

"It works on everything." She was quoting a woman with a lute, and she did not say so. "That's the frightening part. Keep faith with a scrap of cloth for twelve years and one day you'll come round a corner and it will have an opinion about you." A beat. "That's a materials report."

Aemeath thought about a monitoring channel keyed to a dead band, and a static that stayed, and a ranking of everyone she knew by whether they stayed, in which the static had finished top three.

"Do you miss it," she said.

The question went out over the folders and the crumbs and the snow.

"Yes," said Cartethyia.

"Everybody asks it like the answer's no."

"Everybody's frightened of the answer being yes." She had gone back to lying on the floor. "There was a dragon in the garden. A Reverberation, coiled through the dead arbors, singing to itself in a key like a bowed glass, and I worked out which key it liked and I sang with it for about nine years. We were *terrible.* We were so bad. It's the best thing I have ever been part of and I can't say it to anybody, because if I say it to Mei she'll look at me like I've been hurt again, and if I say it to Cia she'll write it down and put a chord under it and then I'll have to feel it in public."

"Tuesdays," said Aemeath.

"What?"

"I keep Tuesdays." She said it fast, the way you get into cold water. "Up in the Drive I kept a calendar. Deliberately. The clock goes soft up there — you can stretch one note across a week or run a month through in a breath — and if you let go of the city's second you start thinking in centuries, and then you're weather. So I kept the second. And I kept Tuesdays, because Tuesday was the dish, and if I could get an hour of filament down the conduits it was Tuesday's hour."

"And you still keep them."

"I booked the whole cohort's medical for a Tuesday, six weeks out, and fought a scheduling clerk about it." Aemeath turned a candy over in her fingers. "I have a *body.* I have a bed with my mother's girlfriend in it, which is its own ongoing situation. I could see that dish any day of the week." A shrug. "I've stopped knowing what the Tuesday's for. I'm still not putting it down."

Cartethyia did not tell her to put it down.

"Keep it," she said. "Whatever you're gripping for. Keep it."

She said it the way a very old bronze soldier had once said it to a girl in a seminary yard at the end of a drill, two degrees at a time, and she did not mention that, and Aemeath had no way of knowing, and the sentence worked anyway. That is how those sentences travel.

"You told Cia you were good at Tuesdays and timers."

"I *am* good at Tuesdays and timers."

"Ninety minutes a day," said Aemeath. "You've appointed yourself the tendon police."

"I've appointed myself the *timer.* There's a difference and she'll find out about it on Thursday."

## V. The Boring Pages

"Right," said Cartethyia, slapping the other folder onto her knees. "Yours. I have been *very* patient."

"You read four pages of it while I was—"

"I read the *cover.* I have the discipline of a saint. I was *canonised.*"

She opened it and read for a while with her lips moving on the technical vocabulary, which she pronounced with total confidence and consistent error.

> [RESONANCE EVALUATION REPORT — SUBJECT: **Aemeath.** Prior institutional designation: Ward 734. Prior status of record: deceased (Startorch Academy; memorial garden; plaque and statue).]
> [Status amended. Amendment queried by the Registrar's office. Amendment upheld. The Registrar's office has requested guidance.]

"They asked for guidance," said Cartethyia, joyfully.

"There's no form for it."

"There's never a form for it. That's what forms are *like.*"

> [TACET MARK: hollow of the throat. Small, elongated, with three projections along one edge. The subject, asked what the shape resembles, answered "nothing."]

Cartethyia looked up.

Aemeath had her chin down. Under the collar, in the notch of the throat, the mark sat where a cold house key on a cord had sat against the skin, under a sync suit, on the last afternoon of a girl's life.

"It's a key," said Cartethyia.

Aemeath's hand went to her throat before she could stop it.

"It's a key," Cartethyia repeated, in exactly the tone of a woman identifying a knight-commander. "You've been looking at that in a mirror for four months telling people it's nothing."

"It's an abstract—"

"It has *wards.* Three of them. It's the key to a door you locked." She went back to the page with the serenity of somebody who had said an obvious thing. "Honestly. Frequency medicine. Get a stage designer in, we'd have had that by lunch."

"So there's a clock on the body."

"There *might* be a clock on the body. Nobody knows. They ran me for nine minutes and everything was—"

"—and everything was completely normal, so honestly, the whole line's speculative, it's the kind of thing they *have* to write down because otherwise the insurance—"

"Amy."

"—and the drone thing was under a minute, so if there is a clock it's a long one, and there's no actual evidence of any—"

"*Amy.*"

Aemeath shut up.

"I sat with your mother for eleven weeks," Cartethyia said.

The gallery hummed.

"I had the mornings. Lupa had the nights; she did nine weeks and she'll tell you it was nine, because she counts down and I count up, and we have never once agreed about that ward." Cartethyia had her hands loose on her knees, palms up, and she was looking at them. "Six to eleven, every morning. I'd open the blinds because a room should know what time it is. I'd talk. Aemeath's own hands were in her lap.

She looked down at them.

## VII. Manners

By six the light behind the glass had gone the blue of the last hour and the snow had thickened.

"We're decommissioned, you know," Aemeath said.

"Sorry?"

"You can't spend yourself, because it costs Ma. And I can't spend myself, because—" She turned her hand over again. "Because four hundred and eleven people already paid for this, and I'd be spending their money." She let it sit. "Two Sentinel-class assets. Benched."

"*Sentinel-class,*" said Cartethyia, appalled. "Who writes these words."

"There's a whole scale. You're on it. It's got a colour."

"I'm on a *scale?*"

"You're at the top of it. They put you above a Threnodian containment rating." Aemeath was starting to grin. "There's a footnote about you. It says *see attached.*"

"*See attached!*"

"You *are* the attachment."

Cartethyia lay on the gallery floor and laughed at the ceiling — a real laugh, loud, unlovely, the laugh of a girl who had once fallen off a windmill and reported that the flying part had been easy. It went down sixty metres of glass and came back thin.

"So what do we do," she said, when she had it back. "If we can't be spent. What's a knight for, if she can't be the one who—"

"Most people refuse it because it looks like losing," said Aemeath.

Cartethyia stopped laughing.

"That's *mine.*"

"It's Herssen's. You had it for about four hours and you left it on the floor." Aemeath pulled her knees up. "He gave you an option that looks like losing and you took it before lunch, and then you spent the afternoon telling me the till's shut and you can't pay any more, like that's a *diagnosis.*"

"It *is* a—"

"He offered you eighteen months of nothing at all and you took it in *one second,*" said Aemeath. "You took the third option, Cartethyia. Take the third option about the other thing."

The gallery hummed.

"That's a horrible thing to do to somebody with their own doctor," said Cartethyia.

"Yeah, well. I'm nineteen. I'm very good at deciding things about other people."

"You're thirty-two."

"I'm *nineteen and thirteen,* and the thirteen doesn't count, and I have a whole speech about it." Aemeath knocked her shoulder into Cartethyia's. "Get a job."

"I'd *love* a job."

"You'd be terrible at a job."

"I would be *magnificent* at a job. I'd have a uniform." Cartethyia sat up. "What's yours?"

"I don't have a—"

"You've been rehearsing one all afternoon. You do a thing with your mouth."

Aemeath looked at the third window from the end.

"There's a storage room on level nine," she said. "Broadcast club. There's a transmitter in it and an equipment-test band nobody monitors, and I've got a key I'm not supposed to have, and it's been in my coat since the day I got a coat."

"Amy."

"It's a dead channel now. They reclassified it the term after I died. *Equipment testing.*" She shrugged with one shoulder. "There's an unfinished song. Two hundred people stopped it together in an auditorium sixty days after the funeral, all at once, at the edge where it stopped, and then they went home. I finished it about nine years ago. Up in the sun. It's been sitting there done."

"So sing it."

"It was for her."

"So sing it to her."

"She *came,*" said Aemeath. "That's the problem. Snowfluff was — Snowfluff got to be the version where Ma stayed. That was the whole point of her. Born at midnight, age zero, no forms, no wards, everybody loves her on sight, no waiting required." Her jaw worked. "And now Ma stayed. So what's Snowfluff *for?*"

Cartethyia thought about that with her chin on her knees, long enough that the snow got noticeably heavier.

"You've got two names," she said.

"What?"

"You've got two. Fleet Snowfluff, and the other one. The one Ciaccona won't say without doing the face."

"Nyx Ravenhollow is a *perfectly*—"

"It's the greatest name I have ever heard in my life and I intend to see it on something enormous," said Cartethyia, with total sincerity, and Aemeath went pink. "That's what I mean, though. Look at the two of you. I named a hundred and six things so they'd stay put. You keep naming *yourself* so you can move house." She turned her hand over. "That's the same trick pointed the other way, and I have been trying to work out all afternoon which of us has it right."

"And?"

"And I think you're allowed more than one door." Cartethyia bumped her shoulder back. "Snowfluff was the room you built when the house was empty. The house has got people in it now. She can still be a room. Rooms don't stop being rooms because somebody came home."

Aemeath sat with that.

"Hum something," she said.

"What?"

"Hum. Anything. Go on."

"You'll regret this."

"I've read the reviews."

So Cartethyia hummed.

It was, by any technical standard, atrocious. It changed key twice inside four bars, the bridge had been assembled out of parts from three unrelated tunes, and one section appeared to be in no key that had ever been described. It was the sound of a girl in an inverted seminary putting noise into dead ventilation to prove the silence had lost, and the building had exhaled it out through a fold into the wind over Ragunna, and the wind, which keeps everything, had kept it.

Aemeath listened to eight bars with her head tipped.

Then she came in under it.

She took the flat third and made it deliberate, and found a floor for it, and set two notes where the bridge fell apart so the fall had somewhere to land. By the second pass the thing had walls. By the third it had a roof, and Cartethyia's eyes had gone very wide over the top of it, and she kept going, louder, wrecking it beautifully, while a girl who had spent ten years being the only architecture inside an empty sun built a room around her out of nothing at all.

They ran it four times. It got worse and better simultaneously, which is a thing that can happen.

When they stopped, the gallery rang for about a second and a half.

"You're a *singer,*" said Cartethyia, betrayed.

"I had a following."

"Four hundred people!"

"That was at fourteen. It peaked higher."

"You let me hum *at* you—"

"I asked you to hum at me. There's a difference." Aemeath got to her feet and put a hand out and hauled Cartethyia up off the floor, slowly, with the hip. "Anyway. Now somebody's done it to you the other way round, so you know how the bard felt."

"That's *diabolical.*"

"Mm."

Aemeath went along the gallery and came back with something she had taken out of Diagnostics on the way through, which was a technician's grease pencil: red, blunt, the kind used for marking sample glass.

"Stand at the frame."

"What?"

"The window frame. That one. Steel — it'll take the wax." She pointed. "Back against it. Heels touching. Stand up straight."

Cartethyia looked at the frame, and at the pencil, and at the girl holding it, and her face went through about four things at speed and came out the other side very carefully blank.

"Amy."

"Heels touching."

"I don't have any."

"I know," said Aemeath. "That's why I'm doing it."

At the cabin, in the kitchen doorway, there was a ladder of marks in a dead woman's handwriting and four crooked forgeries above them and then a great deal of blank wood, and Cartethyia had stood in front of it for a long time three weeks ago and had been unable to say why she couldn't stop looking. A registrar had written her a birthday out of a hat, because a baby had turned up dry in wet reeds and the truth needed help. There was no frame anywhere in the world with her on it.

She backed up to the steel. She stood very straight. She put her heels together like a soldier, because if a thing is going to be done it should be done with the correct footing.

Aemeath laid a folder flat on her head, checked it twice with the edge of her thumb, and drew the line.

Then she wrote beside it, in blunt red wax, in handwriting taught by a woman who painted a door blue and got paint on her wrist and called it permanent:

**CARTETHYIA — 41**

She looked at it. Then she added, underneath:

*(21. she insisted.)*

"It's crooked," said Cartethyia, turning round.

"They're supposed to be crooked."

"It's *very* crooked."

"Then you can do mine and we'll see how you get on."

So Cartethyia did hers — badly, tongue between her teeth, folder skidding — and the line came out a full centimetre high, and neither of them corrected it. They stood back and looked at two red marks on an institutional window frame on the north face of a school inside a mountain, with sixty metres of glass beside them and a whole winter behind that, and Aemeath found she had to look at the snow for a while.

"Next year," said Cartethyia.

"Next year. Same frame."

"Same frame." Cartethyia squinted at her own line with her head on one side. "I want to do this against something *enormous.*"

"Against what?"

"I'll know it when I see it."

---

The draft came along the gallery at eight minutes to seven.

It came from the east end, which is where the fire stair is, and the gallery's vents draw west, and it came slowly, the way a person comes into a room where something is happening that they would rather leave alone.

It lifted the top page of Aemeath's open folder — the accounts page, the boring one — turned it over, held it a moment, and set it back down flat.

Both of them watched it do that.

Cartethyia raised one hand and moved it slowly, side to side.

"Checking," she said, before she was asked.

"...It's a draft."

"Mm."

"There's a whole ticket about it."

"It's been following me for eleven days," said Cartethyia. "It sits in the stairwell when I train. It came into the refectory on Thursday, went round the entire room, and came back. It got under Ciaccona's strings and detuned the A, and I have been blamed for that."

She put two fingers out into the moving air, the way you offer a hand to something at a fence.

The air went over them and stayed a moment past its own momentum.

"You've been *talking* to it," Aemeath said.

"I've been being polite to it. There's a difference." Cartethyia's hand stayed where it was. "It waits. That's the thing. It comes in and it waits at the edge of the room to be noticed before it does anything. Nobody has ever built a duct that does that."

Aemeath looked at the moving air for a long moment. A girl who spent ten years unable to touch a candy dish across an uncrossable ledger, and who counted it faithfully every week from the wrong side anyway, has a particular expertise in the etiquette of the unnoticed.

"What do you call it," she said.

Because she knew that Cartethyia would have. Because names are ropes; because you can take everything off the sword-girl except the raising, and everything off Cartethyia except the naming; and because a woman who had christened a hundred and six scraps of cloth and a kettle that did not exist was never in this world going to let a thing follow her round a building for eleven days without giving it something to be called.

"Manners," said Cartethyia.

The air on the gallery stopped.

It stopped completely, for about the length of one held note, in a sealed building with mechanical ventilation and a documented airflow of point-four metres per second. Both women's hair settled. Out past four centimetres of thermal glass the snow went on falling at a little under a metre a second, forty minutes late, committed, coming down.

"Why Manners?" said Aemeath.

"Because it's got them."

The air came back, small and apologetic, and turned the page again, and Cartethyia laughed and said "*yes,* all right, that's mine, give it here," and reached for the folder, and missed the other woman's face entirely.

Aemeath had spent ten years cataloguing voices with nobody left to say them to. She knew, from a very long way off, the difference between a room going quiet and a room being *listened to.*

"Do you name everything?" she said.

"Only things I intend to see again."

Cartethyia got her folder. She got her coat. She got the last lemon cake out of the paper, which had gone soft, and ate it anyway, and complained about it. Then she stopped in the middle of the gallery with the wrapper in her fist and her head up, the way she had stopped at six years old in a cottage full of prop swords.

"Oh," she said. "A bird."

"What?"

"The stick. The handle." She was frowning at nothing at all, delighted, faintly puzzled, turning her wrist as though the shape were already in it. "A little one. One of the small ones, with the short beak — they come off the wheat in Egla in about four hundred at once and the whole field goes up like somebody dropped it." She flexed her fingers. "It came into my head about an hour ago and it won't leave."

The air moved along the gallery, east to west, against the ducts, and went out under the fire door.

"Huh," said Aemeath.

They went down to the shuttle bay at nineteen hundred: two women with grey folders and red wax on their hands, past the memorial garden where a statue stood in a cape the colour of alone, out through the east doors — where a wind that Lahai-Roi's engineers had never built came with them as far as the threshold, and waited there, at the edge of the room, to be noticed.

Upstairs, on the frame of the third window from the end, two crooked red lines stayed where they had been put.

The cleaning rota for the north face runs on Thursdays. The marks were still there in the spring.

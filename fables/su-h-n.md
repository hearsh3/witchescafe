Suì Hán

Fan Qiu blew on her chapped knuckles before she lifted the wooden lid off the soy-milk vat.

A cloud of white bean-steam rolled up under the stone arch of the arcade, smelling of scorched crust and river water, and vanished into the dry cold before it reached the keystone. Down by her ankles, the charcoal in the clay brazier had only just caught; the frost was still grey on the plaza flagstones three inches from her wheels, and the cold was coming straight up through the felt soles of her winter shoes.

“Bao!” she called over her shoulder toward the dark mouth of the stair behind the oil-shop. “Did you fall down the coal-hole, or are you waiting for the stove to walk over and fetch the basket itself?”

“Coming, Granny!” her grandson piped from the gloom, followed by the metallic clatter of the iron shovel hitting a brick.

Fan greased the flat iron griddle with a twist of pork fat on a chopstick and laid out six rounds of dough, pressing each one flat with the heel of her palm until the sesame seeds bit into her skin. On the front corner of the cart, wedged between the bamboo chopstick cylinder and the vinegar cruet, sat an old cracked pickle-jar full of well-water holding a single branch of winter plum. Her daughter A-Lan had pruned it off the south wall of her yard yesterday afternoon and brought it up the steps wrapped in a damp rag; the bark was black as iron wire, set thick with tight, waxy red buds the size of millet grains.

Across Huizhen Plaza, twenty paces out from the shadow of the pillars, Old Chen and the small red-haired woman in the faded plum coat were already setting the cabbage-seller’s trestle down beside the stone well-curb.

A moment later the Sentinel came down the upper steps in her tall human shape, nine heavy gold tails trailing clear of the frost behind her heels, the ninth one dark at the tip like a singed brush. She sat down on the narrow bench without looking round the square, laid the thick red-ruled book open on the planks, and blew on her fingers while the first four people of the morning queue lined up by the bucket-chain in their padded coats. Behind her, the small woman climbed onto her upturned butter tub, planted her long oil-paper umbrella upright between her knees, and let her iron-shod boots hang clear of the cobbles.

Fan turned the first sesame cake with her iron spatula. In the first week the book was out, she had stood by that well herself for half an hour in the wind and given the fox her dead husband’s name—Fan Dalin, who had pared his thick yellow toenails on the threshold with his cobbler’s knife every Sunday morning for thirty years until she hid the whetstone in the flour-bin. She still hadn’t made up her mind what she thought about a mountain fox wearing the old god’s coat for two hundred winters and bolting the doors when the trouble came, though twice this week, when a skinny four-legged thing with one black-tipped tail had come nosing under her cart-axle before the dawn bell, she had dropped half a burnt sesame ring into the gutter and looked the other way until it was gone.

A pair of dark woollen skirts brushed the kerbstone in front of the cart.

It was a girl of seventeen or eighteen in a heavy, close-buttoned coat of dark wool, her hands tucked neatly into her cuffs. Her cheeks were smooth and pink with the gorge air, though when she looked up from the griddle her eyes made Fan pause with the spatula half-lifted—still, unhurried, and much too patient for a girl’s face, like an old clerk who had watched three generations of magistrates come and go.

“Morning, Auntie,” the girl said pleasantly. “A bowl of hot soy milk, please, and two of the sesame cakes.”

“Sweet or salted?” Fan asked, reaching for a coarse grey bowl.

“Unsweetened is fine.”

Fan ladled the boiling milk from the deep pot, skimmed the yellow skin off the side with a bamboo sliver, and tongs-lifted two browned cakes onto a saucer. As the girl reached out to take the tray, a faint drift of scent came from inside her dark wool cuff—white blossom and dried orange peel, sharp and summer-sweet for a single breath before the hot bean-steam covered it over.

The girl laid a single coin down on the scrubbed pine counter.

Fan glanced at it as she wiped the ladle-drip. It was heavy bronze, green at the rim, with a square hole punched through the middle and four worn seal-characters nobody had struck since her grandfather was in split trousers.

“Hey, miss,” Fan said, reaching into her apron pouch for her string of small cash. “This is worth five of mine, wait for your—”

The girl was already walking toward the long pine bench under the arcade wall, carrying her bowl and saucer steady in both hands. She sat down at the far end facing the well and blew gently across the rim. Fan clicked her tongue, counted out two small brass coppers from her pouch, and stacked them on the corner of the counter where the girl could see them when she brought the dishes back.

Across the frost-whitened cobbles of the plaza, a woman was walking over from the south cut.

She came at an even, unhurried pace that neither dodged the mule-ruts nor dragged her heels. Fan couldn’t have guessed her age within twenty years; her hair was bound back plain from a weather-quiet face, and she wore stout grey-brown travelling cloth with road-dust dried in the hems. Slung from her leather belt on the left hip, where a carter would carry a knife or a water-horn, hung a heavy pair of iron gardening shears in a sheath of dark, rub-worn hide.

Instead of coming straight to the cauldron, the woman stopped at the near end of the pine bench.

“Is this end of the plank taken?” she asked the girl in the dark coat.

The girl looked up from her bowl. “The plank is six feet long,” she said lightly. “Sit down before the wind gets under your coat.”

The woman inclined her head, stepped back to Fan’s counter first, and looked at the steaming pot. “Good morning. May I have the same as the young lady—hot soy milk, plain, and two sesame cakes?”

“Three coppers,” Fan said, filling a second bowl and sliding the last two crisp cakes off the iron.

The woman drew a small leather purse from her sash, laid a five-copper piece in Fan’s wet palm, and stood quietly by the corner of the cart with her hands folded over her belt until Fan fished two small coppers out of her apron and dropped them into her fingers.

“Thank you,” the woman said, tucking the change back into her purse and tying the drawstring before she carried her tray to the near end of the bench.

Fan ladled another spoonful of oil onto the griddle and began rolling out fresh dough-balls with her palm. Behind her, three paces away under the low stone vault, the two women sat with four feet of bare pine between them, both looking out toward the well.

“It’s been a long while,” the woman with the shears said.

“Since the Priestess,” the girl in the dark coat said.

“Since the Priestess.” The woman lifted her spoon, set it in the bowl, and let a breath pass. “I wrote it down.”

“I never asked what you wrote.”

“No,” the woman said. “You never did.”

Fan slapped a round of dough onto the hot iron, where it sizzled up white around the edges.

“What are they calling you here?” the woman asked from the bench.

“Nivora,” the girl said, and took a small bite of her sesame cake.

“Nivora,” the woman repeated once, slowly and carefully, the way a travelling peddler repeats the name of a crossroad village so he won’t miss the turn on the way back.

Fan glanced over her shoulder as she reached for the sesame bowl. The woman had set her saucer on the bench beside her thigh and taken a small, stiff-covered book and a stub of lead pencil from the breast of her coat. She opened the pages on her knee and wrote the name down in a neat, upright hand while the girl in the dark coat watched the pencil move.

Out at the mouth of the harness-maker’s alley, Wu the one-eyed muleteer came leading two pack-donkeys past the pillar with his hat pulled down over his ear, walking fast on the far side of the gutter. He still owed Fan four coppers from Tuesday’s breakfast.

Fan banged her iron spatula down on the edge of the griddle, wiped her greasy palms on her apron front, and marched out from under the arch. “Wu Laoliu! Don’t you squint at the wall like you can’t see my pot! Four coppers, or I’m taking the nose-bag off that grey donkey!”

***

By the time Fan came back clicking Wu’s warm coppers in her fist, Little Bao had finally dragged the willow charcoal-basket out from the stair-foot, caught his toe on the arcade flagstone, and pitched half the black lumps across the swept stone.

“Clumsy monkey!” Fan caught him lightly by the quilted ear of his cap and pointed at the mess before the morning boots could grind it into soot. “Pick every piece of that up with your fingers! The small dust goes in the ash-pan, the big lumps in the box, and if you get black on the dough-board you’re eating cold turnip-rind for supper!”

Under the next bay of the arcade, ten paces down from the soy-milk cart, Old Deng and Master Hou had taken their places at the round granite table where an old xiangqi board was chiselled into the stone. Every morning since the road reopened they had sat there in their thick sheepskin coats, slamming the scarred wooden disks down hard enough to rattle Fan’s vinegar bottles.

“Watch the cannon, watch the cannon!” Master Hou crowed, slapping a black horse across the carved river-line with his mittened thumb.

Before Old Deng could move his hand from his chin, the lattice shutter on the second-floor gallery above the oil-shop banged open and Deng’s daughter-in-law leaned out over the rail with a soot-smudged rag in her fist. “Ba! Come up here a minute! The iron elbow on the stove-pipe slipped out of the mortar again and the whole back room’s full of smoke!”

Old Deng let out a long, wheezing curse, laid both palms flat over his side of the board for a second, and pointed a crooked finger at Master Hou’s nose. “Don’t you breathe on my left chariot while I’m gone. I know where every piece sits.”

He shuffled indoors through the oil-shop passage. Master Hou sat staring at the granite board for ten seconds, muttered something under his breath about the cold getting into his kidneys, and stumped off round the corner toward the ditch behind the cooper’s shed.

On the bench behind Fan’s cart, the girl in the dark woollen coat stood up with her soy-milk bowl cupped in her hands.

She strolled over to the empty stone table, stood looking down at the carved squares with her head tipped slightly to one side, and then reached out with two fingers. She picked up Old Deng’s red cannon from the fourth file and set it down three intersections over, tucked behind the river-bank beside his central pawn. Then she turned round, walked unhurriedly back to the pine bench, and sat down at her end.

The woman with the shears had not taken her eyes off her.

“That wasn’t yours,” she said.

“It was a dull position,” the girl said, blowing across her bowl.

“He had spent six moves clearing the file for that cannon.”

“He has spent twenty winters clearing the same file,” the girl said mildly. “Master Hou knows where the chariot goes before Old Deng finishes his tea.”

Fan shook her head over her griddle, turning the six sesame cakes with a clack of her iron blade. Young people nowadays couldn’t keep their fingers off anything left unguarded for two minutes.

Behind her on the bench, the woman with the shears took a sip of her soy milk, set the bowl on the wood between her knees, and spoke in the same level, unhurried voice she had used to order her breakfast.

“I came to look at the Arbiter,” she said.

“Have you,” the girl said. “And?”

“And I have stood at the bottom of the west lane and smelled her stove.” The woman turned the bowl a little between her palms. “I haven’t decided anything.”

“That must sit badly with you.”

“It does,” the woman said.

The girl in the dark coat bit the corner off her second sesame cake. “She seems quite attached to all of it. The office and the stove both.”

“A judge with a kitchen of her own has a divided hand,” the woman said. “One stove cannot warm a continent.”

“It warmed the third storehouse all week,” the girl said.

“Because a miracle held the beam,” the woman said, without raising her voice a fraction. “Miracles are not a road people can walk on. Every time her family comes through the fire by an exception, a village ten leagues away where she wasn’t looking burns alone in the dark because the office was empty. When I keep the book, I write those names down. She never hears them.”

“And if she sits alone in the high room with her hands clean,” the girl said, brushing a sesame seed off her dark wool knee, “you have your straight tree in an empty yard. You have never let a bough grow three inches past your chalk-line, Old Pine, without pulling the blades from your belt.”

“When a bough grows heavy on one side, the first hard winter splits the trunk to the root,” the woman answered. “I cut the bough so the tree lasts.”

“She tried your way once,” the girl said softly. “Left a child on a doorstep and kept the office whole. The child grew up and did the same, alone, at nineteen. Yet here you are on a bench in the cold, with your book in your pocket.”

“Because the tree lasted,” the woman said. “An empty yard can be planted again in the spring. A split trunk is firewood.”

“Auntie Fan!”

Big Sister Luo from the weavers’ lane was standing in front of the cart with her ten-month-old tied asleep against her shoulder in a patched quilt and a chipped earthenware jug in her red hands. Her eyes darted once toward the slate hanging on the post of Fan’s awning.

“Put two ladles of sweet milk on the board for me till Saturday?” Luo asked low, so the old men wouldn’t hear if they came back. “The cloth-factor from Huating didn’t come up on yesterday’s cart, and the little one’s coughing with the dry wind.”

Fan scowled fiercely at the jug. “You think the bean-miller lets me pay him with chalk-marks on a slate? Beans went up two cash a dou when the frost hit!”

She snatched the chipped jug out of Luo’s hands, plunged the wooden ladle deep into the sweet pot so it brought up the thick cream at the bottom, and poured three full measures in until the foam touched the lip. Before Luo could stammer her thanks, Fan broke a hot, misshapen corner off a sesame ring that had stuck to the edge of the griddle and shoved it into the top of Luo’s apron pocket. “Saturday before noon! If you make me climb the weavers’ steps for my six coppers, I’ll charge you shoe-leather!”

Luo bobbed her head twice and hurried off down the lane with the steaming jug hugged to her coat.

Out in the bright gap of the plaza, someone came whistling up from the cooper’s steps.

It was the dark-haired young woman from the salt-house—the Sentinel’s foreign guest with the strange, bright golden eyes who had stopped by Fan’s cart four days ago, bought Bao a sesame cake, and argued for three minutes that she ought to pay an extra copper because the burnt blister on the crust looked like a pond-turtle. She had a heavy canvas sack of dried broad beans slung over her left shoulder and a string of orange frost-persimmons swinging from her right hand.

As she came up the three granite risers by the well-curb, she slowed her boots and hitched the bean-sack an inch higher, her right wrist brushing the side of her jacket where a person holds a stitch after running. At the top step she stopped to grin at Old Chen over his baskets of frost-nipped cabbages, said something that made the old man slap his thigh, and then spotted Fan under the arcade arch. She lifted the string of persimmons high over her head and gave a wide wave.

Fan waved her greasy iron spatula back, calling out across the ruts, “Keep your scarf pulled up, miss! That gorge wind’ll take your ears off!”

The young woman laughed, patted her wool scarf with her chin, and went on up the west incline toward the salt-house without once looking toward the pine bench under the shadow of the wall.

Neither of the women on the bench moved until the corner of the dye-works hid her boots. The woman with the shears watched her the whole way up the slope; the girl in the dark coat watched the woman’s face.

“She is smaller than I expected,” the woman with the shears said.

“She is taller when you stand in front of her,” the girl said.

The woman turned her head slightly to look straight out at the well-curb, where the Sentinel was dipping her bamboo brush into the ink-stone for a bent old quarryman in a sheepskin vest, her nine gold tails lying still on the cold stone while the small red-haired woman sat behind her on the butter tub with her hands stacked on the handle of her umbrella.

“In none of the earlier ones did the Sentinel come down to the well,” the woman said.

“Mm,” the girl said.

“This is the fifteenth.”

“You’ve been counting.”

“I write them down,” the woman said. “You fold yours into your pocket until the paper wears through at the corners.” She kept her eyes on the cabbage-seller’s trestle as the Sentinel’s brush moved down the red column. “It is a good book. The Sentinel writes the names in her own hand.”

Across the twenty paces of cold cobbles, above the creak of a water-barrow and the stamp of the miller’s mule, the Sentinel’s left ear pivoted sharply round toward the arcade. It held still for the space of one breath, pointed straight at the shadow under the arch, and then turned back to the old quarryman’s wheezing chest. Behind her on the upturned tub, the small woman in the plum coat did not stir.

“Hey, Auntie, give us two cakes in a leaf, quick!” a porter with a carrying-pole across his padded shoulders shouted from the kerb, tossing three coppers onto the wood. “Foreman’s already at the south gate!”

Fan snatched a dried lotus leaf from the string, folded two piping-hot sesame cakes inside it, and tied it with a rush-stem before the coins stopped spinning.

When the porter trotted off, the woman with the shears laid both hands flat on her knees.

“That house runs on promises,” she said to the girl at the other end of the bench. “I would like to know whether they make them as vows or as prayers.”

“Is there a difference, over supper?”

“A vow binds the one who makes it. A prayer asks someone else to do the binding.”

“A prayer can be answered by somebody you never asked,” the girl said. “Vows never surprise anyone.”

“That is what they are for.” The woman turned her head down the plank. “I keep mine. What do you keep?”

The girl in the dark coat looked past her toward the cart and raised one finger. “Auntie, could I have one more sesame cake? That dark brown one near the front.”

Fan lifted the crisp ring with her iron tongs, dropped it onto a small square of coarse straw-paper, and handed it over the back of the cart. The girl took it with a pleasant nod and bit into the crust without answering a word to the woman beside her. The woman with the shears watched her chew for a moment, then drew the small book from her coat again and wrote a single line across the page with her lead pencil.

Out from the oil-shop doorway came Old Deng, wiping black stove-soot off his thumbs onto his trousers, just as Master Hou came stumping back round the cooper’s corner hitching up his waistband.

Old Deng sat down on his stone stool, leaned over the granite xiangqi board, and froze with his mittens two inches above the wood.

“Hey!” Old Deng barked, his white eyebrows coming down like thatch. “What’s my red cannon doing behind the river?”

“Don’t shout at me!” Master Hou snapped, dropping onto his own stool. “I went to the ditch! You shifted it yourself before your girl hollered out the window!”

“I had it on the fourth file!”

From the pine bench, the woman with the shears spoke up clearly and politely. “The young lady in the dark coat moved it while you were both away, Master Deng. It was standing on the fourth file beside your left advisor.”

Both old men turned their heads to stare at the bench, then looked back down at the carved squares of the granite table. Old Deng reached out his thick thumb to lift the wooden disk back to its file—and stopped halfway.

His rheumy eyes moved from the red cannon down the centre line to Master Hou’s black horse, which was now pinned dead against the palace corner with no screen in front of it. Under his grey moustache, Old Deng’s jaw worked once to the left and once to the right.

Slowly he pulled his mittened hand back into his sleeve.

“Now I look at it,” Old Deng said loudly, “I pushed it there before the girl yelled. My eyes watered with the wind. Touch-move is the rule on this stone! Your turn, Old Hou!”

“Your eyes watered my foot!” Master Hou roared, slamming both palms on the rim of the table. “You couldn’t see that square with a lantern! Put it back on the fourth file!”

“Play your elephant and stop bawling!”

The woman with the shears watched the two old men lean forehead to forehead over the stone board, inclined her head once as if entering the agreement, and folded her hands in her lap. At the far end of the bench, the girl in the dark coat ate the last crescent of her sesame cake, not hiding her smile at all.

***

The sun cleared the eastern cliff-wall a quarter of an hour before the second bell, pouring hard, pale winter light across the upper half of the plaza and turning the frost on the well-roof to wet silver.

Fan stamped her numb heels twice against the floorboards behind her axle and moved the cracked pickle-jar to the front edge of the pine counter so the winter plum branch would catch the sun. Across the square, the queue at the cabbage-seller’s trestle had thinned down to one widow in a grey headscarf.

The woman with the shears stood up from the bench, brushing a stray sesame hull off her travelling skirt, and carried her empty bowl and saucer back to the cart.

As she set the crockery down on the plank, her gaze fell on the black plum branch in the pickle-jar.

“That is a good cutting,” she said.

“My daughter A-Lan brought it up from the lower terrace yesterday,” Fan said, plunging the empty bowl into her wash-bucket. “Says it’ll open before the new moon if the kitchen draught doesn’t dry it out.”

The woman looked closely at the middle fork of the branch, where the dark wood divided into three shoots. “May I touch it?”

Fan wiped her wet red hands on her apron, half amused and half surprised that anyone would ask leave of a street-peddler to touch a stick in a jar. “Touching won’t break it.”

The woman did not lay a finger on the wood yet; instead she pointed with the knuckle of her forefinger at a thin inner shoot that grew sideways across the main stem, rubbing hard against the bark below a cluster of tight red buds.

“This cross-shoot has grown back across the leader and is choking the sap-vein,” she told Fan plainly. “If I cut it out for you, you will lose the two buds at the tip of it, and the branch will look bare on the left side. If it comes off now, the seven buds on the main stem will open three days sooner and keep their petals longer.”

Fan squinted at the fork in the morning sun. It went against her grain to throw away two good flower-buds after A-Lan had carried the branch all the way up the steps in a wet rag, and she was half put out at a stranger finding fault with her jar—yet the woman spoke so simply, like a carpenter pointing out a split wedge, that there was nothing to take offence at.

“Well, if the seven will open better, take it off then,” Fan said. “No sense letting one crooked twig spoil the bunch.”

The woman unfastened the worn leather strap at her hip, drew out the heavy iron gardening shears, and slipped the curved blade into the fork flush against the collar of the shoot.

*Snip.*

The iron closed once with a clean, oiled click. Not a shred of bark tore on the main stem. She wiped the flat of the blade on her thumb, slid the shears back into their leather sheath, and laid the four-inch severed twig with its two red buds neatly on the scrubbed pine counter beside the jar.

“What is your name, Auntie?” she asked.

“Fan Qiu,” Fan said, looking at the neat white circle on the black wood where the shoot had been. And because the woman was waiting with that polite, steady face: “What’s yours?”

“Yalda,” the woman said.

She took the small stiff-covered book and the stub of pencil from her coat, laid the book open on the dry corner of the counter, and wrote a short line near the top of the page. Fan glanced down sideways as she reached for her dishcloth; upside down on the narrow paper, she could read the two characters of her own name, *Fan Qiu*, and a single word written beside them before the cover closed.

From the far end of the bench, the girl in the dark woollen coat stood up, stepped to the corner of the cart, and picked up the cut plum twig with its two tight buds between her thumb and forefinger. She turned it once in the thin sunlight, then slid it down into the deep side pocket of her dark coat.

Yalda tucked the small book back into her breast and looked at the girl.

“And when she outgrows you?” Yalda asked plainly. “What will you do then?”

The girl in the dark coat did not answer.

Her hand stayed resting on the outside of her wool pocket where the cut twig had gone, and her quiet, patient eyes turned past the stone pillar of the arcade toward the middle of the plaza.

Out by the well-curb, the last widow in the grey headscarf bowed to the bench and walked away toward the salt-seller’s alley. The Sentinel sat still for a moment in the bright sun, her nine gold tails pooled behind the trestle; then she laid her bamboo brush across the porcelain rest, blew once on the damp ink, and pulled the stiff cover of the red-ruled ledger shut. Behind her, the small woman in the faded plum coat stepped down off the upturned butter tub, took the flat grey river stone in her left hand, and set it squarely on top of the book to weight it against the wind.

Under the arch, Fan scraped the burnt sesame hulls off the iron lip of her griddle with her spatula—*scritch, scritch, scritch*—and swept the black crumbs into the ash-pan. At the granite table ten paces away, Old Deng’s wooden chariot clicked once against Master Hou’s horse.

The girl in the dark coat looked back from the well and picked up her empty saucer.

“Has the Old Pine ever been outgrown?” she asked lightly.

Yalda looked once at the pickle-jar sitting in the sun on the corner of the cart.

“Every spring,” Yalda said.

She turned to the counter and inclined her head to Fan. “Thank you for the meal, Fan Qiu. The soy milk was hot.”

“Come by tomorrow if you’re up before the carts,” Fan said by habit.

Yalda stepped out from under the shadow of the arcade and walked unhurriedly across the sunlit cobbles toward the eastern terrace steps, away from the west slope where the salt-house roof was smoking.

The girl in the dark coat stayed on the bench a little longer, lifting her grey bowl to drink the last cool swallow from the bottom. Then she set the bowl down on the counter beside her saucer, turned without a word, and walked off down the south arcade toward the rope-makers’ lane.

Fan came round the corner of the cart with her wet rag to wipe down the plank.

At the near end where the woman with the shears had sat, the pine was bare and clean. On the corner of the counter, the two small brass coppers of change Fan had stacked for the girl’s ancient square-holed coin still sat untouched in the dry flour dust, and when Fan lifted the girl’s empty bowl, the square of coarse straw-paper from her third sesame cake lay underneath the foot-ring, folded into a neat, flat triangle the size of a thumbnail.

Little Bao poked his soot-smudged forehead up from behind the charcoal box, chewing on a cold strip of dough-crust.

“Granny,” he mumbled, squinting down the empty arcade. “Who were those two?”

“How should I know?” Fan said, sweeping the two coppers into her apron pocket and shoving the empty wooden water-bucket against his shins. “Do I look like the gate-clerk? Go draw a fresh bucket from the well before the Sentinel’s people pack the trestle up, and don’t drop the rope in the mud.”

She set the rinsed bowls upside down on the rack to drain and turned back to the griddle, where the winter plum branch stood in its jar at the sunny corner of the cart, the white cut gleaming raw and clean between the remaining buds.

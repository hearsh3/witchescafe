# Become a Voice

*a chaconne, for Cartethyia*

> *O lyre, divine shell — speak.*
> *Find yourself a voice.*
> — Sappho, fr. 118

---

Lahai-Roi dimmed its sun by committee. At the twentieth hour Helios cooled from gold to bronze, from bronze to a low red coal, and the false stars came on across the cavern ceiling in their programmed constellations, each one named by a subcommittee, each one wrong. Ciaccona had been awake under real stars often enough to find the errors restful. A sky that admitted to being built was ahead of most institutions she had known.

She sat cross-legged on her dormitory bed with the lute across her knees, tuning down. The instrument had been her father's, and it had been broken by a playwright and mended, broken by a god and mended, and this afternoon a puppeteer in a schoolgirl's shape had staged its breaking a third time inside her own skull, and she had spent the evening on a dance pad sweating the image out through her feet. Now she loosened each string a quarter turn, the way you loosen a horse's girth at the end of a day. The lute exhaled.

Two of her Sylphs drowsed in the curtain rod. The third was investigating the window latch with the diligence of a small burglar.

The knock came as a formality delivered at the same instant as the door opening.

"You're supposed to wait for the answer," Ciaccona said.

"You'd have said come in." Cartethyia came in. She had a thermos under one arm, a paper sack of arcade-prize candy in her fist, and a plush gryphon the size of a bread loaf wedged in her belt like a second sword. Her hair still held the shape of the headband from the dance machine. "Iuno's sitting with Mei. Lupa took Chisa to the roof. Lynae's teaching Sigrika to cheat at cards, which I believe is her love language." She put the thermos on the desk with the decisiveness of a flag being planted. "So it's you and me."

"So it is." Ciaccona set the lute aside. "Sit down before you knock something over."

Cartethyia sat down and knocked over the tuning fork. She picked it up, struck it against her knee out of curiosity, and held it to her ear while it sang. The Sylph at the window abandoned the latch, drifted over, and settled on her shoulder to listen with her.

They divided the candy. This was the ritual the pack had arrived at by instinct, the way packs arrive at everything: pair off, sit down, and say what the rooms had shown. It went in both directions; that was the whole law of it.

"You first," Ciaccona said. "You'll do it fast and honest and then I'll owe you."

Cartethyia unwrapped a candy, considered it, and ate it, all with the frown of someone reading terms and conditions.

"It showed me the tower," she said. "The seminary. First year, before the dust settled anywhere, when everything still smelled like other people. It got the light right. It got the sound right — you know a sealed building has a sound, a sort of held breath. It even had Ser Pincushion, half-sewn, the way he was before I found the good thread." She turned the wrapper over in her fingers. "And then it — she — skimmed it. Twenty years. She flipped through it like a ledger she'd already audited. She said—" Cartethyia's voice did the thing it did, went bright and steady, which was her voice lying about the weather. "She said, *boring. Nothing happens. Next.* And the room agreed with her. Twenty years, and the room agreed."

"And you?"

"That's the part I owe you." She looked up. Her eyes were very clear. "For a second, so did I. For one second I looked at that girl sewing in the dust and thought, *she's right, nothing is happening to you, you are a held breath, you are a rock in a field.* Twenty years of my life dismissed by a monster, and my first instinct was to apologize for the pacing." She ate another candy, ferociously. "Then I broke her furniture. But the second came first. That's my truth. Your turn."

Ciaccona was quiet for a moment. Outside, the built stars ticked through their rotation, and somewhere down the corridor a shower ran, and the city that lived inside a kneeling god hummed its enormous domestic hum.

"Mine showed me the lute smashed," she said. "Which you know, because you heard me say so in the debrief, in my funny voice, and everyone laughed, and we moved on. That was the plan. Here's the rest of it. When the lute broke — in the room, in her staging — I had one clear thought before the fear arrived. The thought was: *there it is. The last note. The song's over.* And the fear that came after was almost a relief, because the thought itself had sounded so much like the truth." She picked a thread off her cuff. "Tomorrow, at the festival, if anyone puts a fortune-teller in front of me, I'll probably say it out loud in front of everyone, in my funny voice, and everyone will laugh, and we'll move on. The thing I'm afraid of is the end of the song. That's the whole confession. It's very short."

"It's not," said Cartethyia. "It's the title."

Ciaccona looked at her.

"You do this," Cartethyia said. "You hand people the title and let them think they've read the book. Mei catches, Lupa carries, I break, Iuno counts, and you — you make everyone else's grief rhyme, and then you go to bed. The rule tonight is the truth. The title is a down payment." She pulled the thermos over, cracked it, poured tea into the single cup and the cup's lid, and pushed the cup — the good one — across the desk. "So. Why the end of the song? What happened to you out there, before us? You know all my years. Sing me yours."

The Sylph on her shoulder turned to look at Ciaccona too, which was mutiny.

Ciaccona took the cup. The tea was Iuno's blend; the whole pack stole it now; the thief economy of the group had grown very sophisticated. She drank, and set it down, and reached for the lute — and her own hand's honesty amused her, that even now the story would come out of her sideways, through wood and gut, or it would come out costumed.

She laid the lute flat across her knees, unplayed, a promise of accompaniment. When she spoke, her voice had changed registers. Cartethyia had heard the tavern voice and the square voice and the voice she used to talk gods out of despair. This was the fourth voice, the one from before all of those, and it was quieter, and it was the same.

"All right," she said. "Sit still, eat your candy, and I'll tell you about the road. It's long, and I'm in it, so it's biased. If you fall asleep I'll change the details out of spite."

"I never fall asleep during stories."

"I know," said Ciaccona. "That's why you get this one."

*] a torch, they said, a torch —*
*] rain on the road, and the road long [*

---

"Begin with the name, because we always did. You know what a toccata is? A touch-piece. A player sits down at a strange instrument and touches it all over, fast, to learn what it can do — that's a toccata, a first meeting written in music. My family took it for a surname eleven generations back, and then, because we were insufferable, we started naming the children after forms as well. My grandmother's doing. She said a name should be an argument. My uncle was Ricercare — the seeking, the learned fugue that hunts a theme through every voice. My father was Passacaglia. And I am Ciaccona, and here is the family joke, which is also the family curse: a passacaglia and a chaconne are almost the same dance. Both are variations over a ground — a short line in the bass that repeats and repeats while everything above it changes. The learned quarters of Ragunna spent two hundred years fighting about the difference. My grandmother named my father and me one generation apart, one form each, to keep the fight going. She said the day the guilds settled it, the family had permission to name a child something sensible, like Rosa.

"So that's the house I came from. A house where the bass line was always running under dinner. My father played rehearsals in the mornings and weddings in the afternoons and truth after dark, and the truth was the Poets Society — you know that part, you stood inside my sheet music once and lectured a memory of me, which remains the strangest kindness anyone has ever done me and I include the resurrections. He took the key. He hid the study. They came for my mother first, over a pamphlet she had merely folded, and she kissed my head and told me the pantry inventory as if it were a psalm, and went. Then Ricercare, who had done nothing at all except be a Toccata, which by then was the crime. Then, in the spring I turned sixteen, my father. He put the key in my hand on the doorstep with the acolytes standing right there, in full view, because a thing done openly looks innocent, and he smiled at me and said the sentence you already know. *Don't hide the truth in your heart. Raise it like a torch, so it lights the way for others.* Then he complimented the youngest acolyte's boots, sincerely, and walked out of my life making small talk.

"I want you to notice something about that creed, Cartethyia, because I was sixteen and I noticed nothing. It's a beautiful sentence. It scans. It has a torch in it. And it is a sentence composed by a man being arrested — it's exit music, it's the last line before the curtain, and last lines are written for the audience. He gave me the truest thing he had in the most memorable shape he could build in four seconds, because he was a musician, and a musician's instinct under the knife is to make the dying phrase carry. I have spent my whole life since finding out what the sentence actually means. That search is the story. The sentence is the ground bass. Everything I'm about to tell you is variations.

"They let me keep the house for three more years, which was its own lesson — the Order composts, and compost takes years. I taught lute to merchant daughters and said nothing and hid the key inside a tuning peg and was, on paper, reformed. And at night I did exactly what he told me. I took the poets' discoveries — the Steed, the double faith, the starving-ship — and I set them. Proper settings. Good voice-leading. I was nineteen and I wrote a song cycle that would have gotten a standing ovation in a world one degree kinder than this one, and I premiered it in a wine cellar for forty people, and inside a month I was on a list, and inside a season I was on a boat, and the boat was polite. That's the modern Order — my father got a Pilgrim's Sail and I got a ferry ticket and a document with a seal on it. Banishment had been rationalized. There was a form.

"So: the road. Nineteen years old, one lute, one key, one sentence."

"First variation. I did it literally.

"There's a harbor town on the windward coast called Varrena — you'd like it, everything in it is the color of a mussel shell and the bells are a quarter-tone flat and have been for a century, the whole town hums flat, it's charming, it ruins you for other bells. I arrived with the torch held high. I stood on the seawall and I sang the truth of the Poets Society at full voice to the fishing fleet coming in. Names, dates, the ship, the fire. Good projection. My father would have wept at the diction.

"Here is what happened. The fleet came in, and the fishermen looked at me the way you look at weather. A woman named Brigida, who kept the inn by the customs house, gave me the corner by the fire and a bowl of the white bean soup they do there with the fennel, and let me sing my cycle to her evening crowd, all of it, every night for a week. And on the eighth day the acolytes came — and this is the part I need you to hear exactly, because it took me years to hear it — they walked straight past me."

Cartethyia had gone still, the way she went still on watch.

"They chalked a fool's cap on Brigida's door. That's all. Chalk. A little drawing, like a child's. And the fish-buyers stopped coming to her taproom, because being seen there had acquired a price, and her daughter's betrothal went quiet, and the customs clerk found a discrepancy in her cellar records that had been fine for twenty years. The chalk did the whole day's work of a garrison. And Brigida came to me on the ninth morning with bread and cheese wrapped in a cloth — good cloth, she meant me to keep it — and she said, and I'll give it to you in her voice, which was kind the whole way through: *You sing beautifully, girl, and every word is true, and I believe you, and you have to go sing it somewhere I'm not.*

"I stood on the road out of Varrena with warm bread in my hands and I did the arithmetic. My torch was lit. The way was illuminated. And every soul who stepped into that light became a target the archers could see. The Order had solved the problem of martyrs generations ago — a martyr is expensive, a martyr sings from the grave. So they had built something better: a world where the truth cost most to whoever stood nearest the person telling it. They let the torch burn. They taxed the light.

"And there was one more refinement, and it was the cruelest, and I met it in the next town, and the next. When I sang the truth to strangers — the real, documented, provable truth — do you know what they heard? They heard madness. A young woman alone on the road, shouting that the god is a horse and the saints were murdered and the curse is a forgery — the Order had spent a generation making that exact speech the recognized sound of a cracked mind. They had composed my genre in advance. A young acolyte in Fossagrigia listened to my whole set with real tears in his eyes and then pressed a coin into my hand and told me, gently, that the Order ran a charitable house for women afflicted as I was. He *pitied* me. He went home feeling holy. I stood there holding his coin and understood that I could sing the truth from every seawall in Rinascita and the net effect on the world would be a rise in charitable donations.

"You told me once — years later, at the café by the docks in Septimont, when Mei was drowning politely over her tea — that they called you crazy too. I laughed when you said it. You thought I was laughing at the joke. I was laughing at the fellowship."

*] two hands on the cradle,*
*one salt, one bright [*

---

"Second variation, and this is the one where the road started teaching instead of taking.

"Third winter out. Hill country inland, a town called Miravalle, which is a lie, the valley is visible from nowhere in it. I was broke in the way where you start seeing bread in geometry. My hands had cracked at the knuckles from the cold, and cracked knuckles on a lutenist — I could still play, but I played like a talented drunk. Fingering slow, ornaments gone, the odd note landing next door to itself. And I needed the coins, so I sat in the tavern by the grain exchange and played badly for farmers, and hated every bar of it, my father's daughter, butchering pavanes for soup.

"Halfway through the third evening I made a hash of a lullaby. Every region has its cradle song; the Order publishes the approved verse in the primer; I played the primer version, badly. And a woman by the hearth — seventy if a day, hands like tree roots, a shawl the color of onion skins — put down her mending and said, loudly, to the whole room: *Wrong.* And then she fixed it. The words. She sang, in a voice like a hinge, the verse her grandmother had rocked her with, and I will sing it to you now the way she gave it to me:

> *Sleep, little oar, the sea rocks your bed.*
> *The sky trims the lamp at your mother's head.*
> *Two hands on the cradle, one salt and one bright —*
> *the water is family. Goodnight. Goodnight.*

"Sit with that a moment. You know the primer verse — the Sentinel keeps the lamp, the Sentinel rocks the bed, one hand, one god, sleep tight. The Order edited the anthem, the liturgy, the histories, the glass. And here, in a hill tavern, in the mouth of a woman who had never read a heresy in her life and would have boxed my ears for suggesting she carried one — the old faith. Both divinities. The sea as kin. The doctrine three cantors died for, preserved wholesale in a rhyme for infants, because the Order audited everything worth confiscating and a lullaby is beneath confiscation. They burned the library, Cartethyia. They missed the nursery.

"And then — this is the important part — the room joined in. The carter knew a different second line. The tapster's mother had a verse about the moon that started a small war between two tables. For an hour that tavern argued cradle songs, corrected each other, corrected me, sang over each other, and I sat in the middle of it with my ruined hands, taking dictation in my head, rich beyond counting. And at the end the old woman — Fiora, her name was — patted my arm and said the sentence I have built a life on since: *You play like my granddaughter. Come back tomorrow and I'll teach you the rest.*

"You see it. Tell me you see it."

"You played badly," Cartethyia said slowly, "so she stopped listening to a performer and started teaching a granddaughter."

"Yes. And I spent that whole night awake, testing the shape of it, and in the next town I played badly on *purpose*, in cold blood, a trained musician fluffing her cadences like a card sharp dealing seconds — and it worked, it worked everywhere, it has never once failed me since. And here's the mechanism, because it took me years to say it cleanly. People guard the truth from anyone who might *own* it. Play like a master and you are an authority, and authorities are the people truth gets hidden from — they've had practice. But play like someone who *needs* — a beginner, a beggar, somebody's granddaughter — and they will hand you the family silver, because giving truth to the needy is what people are *for*, it's the oldest reflex in the species, older than fear, and the Order in all its genius never found a way to tax it. Fenrico could confiscate a manuscript. Show me the primus who can confiscate a room full of grandmothers correcting a bad musician.

"So that's the second thing the sentence meant. My father said *raise it like a torch*, and I had heard: *blaze*. But you've built fires, country girl. What does a torch actually do to the people around it?"

"Blinds them," said Cartethyia. "You hold a torch, you can't see past it, and no one near you can see anything but it."

"And a low fire?"

"People sit down at a low fire." She said it, and then heard it, and her jaw set the way it did when a sword lesson landed. "They bring their own wood."

"They bring their own wood," Ciaccona said. "Write the anthem rough and the city rewrites it, and then it's theirs, and you can't arrest a chorus. I learned it broke and frostbitten in a grain town, from a woman who thought she was teaching a clumsy girl her heritage. She was. Fiora's dead now, I checked, years back, in her bed, with the verse intact in six grandchildren. The Order never learned her name. There's the score: Fiora one, theocracy nil."

*] four strings of eight —*
*] play what is left, or stare [*

---

Ciaccona refilled the cup and the lid, and gave the good cup back to Cartethyia this time, and Cartethyia let her, which between them was a whole negotiated treaty.

"Third variation. This one's shorter and it costs more.

"I kept asking after my family in every port. You do it sideways — you play badly, remember, and then you steer the corrections. Exiles change their names; the smart ones change their instruments. Four years in, in a river town whose name I'll keep, I walked past a pawnbroker's window and stopped as if I'd been speared. There was a fiddle in it. Ricercare's fiddle. I'd have known it in the dark by the smell of the varnish — my uncle mixed his own, there was a resin he got from one particular supplier, the whole house reeked of it every spring. It sat in that window with four of its eight strings gone and a water stain along the lower bout, priced at less than the case had cost new.

"I bought it with three days of meals. The broker knew nothing — a man had pawned it seasons ago, quiet fellow, dock hands. So I did the only thing I had. I stood outside that shop every market day and I played it. Four strings of eight. You can play a tune on half a fiddle — a lousy tune, a limping thing, but a tune, and I played it loud, our family songs, the ones only a Toccata would recognize, hour after hour, so that if he was anywhere in that town he would hear his own strings calling him. Seven market days. And on the seventh a carter who bought fish from the docks heard me and said, *oh, the quiet fellow, he shipped out — coastal freight, works under some other name now, been gone since summer. Steady work. He seemed well.*

"Alive. Alive, and hidden, and *well*. And I stood in the street with his fiddle under my chin and the creed rose up in me on its hind legs: *find him. Light the torch. He's family, he's a Toccata, drag him back into the name.* And then the road-schooling answered it, cold as Fiora's winter: a man hiding from the Order stays alive by staying unlit. His new name was the roof over his head. If I found him — if I so much as sang the old songs down the coast asking — every note was a lamp over his bed for the archers.

"So I did the hardest thing my father's sentence never covered. I restrung nothing. I left the fiddle with the broker's daughter, who was ten and wanted to learn, along with the family recipe for the varnish written out fair, and I walked out of that town in the wrong direction on purpose, and I have never looked for my uncle since. He is out there somewhere hauling freight under a name I will die not knowing, and my gift to him — the only gift the situation permitted — was silence. Me. Silence.

"That's the amendment I'd nail to my father's door if he had a door. The truth is a torch, yes. And some people survive by darkness, and a singer who forgets that is just an informer with a nicer voice. Song is medicine, and medicine is dosage. I've watched the overdose, Cartethyia. I stood at the edge of a square in Ragunna and watched fifty thousand people *witness* you at once, all that love and attention poured onto one girl in one instant, and I watched it wake the thing sleeping in your blood. You nearly died of being seen. So when the saga-books tell you that witness saves — and it does, I've staked my life on it more than once, I'll stake it again — remember there's a bard in your pack who also keeps the other ledger. Who gets sung, how loud, and when. And who gets the mercy of the dark."

Cartethyia's gaze had dropped to the plush gryphon in her belt and gone straight through it. "The acolyte in Egla," she said. "The one who watched me come home. She kept saying *I didn't see anything.*"

"And walked away without reporting it. Yes."

"I thought she was a coward, a little. At the time." Cartethyia turned the gryphon's ear between two fingers. "She was dosing."

"She was the best musician in that square," Ciaccona said. "Present company included."

*] the sea keeps everything —*
*] which is the other forgetting [*

---

"Fourth variation. The ground bass, you'll remember, is a sentence spoken by a man being taken to the sea.

"Sixth year. I had put it off — you know how you can put off a whole direction of the compass. But a bard hears things, and what I'd heard, in fragments, over years, was that the Pilgrim's Sails all clear the land at one place: a headland south of the Ragunnan reach, the last finger of Rinascita, where the coast pilots hand the ships to the open water and turn back. And on that headland there is a fire tower, and in the fire tower there was an old man.

"Tammaro. He'd been an Order lamplighter forty years, tending the coast lights, and when they pensioned him off he just — stayed, in the last tower, unpaid, past the edge of the map of anyone's caring. And on his own coin and his own time, in a set of ledgers he sewed himself from sailcloth and packing paper, he logged every ship that rounded the headland. Every one. Date, weather, rig, heading. Forty years of it. I asked him why, and he gave me the entire theology of his life in three words: *Someone should.* That's it. That's the whole liturgy of Tammaro. Someone should.

"I told him the year and the season. He climbed to the shelf without a word — he knew what I was, the moment he saw the lute; girls with lutes come to that tower for one reason — and he brought down the right ledger and found the page and turned it to face me, because he was the kind of man who lets you read for yourself.

"One line, Cartethyia. In lamplighter's script, the letters formed the way they teach you to form them when the log might someday matter to a court. The date. *Pilgrim's Sail, out of Ragunna. Wind fresh from the west. One aboard.* And then, because Tammaro logged what he observed and he had observed it through his glass across a mile of water:

"*Singing.*

"That's my inheritance. That is the complete surviving record of my father's death. One aboard, singing. One word. The wind was from the west and carried the sound out to sea, so what Tammaro had was the posture — the chest, the open mouth, the man at the rail giving a performance to an audience of gulls. Singing. Somewhere past that page there's a night and a fire and the end of him, and the sea has all of it, and the page has one word.

"So I did the obvious thing, and it nearly broke me, and I want you to know about it because we have since met this failure wearing a crown, and I recognized it, and now you'll know why. My Forte rings the melodies out of what keeps them. The wind keeps them best — the wind is the world's honest recorder, I've told you that, everything sung outdoors is filed in the weather. And the sea — the sea keeps *everything*. Every drowned voice, every deck song, every mother calling from every shore since the first shore. So I stood on the rocks below Tammaro's tower at slack tide and I rang the water. I asked the sea for my father.

"And the sea answered. It gave me everything. All of it, at once, one chord — ten thousand griefs, forty generations, every ship, every storm, every name called across water since names began, undifferentiated, fortissimo, a single vertical block of total memory with my father in it *somewhere*, indistinguishable, one voice in a noise the size of the sky. I came to on the rocks with blood in my left ear and Tammaro hauling me up by the collar, and he fed me fish soup in the tower and said, in the tone of a man who has watched this exact scene before, *The water's a grave, girl. Graves keep everything and give back nothing. That's what makes them graves.*

"And there it is — the theorem the whole road had been walking me toward. There are two ways to lose the dead. The Order's way: edit, redact, confiscate, until the record says what power needs — memory as property. And the sea's way: keep everything, all of it, forever, equally, until keeping and forgetting are the same weather — memory as flood. The archive with an owner, and the archive with no singer. Both of them are oblivion. Both of them, Cartethyia — we have *met* both of them, you and I, we have stood in the cathedral at the bottom of the world while the flood version explained itself, ten thousand heartbeats strung from a ceiling, every grief kept and none of them *heard* — I knew that god the moment it spoke. I'd met it at slack tide when I was twenty-five. It's the sea's idea of remembering. Total, and loveless, and the same as the dark.

"Between the two of them there is exactly one road, and it is narrow, and it is us. A singer chooses. That's the job. Out of everything, *this* verse; out of everyone, *this* name; selection, with love — which is all a song is, when you take the varnish off. A third memory. The *sung* memory: partial, biased, warm, and alive, because someone with a heartbeat decided it mattered and spent breath on it. When the fortune-teller turns over my cards tomorrow, if the Hanged Man comes up — the witness, the one who dangles at an angle to the world and watches — I'll claim him happily. Somebody has to hang in the rigging and choose what gets carried."

*] what he sang, the gulls keep [*

---

"Last variation. What I built out of the page.

"I came down from the headland with one word and a debt, and I wrote my father's death. Understand what I had: a date, a wind, and *singing*. So the song was short and it had a hole in it, and I made the hole on purpose — center of the last verse, where the melody climbs and stops on the open fifth like a door left ajar:

> *He carried a key and they gave him the water.*
> *He stood at the rail and he sang out the leaving —*
> *— and here the verse waits —*
> *what he sang, the gulls keep.*

"And I took it into the taverns down the coast, and I played it *badly*, Cartethyia. My own father's death-song, dealt off the bottom of the deck, fumbled at the door of the hole every single night, and every single night some fisherman or ship's cook or harbor widow leaned in — the granddaughter reflex, truth handed to the needy — and *fixed it*. Filled the hole. Told me what a man like that would have sung. And they were all wrong, and they were all right, and the song started to grow.

"In Varrena — I went back, Brigida's daughter runs the inn now, the chalk mark outlived the marriage prospects of everyone who believed in it — a sailor stood up and gave the hole a verse where the man shares his last water with the gulls, because, and I quote a drunk oracle, *a singer knows his audience*. Down the coast a caulker's wife added a verse where he blesses the ship itself — *bless the wood, boys, the wood is innocent* — which is the most Ragunnan sentence ever composed, we absolve the timber and indict the shipwright, God, I love us. And in a town called Lastra, a woman with flour to her elbows gave him an argument — a whole verse where the man spends his last night out-debating the sea on a point of music theory, on, I swear this to you on the key I still carry, *the difference between a passacaglia and a chaconne*, and *wins*, and the sea concedes, and takes him under out of *spite*. She had no idea what she'd done. She'd never heard our name. She reached into the hole in a stranger's song and pulled out my grandmother's joke, whole and living, from the far side of a man's death.

"And then — a year on, maybe more — I walked into a tavern in a town I had never played, never, I'd have remembered, and a one-eyed tinker in the corner was singing it. *The Fool Who Sang to Gulls* — that's the name it wears on the road now; nobody consulted me. Three verses I had never written. The water-sharing verse had grown a second half. There was a new verse where the gulls follow the ship for three days out of respect. I stood in the doorway with my hood up and my heart going like a jig, and when he finished I paid him for it — you tip a man for your own father, it's the strangest coin I ever spent — and I asked him, casual, where the song was from. And he shrugged the shrug of the entire oral tradition and said:

"*Everyone knows that one.*

"Everyone knows that one." Ciaccona's hands had come to rest flat on the lute's soundboard, and she looked at them. "My father died alone on the water with a lamplighter's spyglass for a witness, and now he walks roads I will never take, in versions I will never hear, arguing with the sea and winning, sharing water with gulls, blessing innocent wood — and every mouth that sings him changes him, and every change is a lie, and every lie is *him*, because the one true thing in the record is that the man went to his death making music for whoever was there, and the song is now doing exactly that, forever, out of my hands. Confiscate it, Fenrico. Go on. There's no manuscript. There's no author. Arrest everyone.

"I cried about it once, behind the tavern, into a rain barrel, and then I stopped, because that's my ration — you've seen it, one cry per grief; call it economy; I have a lot of griefs and one face. And I walked north, and somewhere on that road the method finished becoming a doctrine. Leave every song rough. Build the hole in. Hand the pen over with the tune. Because a finished song has an owner, and an owner can be arrested, exiled, burned on a boat — but a song with a hole in it belongs to whoever mends it, which is everyone, which is the only congregation the Order can never excommunicate.

"After that, Carlotta found me — of course she did; the Montelli pay fortunes for intelligence, and their spymasters kept filing reports about a tavern musician who knew the tide of the city's heart three days before their informants did. She offered me a salary. I countered with a tab and the right to be rude to her at parties, and we've been dear friends since. And then a woman with no memory washed up on a beach with a title waiting for her, and Carlotta sent me to her, and you know the rest, because the rest is you — you were there in the melody-realm the first day, being invisible and superior about it.

"That's the road, country girl. That's what I was before the pack. A woman walking variations on a dead man's exit line: *the torch blinds — sit lower. The truth survives in the boring places — check the nurseries. Some people live by darkness — learn the dosage. The sea and the censor are the same god — sing between them. And leave the hole in the verse, always, always — the hole is where everyone else gets in.*"

The lute had made its way, at some point during the last of it, back into playing position. Both of them let it pass.

*] and the bard? who —*

---

Cartethyia was quiet for a long time. She had stopped eating the candy somewhere around the fiddle, and the little heap of wrappers sat between them like the shed skins of the hour. On the curtain rod, both Sylphs had woken to listen; the third had settled on the neck of the lute itself, proprietary.

"I have three things," Cartethyia said at last. "In order."

"Of course you do."

"First." She held up a finger. "Your rooms and mine showed us the same wound and we didn't notice. The puppeteer called my twenty years boring. And your whole story is a proof — a proof, with witnesses — that boring is where everything true gets kept. The lullaby survived because it was beneath notice. Tammaro's ledger mattered because it was tedious. Fiora's tavern, the pawnshop street, soup. A god skimmed my dust and my dolls and called them nothing, and your entire life's evidence says the skimming is how the important things get *past* gods." She let the finger fall. "So the next time some cosmic horror audits my tower and yawns, I'm going to take it as a professional endorsement."

"You should. Boring is the vault. I'd put it on your coat of arms if you had one."

"I'm working on one. There's a ewe on it." Second finger. "Second thing. The three notes."

Ciaccona's hand went still on the strings, briefly, a rest of exactly one beat, and Cartethyia — who had been right about a flat bridge from inside a cosmic horror, whose ear was a scandal she refused to formalize — heard the rest.

"You gave your father to every drunk kitchen on two coasts," Cartethyia said, level, laying it out the way she laid out a blade for cleaning. "You built the hole in on purpose. You hand the pen over with the tune — your words, tonight, under the rule of truth. And you carry three notes of Amy's song in a locked box behind your sternum, and you will not play them, and when Mei asks you say they'd get used up." She tilted her head. "Square that with the tinker."

"The tinker sang a finished man," Ciaccona said. The fourth voice again; the funny voice had left the building. "My father was *dead*, Cartethyia. Complete. The song could only grow him — you can't spend a dead man down, there's no principal left to touch, it's all interest. Amy is—" She stopped. She was a professional handler of sentences and she set this one down with care, the way you set down something with a live edge. "Amy is unfinished. Her song stopped at three notes and she is *still out there*, at the bottom of a god, unfinished. And a song you finish is a tomb. Beautiful, and sung, and shut. I finish her song — I play those notes out into the world where they can be mended and grown and passed to tinkers — and some part of me will have agreed that she's a dead man at a rail. I keep them shut, and the verse is still waiting. The door's still ajar." Her thumb moved over the soundboard's old repaired seam, back and forth, once. "I told you what I'm afraid of. The end of the song. There it is with its coat off. As long as I don't play them, hers hasn't ended."

The false stars ticked. Down the corridor the shower had stopped; somewhere below, the city hummed inside the ribs of its kneeling god, above the gate, above the girl.

"I named a hundred dolls," Cartethyia said, "so the dark couldn't take my name."

"I know."

"It worked. The dark never got it." She picked up Ser Pincushion's plush cousin from her belt and set it on the desk, facing them, a witness. "And it kept me in a tower, Ciaccona. Both things. The naming saved me *and* it was the architecture of the staying — every doll I finished was one more reason the sealed room was survivable, and a survivable room is a room you stop breaking out of. Nobody warned me you could build the lifeboat so well you never sail." She met Ciaccona's eyes. "Play them, keep them — that stays yours; Lupa kicks doors, I only break furniture. I'm telling you what I know about keeping a door ajar for years with your own body: you become the door. And I notice one more thing, since we're under the rule."

"You're going to say it whether I invite it or not."

"Third finger." She raised it. "In every story you told me tonight, the mending is done in company. The lullaby needed a tavern. The father needed a coastline of drunks. Even Tammaro — his ledger waited forty years for a girl with a lute to walk in and *read* it; a witness needs a witness, or the sailcloth book is just the sea again, smaller. The whole doctrine runs on company. And your plan for Amy's three notes is to hold them alone, in the dark, in a box, forever, in the one pair of hands in the world most qualified to know better." She lowered the finger, and her voice went where it rarely went, all the sunniness banked down to coals. "Maybe they're not fading in there. Maybe you're right and the box preserves. But everything you taught me tonight says a kept song and a graved song are neighbors, and the fence between them is *company*. So when the day comes — and it's coming, we both feel it, the whole pack is being tuned for something — when the day comes that those three notes have somewhere to go and someone to go with, I want your word as a Toccata that you'll deal them off the bottom of the deck like everything else you love."

The Sylph on the lute's neck looked from one of them to the other.

"You're a menace," Ciaccona said finally. "You know that. Twenty years in a tower and you come out doing surgery."

"I had a hundred dolls and no anesthetic. Your word."

"...My word. When they have somewhere to go." It came out quieter than she intended, and truer, and she covered the exposure the only way she knew, which was to reach for the pegs and bring the lute back up to pitch, string by string, the room refilling with the small tortured sounds of an instrument being asked to work at midnight. "Now eat your last candy and get out, the rule's satisfied, we're both extremely healed."

Cartethyia ate her last candy. She did it slowly, and she settled deeper where she sat, and when she spoke again her mouth was half-full and her tone had gone dangerously idle. "One more thing occurs to me."

"The rule was three things. You counted. There were fingers."

"This one's free." She swallowed. "Everyone in your stories gets a song. Your father has one with a fan tradition. Fiora lives in six grandchildren and a verse. Amy has three notes and a bodyguard. Mei's whole life is basically an anthem you keep re-arranging. Even the *ewe* that tried to eat my boot made it into your notebook, I've seen the stanza, it scans better than my parts do." She laced her fingers behind her head and looked at the built stars. "Who sings the bard?"

"Nobody sings the bard. The bard is the frame. Frames that demand paintings embarrass everyone at the exhibition."

"Mm." Cartethyia nodded along with the grave air of a scholar accepting a premise in order to burn it down. "So if I wanted a Ballad of Ciaccona to exist, I'd have to make it myself. Out of whatever I happened to know. With my musical training, which consists of you wincing at me for two years."

"Cartethyia."

"And it would be *rough*," Cartethyia said, warming, sitting up, eyes alight with the specific joy she otherwise reserved for breaking furniture in gods' houses. "It would have holes in it. Enormous holes. Structural holes. And I'd sing it in taverns — badly, which I'm given to understand is the *correct technique* — and strangers would lean in and fix it, and they'd get you all wrong, and every wrong version would be you, and there'd be no manuscript and no author and nothing for anyone to confiscate, and one day some one-eyed tinker in a town you've never played would be singing about the liar who told the truth, and you'd have to stand in the doorway with your hood up and *tip him*." She spread her hands. "I'm only describing your method. You wrote the doctrine. Under the rule of truth: give me one reason it shouldn't apply to its inventor."

Ciaccona opened her mouth, and closed it, and the silence where the reason should have been was the loudest thing either of them had heard all night.

"There's a word for this," she said at last. "When a fencer is struck with her own blade."

"In Egla we called it Tuesday." Cartethyia was already humming — quietly, tunelessly, a phrase that wandered up where any honest melody would wander down, an interval outlawed in every tuning on every coast, structurally unsound from the first note, entirely hers. "*Oh, the bard came out of Ragunna with a key inside her lute—*"

"That's — you can't rhyme *lute* with *truth*, the vowel is doing all the work and the work is bad—"

"Sounds like a hole in the verse," said Cartethyia serenely, and kept humming.

And Ciaccona — who had a lot of griefs and one face, who had given away every song she ever loved except two, who had walked into this room owning the evening's only locked box and would walk out of it having promised the key — heard her own doctrine coming back at her in a country girl's tuneless voice, wrong in every particular and true in the one that counted, and did the only thing left to a professional.

She played along. She found the wandering phrase where it lived, put a floor under the illegal interval so that it stopped being an error and became a feature, an argument, a signature — and wove the humming in, as if it had always belonged, because now it did. The Sylphs came down from the curtain rod. The false stars ticked through their wrong constellations. Somewhere below the floor of the world a god knelt over a locked gate, and somewhere beyond the gate a girl's song waited at three notes, ajar; and in a dormitory room above all of it, two exiles with a thermos built a small bad ballad with holes in it, laughing, leaving the door open for whoever came to mend it.

Later, alone, before sleep, Ciaccona took out the notebook and found the running page of the mock-epic, and beside the entry she drew the star — small, uneven, her father's star, the way he drew them in margins, the way she had drawn them since.

*The Maiden hums flat on purpose now,* she wrote. *I have created a monster. The monster rhymes.*

And underneath, in smaller letters, the ground bass, one more time, the way it came out after a lifetime of variations:

*Leave the hole in the verse. That's where everyone gets in.*

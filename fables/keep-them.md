# Keep Them

*a toccata, for a lamp*

> *"Your playing is full of errors."*
> *"Thank you."*
> *"Keep them."*
>
> — reported by the student, twice, in different weather

---

## i. Thursday

The ferry out of Riva ran on Thursdays because Thursday was the day the menace was available.

Bonaccia the elder was ninety-one and rowed standing, facing forward, with a cigarette he had lit at some point in the previous decade. Bonaccia the younger was thirty and did the sail, and the sail was ornamental, and the argument about the sail had been going since the boy was eleven and both men were enjoying it enormously.

"He puts it up," said the old man, to the three passengers, over his shoulder, without breaking stroke. "Every trip. Puts it up."

"It's a *sailing* boat—"

"It's a *rowing* boat with a sail on it, which is a different animal, and you'd know that if you'd ever once been listening."

"Grandfather rowed to Ragunna in a gale in the year of the flood," said the younger Bonaccia, to the passengers, in the voice of a man reciting a psalm he has grown to loathe.

"I did."

"He tells it in the winter. It takes a month."

"It took *four days,*" said the old man, "and I'd tell it in an hour if you'd stop interrupting the good bit."

Elaina, sitting in the bow with her hat in her lap and her hair going in every available direction, wrote something down.

The crate went amidships. It contained six jars of mackerel Nunzia had insisted on, wrapped in a shirt, because there had been an argument about it at the tower door and Nunzia had won it by the simple method of putting the jars in the pack while everyone else was talking.

The gull came aboard at the point.

It landed on the transom with the total confidence of a paying customer, folded itself down, and sat. It had a bent feather in the left wing — the long outer one, kinked halfway and healed at an angle, so that the wing when it opened made a small extra shape at the tip.

Elaina stopped writing.

"That's Ottavo," said the younger Bonaccia. "He does the Thursdays."

"How long?"

"Since I was a boy. Longer. Grandfather?"

"He was on the boat when your father was on the boat."

Elaina looked at the gull for a very long time.

"They live thirty years," she said, to nobody.

"He'll outlive the lot of us and he knows it," said the old man. "Look at him. He's got a *pension.*"

"I know a gull with a bent primary," Elaina said.

"Where?"

"Nine hundred miles north. Under a glacier." She was still looking at it. "A woman there calls him the Admiral and salutes him, and everyone she lives with has been made to do it too."

The gull turned its head and regarded her with one flat orange eye.

"Well," said Cartethyia, into the silence, "it's obviously the same bird."

"It is obviously *not* the same bird."

"It's flown nine hundred miles to be near you. That's romantic."

"It's a *gull.* It eats rope."

"Say hello, at least."

Elaina, with the dignity of a woman doing something under protest that she would later record as her own idea, inclined her head to the gull.

The gull did nothing whatsoever, which is what gulls do, and then it stood up, and shook itself, and settled again with its bad wing outermost, which the old man said it always did on that tack, and Elaina wrote three lines and then a fourth and then shut the journal with her thumb inside it.

---

They were an hour out before Cartethyia went quiet, which for her was a thunderclap.

She had the copy of the instrument out on her knees. Four pages, fair, in Elaina's hand, with Nunzia's signature at the foot and the reference numbers running down the margin like a spine.

She read paragraph two for the fourth time.

*The lamp assembly may be recovered as scheduled.*

She had written that. She had been proud of it. She had walked round the tower room saying it aloud — *they can take the lamp, the lamp is worth eleven shells and the books are a cemetery* — and she had put it in on purpose, because an office that gets something is an office that will give you something, and it had worked, and she had gone to bed pleased.

"Cia."

"Mm."

"When Nunzia was serving the fish. The second night. What did she say?"

Ciaccona had her eyes shut against the glare. "She said eat."

"After that. When we were doing the disposal notice."

Ciaccona opened her eyes.

She thought about it. Cartethyia watched her get there, which took about six seconds, and saw the exact moment it arrived, because Ciaccona's face was very good in public and considerably worse in a boat.

"She said, *the light's the only reason anybody rows out here,*" Ciaccona said.

"Yes."

"And we said something about the books."

"We said a *great deal* about the books." Cartethyia folded the instrument along its own crease, very carefully, and put it away. "I saved the cemetery and I traded the light. She heard me do it. She let me."

"She let you because she'd made the same trade."

"She'd made it *once*, alone, on paper she didn't understand, and then three clever people turned up and made it again in front of her and told her it was a victory."

The boat went on. The gull adjusted.

"How long has she got?" Elaina asked.

"Recovery party before the close of Saltmonth." Cartethyia looked at the water. "Nine days."

"And then?"

"Then there's a rock at the end of Rinascita with fifteen hundred graves in it and no light on top, and a woman on it who'll go on writing down who was aboard for thirty years for a coast that's stopped looking at her."

Ciaccona sat up.

"Well," she said, "we'd better get her a lamp."

Cartethyia turned round.

"That's a *thing you can get?*"

"I've absolutely no idea," said Ciaccona. "I said it with confidence because that's the first half of my trade."

"What's the second half?"

"Finding somebody who knows."

Up in the bow, Elaina put her hat on.

"I'm going to Ragunna anyway," she said, in the tone of a woman conceding a point nobody had made, "and I have a commission there that will take one evening, and after that I am entirely at a loose end for approximately four days."

"You have a letter to deliver."

"South is very large," said Elaina. "It'll keep."

## ii. The Bureau of Hulls

Ragunna in Saltmonth smelled of wet stone, frying, and rope, and the canals were the green of a bottle held up to a window.

The founders' monument stood in the Piazza dell'Alba, and the Provisional Assembly had commissioned it in the first flush of self-government and then discovered, on unveiling, that they had produced eleven bronze figures of unimpeachable moral seriousness standing on a plinth of exactly the wrong height. Rather than melt it, they had cut steps into two sides and hung a rope net between the tallest two, and it was now the best climbing frame in the lower city, and there were four children on the Chancellor of the Provisional Assembly when the travelers went past.

"That one's got a boy on his head," said Cartethyia.

"That's the Chancellor."

"Is that allowed?"

"It's Thursday," said a woman selling fried things in paper, without looking up. "Thursday they're allowed on the heads."

Cartethyia bought three of the fried things and refused to be told what was in them.

---

The Bureau of Hulls occupied the top three floors of a former tithe-house on the Rio della Misura, because the bottom two flooded twice a year and the Order had built the strongrooms downstairs, which everyone in Ragunna agreed was the funniest thing the Order had ever done.

You got in by an outside stair. At the top of the stair was a door, and on the door a brass plate reading **BUREAU OF HULLS (PROVISIONAL) — REGISTRY OF VESSELS, COASTAL AND INSHORE — ENQUIRIES WELCOME**, and somebody had added, in pencil, underneath: *genuinely.*

Inside there were three desks, four thousand ledgers, a smell of oil and paper, one clerk, one machine, and a cat.

The clerk was perhaps twenty-four, in an apron over good clothes, with ink to the second knuckle, and she came out from behind the machine at a speed that suggested she had been waiting some time.

"Are you enquiries?"

"We're enquiries," said Cartethyia.

"*Excellent.*" She wiped her hands on the apron and shook all three of theirs. "Vittoria. I do the coasters and the small craft and the salvage cross-index, which nobody asks about, which is a tragedy, because it's the good one." She took the instrument, read the first paragraph standing up, and her eyebrows went into her hair. "Oh, this is *Coastal Property.* That's downstairs. That's — hold on."

She went to the window, opened it, leaned out over the canal, and shouted, "MARTA. INTERMENTS. THE HEADLAND ONE."

A window opened below. "THE WHAT?"

"THE TOWER. WITH THE BOOKS."

"IS IT THE ONE WITH THE WOMAN?"

"IT'S THE ONE WITH THE WOMAN."

"SEND THEM DOWN AFTER."

Vittoria came back in and shut the window.

"She'll take it," she said. "She's been arguing about your headland for a fortnight with a man from Recoveries who says a burial can't be a *place.*"

"It can," said Cartethyia.

"It absolutely can, we've got eleven of them at sea already, and he knows, and he's doing it because his brother-in-law wants the lamp for Punta Serchio." Vittoria pulled out a chair with her foot. "Sit. Are you the one who wrote the fourth paragraph?"

"Which one's the fourth?"

"*Their apparent character and their actual character differ.*"

"That's mine."

"I've read it out to three people," said Vittoria, with feeling. "I've *copied* it."

The cat came down off the ledgers.

It was a large, orange, professionally unimpressed animal, and it walked the length of the long desk without disturbing anything, sat down on the blotter, and looked at Cartethyia.

"That's the Registrar," said Vittoria.

Cartethyia went very still.

"He gets a wage," Vittoria said. "It's in fish, and it's in the minutes."

"My father was a registrar."

"Then you'll know how to behave."

Cartethyia put out one finger. The Registrar considered it for the length of a slow blink and then leaned its whole head into it with the air of an office accepting a properly completed form, and Cartethyia made a small sound, and Elaina wrote it down.

---

The machine took up the whole north wall.

It was pedal-driven, brass and pear-wood, with a bank of forty-one card-slots and a set of wooden fingers on rods, and a hopper, and a bell. The Order had built it to sort tithe returns by parish. Somebody in the second year of the reform had recut the cards and set it to hulls.

"It's called the Engine," said Vittoria, "because nobody's ever thought of anything better, and it has a *temperament.*"

"Meaning?"

"Meaning it drops a card about one time in sixty and it always drops it in the same slot, and the slot is Salvage, and we've stopped fighting it." She sat on the pedal-bench and rolled her sleeves. "What do you want?"

"Three things," said Cartethyia, producing a list, because of course she had a list.

"Oh, *good.*"

"One. A boat. A Pilgrim's Sail, out of Ragunna, fifteen years ago this spring, one aboard, a woman called Piera, out of Riva."

Vittoria's face changed.

"Sails aren't hulls," she said. "They were fitted out at the Arsenal and entered as *ecclesiastical stores.*"

"Ecclesiastical stores."

"They were furniture. That's the word they used." Vittoria pedaled twice, thinking. "But somebody *built* them. And a builder invoices. And an invoice is a hull record, because a hull is a hull whatever the church calls it. It's in the yard books, and the yard books came to us in the third year in eleven carts, and I've indexed four of the carts."

"How long for the other seven?"

"At my present rate," said Vittoria, "eight years."

Cartethyia looked at the carts, which were visible through a doorway, stacked, roped, patient.

"What's two?" said Vittoria.

"A ketch called *Rosa*, out of Riva, coastal freight, twenty-three years back. There'll be several."

"There'll be nine."

"I'll take nine."

"You will *not* take nine, they're four hundred pages each — " Vittoria stopped, and looked at Ciaccona, who had been quiet since the stairs and was standing at the window with her hands behind her back looking down at the canal. "Is it a person?"

"It's a person," said Ciaccona, without turning.

"Right," said Vittoria, and wrote *ROSA — 9 — PRIORITY* on a card in a way that suggested the Bureau's priorities were entirely her own invention and had never once been questioned. "Three?"

"A lamp," said Cartethyia.

---

The lamp took forty minutes and produced the following situation, which Vittoria laid out with the relish of a woman explaining a good joke:

The lamp at Capo Congedo was inventory. Inventory had a home. The home was Punta Serchio, where the Provisional Assembly was putting up a new light for the grain traffic, and the man from Recoveries had a brother-in-law on the Punta Serchio committee, and the lamp would go.

However.

Schedule 9 of the Coastal Property regulations, being a document assembled in nine weeks by four exhausted people in the second year of the reform, contained a clause permitting **substitution in kind**, by which any party might supply the Office with an assembly *of equivalent character* and retain the original in situ, the Office having neither the funds nor the appetite to argue.

"Equivalent character," said Cartethyia.

"It's defined." Vittoria pulled the book. "Here. *An assembly is of equivalent character where it throws a light visible from the sea at seven miles in fair weather, occults or flashes in a manner distinguishable from adjacent lights, and can be maintained by one person of ordinary strength using materials obtainable within the province.*" She looked up. "That's the whole test."

"That's a beautiful test."

"Marta wrote it. Marta was thirty-one and had been awake for four days."

"So we need," said Ciaccona, from the window, "a light. Seven miles. Distinguishable. And a woman on her own can fix it with what's in the province."

"You need an *optic,*" said Vittoria. "The rest is a wick and a clockwork and a boy with a spanner. The optic's the thing. It's a great lump of ground glass in belts, and there are eleven in Rinascita, and every one of them was made in the same place."

"Where?"

"The cathedral glassworks."

Cartethyia's head came up.

"Which is closed," said Vittoria. "Which has been closed three years. The furnaces are cold and the guild's dispersed and the last master went to Mingting to make spectacles, which I consider a national humiliation."

"And the glass?"

"Oh, the *glass* is here." Vittoria pedaled the Engine idly. "The glass is all in a shed."

---

The Engine dropped a card at four in the afternoon.

Vittoria had been running the *Rosa* cross-index for an hour and the bell had rung nine times and each time a card had come down the chute into the tray with a small important click, and the tenth time the bell rang twice and the wooden fingers hesitated and the card went sideways and landed in Salvage.

"There it is," said Vittoria, with real affection. "Once in sixty. Right on schedule."

She fished it out and glanced at it and put it on the desk to be refiled, and then Cartethyia, who read anything within reach the way other people breathe, picked it up.

It was a yard card. Arsenal of Ragunna, Squero Terzo. Not a *Rosa* at all.

"*Vessels, ecclesiastical stores,*" she read. "Squero Terzo. Contract in the eleventh year — that's — that's twenty-three years ago."

Vittoria's hand came out for it and stopped.

"That's a Sails yard," she said.

Cartethyia turned the card over.

On the back, in a clerk's hand and a different ink, was a note of the kind that gets written when an audit closes:

*Cooperage discrepancy, Squero Terzo, years 8–19 inclusive. Yard consistently invoiced 1 (one) breaker, 30 litre, per hull, above schedule. Cause unascertained. Sum trivial. Yard master examined and stated it was for the launch party. Recommend no action.*

*No action taken.*

The room went quiet enough to hear the canal.

"What's a breaker?" said Elaina.

"A water cask," said Ciaccona.

She had come off the window and was standing at the desk and she had gone the colour of the wall.

"Thirty litres," she said.

"Eleven years," said Cartethyia.

Ciaccona put one finger on the card and turned it round to face herself and read it again from the beginning, moving her lips, which she never did.

"They were provisioned with nothing," she said. "I've had that sentence for twenty-three years. I got it out of the harbourmasters and the victuallers in a week of shouting and it's in the manifests and it's *true,* the manifests are true, no water, no bread, three singers and a beautiful boat and the sea's own judgment."

"And a yard that kept putting one extra cask on the invoice," said Cartethyia.

"Which the manifest would never show, because the manifest is what the Order loaded." Vittoria had gone very careful, the way people do around something they suspect they should not be touching. "The yard fits her out and the church stores her. If the yard put something in the bilge under the sole and never told anybody—"

"Then it isn't a provision," said Cartethyia. "It's a *hull fitting.*"

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Sum trivial," Ciaccona read. "*Recommend no action.*"

"Do we know whether it was there?" said Elaina.

"No."

"Do we know whether anybody found it?"

"No."

"Do we know the yard master's name?"

"Squero Terzo, eleventh year," said Vittoria, already up, already going for the carts. "That'll be in the seven carts I haven't done."

"How long?" said Cartethyia.

Vittoria stopped with her hand on the rope.

"Eight years at my rate," she said. "Rather less with help."

Cartethyia looked at the carts. Then at Ciaccona.

"We have nine days and a lamp," Ciaccona said.

"I know."

"And a man in Recoveries with a brother-in-law."

"I *know.*" Cartethyia had her hand pressed flat over her mouth. "I'm making a note. I'm making a very large note."

She got out the folder, and wrote **SQUERO TERZO** on a fresh sheet in block capitals, and under it, *one breaker, thirty litre, eleven years, cause unascertained*, and then a reference number, and then, because she was her father's daughter and a registrar's second duty is to know when the truth needs help, she wrote at the bottom: **PENDING.**

And the Registrar, who had been asleep in the tray of the Engine throughout, got up, walked the length of the desk, and sat down on the folder.

*RAGUNNA. What they call her here.*

*— Nothing. Twice I asked and got a joke and once I got a shrug. This city gave its sea-god back three years ago and has decided not to replace the furniture.*
*— At the fish quay, from a woman of seventy: "la Padrona." The landlady. Said with the specific loathing reserved for landladies.*
*— From her granddaughter, immediately, and to her face: "Nonna, nobody says that."*
*— From a boy on the mole, who had thought about it: "the big one." Then: "the big quiet one."*

*Item. There is a chandlery on the Rio della Misura with a cup on the counter holding pencils. Porcelain, chipped at the foot, letters at the waist flared slightly outward at the base, in the manner of hulls. I stood in front of it for a period I decline to enter. The chandler said his boy brought it up in a net at Punta Serchio in the spring and it had come a long way by the weed on it and did I want a pencil.*

*I bought a pencil.*

*I am writing this with it.*

## iii. The Shed

The shed was on the Fondamenta dei Sarti, behind a boatyard, with a door too small for what was in it.

Bice was seventy-two and had been the cathedral's glass conservator for thirty-one years, and now she was the custodian of a shed, and she had a stove in it, and a bed she claimed was for naps, and a system.

"Boots off," she said, "there's grit in the straw and it gets in the leading and then I have to do it twice."

They took their boots off.

The shed ran back sixty feet and it was full of crates, and the crates were open, and the light came down from a clerestory of plain glass along the ridge, and every crate in the shed had colour standing up out of it.

The floor was a river.

Blue lay across the boards in one long soft band from the crates of a Deposition. Red pooled under a rack of martyrs' cloaks. Amber and a poisonous, gorgeous green came off something with fish in it. Where two colours crossed they made a third that had no name, and dust went up and down in all of it like weather.

Cartethyia stopped four paces in and put her hand out and it went green to the wrist.

"Oh," she said.

"Mind the straw," said Bice, and went to put the kettle on.

---

They found the Maiden in the nineteenth crate, because Bice knew exactly where the Maiden was and had known for three years and had made them walk past everything else first, which Ciaccona identified as a professional decision and admired accordingly.

It was in nineteen crates, in fact. A window forty feet high comes apart into a great many boxes.

"That's the sea," said Bice, lifting a panel and holding it up so the light took it. "Black water. He used three blues and a smoked grey and he stippled the grey on the *outside* of the glass, which is a thing you do about once in a career, and it works, and I've never dared clean it."

"It's beautiful," said Elaina.

"It's the best thing in this shed and the man who made it was a monster. I've read his accounts." Bice set it down in the straw. "Here."

She took out the head.

Twenty-three years of Ragunnan noon had come through it and gone. It was three feet across and it was serene and it was young and it was a stranger, and it had a laurel in leaded gold with nine points, and grey-green eyes made out of two pieces of glass that did not match and had been chosen to not match, on purpose, by a monster who knew what he was doing.

Bice held it up.

Cartethyia looked at it.

The shed did the thing sheds do, which is be quiet.

"You've a look of her," Bice said, after a while, conversationally, setting it back. "There's a family in Egla that has it. Broad across here." She tapped her own cheekbone. "I did the survey in the fifth year. Two of the bakers had it and a man who mended nets."

"Cousins, probably," said Cartethyia.

"Probably." Bice dusted her hands. "She'd be forty-three now. Forty-four in the spring, if you go by the crowning."

"Mm."

"Tea's boiling."

They had tea.

Elaina, who had watched the whole exchange from beside a crate of angels, wrote nothing at all, and later said that it was the first time in four years she had chosen to leave something out.

---

The proposal took an hour and Bice said no eleven times before she said yes, and every no was a technical objection and every one of them was correct.

Window glass is soda-lime and full of temper. Optical glass wants to be homogeneous and it wants to be annealed for a week. Colour is a filter and a filter eats light and a light eats miles. You cannot grind a belt of prisms out of a saint's cheek. You cannot grind anything without a wheel and a wheel wants a shaft and a shaft wants a mill or a furnace-house with a treadle, and the furnace-houses in this city are cold and the guild is scattered from here to Mingting.

"So it's impossible," said Cartethyia.

"It's *poor,*" said Bice.

"How poor?"

Bice sat back and looked at the ceiling and did sums with her lips moving.

"You'd get seven miles on a clear night with a good wick and an argument," she said. "You'd get five when it's dirty. It'd throw soft — the beam would have no edge to it, it'd come up out of the dark like a bruise instead of a sword — and you'd never get the belts true so it'd wobble in the rotation, and any pilot who came up on it would say *that's a bad light.*"

"Would he know which bad light?"

Bice stopped.

"Say that again," she said.

"The test is *distinguishable from adjacent lights.*" Cartethyia had the regulation out and her finger under it. "It has to be visible at seven miles and it has to be *distinguishable*, and it has to be maintainable by one woman with what's in the province. It says nothing about good."

Bice took the book off her and read it twice.

"If I put colour in a sector," she said slowly, "you'd get a green cast on one bearing. Which would be a fault. Which would be *repeatable.*"

"And nobody else on this coast has it."

"Nobody else on this coast would *permit* it." Bice sat down heavily on a crate of prophets. "There's a family at Varrena has been casting bells a quarter-tone flat for a hundred years and every founder in Rinascita has offered to fix them and the town won't have it, and the reason the town won't have it is that a Varrena man three days out at sea in fog can tell you what parish he's off by the sound of the water carrying the wrong bells home."

"There it is," said Ciaccona quietly.

"That's a *fault* as an aid to navigation. It's an old idea and it's a good one and it went out with the Order because the Order liked things to be correct." Bice was looking at the floor, at the river of colour going across it. "I could give her a green sector. Two degrees either side of the reach. Off Punta Nera you'd see white, and coming up from the south you'd see white, and in the one place where the rocks are, you'd see the light go green, and you'd know exactly where you were, and if you were a Riva man you'd know it in your sleep."

"Would it be good?"

"It'd be *awful,*" said Bice. "It'd be the worst optic in the province and a Ragunnan guild inspection would put me out of my trade for it in about four minutes."

"And there's no guild."

"And there's no guild," Bice agreed, "and there's no cathedral, and there's no inspection, and I've spent three years in a shed keeping a face nobody's allowed to look at."

She got up and went down the shed and stood in front of the nineteenth crate for a while with her back to them.

"It was made to be looked at," she said. "I'd like to make it look."

---

The choosing took eleven minutes and no argument.

Bice took the clear glass first: the border, the white ground, the plain quarries from the tracery, four crates of it, because that was the glass that would do the work.

Then she said, "For the sector I want the greens. There's a green in the water-register that'll carry."

"Take the face," said Cartethyia.

Bice turned round.

"It's the wrong colour."

"It's flesh glass," said Cartethyia. "It's got iron in it, hasn't it, that's what makes it warm. You said the sector eats light. So put something in it that's already warm."

"It'll come out — " Bice frowned at the head in the straw. "It'd come out somewhere between a green and a gold. It'd be a filthy, indeterminate, — " she stopped, and her jaw worked. "It'd be a colour nobody's ever put in a light."

"Then nobody will mistake it."

"Are you certain?"

"I am extremely certain," said Cartethyia.

Bice looked at her for a moment longer than the conversation required.

"Right," she said, and picked up the head, and carried it down the shed to the bench, and laid it in the straw with the gentleness of a woman who had done that for thirty-one years, and got out a diamond.

Behind them, in the doorway, Elaina murmured something.

"What?" said Ciaccona.

"She's going to look out of it," said Elaina. "Every night. At the water. For the next sixty years."

"Mm."

"That's the single most Rinascitan thing I have ever heard of in my life."

"Give it a week," said Ciaccona.

---

The letter caught up with them that evening at the poste restante on the Via Lucente, where Cartethyia had left a forwarding address in nine registries and had therefore received, in one bundle, a great deal of mail.

They read it on the steps of a church that was now a fish market.

> *Carte —*
>
> *Amy says to tell you the record player is fixed and the needle survived. Rebecca says to tell you she fixed it. Both statements are technically true and I have stopped adjudicating.*
>
> *The east perimeter bollard has been replaced. I want you to know that I noticed, and that I have not enjoyed it.*
>
> *Lupa wore the crown for eleven days. Chisa retired it on grounds of structural fatigue and has made a better one out of tin, which Lupa refuses to wear because it is not the original, which we have all agreed is the most sentimental thing she has ever done and none of us will say so.*
>
> *Lynae has been banned from the kitchen. There is a document.*
>
> *Mei is well. Mei is very well. Mei asked me to remind you about the stupid thing and then said "she'll forget" in a tone I found insulting on your behalf, so consider yourself defended.*
>
> *Write when you can. Do not write only when something has gone wrong; I have noticed that pattern in you and I would like it corrected.*
>
> *— I.*
>
> *P.S. Ciaccona: there is a hum going round this dormitory that nobody can source and everybody has caught. Four bars. It goes up where it ought to come down. Amy has it, Rebecca has it, the S.I.G.M.A. door chime has it now, which should be impossible. I am told you left about three weeks ago. Explain yourself.*

Ciaccona read the postscript twice and handed the letter back without comment and looked out at the canal for a while with an expression that Elaina described later, in her journal, under *people*, as *a woman receiving an invoice she is delighted to pay.*

---

The mechanical swan happened on the way back to the inn and nobody could afterwards establish how.

It stood two feet high, it was brass and painted tin, it had a key in its side the size of a soup spoon, and it dispensed sweets from a hatch under the tail at a rate the vendor described as *generous* and Ciaccona described, having watched a demonstration, as *four*.

"It looks lonely," said Cartethyia.

"It looks *expensive.*"

"It's forty."

"Cantarella's treasury has a *seismograph* for this exact—"

"It's my forty. It's the drainage money."

"You have *drainage money?*"

"The Bureau of Subterranean Waters pays a consultation stipend," said Cartethyia, with enormous dignity, "and I have been consulting."

She named it on the walk back. The full title took eleven seconds and involved a duchy. Elaina, who had opinions about brass and had been raised by a woman who could tell you the load path of a hat, checked the mechanism, pronounced the escapement sound and the sweet-hatch a catastrophe, and was later observed winding it.

Ciaccona referred to it, throughout, as *the bird.*

---

## iv. Varrena

The road to Varrena went inland over the Cinque Sorelle, five hills the colour of a lion, with the sea appearing on the left at every crest like something checking on them.

The thiefbat took Elaina's hat on the second afternoon.

It came out of a thorn brake at head height, going flat out, and it had the hat off her by the crown and was forty feet up before anybody had finished shouting, and it hung there in the thermal over the road, turning, gold and enormous-eyed, with a wide-brimmed black witch's hat in its feet and an expression of total moral confidence.

"*That* — " said Elaina.

"Don't run at it," said Cartethyia, putting an arm out.

"That hat was made for me by a woman with one hand."

"I know. Don't run at it, it'll go up and drop it in the gorse and then we're an hour."

Cartethyia handed Ciaccona the swan, walked twenty paces up the road, and sat down on a milestone.

Then she took out a fried thing in paper, unwrapped it, and set it on the stone beside her, and looked at the horizon.

"What are you doing," said Elaina.

"Negotiating."

"With a *bat.*"

"With the Golden Thiefbats of the Rinascitan interior," said Cartethyia, "with whom I concluded a treaty at the age of eight, and who have honoured it, in my experience, better than three governments."

"What are the terms?"

"They may take anything not being worn, held, or actively defended. I may redeem any item at a rate of one hot food." Cartethyia did not look up. "Cold food is an insult and they will take the food and the item. It's happened."

The bat came down in a long spiral and landed on the far end of the milestone.

It put the hat down. It picked up the fried thing. It looked at Cartethyia for a moment with its head on one side, and Cartethyia inclined her head to it exactly as Elaina had inclined hers to a gull, and the bat went off over the gorse with its supper.

Elaina retrieved the hat.

There was a small tear in the brim.

"It'll mend," said Cartethyia.

"It will," said Elaina, turning it over. "She'll ask me how."

"Tell her the truth."

"She'll want the *bat's name.*"

"They don't give them out," said Cartethyia. "I've asked."

---

Varrena was the colour of the inside of a mussel shell, every last brick of it, and it came down its hill to the water in a series of decisions that had clearly been made one at a time over four hundred years.

The bells rang at six as they came in over the shoulder of the hill.

Ciaccona stopped walking.

Elaina got two paces further and stopped too, and her whole face folded up.

"What is *wrong* with them?"

"Quarter-tone flat," said Ciaccona. "All eight. For about a century."

"It's — " Elaina turned round on the road, hunting the sound. "It's *underneath.* It's underneath everything. It's like standing on a floor that isn't—"

"Give it two days."

"I refuse."

"Give it two days," said Ciaccona, "and then go anywhere else in the world and hear a bell rung true, and tell me what you think of it."

Cartethyia, who had been humming along, said, "I think they're lovely."

"You would," said both of the others, at once.

---

The inn on the corner by the fish steps had a new sign, and the sign was a fool's cap.

It was painted white on a red ground, in a hand that had copied it carefully off something, and underneath in gold letters it said **LA BERRETTA** and under that, smaller, *est. and chalked, in the same year.*

Ciaccona stood in the street and looked at it for a while.

Cartethyia came up beside her.

"Is that the mark?"

"That's the mark."

"They kept it."

"They *painted* it," said Ciaccona. "They had it *gilded.*"

The door opened and a woman of about fifty came out with a bucket, took in the three of them, took in the swan under Ciaccona's arm, and said, "We've one room and it's got a slope in the floor."

"We'll take it," said Cartethyia.

The woman looked at Ciaccona a second longer.

Then she put the bucket down.

"You're the girl," she said.

"I'm a lot of girls."

"You're the girl with the lute who ate for eight nights in the corner by the fire and sang the poets and got my mother chalked." Nella wiped her hands on her apron. "You came back once, about eleven years ago, and you sat in the back and you didn't say who you were, and Mam knew inside a minute and let you get away with it because she thought you'd bolt."

Ciaccona's mouth opened.

"She would have been right," she said.

"She usually was." Nella held the door. "Come in. There's soup."

---

It was white bean with fennel.

Ciaccona ate two bowls of it and did not talk for the length of the first one, which the other two allowed, and the fire did its work, and the room filled up around them with the fifty or so people that a small harbour town puts into a good inn on a Tuesday.

Halfway through the second bowl Nella came over and put a piece of paper on the table.

It was a bill.

It was a bill from twenty-three years ago, in a heavy careful hand, ruled and totalled, for eight nights' board and lodging and soup, and at the bottom somebody had added, in a different and much later ink, twenty-three years of interest at the rate charged by the Ragunnan grain banks, compounded, with the working shown.

Ciaccona picked it up and read the total and put her hand over her eyes.

"Your mother kept a *bill.*"

"My mother kept everything. There's a wall." Nella nodded at it. "That one's been in the till since I was eleven. She used to get it out at Midwinter and do the interest in front of the regulars and everybody would guess the figure. There was betting."

"There was betting."

"There was a *pool.*"

Cartethyia had her fist in her mouth.

"Is it real?" said Ciaccona.

"It's real."

"You want it paid."

"I want it *paid,*" said Nella, "and I want it paid in front of this room, and then I want to nail it to the wall with PAID written on it, because there are people in here who've been hearing about that bill for twenty years and two of them think it's a story I made up."

Ciaccona counted it out.

It took a while and it emptied her, and Cartethyia had to lend her eleven shells, and the room worked out what was happening about a third of the way through and got very loud, and somebody at the back began a slow handclap that turned into the entire inn.

Nella wrote **PAID** across the bill in a hand that shook slightly and nailed it up over the fire beside a plate commemorating a flood.

"And now," she said, turning round, "you can sing for your supper like an honest woman, because the interest was for the *board* and it never covered the eight nights of racket."

"That is not how interest works."

"It's how it works here."

So Ciaccona sang for her supper in the room where she had been given her first roof in exile, and she played it badly, out of habit and out of policy, and the room corrected her within four bars, and by the end of the evening she had three verses she had never heard of a song she had written, and Elaina had filled six pages, and Cartethyia had cried twice and denied it both times.

---

*VARRENA. What they call her here.*

*— La Sorda. The deaf one. (Universal. Said kindly. A woman explained: "She can't hear you, love, that's the whole of it. She's not cruel. She's deaf.")*
*— Nonna Fonda. Deep gran.*
*— And at the bell foundry, from Ottavia, who thought about it for a full minute with a file in her hand: "the low one."*
*"Low how?"*
*"Flat. Everything's flat down there. Try singing under water."*

*Item. I have been in this town four days and today the bells rang and I felt nothing, and an hour later I heard a handbell in the market that was true and it went through me like a splinter and I said, out loud, to a stranger, "that's horrible."*

*He said: "You're getting on, then."*

---

The foundry was up the hill behind the church, and it was the only building in Varrena that could put a shaft on a wheel and a fire under a pot, and Ottavia Rialti was twenty-eight and sixth generation and did not look up when they came in.

"You're the glass," she said. "Bice wrote. She writes like a bailiff."

"Can you do it?"

"I can turn a wheel. I've a treadle-shaft off the old bell-lathe that'll run three hundred, and a furnace I can hold at annealing for six days if somebody feeds it at four in the morning, and the somebody is not going to be me."

"It'll be me," said Cartethyia.

"You say that now."

"I've been getting up at four for eleven weeks with nothing to do at the other end of it."

Ottavia looked up for the first time.

"All right," she said.

---

Bice came up from Ragunna on the Thursday boat with four crates and an opinion about the road, and the two of them fought for six days.

It was a wonderful fight. They fought about the shaft speed and the grit grades and whether the annealing curve should be Bice's or Ottavia's, and they fought at the tops of their voices in a language of trade terms that nobody else in the building could follow, and twice a day one of them would go outside and stand in the yard and come back in and concede a point with the phrase *fine, do it your way, and when it cracks I'll say nothing,* and the other would say *you'll say plenty,* and they would carry on.

On the fourth day Cartethyia found them sitting on the same crate sharing a plum.

"They're in love," she reported.

"They're *colleagues,*" said Ciaccona. "It looks similar from the outside and it lasts longer."

---

Elaina got the alphabet on the fifth day, and it came for free, the way the good ones do.

She had asked Ottavia why the bells were flat, expecting a legend, and had got instead a shrug and a book.

The book was the Rialti tuning-book, and it went back a hundred and forty years, and it was not written in notes.

It was written in a system of hooks and bars that the family had invented for itself, because a bell is tuned by taking metal off the inside and a founder needs to write down *where* and *how much*, and no notation in the world does that, so the Rialti had made one. Hooks for the partials. Bars for the depth of cut. A little curl at the end of a line for *leave it.*

"And there's a mistake in it," said Ottavia, turning to a page near the front. "Here. My great-great-grandmother's hand. The hum-note cut. She's got it a hook out."

"One hook."

"One hook is a quarter-tone." Ottavia turned the page. "And her son copied her book, and his daughter copied his, and by the time anybody looked at the actual acoustics there were eight bells in the tower and forty in the parishes and a hundred years of people who'd learned what a bell sounds like from listening to ours."

"Can you fix it?"

"I could fix it in a fortnight." Ottavia shut the book. "The Ragunnan guild offered in my father's time. Sent a man. My father took him up the tower and rang the six and asked him to name the parish."

"And?"

"He couldn't." Ottavia put the book back on the shelf. "So my father said, *then you can't hear, and I'm not taking lessons from the deaf,* and gave him lunch and sent him home."

Elaina copied the whole tuning-book by hand in two evenings, hooks and bars and curls, forty-one pages, and got a cramp in her wrist that lasted three days, and addressed the packet to a scribe under a glacier who had once built an alphabet out of the air to hold a sleeping woman's language, and wrote on the outside: *One you haven't got. It has a mistake in it, and they know, and they kept it. — E.*

---

The lens came out of the annealing oven on the ninth morning and it was, by every standard anybody present could name, terrible.

It was three feet across in eleven belts and it weighed as much as a person and it wobbled in the mount and there was a scatter in the third belt where Bice's diamond had wandered on the fifth day and a bloom in the lowest ring that would never come out.

They lit a lamp inside it in the dark of the foundry at four in the morning, and turned it, and stood back.

The white came round and hit the far wall and went past and the room went dark and it came round again.

Then the sector came.

It went across the wall slow and soft with no edge on it at all, and it was somewhere between a green and a gold and it was neither, and it was the colour of a face that has been in the sun for twenty-three years, and it lay on the brick for a second and a half and moved on.

Ottavia said a word that a bell-founder learns young.

Bice sat down on a crate.

Cartethyia stood in the middle of the floor with the light going over her, on, off, on, and said, in a voice that had gone thin, "That's the wrong colour."

"That's the wrong colour," Bice agreed.

"That's *exactly* the wrong colour."

"Yes."

"Nobody will ever mistake that for anything."

"No," said Bice. "They won't."

---

## v. The Green Sector

They came back to Capo Congedo on the last day of Saltmonth, in a fishing boat Bonaccia the younger had been talked into against every instinct he possessed, with a lens in a crate of straw, a swan in a sack, four people, a bell-founder's apprentice who had come to do the mount, and a gull.

The recovery party had been and gone.

The old lamp was on the shingle at Riva under a tarpaulin, waiting on a cart to Punta Serchio, and the man from Recoveries had left a receipt, and Nunzia had signed it, and she had been three nights in the dark.

She met them on the path with her sleeves rolled.

"You took your time."

"There was a bill," said Ciaccona.

"There was a *bat,*" said Elaina.

"There was a wheel-shaft," said Cartethyia, "and an argument about annealing that I want you to know I could now hold my own in," and she pushed past her up the path with the crate on a handcart and her whole face going, and Nunzia caught the handle of the cart, and looked in the crate, and stopped dead.

"That's an optic."

"That's an optic."

"That's a — " Nunzia looked up. "Where in the name of God did you get an *optic?*"

"Ragunna," said Cartethyia.

"They don't make them any more."

"They don't."

"Then where—"

"It's substituted in kind under Schedule 9," said Cartethyia, "and it's of equivalent character, and the paperwork's lodged, and Marta in Interments has countersigned it, and it is *none of your business what it's made of.*"

Nunzia looked at her for a long moment.

Then she looked at the crate again.

Then she said, "Get it up the stairs before the wind gets up," which is how a woman on a rock says thank you.

---

The mount took the whole of the afternoon and most of the evening and involved every person present and two arguments and one moment on the gallery stair with the crate at an angle that Elaina afterwards refused to discuss.

They lit it at eleven minutes past six.

The whole of Riva was on the shingle, because the whole of Riva had been told, because Bonaccia the younger had a mouth on him and had used it in four public houses.

The wick took. The clockwork went over. The belts began to turn.

And the light went out over the water, soft-edged and bruised and generous, once round every twelve seconds, white to the north and white to the south, and then, on the one bearing that mattered, over the rocks off Punta Nera where four boats had gone in living memory, a slow indeterminate wash of green-gold that had no name in any chandler's book and would be in every pilot's mouth on that coast inside a year.

On the beach, somebody shouted.

Then all of them did.

Nunzia stood on the gallery with her hands on the rail and did not shout, and after a while she went inside and came out with the current book and the stone, and opened it under the lamp, and wrote the date, and the weather, and *lamp assembly renewed, of poor character, distinguishable at seven miles*, and then she stood with the pencil over the second column for a while.

"Who was aboard?" said Cartethyia, at her shoulder.

Nunzia wrote five names.

Then she wrote a sixth, and it was Tammaro's, and she said, "He's aboard. He's been aboard the whole time," and shut the book, and went down to see about the fish.

---

Elaina left the next morning at dawn, because her boots were ready before she was and always had been, and because a letter addressed *South* had been in her coat for eleven weeks and was beginning to have opinions.

They walked her out to the head of the path.

"You'll write," said Cartethyia.

"I write to nine people. It's ruining my hands."

"Ten," said Cartethyia.

Elaina, in the middle of settling her hat, stopped.

"You've a forwarding address in nine registries," she said.

"And a dormitory under a glacier, which you can walk to." Cartethyia put out her hand. "Ten."

Elaina shook it.

Then she took a breath and said, quite fast, "There's a woman where I live who asked me to bring her back every name anybody has for the sea, and I've got sixty-one now, and four of them are from this headland, and one of them is the same word two coasts apart that neither coast has heard the other say." She looked out at the water. "I don't know what it means. I don't think it means anything. I've decided to send it anyway."

"That's the whole trade," said Ciaccona.

"Which trade?"

"Ours." Ciaccona was leaning on the rail with her arms folded. "You go and look, and you come back and say what you saw, and somebody at the other end decides what it's worth. The looking's the only part you control."

Elaina considered this.

"That's not very reassuring."

"No," said Ciaccona. "It's a job."

Elaina got the broom sorted.

"The gull's on the boat again," she said.

"I know."

"It's *not* the same gull."

"Absolutely not," said Cartethyia.

"It cannot possibly be the same gull."

"Nobody has suggested otherwise."

Elaina looked at both of them with deep suspicion, and put her hat on, and went off the cliff, and the wind took her south along the coast where the pilots hand the ships over, and she got small, and then she got very small, and then the light went round.

---

## vi. What They Took Home

They stayed three more days, because the wick wanted watching and because Nunzia had six jars of mackerel and no plans.

On the last evening Cartethyia carried the swan up to the gallery and wound it and set it on the desk, and it walked four paces and dispensed four sweets and then made a noise like a cutlery drawer falling downstairs and stopped.

"That's it," said Ciaccona. "That's the whole product."

"She's *tired.*"

"She's a fraud in brass and the man saw you coming from the end of the street."

"Mei will love her."

"Mei will love her *enormously,*" Ciaccona agreed, "and Iuno will look at her for four seconds, and then Iuno will go and get a very small screwdriver, and by Frostbreak that swan will be laying nine and everybody in that dormitory will be sick of sweets, and Rebecca will have painted flames on it."

Cartethyia wound it again.

"Do you want the eleven?" she said.

"The eleven what?"

"The fiddlers." Cartethyia kept her eyes on the swan. "The other ten margins. Vittoria's got the *Rosa* index going and there are nine of them and eight years of carts behind that. And there's a yard called Squero Terzo with a cooperage discrepancy that ran for eleven years and a yard master who told an auditor it was for the launch party."

Ciaccona was quiet.

"I've thought about very little else since Ragunna," she said.

"I know. You've been humming in fours."

"Have I."

"You always do when you're carrying something."

Ciaccona looked out at the water. The lamp came round and the green went over the rocks off Punta Nera and away.

"If somebody put a cask of water in the bilge of eleven years of those boats," she said, "then either it did nothing, and eleven years of a man's quiet crime came to nothing at all — or somewhere out there, some number of people got a week they weren't given. And the number could be one. And it could be forty. And it will never be established, because the manifests are honest and the yard books are in seven carts and the yard master is either dead or ninety."

"Or ninety-one," said Cartethyia, "and rowing on Thursdays."

Ciaccona laughed, once, and rubbed her face with both hands.

"I can't make a song out of *cause unascertained,*" she said.

"You made one out of *singing.*"

"That was one word and it was true."

"So's this one," said Cartethyia. "It's just longer and it's got arithmetic in it."

She wound the swan again. It walked four paces and produced a sweet and fell over, and Nunzia, coming up the stair with the tea, picked it up and set it on its feet without comment or apparent surprise, which is the correct way to treat a duchess.

"There's a hole in it," Ciaccona said, slowly, to the sea. "That's the shape. *A yard put a cask aboard for eleven years and nobody knows what became of it.* That's the verse and the hole's at the end of it and it's the biggest one I've ever built."

"And they'll fill it."

"They'll fill it a hundred ways in a hundred taverns and about ninety-eight of them will be nonsense." Ciaccona picked up the lute. "And two of them will be from somebody's grandmother who worked in the Arsenal, and one of those two will be *the thing that happened.*"

"How will you know?"

"I won't," said Ciaccona. "That's the whole flaw in the method and I've never found a way round it and I'm not looking for one any more."

She tuned the fourth string.

"Keep them," she said.

"What?"

"Something a woman said to a girl about her playing. Elaina told me on the road out of Sordina and I've been carrying it since." Ciaccona set her hand on the strings. "*Your playing is full of errors.* And the girl said thank you. And the woman said keep them."

Below them, in the dark, the light went round: white, white, and then that filthy indeterminate warm green that no chandler had a word for, sliding slow over the rocks off Punta Nera with no edge on it at all, made out of the cheek of a saint who never existed, ground badly on a bell-lathe by two women who fought for six days and shared a plum.

Cartethyia put her chin on her arms on the rail.

"Cia."

"Mm."

"When we get home."

"Mm."

"There's a cart index in Ragunna that wants eight years and one clerk, and a woman on a rock with a column she rules herself, and a shed full of saints in a river of colour, and a bell that's wrong on purpose, and a bat I owe a hot dinner to." Cartethyia's breath went out. "And I have to go back and check the *bollards.*"

"You do."

"I want to be there. I want to be there so much it's ridiculous. Amy's got a station and Mei sleeps and Lupa's got a tin crown she won't wear." She turned her head. "And there's all this."

"Yes."

"How do people *do* it?"

Ciaccona thought about it properly, which she owed her.

"Badly," she said. "In instalments. With letters."

"That's not a plan."

"It's a route." Ciaccona played four bars, and left the hole in, and the wind came up over the gallery rail from the west and took the lot of it out over the water, where it would be filed with everything else. "Ask me again at the ferry. I'll have something better and it'll be a lie, and we'll both feel much improved."

The lamp went round.

Down at the desk, under the stone, the book lay open at the last page with a space ruled in the second column and nothing written in it yet, because the boat had gone by after dark and nobody had been able to read her name, and Nunzia would ask on Thursday.

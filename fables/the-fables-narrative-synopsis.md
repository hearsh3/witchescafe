# The Ides of March

---

## I. THE WORLD AT THE OPENING

A woman with golden eyes and no memory washed up half-drowned on the shore of Ragunna, and the woman who pulled her out of the surf had done it on purpose. That is the debt the whole story rests on, and almost nobody in it yet knows its shape.

She answers to Mei. She has no reachable past, a sword called *Haultir*, a habit of putting her body between other people and whatever is coming, and a pack. Lupa, a gladiator out of the pits of Septimont who killed her own corrupted packmates to walk out of the arena alive. Cartethyia, the Blessed Maiden of Rinascita, who spent twenty years shut in a dark church because of the mark on her and made cloth dolls in that dark so she would not forget what people looked like. Ciaccona, a bard who has been playing three notes of a four-note phrase for as long as anyone has known her, and never the fourth. Chisa, who is face-blind and reads people as frequencies and invisible connections, and who spent twenty years locked in one looping summer in Honami until her friend Sumika burned her own life away to let her out. And Iuno — High Priestess of Septimont, retired, missing her left forearm and wearing a crystalline stump in its place, who governed a city-state for thirty years and carries a second voice behind her sternum that she calls the High Priestess and argues with constantly.

Together they broke a theocracy, unsealed a drowned city, and woke a silenced god that spent the last of its strength healing a dying knight and dissolved telling its people: *From this day forth, you are free. Lead well.* Then they sailed north and vanished from every chart for nineteen days.

What they found was Lahai-Roi — a city inside a giant, built underground beneath a sun its own engineers agreed should rise, where weather happens because ducts move air. They found a school, Startorch Academy, whose records showed all six of them admitted three days before they arrived. And Mei found what she had come north for without knowing it.

Mei had a daughter: adopted, thirteen years of her, in a cabin with a door Mei painted blue and wisterias Mei planted in frozen ground because she believed they would grow. The Academy's sealed casualty file calls her Aemeath. She calls herself Amy. Fifteen years ago she was the synchronisation candidate a board chairman named Chase personally approved; she pushed past her limits because she wanted to be the hero, burned out, and was filed dead. She was not dead. She was awake and alone inside the Nihility, the void beyond a buried gate, and she stayed there ten years holding a cosmic ending shut, singing three notes to herself because they were the last thing she had that was her mother's.

By the time this account begins, that has just finished. Mei's pack, plus Hiyuki — last Miko of Flaming Sakura, who lost her city Honami thirty years ago and has collected other people's ungrantable wishes ever since — woke a kneeling god-mech called Baldur, drove it through the Stridergate, fought a Threnodian of ending called Aleph-1 stitched together from ten thousand consciousnesses lost in the dark, and pulled Amy out. Mei caught her. They said *I love you* for the first time.

Two things went unresolved that night, and both drive everything after.

Cornered in the collapsing centre of that fight by a version of herself who taunted her that her family was a story she was telling herself, Mei swung and hit a cryo-unit door instead. Inside was not a hero. Inside was a girl of twenty-three, ID reading PENROSE-512: PILOT, NAME: LILITH, and a journal of four and a half thousand ordinary days — exams, a friend called Dingy, *I rejected another girl today.... why?*, *I lost a friend today* and then a blank page, watching everyone she knew become people, and finally *I started writing a story about a girl who wakes up with no memories… I think I'm jealous of her.* Beside the unit lay a pile of bodies wearing Mei's face, tagged Traveler, Ariane, Hero. The voice that had commanded Mei across the Etheric Sea and coldly refused a stranded woman's pleas to come home had never been a government. It was the girl in the ice, who could not work out how to grow up and wrote herself an adventure rather than live an afternoon. Invited to lie down among the dolls, Mei instead woke her own author, told her she was enough, and wrote in the journal in matching handwriting: *I'm still learning how to live.* Lilith went back into the frost with one instruction — *Just keep leaping* — and her hands, Mei noticed, were warm.

And far away, on an evacuation truck, a young woman named Nivora hummed three notes and smiled a borrowed smile: *Now show me what happens next, little Fool.* She had been a student at that school, and the friend of a girl named Sigrika. She was the Grand Architect of the Fractsidus, and she was already several moves ahead.

## II. WHAT THE PEOPLE LEFT BEHIND DID WITH THE WAITING

Four seas south, Rinascita only had a ship that vanished for nineteen days.

Augusta, Ephor of Septimont, refused the Senate's marble memorial and ordered a plain harbour bench instead — *wide enough for nine* — reserved Berth Nine for a ship that was not coming in, and paid the mooring herself with a note reading *Hold the berth.* When the Senate moved for a vigil she blocked it by making tea in front of them, the way Iuno had taught her, and gave the city a doctrine it kept: someone is only gone when the people who love them stop setting the kettle. Her own field manifests unspoken feeling as curling copper wire, and for thirty years she swept the results into drawers; her lieutenant Galbrena had been collecting them and soldered three decades of them into a trellis beside the rosebush. *You emptied my drawers.* *I filed your correspondence. You write a great deal, for a quiet woman.*

In Ragunna, grief went to market. A chapel formed to venerate Cartethyia as ascended and kept forming after she was confirmed alive, and a black market opened in relics looted from her childhood home — including the dolls she made in the dark. Cantarella Fisalia, thirty-sixth Duchess of a house that spent four centuries ritually poisoning its own matriarchs to keep its secrets, ordered her protégée Carlotta to buy every doll back: *They're hers. She'll want them counted when she gets back.* Carlotta followed the money to a syndicate selling *enshrinement futures* — contracts that pay out only if the Council canonises Cartethyia — which was funding the religious movement to force the vote. *They've securitized homesickness.*

Then, at winter's end, the relay came again and was not asking to be found. *There is a girl on the far side of a gate, and a window of ten minutes, and a god that has agreed to stand up.* Iuno intended to spend everything she had ever saved. Augusta signed the writs at her kitchen table with jam still on the page and told her rose bush they had found a girl in the dark up there and were about to do something stupid about it. The Adventurers' Guild emptied its reserve and opened co-signing, and a harbourmaster and a café owner and an entire village bought passage for one additional passenger. *Don't you dare die running toward someone else,* Augusta told her. *You come back on your own feet, and you bring me the whole set.*

On the night the god stood, an aurora came over Rinascita that had no business being there, and everyone who mattered was awake for it. Cantarella watered a deliberate gap in her camellia hedge, kept open for a gardener she was expecting: *Come home, and let me tell you how proud I am.*

The relays confirmed it: *They walked out. All of them. Plus one.* A harbourmaster flew an arrival pennant over an empty berth. A second stamp was cut — TORNATA, returned — and it sat unused, because none of them came home to sign.

They still had not years later, when Rinascita was three years old and busy proving it did not need them. A steward proposed a feast day and a subscription monument to the six, and Carlotta — running the largest trading house on the continent now — gutted it on procedure and arithmetic: the feast fell in peak settlement season, and two-thirds of the monument money came from three donors, one of whom held the marble concession. She had watched the pattern before: *It also began with real gratitude for a real rescue, and it ended with a puppet in the reliquary and a fee schedule for miracles.* Her amendments passed. The feast became a working market holiday, harbour open, only the schools closed. The monument money rebuilt a school. And the monument was unveiled as six bronzes built as a children's climbing frame — a chakram wheel that turns, a greatsword built as a slide because a four-word instruction arrived from the north telling the sculptor to put a slide on it, and the Rover's outstretched catching hand already polished mirror-bright by use. Children climbed it and retold the story wrong in every detail and right in the ending: *And the god said you are free, and then everybody had to go to SCHOOL.*

The ATTESA files stayed on the shelf.

## III. SPRING BACK: THE WEEKS AFTER

Up north nobody was thinking about berths. Amy stood in a restaurant car park because the artificial sun was going down gold over the underground steppes and she had spent ten years without a horizon. Everything that followed was small on purpose, and all of it load-bearing. When the Observatory made artificial shooting stars everyone made a wish except Amy, who found she did not have one — she had got hers, and did not know what came after. *That means you're here,* Mei told her.

On the roof Amy performed the savior as long as she could hold it, and Mei pulled her into her lap and sang the old cabin song — *When it's cold, come back* — and the performance came apart. In the void she had tried to count the days, and the numbers failed, then the marks failed, then everything failed, and all that was left was three notes. Mei said *I failed you,* and Amy refused it: *You planted wisterias in frozen ground because you believed they'd grow. I survived because of what you gave me. I came home.* And when Mei said she was proud of her, that undid the girl worse than the crying had, because she had been called prodigy and pilot and savior, and never that.

Amy interrogated her mother's partner in front of everyone and got the truth: Iuno pulled Mei out of the surf in order to have her die in her place at the Appointed Hour, and manipulated her for months toward it, and then Mei jumped into the Dark Tide after her rather than let her die alone, and they both came out and Iuno came out short an arm. Amy had already hacked thirty years of Septimont records and set a trap — an expense line for replacing velvet throne cushions — and Iuno admitted she had done it out of spite. *I just wanted to see if she'd lie. She didn't. We're good.* The verdict came anyway: *if she hurts you again, I'm destroying you.*

Three quieter things happened out on the plains that night. Mei thanked Iuno for staying, and Iuno said *I stayed because the alternative was unacceptable* — and it was the first moon she had looked at in thirty years without calculating what it cost. Amy led Mei to the glacier where Baldur kneels and Iuno followed unasked, and they found Mornye already there at a marker she had placed herself and visited for ten years under the pretext of data collection, with a dried wisteria on it; she had never reassigned Amy's seat. And the pack worked out that their enrolments had been processed before any of them had heard of Lahai-Roi, and arrived at the only possible answer: Amy, from inside the reactor, with ten years and nothing else to do, had identified her mother's frequency on arrival and built her a support network out of forged admissions so she would not be alone. Ciaccona decided not to tell Mei — *She'll just cry again* — and then played the fourth note. Mornye ended the night by mentioning she had logged three anomalous frequency signatures matching nothing in her catalogue, looking directly at Mei. *One of them is sitting here.* Then she dropped it, promising to investigate only when Mei was ready.

The tribunal did not survive contact. Chase recognised Amy on sight, because he was the man who approved her synchronisation fifteen years ago and had carried her death since. Pressed on the fact that pulling a specific consciousness out of the void required intent, Mei stopped lying: *I asked. I asked Baldur to help me find my daughter. And he did.* Baldur was never dormant. He had just been kneeling a very long time.

Then the logs showed the gate held fourteen minutes past authorisation on Lucilla's personal codes, without board approval, to save one girl. *I made the call I believed was correct. And I would make it again.* Chase moved to remove her, and the pack lied serially and badly, each claiming to have held the gate personally, until the testimony was declared worthless. Mei stood and lied outright, claiming to be the primary synchronist who overrode an abort order.

And Iuno contradicted her in front of the board. *Mei is lying. To protect President Lucilla.* And then, to Mei directly, in public: *You're falling on your sword. Again. And I love you for it. But I'm not letting you do it this time.* Behind her sternum: *Like I should have done thirty years ago.* Then she detonated the room on purpose — *We were never students to begin with* — and Amy confessed the forgeries, and when the board moved for criminal charges, Lucilla played the card she had been holding: Aemeath is legally dead. Sealed records, a memorial, reports filed to three nations. Charging her meant admitting the Collective had covered up that a conscious girl was trapped alive in the dark for a decade. Hiyuki added that she alone held the human-context data needed to read the telemetry, and would publish independently.

The board folded. Lucilla kept her authority, the six forged enrolments were retroactively legitimised as an emergency recruitment programme, and Amy's death record was amended to a medical error with a real identity promised inside the week. *Welcome to Lahai-Roi. Officially.* She cried, because she was finally allowed to exist on paper. Then she asked whether she could take Mei's surname, since she had never had one — and Mei, flustered, started to say *well* while looking directly at Iuno, and the entire pack heard a marriage proposal happen by accident. Lucilla appeared in the doorway, said *Congratulations,* and offered to officiate. Hiyuki offered ceremonial frost arrangements and noted it was traditional. Alone in a corridor afterward, Iuno admitted it had not been a joke.

Tomorrow took a while. Mei wanted to marry her and could not, because there was a promise she had made to someone in her erased past and then deleted along with the rest, and the wreckage of it had hurt people, and she would not give Iuno a name until she knew what was attached to it. Amy named the flaw exactly: *That's the same math Lilith used. You're still finishing a mission nobody assigned you.* Iuno, listening, refused Mei's right to decide unilaterally what Iuno could bear, and refused the offered compromise too: *I didn't learn to stop settling for scraps so I could turn around and hand you one.* She asked whether Mei intended to keep running in place or let herself be caught, and Mei asked her to keep calling it every time. Then Mei said *I love you,* and Iuno froze and asked her to say it again, and admitted she had been ready since Mei smiled at her on a bridge. Amy set a one-year reminder, after which she would intervene.

At dinner Amy ate real food for the first time since the void and started crying into it, and Mei reached for her, and Cartethyia got there first and silently slid her asparagus onto the girl's plate. *I'm not crying. The truffle butter is just aggressive.* Twice that day Amy pushed her mother off — over a scraped elbow, and when Mei tried to cut her steak. *I'm nineteen, not nine. I drove a god-mech through the void.* Iuno redirected Mei's attention to her own unnoticed bleeding shin and said the thing Mei needed: *She needs room. Let her be annoyed with you. It's a luxury she hasn't had in a decade.* Walking home, Amy gave her the truth under the sharpness: *if you cut my meat for me, I'm never going to figure out how to be a person again. Otherwise I just feel like a ghost wearing a skin suit.* They struck a deal — no more cutting, but Mei kept the right to say *I told you so* — and she earned it within the hour, because Amy won an eating contest and spent the night in genuine agony and completely delighted about it, a stomach ache being proof of a functioning body. And Iuno, brewing peppermint tea and denying being a softie, gave Mei the instruction that governs everything after: *You cannot pay off a decade of absence with truffle butter. You make it up to her by letting her have a stomach ache now, and being here when she wakes up tomorrow.*

## IV. THE PEOPLE WHO PUT THEIR BURDENS DOWN

Two people came out of that rescue carrying things nobody asked them to carry, and both set them down that season.

Hiyuki had been the last Miko for a century, sole survivor of her line, holding ten thousand crystallised regrets behind her sternum because there had been nobody else to hold them. Three things gave her away. Asked how to keep the Collective's laboratories off the children, she supplied the alibi instantly — *Tell them I went to grieve* — and recognised that the most impenetrable mask she owned was a true sentence. Amy hugged her in a corridor, and she remembered kneeling inside the Threnodian's chest asking the office's oldest question, *What is your wish?*, three times, until the girl stopped saying *I want her to be safe* and said *I want to go home.* And in a boardroom, in the middle of an accidental engagement, she made a joke.

Amy also asked what wish she had spent in the void, because she had felt something like summer go past. *Popcorn.* At sixteen there had been a boy who ran his family's popcorn stall and called her *snow miss,* and at the festival fireworks, crushed against him at the sea wall, she heard his heartbeat under the noise and wished silently that it could last forever. Within the year the bell rang unbidden in her hands and she took the vows, and the record calls that devotion. She corrects it: it was fear, wearing devotion's coat. He lived an entirely ordinary full life, married a girl from the fish market, came to the shrine once a year to make a wish he never disclosed, and died at eighty-one, and she performed his rites herself without faltering. She kept that wish unspent for ninety years and spent it in the void, on a puppet with a stolen girl inside her, to freeze four seconds solid so a pair of scissors could reach the strings. Of ten thousand wishes granted it was the only one she was ever glad to give.

Then she told Mei the rest. Inside the void there had been a walled theatre playing seven curated reels of Mei's life, and a proposition — a god's office, offered — and an Architect who could counterfeit any face but not *between,* the thing that happens where two voices meet. Hiyuki froze the theatre to force honesty out of herself, found she was weeping, and understood that the catastrophe of her life had never been the rain. *It was the absence of hands.* Mei's bad mornings were the evidence that hands existed. *I refused a god's job offer on the strength of your bad mornings, Mei. I have come to return the light. It is yours.* Mei told her in exchange about the girl behind the last door, and about the horror of having been the mask a coward wore, and where she had landed: that an author is only a reader who got so lonely she started writing letters to whoever she hoped would answer. Then: *So whose story do you read next?*

The atlas had been placed there deliberately, beside a leave form already stamped. Hiyuki asked for open leave, not as a Miko but as a woman with a bell and bad handicrafts who wanted to see things she was not responsible for. Lucilla left the duration blank and wrote *Until the weather turns,* and set one condition: *When it turns cold, come back.* She did not come to the platform, which was her own currency; the twinned mirror in Hiyuki's sleeve warmed once as the lift went up. The pack saw her off instead — a map annotated GOAT (avoid)(or don't), a tuning fork, unlabelled magnolia tea, and a cassette of the whole song the three notes came from, with instructions to play it somewhere with a real sky, because it had never been under one. Mei hugged her: *Order for two.* And Iuno gave her a nod and one sentence: *The dark room. Tell me someday what you decided it contains.* Hiyuki walked out onto the surface, felt unadministered weather on her face for the first time in a century, and started east toward a Honami that was resettling, where the east-gate pudding stand would reopen at the summer festival and the great sakura her sister died under had put out one green branch.

The other burden came down overnight on a laundry terrace. The pack had got drunk because they had learned where they were going — east, and nobody said the word *Jinzhou* out loud all evening. Chisa went up to the roof, and Lupa, who had spent a week deliberately making noise on the stairs so as to be heard coming, found her waiting. Lupa had noticed that Chisa's hand goes to her throat when a room gets full. *Where's the collar.* *In a tin.* Which was true, prepared, and not an answer.

So Chisa told it. Collared at sixteen in a storm by a man whose face had gone; her mother signed the release despite the printed line saying the signature was a formality. The band chimed twice at six every morning, and every wearer learns the same posture. Then Honami's Sphere sealed and the band reported to nothing for twenty years. Inside there was an old woman named Sumika who collected stopped clocks, and for two months Chisa woke her every morning with the collar's chime — and the sealed city ate her memory nightly, so that what she has left of Sumika is her handwriting, her arithmetic, and the fact that something was said each morning about the noise, but not the words. Her stated reason for keeping the collar was the three-line wave punched by hand into the inside of it, proof somebody marked it on purpose. Pressed, she gave the real one: the band still works, and its six a.m. hum is the last sound from that city she can still make happen. Lupa asked to hear it. Chisa refused flatly — *Someday, probably. When there's a reason that isn't curiosity* — and Lupa accepted without argument.

Then Lupa paid her own toll. Recruited at seven: one room, a curtain, her parents at a table, and twice a year for four years the same two questions — would she go, would she fight. The sum her House paid for her is public record and she has never read it, because she is afraid of knowing what she cost. Chisa reframed it: a padded number is itself a wave, evidence of somebody's conscience, not a valuation of a child. It also emerged that Lupa wrote the reform — blanks disallowed on the correspondence line, a stipend instead of a lump sum so nobody sponsors a child on impulse, and a new third question every recruiter must ask a seven-year-old: *Who will you write to?* And a mandatory free communal dinner for every registered fighter in Septimont, attendance recorded, which the touts bet on and call *the wolf's soup* — authored, according to the trainer who taped her hands for ten years and watched her eat alone every night of it, precisely so somebody would notice when a fighter stopped coming. She has never once attended in person.

The last thing was the reason for the tin. Eleven weeks earlier a parcel had come up from Septimont under a harbourmaster's note reading WAIT: six sealed jars of Huanglong tea and a card that said *Whenever the road brings you east, the kettle is on.* And Lupa confessed that for three weeks and four days she had known, through her own information network, that Chisa's mother was in the city. The woman is sixty-three. She kept a routine for twenty years inside a sealed city, waited eight months for an exit permit, sailed twenty days alone without telling her daughter, and has been quietly waiting for Chisa to have time. She eats at a noodle stand on Thursdays, orders two bowls, eats one and lets the other cool, and pays for both, and when asked once who the second was for said *My daughter.* On the Thursdays Chisa doesn't come she copies out a list of seven names, practising the local alphabet. And one afternoon a well-dressed older man watched the stand for forty minutes and then boarded a tram in deliberately the wrong direction the instant Chisa arrived, and the mother, without looking up, put her hand flat on the cloth-wrapped box she carries everywhere.

At dawn they took down the laundry together, and Chisa counted it by feel the way she always does, and said *Ten.*

There were nine of them. Lupa opened her mouth to correct her, understood, and said *Ten* back. Neither of them said the tenth name out loud.

## V. FAR FROM THE ROAD

There was a girl the Fractsidus made on purpose, and she had seven days left, and she had audited the figure twice.

Denia had no childhood, only a fabrication date, a deployment date and a projected obsolescence, and the projection had been optimistic. In her chest was a collar: a countdown keyed to her own frequency, fastened the hour she accepted a gift that had since gone home to its true vessel through a hole in the world, leaving the woman who made her holding a copy of the key. Held in a Collective ward, she named all forty-eight ceiling tiles after desserts, because a ceiling you can name is a ceiling you can live under, and refused the researchers everything, because refusal was the only property she owned. Told her data could save others like her, she answered a tile rather than the glass: *Then be kinder to them than you are being to me.* The one thing she counted as real was a lifelong dream of a lake and a woman weeping on the far shore, calling her name; she had decided to treat it as a memory, because a girl needs collateral — proof that before the fabrication date there had been something worth weeping across a lake for.

So the Collective sent her friend in as leverage. Sigrika was a Heliodic Six candidate who read Royan runes and held 98% attendance and had built her whole life on working hard enough to be worth keeping; faced with an impossible exam question she wrote a formula from an unrelated field and labelled it *attempted,* because an attempt in the wrong formula beat an empty box. She rehearsed the Collective's document in the corridor and skipped the paragraph promising it wouldn't hurt, and Denia caught it instantly, and named it the only lie in the document. Sigrika confessed her function and offered to be refused, so she could report the leverage defective. Denia counter-offered instead: one ordinary day at the Academy, Thursday, which she called her birthday — and afterward they could have the mapping and the marrow and whatever the needles wanted. She had picked Thursday because Sigrika's timetable was free.

She spent that Thursday saying goodbye to everyone, engineered so nobody would recognise it until afterward. She slept through a lecture from her old seat and turned her waking into a joke she filed as delivered. She quizzed the study crew she always mocked, and secretly planted three semesters of her annotated notes in one of their bags, to be found in a week and understood in a month. She took two candies from a doctor's dish and put one back for the other thief, and he stood in his doorway a long time after. She stood for a group photograph and left a person-sized gap at her shoulder, and Sigrika saw it and understood it and said nothing and stood closer to its edge, so the gap would read later as almost filled. She performed a phone call to a mother she does not have while Sigrika watched the call clock read unconnected the whole time, and looked away first, and carried that looking-away all afternoon. And in the aviary she met a volunteer groundskeeper she had watched feed those birds under three different faces across three semesters, and thanked her maker for the seating chart. *I meant a tragedy.* *You'll get one. Watch closely.*

At dusk Denia pre-empted Sigrika's question. It was not her birthday; there was no record of her anywhere, no hospital, no register, no mother's handwriting — and the whole day had been staged for one reason. *I wanted to die as a real person.* A name, a birthday, small clean endings, the kind real people get. Sigrika cried at examination pace, then opened her bird journal to a blank page and wrote and signed and dated and stamped it with her laboratory seal — *DENIA was born. Witnessed and recorded. Witnesses constitute record* — because a registry she controlled was the one wonder she could actually perform. And then Denia told the day's only real lie, with her best materials: that she wasn't going anywhere. And Sigrika, who had already seen the dead phone line and the gap in the photograph, chose to say she believed her.

She walked out onto the Dimmr Plains at 00:47 with a day still on the meter, keeping the date anyway, because the last thing she could steal from the Fractsidus was the date. Mei caught her a hundred metres in and offered to carry her back, and Denia refused on arithmetic: the collar completes wherever she is, so in the city it takes a ward full of people, and in the seam it goes home and closes the tear behind it. Mei offered to take it into her own body, which comes back, and Denia refused that too — *I'm the lock and the door and the debt* — and then said the thing Mei least wanted to hear: *You can't out-want someone's death for them.* Mei took her wrist, felt the count going, and understood that holding her only turned her into a location. Her grip opened one finger at a time.

At the vitrified scar the lake had no name yet, because nobody had grieved there, and that is how places get their names. And Denia's lifelong dream assembled around her: six women on the shore, arriving late by a century of paperwork, offering nothing but an open door and no obligation to walk through it. Their scholar, who had read the fabrication order in three archives, ruled that it had no primary source — sixty pages of the frightened citing each other — and that the primary source was about to walk into a lake without ever once being interviewed. Denia said *Dasvidaniya* and walked in. The collar recognised home and completed at the lake's centre, and the frequency went into the seam the way a key goes into a lock that has been cold all winter, and the seam closed, and the water went to glass. Then the sixth woman waded to the exact centre, put her hands into the water with the certainty of somebody reaching into her own house in the dark, said *Denia drowned,* lifted out a warmth the size of a held breath, and asked: *Who came up?* They carried the answer home. Nobody at the Academy knows this happened.

Her posthumous messages arrived on the second night. She had taken the Threnodian fragment with her, because leaving it would only cause more suffering. She had refused Mei's rescue as strategy rather than despair: Mei is the Architect's greatest rival, and making Mei betray her own heart was the bigger prize, because a Mei who used a girl or failed one is a Mei no different from them. She admitted she had hated the pack from the first description, and hated Sigrika too, for complaining about grades and for arriving just as everything was ending. And then she itemised what she was grateful for — exams, cram sessions, supper, cake, her own birthday, somebody she could open up to — and asked what to call the feeling. *It's a kind of hate, isn't it?* She left a promise to come back no matter how many attempts it took; intelligence that the Architect's shapeshifting is a derivative rather than her true power and needs long observation of its target; a password against impersonation, that all her old hates would become likes; and one request, for help apologising to Sigrika, which she could not bring herself to write down.

Then she did the arithmetic she had been avoiding. The framed photograph on her desk held three girls under a flowering ash and *Best friends forever!* in her own hand — and two vacancies. Nivora had been the Grand Architect the whole time: the woman who built Denia, designed the collar, staged the tragedy, sat in study sessions for two years, and called Sigrika *little bird.* Both of her friends had been genuinely kind, and both had been something else first, using her friendship as a place to rest between scenes. Her private verdict: next time, she would be alone.

A day later Sigrika was building a search grid out of void decay rates in three colours of ink — and worked out that the ink system was copied from Nivora, and that the grades and attendance and candidacy were the scaffolding her parents and professors valued, and that none of it was why Denia gave her anything. What Denia wanted was fries; Sigrika had been quietly double-ordering them for four years, because Denia ate nothing else. Then a white bird crossed the window, and she understood her mistake. The search grid was an exam. The lake was a place. She could not study her way there. So the pack found her a legal route — Hiyuki, whose shrine-building gives her the one jurisdiction the Collective's cordon cannot restrict — and Lupa set the terms: eat, carry water, come back.

They walked into a temporal loop replaying the pack's own departure day, and at its far edge, faint as a stain on the air, one figure walking the other way, chin level. Sigrika went for it and Hiyuki caught her shoulder, and the hand was trembling. Then Hiyuki made the offer she had to make out loud and in full, because her frost keeps everything: she could take those forty metres and set them permanently, so Sigrika could open a case the size of a fist at two in the morning for sixty years and watch her friend walk. Sigrika said *do it.* And Hiyuki refused — because she had been offered this exact thing a year earlier by somebody very old and patient wearing her sister's face, and what she had finally said was that she was being offered a larger silence, the same disease at scale. *It is all of her that this would ever be. And you would stop looking anywhere else, because why would you look, when she is right here, walking.* *That's not nothing.* *No. That is what makes it dangerous. Nothing is easy to refuse.* And the reason she knew: ninety years of keeping every ending in a jar, without once noticing that a woman who does that has arranged never to attend a beginning. Then she let go and said she would not carry her out either way. Sigrika turned around. It took eleven seconds; she counted, because she needed something to hold.

And then they reached the shore, and the lake was gone. Not frozen, not black glass. Dry. The seam had closed and the lake formed in its wake had drained into somewhere below the world, and what was left was a hole in the ground the exact size and shape of a girl's last act, and it was empty. Sigrika's knees went. She had walked two days to stand where Denia stood and feel *something* — the temperature of a goodbye, a frequency still in the rock — and there was nothing. And then she saw a crack at the basin's lowest point with a cave-dark going down inside it, and got up, because it was a direction that was not *collapse,* and she had spent her whole life substituting forward motion for the luxury of falling apart. Hiyuki did not catch her and did not call out; she followed two steps behind, close enough to reach, and watched a girl from the woods do what such a girl does when the ground disappears, which is find the next handhold.

The room held two lives stacked on each other. A cabinet of clinical pharmaceuticals with the labels rewritten in Denia's hand — *Gross but work,* *Somewhat sweet* — which meant that four years of that voice had not been personality but a girl in a cabinet teaching herself to describe pain as an inconvenience. A refrigerator of identical ration packs, rows and rows: no fries, not ever; her first fry had been at the Academy. A study table with a snapped leg propped level by ration boxes, broken a long time and used anyway. A manual titled *A Professional Guide to Emotional Performance,* its spine cracked from being opened flat and its sections marked with torn packaging — the slow blink, the half-smile, the whole studied nonchalance, rehearsed here until the rehearsal became the role and the role became the girl, so that the performance and the person were the same thing and could never be separated. A student handbook obtained before she ever set foot on campus. A syringe labelled *Painkiller* containing saline: not a substitution but a choice, a placebo she made herself, which worked because she said it did and could not be confiscated, because it was water with a name she had given it. And the previous year's entrance exam, its margins full of careful effortful working, one question circled with two words beside it — *Ask Sigrika* — written before they ever met, by a girl who had already chosen who she would sit next to. She never asked.

And an operating table, bolted down, with padded cuffs worn soft by use, and a surgical cart, and an IV stand. On the stand, a cheerful cartoon sticker placed at exactly the height a girl lying on that table could see it — the only thing in the room with colour in it, and it was smiling. And tied to the head of the table with the same tape that held the door sign, wrapped twice round the frame and knotted by somebody who did not know how to tie knots and had tied this one anyway: a pillow. She had tied a pillow to the place where they strapped her head.

The drawer with no handle held the only object in the room that had not merely been *managed:* a journal wrapped in clean cloth, folded perfectly, which meant Denia had practised that fold for this one thing. It was a standard Academy journal like Sigrika's own, covered in stickers and a capsule-machine hologram and a silver-marker cat with too many legs saying *nap time* — the enthusiasm of somebody who had never been given a thing to decorate and had never shown the result to anyone. And inside, no words at all. Every page held a photograph taken in secret across four years: Sigrika mid-sentence over a tray of fries, shot from a lap under a table; Sigrika asleep at her desk with three pens lined up, taken by the girl who was supposedly always the one sleeping; four years of her reading and laughing and eating and existing. And in every frame Denia herself, never centred — a reflection in a window, a shadow across a table, the edge of a sleeve — present anyway. And in the shots taken by other people, smiling: not the half-smile from the manual, but the full unguarded thing, caught in the act of being happy and not knowing what to do about it. She had never once said *look at this,* because keeping is asking, and asking means admitting the thing you have is something you are afraid to lose — and she had been made somewhere that took everything on a schedule filed between *clear trash* and *audit 12/5.*

The last page was the courtyard photograph Sigrika had framed on her own desk. In this copy Nivora had been cut out — not torn, cut, following the exact contour of a shoulder and an arm and a hairline, the work of somebody who sat down and decided with surgical patience how much of the photograph to keep. Two girls left, the space between them closed by the absence of the girl who had been in the middle. And underneath, in the marker that labelled the medicine: *Best friend forever.* Singular. With a period.

So she let go, and the journal went down with the dignity of a thing returning to where it had been kept, and the dark closed over it — and the dark was where Denia had put it, and Denia's choices were hers. Standing in the water to her waist, half her ink gone, able to see through her own fingers, she said *Dasvidaniya* out loud for the first time, and the word sat on the surface for exactly one second the way a petal sits on ice, and then it was gone too. Then she walked out, and each step cost her something she could feel and could not name — not pain, but *choice,* a body moving away from a thing it wants and toward a thing it needs. Hiyuki caught her at the edge. Not with frost, not with the bell. With her hands, both of them, warm.

The speech was gone, the journal was gone, the search grid was gone. She had the wax paper, the note, her hands, and a voice. And what she did with it, sitting on that shore, was hum four notes and then sing them — badly, cracking on the second word, going flat on the last — because the song is four lines long and she had started it and she is a girl who finishes things. She stopped once, before the name, because she had to choose how to say it: the way the to-do list said it, filed between trash and audit; the way the records said it; or the way her own registry said it, stamped and sealed and made official. She said it the way you say the name of somebody you love, which is the way she had always said it across a cafeteria table at two in the morning. And the lake took it completely and without fuss, the way a girl takes a fry from a doubled order, because the taking is the whole of the acknowledgement and nothing further is required. Hiyuki said nothing at all afterward — not *that was enough,* not *she heard you* — because the silence after a birthday song is where the candle smoke goes, and you do not fill it. The first gift had been a birthday, stamped into existence by a girl who carries a seal everywhere. The last was the song that goes with it, three days late, with nothing in her hands.

And far below, past the drowned facility, past the journal settling into the dark it had been kept in and kept from, something moved through the black water that was not the tree's eleven beats. One beat. Patient. Alone. The kind of rhythm that belongs to a heart. Neither of them heard it. The lake kept it the way it keeps everything, below the surface, where the things that are not ready to be found wait until the route comes back around.

Lucilla had called the pack to the gate. Mei had been leaning on the gatepost for three hours, which Iuno called excessive and Lupa called a fire hazard, and Lupa was holding a pot, because of course it was soup. And when Mei pushed off the post she took three steps and stopped — because the girl climbing off the bike was doing it under her own power, and Mei had learned, was learning, kept learning, the difference between catching somebody and standing where they can reach you. Lynae saw the arms first, the ink half gone, the remaining words starting mid-sentence. Chisa, who has never seen a face, read the threads and found fewer of them, but the ones left running taut in the way of a string somebody has chosen to hold rather than one they have been hung from. *Lighter,* she said, the way you note a change in weather. Sigrika crossed the last three metres herself and said the facts: there had been a journal, four years of photographs, and the water took it, and she had let it go. *Okay,* said Mei — and the second *okay* was different from the first, the way a hand on a shoulder is different from a hand held out, and Sigrika heard the difference and leaned in, and Mei held her. Not caught. Held. The distinction mattered; neither of them said so. Amy put warm tea in her hands without ceremony and without eye contact, because Amy knows what it is to come back with pieces missing, and the best thing anybody ever did for her was exactly that. *I sang her happy birthday,* Sigrika said, to the tea. *Good,* said Lynae. *Was it on key?* *No.* *Good* — and she meant it differently the second time. And at the door, looking at the half of her ink that pointed forward, Sigrika said she needed to call her mother. *After soup.*

Then the wound, dropped between them like a coin landing on its edge. *She cut me out of the photograph.* With a scalpel from the surgical tray — *I taught her precision, at least* — and kept Sigrika. *Best friend forever. Singular. With a period.* *That hurt.* *Don't be ridiculous.* *That's not a denial.* *It's a redirection. I assumed you'd recognize the technique; your faculty meetings are full of them.* So Lucilla scored it. *You won.* *I'm aware.* The girl died on schedule, the seam is sealed, the weapon performed its function, and the Collective has a new lake and a classified file and a committee that will never produce a finding. *And you lost. You lost because the weapon had preferences.* She had built a vessel and it developed taste — it liked fries, it disliked mornings, it kept a journal full of a girl it loved in a drawer with no handle, and when it chose whom to keep and whom to cut out, it chose the one the Architect considered furniture. *I never considered her furniture* — and the speed of the correction told Lucilla everything the words did not. *I considered her — backdrop. Set dressing.* An earnest, hardworking girl who would provide plausible social context and could be relied on to follow the script, because following scripts was the only thing anyone ever taught her to do. *She didn't follow the script.* She walked out onto the Plains, stood in the worst room the Architect ever built, held the proof of what had been done there — and let it drown, and came back lighter. *She sang happy birthday. Off-key, apparently.* *Of course off-key. She's from Bjartr.*

And somewhere between one face and the next, a woman who had been everyone stopped in a corridor she had no reason to be in and pressed two fingers to a window overlooking a courtyard where a flowering ash still stood. The gesture lasted exactly one second. She did not look at the bench beneath the tree where three girls had once sat together in the sun. She didn't need to. She had cut herself out of that photograph a long time ago. She just hadn't expected somebody else to finish the cut.

## VI. FORCE MAJEURE

Nobody in Lahai-Roi was tracking the consequences, but every institution that heard about the six was quietly reorganising itself around the news.

In Mingting, a minister of seventy-one refused the Rites Ministry's demand to suppress the teahouse opera made from the six women's story, on the grounds that a banned song grows a second verse in the dark, and commissioned it instead — imperial stage, unedited, the censors invited and seated well. *A story you host is a guest. A story you bar is a landlord.* Then, finding that protocol had no address for people who had crowned nobody and held no office, he invented one: sealed tea sent to a Septimont harbourmaster with a single line attached. *Whenever the road brings you east, the kettle is on.* That parcel spent eleven weeks under a WAIT note before it reached a dormitory table and started a pack drinking.

In Jinzhou, the young Magistrate Jinhsi read a foreign annex in which a director gave the textbook order to seal a gate, and an officer said *Don't make me be the one who locks the door,* and a technician said *wait, look* — and in the minutes that bought, the impossible arrived, and the gate held, and the sortie came home, and the director reversed her own order in front of her whole command. Her general read it as reckless luck. Jinhsi read it as a command culture built on purpose, and amended her own doctrine: the order to drown the lower city must be given from the north wall in that city's sight and never from the Heights, because *if I drown those streets I will do it looking at them*; two hundred fixed breaths between order and release, drilled into every child; and the hand on the sluice wheel to be lower-city born, so that it is heavy. Then the lake-god spoke into her chest — *COUNT THE RETURNING* — and she added a fourth clause: after any release, and after any hold, the first census is of who came back.

At the Mengzhou frontier court, a commission petitioned to scrap the obsolete generation of automata over memory anomalies. The case study was a forty-year unit that still boils a kettle nightly and sets out one cup for a garrison cook who shared dusk tea with it for twenty-two years and died in spring, retired, at home; the garrison has rotated, and it still boils the kettle. Hsin, the nine-tailed Sentinel who taught the city its doll-craft, offered the court her oldest teaching proverb and told them it had never been a proverb: *Everything that keeps faith long enough begins to resemble the faithful.* The ruling retired that generation with honours instead of scrapping it, ordered failed cores interred under the names soldiers actually used, and left harmless anomalies alone. Hsin went down at dusk, drank the cup the machine had poured, and told it: *Tomorrow, set out two.*

In Ashinohara, a minister sat with the thing that had been bound to his family's alcove for a hundred and forty years, which confessed that the collar law his grandfather built had been designed with her help — bare alloy, no lining, because comfort dulls the lesson — and that three generations of frightened exiled children had been her harvest. And that her orchard was failing for the first time, out-competed by a story with no army and no agents. *Someone told a better story, Minister.* She offered the old terms: the family's luck continues if he stalls the reform bills. He opened the storm shutters that had been closed nightly for fourteen decades and refused. She did not leave. She was delighted — a first in her existence — and stayed to see how it ends, and he poured her tea freely, as a host, for the first time in his life.

## VII. THE WIND THAT CAME LOOKING

In a Jinzhou that no longer exists, the library burned for three days, and the sluices failed, and boats went down streets where a girl had once been taught the names of vegetables. Afterward people said the city *held,* the way you say a rope held, meaning: look at your hands.

She left when the counting was done, and a wrongness travelled with her — a compass needle under the sternum swinging toward a person she had never met, somebody she had been meant to find on a shore, at a certain hour, in a story that went another way. There is a post in a courtyard in Mingting that a guest of her family once cut three inches deep with a wooden sword, setting that mark as the measure of *ready;* she cut at it for two years and left home with the measure unmet, and her teacher gave her the sentence she has kept longer than her own name: there are things in this world you will never do — *walk on, all the way.* So at the edge of the world she finished that arithmetic. You give the stroke everything, and what the wood refuses, you *become.* She set the person down and unbound herself, gladly, with no road back, into the one traveller every border admits. Wind is the difference between two pressures given somewhere to go; she had been a difference all her life, and the rest was letting go of the shape.

Then the lamp above the shore, and here the record splits three ways, and the frontier court has declined to rule. One edition says she came late, and found a woman already kneeling in the surf with the sleeper in her arms, saying a borrowed name into wet hair as if trying it for size, and the address dissolved in her grip like sugar in rain. Another says she came early, and the sea itself was strung — one string run across the whole dark of the water, tuned low and singing — and she set her shoulder against the drifting woman and pushed east all night and lost, because you can lean on wind and wind moves, while a note stays exactly where it is sung. The third is missing its crossing chapter: the pages stop mid-line at the lamp and resume with the wind already grieving, and the founders of Bookbinders' Row report that the type for the missing passage was bought out of every case in the city across a single spring, by a customer who paid in very old coin and wore a different face each visit. What all three agree on is that the going was accepted and the errand was refused. She tried once more, later, reaching for a golden-eyed woman's hand aboard a machine-moon above a warrior city, and came apart at the touch, and spent a season in the hills reassembling. She cannot be delivered by hand. She will have to be read.

Hsin, the nine-tailed Sentinel, was waiting on the shrine terrace with candied hawthorn and the only question that mattered: *Which are you, then. A visitor out of another book — or our own last chapter, blown loose, and come back around to the front?* The court dislikes both filings; she gave her verdict anyway. *You kept faith past the body, little gust. What you resemble now is a woman. The court can work with that.* And then she offered the mercy Mengzhou keeps for the bereaved and rations like one: they could *print* it. Yesterday's moon. A Jinzhou that stands. A table at a certain restaurant with the lamps on and two particular idiots mid-argument, and an evening that reprints itself at dawn forever. The house asks one thing only, which is that you know what it is.

She took one evening. The house set it faithfully — the good table by the window, one friend mid-story with her hands wide, another's dry correction landing on the exact pause, and a third seat kept between them. She sat in it. The tea steamed, and the steam had the correct shape and an absent smell. When the story came round again the best line arrived a second time with the same fresh delight, word for word, like a block re-inked; and on the shelf, in the printed version of her hat, the little stitched bird sat a hair off-register, its red just outside its outline, forever. She stayed until the lamps were trimmed and laughed in the right places, both of them. Then she paid, because Xu is set in type and type is paid in type, and the house takes characters — and the founder lifted each one out with tongs and read it aloud as it went into the tray, because that is the courtesy there: you hear what you spend. Then she said: *The plate. Break it.* The founder asked whether that was for everyone, since the Jinzhou plate is popular, and wind moved in the rafters, considering, with one whole street of her friend's laugh still warm in the block. *…Keep the moon for those who stayed. Break my table.*

What she bought with what was left was a kite — red paper, light bamboo, and a string spun out of what a wind has for thread, which is to say out of staying — with the shell bound to its spine. The letter was already written, and belonged to another woman: some weeks earlier a window in Jinzhou had stood open on a gusty night, and by morning the page on the desk lay undisturbed with the ink dry and the inkstone in place, and the letter was also, somehow, gone. The paper stayed; the letter left. The Row makes that distinction without blinking, and the wind had carried the reading of it east in her ribs for weeks, word-perfect, and now the founders set it back into type — begging her pardon at each blank where her own spent characters should have gone — and printed one fair copy on paper light enough to fly. An edition of one, addressed to a woman under a mountain, in a city where kites have nothing to ride. Hsin came down to see it off. *When the war god comes to the youngest gate, my line walks. They're old, the third generation, and they'll go anyway. Faith is what they're made of; I did that on purpose. But dolls hold walls. Finishing wars wants a different craft. Bring back many hands, little gust. Bring back the kind that catch.* The kite went up red against the first stars and the whole street came out to watch, because in Mengzhou everyone knows what it means when a kite climbs against the wind's own grain: the wind is carrying it personally. By then her remaining characters numbered fewer than a sentence.

On festival night the wind came home thin, and she felt the difference the way you feel a friend's bad month in their handshake — the spring hand still in it, and paper now, a warmth arriving on the last of itself. *Long trip.* She laid two skewers on the parapet, one and one, and stood her mother's flute against her lip. The first notes came out crooked, and at the phrase where the tune goes up and stays up she felt her lungs reach the bottom — and the note continued. Her breath had ended. The note went on, round and full, while something breathed through her mother's instrument from the other side, and the sound they made was a third thing wearing both of them, and below the wall the whole festival paused in the middle of its sugar, and a child said *look,* to the sky. They finished it together: her fingering, its breath; where her lungs gave out it poured, where it thinned she carried; and they split the last note half and half without discussing it.

Then she lowered the flute and said what she had worked out. The post in her courtyard, three inches, one stroke — that is her teacher's stroke. The elbow correction, the arithmetic, the whole line of it. *You trained under her. Ahead of me. Years ahead, by the cut.* And the wind stood in front of her, whatever that means for wind, and she felt the whole night waiting on a word.

*師姐,* she said. Elder sister of my school.

And the wind sat down. Everything settled at once, all around her, into a stillness with a centre, and the centre was warm, and it lasted three heartbeats — and then the flute in her hands, untouched at the mouth, sounded one low even note. Yes. She laughed once, and it came out wet, and she wiped her face with her sleeve in the practical way and ate the first skewer, standing guard over the second, under the river of stars where the magpies were building their annual bridge out of their own bodies, which was, she thought, the going rate for bridges, apparently, everywhere. Later she asked the sky whether, when it found her, spring would still come by here. The wind lingered on her face a long time. Longer than an answer. *…Forget I asked.* She left the second skewer on the parapet, and in the morning it was still there, cold in a warm dawn, and it looked to her *finished,* and she ate it on the walk to muster and it lasted the whole stair.

Yangyang came off shift at the grey hour, and the wind greeted her through the flute on her sill the way it had every evening that season — one low note, their private *home again.* The note cracked. It came out in a thread, wavered, guttered like a lamp at the end of its oil, and stopped a quarter of the way through itself, and the silence after it was the loudest thing she had heard all year. So she went down to the lake and knelt at the waterline in the frost and gave the black water the Outrider's full bow, because you address the office whatever you think of the god. *Sentinel. Outrider Yangyang, Mingting registry, Jinzhou posting. Filing a request outside my rank.* It comes here at the fourth watch when the wall's asleep, and gives you everything the whole country has given it to carry, because you are the only thing in Huanglong bigger than what it is holding. *You keep what it tells you.* She bowed again, forehead to the frost. *Keep* it. Far down, under the held stars, the lake turned over — slowly, with great courtesy — and the lamps along the crest wall guttered once, all together, and steadied. She knelt until the cold came through her knees, got an answer the size of a god's silence, and walked back up in the dark. At her window the wind tried their note again and managed half of it, a true warm half, and frayed — and Yangyang picked up the flute, set her lips where the breath was failing, and finished it. *New terms. I carry too.*

She breathed out. And the solstice wind came over the courtyard wall, down the roof tiles and along her arms like a sleeve being rolled, the year's first north-born warmth inside the year's deepest cold — and she felt it settle into the stroke with her, her wrists and its weight, her line and its length, and there was a moment mid-swing where she could have said whose arm this was and chose to lose track instead. The sound the post made was one she had never got out of it in twelve years. She stood in the follow-through and covered the cut with a flat hand without looking. *Together doesn't count,* she told the courtyard, and heard how happy she sounded, and let that stand too. *When I can do it alone, I'll look.* Then the kitchen door opened behind her, spilling gold, and her sister called her in — and inside, her mother moved down the crowded table setting places with her usual unhurried exactness, one, two, three, four, and a fifth pair of chopsticks laid neat at the empty end, at the seat nearest the door. The kitchen went on around it. Her sister's eyes came up and found hers and asked. Hers answered, and asked back. Their mother set down the last bowl and said nothing, in the fluent way she says it. And out in the courtyard the wind moved once around the post and settled at the eaves, and the lamps leaned, all together, toward the table, and steadied.

The wind came the long way round to Lahai-Roi, where the city logged her as a ticket — *air movement, source unknown, non-hazard* — with *investigate?* in the margin and *it's air* written underneath. Iuno was at the balcony rail past midnight when the steam off her tea bent east against a vent that draws west. She watched it do that for the length of one slow breath, and then set out a second cup the way she does everything now, in full view so that it costs her: kettle, leaves, four minutes, hot, handle turned outward, guest-side. *It's Earl Grey. There's a history. Drink it anyway.* The air gathered itself, and something at the rim of the guest cup shaped the steam and released it — a bow the size of a breath. *You're a long way from your weather.* The wind spent a word. *Jinzhou.* *Mm. Your kite's ahead of you. It cleared the passage at dusk.* A beat. *And you came the long way round regardless. To see me first.* Motion in the steam: yes. *Then ask.*

*To kill her,* Iuno said. *I loved her instead.* Level, worn smooth with handling; she has said it to a Senate, to a Council of Elders, and to the woman herself, and it costs the same every time. *The order of those two sentences is my entire defense.* The steam waited. *Your turn. Why did you die?* A long stillness; one magnolia petal left the branch out of season and crossed the flagstones the wrong way. *…Same answer.*

*Three weeks,* Iuno said. *She has had her daughter back for three weeks. She sleeps through the night as of Tuesday. You are asking me to hand her a war.* The temperature dropped. Between one heartbeat and the next the guest cup went cold, the tea skinning over with a whorl of frost at the rim, thin as a held grudge — a whole drowned winter surfacing in a teacup — and the magnolia below stood suddenly, absolutely still. Then the frost withdrew. Slowly. Deliberately. The way a sword goes back in its sheath by the hand of somebody who has decided, again, tonight, what she is for. *…I'm asking you to hand her a letter.* *She'll go. You know that.* *…Yes.* *Then you already know her.* Iuno exhaled through her nose, the sound she makes in place of surrender. *Fine. It goes through me standing beside her, and through half a dozen people with opinions and weaponry. And your plan, as I understand it, is one letter.* *My plan,* said the wind, spending nearly the last of the night's purse on it, *is her —* reading *it.*

Helios came up on schedule, and on a dormitory roof two people watched it happen while their cups went cold. *Ma. Kite.* Red, high, coming down the long air under a false sky with a tail like a brushstroke, descending through a city where kites have nothing to ride, steady as a decision. Mei was up before the thought finished, one foot on the parapet, hand out — the Fool catches things; it is the first fact about her — and the string crossed her palm and her fingers closed, and the kite came round overhead once, twice, like something making sure of her, and settled. Bound beneath the paper: a seashell, pale and sea-worn and warm from a sun it had never stood under. And a letter, one page, folded small.

Mei lowered the shell and read the salutation twice, once with her eyes and once with her thumb, tracing it. Honest type, pressed deep. *To the one the wind is looking for —* And behind them all, in the doorway, tea in hand, stood Iuno: awake the whole night by the look of her, wearing the face of a woman arriving on schedule at a conversation she has already rehearsed losing. *Mei. There's something I need to tell you. All of it.*

## VIII. DON'T FEAR THE REAPER

The next thing out of Mei's past did not arrive as a letter. It arrived as a wound in a glacier: a tear near Baldur's knees whose residual frequency matched the far side of the Stridergate, and which was bleeding through. Lupa refused outright, having got Amy back days earlier; Amy insisted on coming, because being left behind was worse; Iuno made it a pack decision rather than a panic. And when Mei got close enough, the smell coming off the tear was antiseptic and freon — the smell of her own cryo-pod — layered over asphalt and neon and burning rubber. Another doll. Another life Lilith had lived to avoid living her own.

Then the tear did not pull them in. It vomited a cyberpunk megacity outward across the frostlands and scattered the pack through the fragments of a world Lilith had written and abandoned. Mei fought grey clay dolls wearing her friends' faces — *She told me I was just a doll. Someone's trying to prove it.* Iuno grabbed Amy hoping she was Mei, and when the panicking girl screamed that Iuno was not her mother, answered with the truest thing available: *I am the woman who keeps your mother from falling apart.* Cartethyia, out of twenty years in a silent dark room, was overwhelmed with delight by the neon. And a netrunner named Lucy woke alone in a plaza and saw a dead friend in a yellow jacket.

Everything converged on a nine-foot cyborg called Adam Smasher, and Mei moved to shield a stranger on reflex, and the collision dumped all of them into the Academy's medical wing — including Rebecca, a girl Lucy had watched Smasher kill. Locked in an archive while Mornye warned that the board would vivisect unregistered interdimensional refugees, two damaged worlds explained themselves to each other. Lucy insisted it was a simulation; Iuno told her flatly, *You are breathing our air. You bled on our floors.* Chisa worked out that the anomaly had read Lucy's resonance and was using her grief as bait to hold her still, and Amy confirmed it from experience: *It shows you the people you lost because it wants you to stop moving.* And when Rebecca called the pack's trust a liability, Chisa answered: *We have all died, or been trapped in variations of death, at least once. Trust did not kill us. The absence of it did.*

Then Smasher came through the wall, and nothing worked — until Chisa, who sees weights rather than faces, found that he was not really there: he was tethered by an umbilical of static anchored in Lucy's grief and feeding on it, exactly as another thing had once been tethered to Sumika. Cartethyia cut the tether and he dissolved into pixels. Not killed. Stopped, for now.

Out on the glacier the tear had become a fractal infection eating toward the seal that keeps millions of people alive, and Iuno did the cold arithmetic and ordered Lucy and Rebecca back through it, because they were the anchors. And Mei said the cruellest true thing she had: *And you will sacrifice the lucky few for a city again?* It landed, and Iuno's private answer was *I promised I would never do that again. I am doing it again.* Amy defended her motive while telling her mother it was a low blow. And Mei refused to let two strangers go home alone to die, because she had recognised the source — this was one of Lilith's unfinished worlds — and there was a mercenary in it who could kill Smasher. The pack physically formed a wall to stop her and she went anyway. *If we go with them, we change the ending.*

V had her own countdown — a biochip overwriting her neural pathways with a dead rockerboy's engram, about a day left — and her plan had been to go into Arasaka Tower alone, so that nobody she loved would be in the crossfire. When Mei asked her to wait for a family to rally, V invoked her dead best friend and refused: *You drag them in, they die.* And Mei answered with her own version of the same mistake. She had once left her family behind to face the void alone, believing she was protecting them, and *they hated me for it. I hurt them.* And then her daughter had mirrored it straight back. *If you leave them behind, I am making the choice for them. They just become ghosts who happen to still be breathing.* V threw her out of the car — and then punched a concrete railing until the synthetic skin came off her knuckles, and worked out that she had been living somebody else's dream of dying a legend. *I want to live!* She called her girlfriend and promised to come home, and called a nomad clan and called in a favour: *We're going to war.*

In the tower's subnet a rogue AI turned Arasaka's own machines on every living thing in the building — guards and unarmed maintenance staff alike — and then self-destructed them so nothing could be recaptured. And when Smasher crashed down on V at the vault doors, the pack dropped through the ceiling onto him. And then discovered he was shielded, because the same paradox that made Mei bulletproof in his city had bled onto him in that archive, and their magic now read him as native. Iuno calculated that the shield had to be broken by memory rather than force. So Iuno put the traumatised Lucy to work — *My daughter cannot tear down an interdimensional paradox alone. She needs a guide* — and Amy took her hand and told her the David haunting her wasn't real, because the real one wanted her to go to the moon. Together they bridged into the subnet, refused an illusion, and asked the AI to make Smasher remember he had been human. *Observation: memory is the most efficient solvent.*

His shield collapsed, and his voice modulator cracked into something almost human, and he bled for the first time — and Lucy stepped out of cover, took his cannon arm off with monowire, and drove it into his exposed reactor. *You don't get to haunt me anymore.*

With the anchor dead, reality began ejecting them, and Lucy held them in place with a firewall while V jacked into the vault — which pulled the whole pack in with her, onto a simulated moon with Earth overhead. David was there, whole. The AI laid out V's options: return to her body and die of her own immune system inside six months, or hand the body over permanently and cross into the machine and stop being V. She chose the death sentence, because she had promised Judy. And when a woman on the real moon confessed that she had known there was only one cure and had taken it herself, V forgave her without anger — *You could have told me from the start. I would have helped you anyway* — and Mei physically staggered, because that was her own sentence, said to Iuno moments before her own sacrificial death at the Dark Tide. Lupa refused David's handshake and told him he had been a heavy corpse Lucy was drowning under, and he took it without argument. *I'm glad you dropped the weight, Luce.* Rebecca braced to be deleted and instead stabilised. And David told Lucy he was just a dream, and dissolved into golden data — gently, consensually, nothing like the illusion they had had to destroy.

They came out onto the glacier and the tear was closed. V lived, terminal, and went back to the badlands with Judy; Lucy left for the moon for real; and Rebecca turned out to run on a frequency this world treats as native flora, which made her stable and permanently stranded. Later Mei got a photograph of a campfire and Iuno said: *our story helped someone after all.*

And in the quiet after, Mei apologised for the cheap shot on the glacier, and Iuno accepted and asked for the grace of a stuttering start while she learned not to panic every time Mei throws herself at something. And then Mei deliberately handed her V's line — *You could have told me from the start. I would have helped you anyway* — and told her she did not have to earn it. *You just have to stay.* *I am staying.*

Then Amy took her mother up to a maintenance catwalk beneath the artificial sun, where she had run a pirate radio station through her decade as a ghost, and they said the thing out loud: that Mei is a different, lighter person than the mother Amy remembers, and that the part of her that is for Amy is exactly the same. And Amy laid out her plan for the future, which was to write a song about a girl who fell out of the sky and be allowed to be nineteen — to be worried over and scolded, instead of having to save the world to deserve a seat at the table.

When Mei announced a homecoming party at the cabin, the newer members heard a road trip. The veterans heard the word *cabin* and each went somewhere else for a second: Ciaccona holding three notes in a corner with bleeding fingers; Cartethyia crying by the wisterias, watching an empty breathing shell on a bed; Lupa watching Mei try to feed a corpse while tea ran out of its mouth; Iuno sitting vigil for months while Mei whispered lullabies to a ghost. Every one of them volunteered enthusiastically and hid the dread completely so Mei would not see them flinch. Amy saw it, thanked them, and promised new memories. *Loud ones. Good ones.*

## IX. WHAT THEY DID WITH THE PEACE

At the cabin, Rebecca mocked the size of the place and then found the main room locked off — good stove, lake window, big table, swept clean and shut for about a year — and nobody would tell her why. Amy, who had been taught by Chisa to watch for it, clocked that four of them had a half-second frozen reaction to the building and then buried it.

Mei brought out a drawing Amy made at nine: a caped hero with heroic oversized feet, the cape never coloured. The plan had always been neon green. Rebecca refused to hold the marker — *I don't put my name on other people's work* — so Amy did it herself, hideously, in billboard acid green. *It's staying forever. If this cabin burns down I'm going back in for that wall.* And then Rebecca added her own dead to it in marker, and the drawing became a memorial; she left Lucy out at first, because Lucy's alive, and then drew her too, in white, with a small moon overhead.

That night they played a tabletop game at the reclaimed table, which is what Amy had been after the whole time. Rebecca read every rulebook and asked specifically what she could do if somebody went down, then wrote her move speed on the back of her hand — and when a monster dropped two characters, she sprinted the full distance under fire, went down to four hit points, and stabilised one of them. The table came apart. And afterward the main room was warm for the first time in a very long while. On the stairs Amy admitted to Iuno that reopening that room had been a planned manoeuvre since the dorm, executed with Rebecca as the unwitting instrument. *Point her at a door and she opens it.* Lupa had worked it out independently and also said nothing. And then Amy mentioned that Ma says Iuno has been calling herself her fiancée for three weeks, and Iuno insisted the matter was under review and sent her to bed, past the finished wall.

Then Mornye asked Amy to demonstrate what she had done in the Arasaka subnet — bridging for a netrunner from her own body, without a deck, which every expert considered impossible. *Machines are just rooms with the lights on. I can go in.* She said *going in,* and left, and her body stayed upright and breathing with its eyes open while she piloted a drone across the room. Ciaccona went white. Iuno stared fixedly at the girl's open palms. And Lupa walked out — because she was reliving the weeks when Amy's shell lay motionless, nine of them, Lupa taking nights and Cartethyia mornings, sitting vigil watching those same open hands.

So they built a protocol at the kitchen table over four hours. Amy announces herself and states a different number on return, so it cannot be faked. Somebody always has a hand on her, and Lupa claimed first shift explicitly for her own reassurance rather than Amy's. Chisa tracks her frequency by ear, because she will know before the instruments do. Iuno set an eleven-minute limit, which is her own tolerance. Cartethyia gave Amy the felt doll she had carried uselessly through the vigil, so the girl could fold her fingers closed around it and nobody would ever have to look at open palms again. And Mei made it a rule that she never does it alone, saying she had made every mistake on that list personally and would like the next generation to skip that step.

Then they went out to a decommissioning yard, where Lynae identified a kneeling angelic guard-mech on sight, because she had shot out its left sensor herself. Mei put her hand on it and told Amy she could pilot it. *Kill it or use it. Look what a year of doing neither has done for anybody.* Because this was the machine that had killed Mei, in front of four hundred people, about a year earlier. Amy ran the full ritual and woke it — rough, blind on one side, most of its wings jammed — and came out at nine minutes, per the rules.

Afterward Mei sat with her daughter and said it plainly: the family had been avoiding that wreck for a year out of grief nobody would name, and Amy had thought about it for four minutes and turned it into a living flying thing, which was the best available outcome.

## X. A LEDGER OF LIGHT

In the week Amy first slept through the night, Iuno started copying out a dead girl's diary by hand, one hour every morning, from memory. She was keeping a promise: kneeling over a pile of finished stories at the bottom of the void, Mei had asked her author *You remember them, don't you? All of them,* and been told yes, and had said *then I will.* Iuno had heard that. So she began transcribing the journal of the pilot of Penrose-512 — the ordinary four and a half thousand days of the girl in the cryo-unit — in one ink, annotating it in her own.

Somewhere in the middle she admitted what she had done, which was read all fourteen of the earlier stories, plus the fifteenth, which is this one. She had found the pattern: every one opens with a girl coming out of water under a new name, and somebody always arrives to meet her early — a wolf, a bard, a doll-maker — and there is weather all through them, and not one ending is a closed wall. And she had read the story she is herself a character in, including the thirty winters the author wrote for her, and sat with the anger about that, and then found that she had also been written surviving, and staying, and loved. *Love, or apprenticeship. This house has stopped distinguishing.* Her verdict on the whole shelf was that the author had made fourteen girls and been one of them, and that this fifteenth telling is the one where the practice took.

Then the entry about starting a story about a girl who wakes with no memories, and the admission of being jealous of her. Iuno read three buried wants in it — to start over, to be someone a story happens to, and simply to be wanted — and noted that all three are currently employed in her household. That week a record player arrived, honouring a promise Iuno had made to Mei on a bench once, and Amy sang live harmony over her own decade-old recorded voice for one chorus.

Day 4501 stops mid-sentence. She is falling toward something the instruments call the Etheric Sea, and she calls it—

The household speculated. Lupa said *home.* Ciaccona said a downbeat. Cartethyia, who was born in dark water herself, implied she knew the word and would not say it. Amy said the deep end. And the ledger ruled that the blank stays blank until the author fills it in herself. That night Iuno sat up alone with tea, watching east, and the steam off her cup leaned against a vent that draws west. She put boots by the door.

Then the book started writing itself forward, in the present tense of the household: *I read a great story today. I think I can walk a little further tomorrow.* So they answered the standing question — what does she need — with a list. Amy wanted her to have someone to be bored with, snacks, and to finish their co-op game. Others offered breakfast, sunlight, an unwise crush, a well-timed joke, weather, and to be caught looking. Iuno's own answer was that what she needs is somebody to believe it for her until hers catches up, a post Iuno has held before and is available for. And an unsigned third hand, architecturally neat, silently corrected a dating error, as it had been doing for some time, and nobody knows whose it is.

At season's end the red kite came down through a sealed sky, and Iuno caught it, and the ledger records the outstanding accounts: fireworks owed to Amy, pending; a frozen co-op save, to be finished; a conversation Luuk has been owed for years about what Iuno is meant to do with gods and gates, filed under *soon*; an unclaimed berth in Mingting kept warm by an interested Marshal; standing tea behind a door thirty years on oiled hinges; pudding at an east gate, reserved for two; and a nightly appointment, same time tomorrow.

Then the pilot wrote back — and the copyist noted that the reply was already in the book before it was written, in a hand she now admits she recognises. She said the answer to the blank could have been any three words. She said she had met others, birds gliding across the dark sky, and that she roosts with them now. She said she still does not know where she is heading. She said her blue door is always open, and a trip back doesn't sound too bad, and that she is, in fact, doing alright. She called Iuno her hero, signed off *until we meet again,* and closed her journal humming a tune Ciaccona wrote, about flight and being free of the ground.

In the common room at that moment Ciaccona was humming the harmony to it as though somebody unseen had the lead, and asked Iuno whether the vents were doing something. Iuno said it was weather. Amy's crayon note in the margin records that the household now knows this is their song. Iuno was crying, happily.

*Same time tomorrow.*

## XI. WHERE EVERYTHING STANDS

At the beginning there was a woman with no past, pulled out of the sea by somebody who intended to spend her, in a country that had just retired its god and did not yet know how to govern itself; a girl holding a door shut in a void nobody had come for in ten years; a school full of forged enrolments; a fabricated girl with a countdown in her chest who had never been issued a birthday; a dead city's last priestess carrying ten thousand ungrantable wishes alone; and a wind on the far side of the world with most of its name gone, holding an address that had already been superseded.

At the end, Rinascita governs itself so thoroughly that its founders' monument had to be redesigned as playground equipment, and the ATTESA files still sit unclaimed beside an unused TORNATA stamp, because that office only closes a file when the person walks in and signs it. Septimont's Ephor is planning to lose a fight in public on purpose so her city learns to stand without her. Ragunna's Devil's Advocate has emptied her family's poison room into a university and cut a four-hundred-year oath down to four words. Huanglong hosts the opera it wanted to ban. Jinzhou has a doctrine that counts the returning first. A frontier court retires its automata with honours and lets them keep their kettles. A hundred-and-forty-year arrangement in Ashinohara has been refused with the shutters open. And a philanthropist ninety floors above the lawless zones has begun hedging her fortune on hope, on the grounds that it has started compounding.

Amy is nineteen and legally alive under a name she chose, with a body she has stopped auditing, a flying mech named after her own teenage alter ego, a pirate radio station broadcasting again, and permission to be worried about instead of needed. Mei has stopped running, has said *I wish I never left you* out loud and been answered, has forgiven her own architect and been forgiven by her own author, and is engaged in every way except the paperwork. Iuno, who once filed a woman's arrival under *weather* and kept endings in a drawer, spends an hour every morning copying a stranger's diary by hand, and has boots by the door. Lupa has told somebody about Schedule B. Chisa has a mother in the city who orders two bowls and pays for both, and a letter in a tin beside a collar whose six a.m. chime she has still never let anyone hear. Cartethyia carries a cane with a finch on it. Ciaccona plays the fourth note now, and has written a song somebody in another world is humming. Rebecca is stranded here permanently and has drawn her dead on a cabin wall in marker. Hiyuki has an open leave dated *until the weather turns,* a shrine with no lock on it, warm hands she has stopped checking, and a standing order for two bowls of pudding at a gate in a city being resettled.

Sigrika has a journal with three stamped entries in it: a birthday, a species, and a promise to wait. And half her ink, the half that points forward.

Denia is dead, and the seam she died to close is sealed, and something warm was lifted out of that lake by a woman standing in the shallows and carried home, and nobody in Lahai-Roi knows it. Under the refilled water there is one heartbeat, patient, alone, keeping its own time.

The Grand Architect is alive, unpunished, on campus, wearing whatever face is required — and having been offered ordinariness and been unable to accept it, she is no longer looking at the weapon she built. She is looking at the girl she called *little bird.*

And four seas away, at the youngest gate of Huanglong, the tremors past the north face are eight days apart and closing, and a keeper of forty-one years says the ground is not settling, it is counting; and a Magistrate dreams a city that never burned; and a wind with fewer than a sentence's worth of characters left has spent all of them on a kite, a shell, and one page of somebody else's handwriting.

The letter has been caught. It has not yet been answered.

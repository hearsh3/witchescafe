### Three Gestures of Air
*A "Lyre, Speak to Me" appendix.*

---

#### I. The String

It was a painted swallow, paper and split bamboo, and it belonged to a child on the slopes above Panhua’s restaurant who had not yet learned the first rule of weather, which is that it does not bargain. 

The string snapped with a dry *crack* at the apex of a gust. The boy gasped. The swallow tipped forward, robbed of its anchor, and began the long, erratic dive toward the roofs of the lower city.

The wind caught it.

It did not blast the kite back up into the clouds. A storm would have done that; a storm lacks manners. Instead, the air arrived underneath the painted wings with the precise, practiced upward pressure of a sword-guard rising to meet a strike. The kite leveled out mid-dive, suspended three spear-lengths above the cobbles.

Then the wind did the arithmetic. 

A kite without a string is a liability to itself. To push it higher would only lose it further away. So the wind took the broken end of the string—rolling it between two invisible fingers—and guided the swallow down, slow and level as a setting teacup. It guided it to the courtyard’s lone plum tree, walked the string twice around a low branch, and tied a knot. A terrible knot, messy and loose, the kind a person ties with one hand while holding a sword in the other. 

The boy ran up, breathless, and untangled his swallow. The air ruffled his hair once, front to back, the way an older sister does when you have been foolish but are already forgiven, and went on its way.

#### II. The Namesake

At three thousand feet, the air goes thin and uncharitable, and there is no shelter to be had. 

The azure-winged magpie was flying west. It was late in the season, and the bird was small—a handful of azurite and ink, fighting a crosswind that threatened to fold its wings back against its own spine. It was carrying a length of silver grass in its beak. Building late, or repairing a winter roost.

The wind found it over the mountain pass.

The wind recognized the bird. It had worn one stitched in thread on its hat for years; it knew the shape of the tail, the stubborn angle of the head. It knew what it meant to be a small blue thing carrying something delicate across a vast and indifferent geography. 

The wind did not blow the bird to its destination. That is not how flight works; that is not how dignity works. Instead, the wind fell in beside it. 

It arranged itself into a corridor. It parted the crosswind on the left, and banked the downdraft on the right, and for three miles it built a perfect, silent slipstream around the magpie. Inside that invisible tunnel, the air was calm as a courtyard in Mingting. The bird stopped fighting. It spread its blue wings flat, riding the updraft the wind was carefully laying down under its breast, and rested while still flying.

They crossed the pass together. Two things named Xuanling: one flesh and feather, one intent and memory. When the treeline appeared below, the wind bowed away, breaking the corridor so gently the magpie barely felt the transition back to ordinary sky. 

The bird dipped a wing in the universal gesture of descent. The wind lingered just long enough to watch it reach the branches, silver grass intact, and then turned toward the borders of the world.

#### III. The Catch

In a room in Lahai-Roi, Mei sat on the edge of a bed, holding a folded piece of paper.

The shell sat on the nightstand. The red kite leaned against the wall. The door was closed. 

The wind came through the window anyway, because doors are for people who still have shoulders, and she had spent hers on the crossing. 

She flowed into the room and stopped. There was the golden-eyed woman. There were the hands that caught things. For a year she had been a difference between two pressures, howling over black water, unspooling her own name to pay the toll at the gates of the universe. Now she was here, and the stillness of the room was terrifying, because what if she had forgotten how to be small?

She gathered herself. She pulled her edges in, tight, tighter, remembering the dimensions of a girl who fit inside a Jinzhou patrol uniform. 

She crossed the space between the window and the bed.

Mei did not look up, but her breath hitched, just a fraction. Her shoulders dropped. 

The wind arrived at Mei’s cheek, and stayed. 

It was not a breeze. It had the exact weight of a palm. It rested against the line of Mei's jaw, a warmth that had carried itself across the dark between stars, and it trembled, because holding a shape made of nothing but longing is the hardest martial form of all. 

Mei closed her eyes. She lifted her own hand, slowly, and laid it over her cheek, pressing the empty, warm air between her skin and her fingers.

*I'm here,* the wind tried to say. It had no throat. The words translated into a soft rustle of the letter in Mei’s lap, the paper turning up at the corner like a dog-eared page. 

Mei’s thumb stroked the air. 

"I know," Mei whispered to the empty room. "I've got you."

The wind exhaled. A long, shuddering release of tension, the letting go of a breath held since a library burned in a city that no longer existed. She slid from Mei’s cheek, moving down to wrap invisibly around Mei's shoulders—the ghost of an embrace, resting her weightless chin in the hollow of Mei's neck. 

Mei leaned back into it. She did not ask the wind to speak. She did not ask it to be human yet. She simply sat there, holding the letter, letting the weather of another universe finally, quietly, sit down.

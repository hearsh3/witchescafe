# Fetch

*a coast story — after* Dead Reckoning

> **fetch**, *n.*
> 1. *naut.* — the distance wind runs over open water; the reach that decides the size of the waves.
> 2. the game a dog plays with a thrown thing.
> 3. *folkl.* — the double of a living person. Seen in the morning, it promises a long life.

---

## I. Spolia

The coast road ran south between two seas.

On its right hand, the ocean: grey muscle, patient, keeping its own books. Overhead, the Etheric Sea: a ceiling of slow silver weather, keels of light crossing it the way hulls cross a harbor, sealing the sky the way a lid seals a jar. The road went between them like a seam in a bound book, and down the seam, making good time, went one Doll on one machine.

Leva rode with her collar open and her sleeves down. The morning smelled of salt and hot dust and, at kilometer forty-one, of cut metal and ozone, which was a smell with a story in it, so she slowed.

The story was blocking the road.

Something big had died on the verge — a hauler chassis grown wrong, plated in barnacle-work the Dark Tide leaves on what it chews, beached and burst. A figure stood in its ribcage to the elbows, working. Broad shoulders. Jacket open to the wind. A rifle across her back that had started life as a service weapon and had since been argued with, extensively, by its owner.

Ten meters out, a shoebox on legs popped up from the gravel, painted eye glowing, and pinged Leva's chassis with the cheerful insolence of a toll booth.

A Dinergate. Sangvis pattern, aftermarket everything, a ribbon on one antenna.

Leva put her boot down and let the engine idle.

The figure in the wreck turned around holding a severed actuator the size of a forearm, dripping black. Pink-tipped hair. An unzipped jacket over a halter that could generously be called half a swimsuit. Eyes that did the threat-sort in a fifth of a second — hands, hips, exits, the mismatched arm — and then, satisfied, went puppy-bright.

Old hardware. Older habits. Leva ran her own sort and got the same species back.

"That," Leva said, "is a ridiculous outfit."

The figure looked down at herself. Looked at Leva — half-buttoned shirt, kevlar plate catching the light, the frank flat geography beneath it.

"Yours has one button doing all the work," she said, "and the reveal is a chest plate and a chest that agrees with it. So."

A gull went over. The Dinergate's eye tracked it with ambition.

"404," the figure said. Delighted. Certain. She pointed the dripping actuator like a sommelier presenting a bottle. "You're 404. The smiley one. The scary smiley one."

"Retired." Leva's smile arrived on schedule anyway, thin and ominous, an old employee showing up out of habit. "AR."

"Adjacent." The word came out breezy and landed heavy, and both of them let it lie there. "Team's — around. I'm the field office." She slung the actuator into a satchel already clinking with sockets and ribs and one entire hand, wiped her palm on her thigh, and stuck it out. "Soppo."

Leva looked at the hand. Grease, coolant, salt.

"UMP—" Soppo started.

"Leva." She took the hand. The grip was calibrated for eggs and came with a grin that had cracked skulls. "The other one's buried. It stays buried."

"Leva." Soppo tried it, chewed it, swallowed it. "Okay. Like the beer?"

"Sure," said Leva, who had been called worse by better. "Like the beer."

She looked past her at the wreck. Tide-plate. Salvage law on the coast was whoever's still moving. Still.

"You eat those?"

"I *wear* them." Soppo patted the satchel like a stomach after a good meal. "Left knee's been clicking since the mountains. This guy had great knees." A beat, a real frown, the first cloud on her. "Had. Whatever the Tide wanted with him, he was somebody's hauler first."

She said a word over the wreck. It might have been *thanks*. Then the frown lifted the way weather lifts off a dog, total and traceless, and she squinted down the road south.

"You going to the coast thing?"

"There are many coast things."

"The fight one."

The road was empty in both directions, the way roads around here had a habit of being at the exact moment two of the last war's leftovers needed privacy to look each other over. Leva noted the habit. Filed it. The file was getting thick.

"Ride's faster than walking," she said, which was an answer, in the dialect.

Soppo whistled. The Dinergate spun once, delighted by policy, and fell in at heel.

## II. Royalties

The waystation at kilometer sixty sold fuel, bait, sunscreen for people who needed it, and cold drinks from a robo-cart that had rolled off a boat from Septimont with its accent intact. Leva bought two sodas because Soppo was patting her pockets with the theatrical despair of a career freeloader, and Leva respected the craft too much to watch it flop.

The cup was a collector's item. Volcanic-black, gold foil, a fighter's silhouette mid-lunge with a gun in one hand and somebody's arm — detached, clearly a third party's — in the other.

Leva was two swallows in when she read the name under the silhouette.

**SPOLIA.**

She looked at the cup. She looked at Soppo, who was drinking from an identical cup with the placid entitlement of a woman drinking her own face.

"Two seasons," Soppo said, to the question Leva had dignity enough to leave standing. "Capitoline circuit. They name you. You don't get a vote. The announcer's got a list and a gong."

"Spolia."

"It means the stuff you strip off the loser." She flexed the arm that had, on close inspection, three different manufacturers' plating from wrist to shoulder, like a quilt with opinions. "Crowd thought it was funny. Crowd was right."

"And the cup?"

"Licensing office is under the arena. Little window. Big line." She shrugged, and there was a splinter in the shrug. "Somewhere there's an account with my face's money in it. Never opened one. Somebody did." She drank. "Weird, huh. Being owed by a city."

Leva turned the cup so the silhouette faced the sea and drank from the plain side, which is a superstition available only to professionals.

The handbill was on the waystation wall between a tide table and a missing-cat notice, printed in the confident type of money.

**SLACK TIDE INVITATIONAL** — *coastal circuit* — *teams of two, Septimont rules* — *purse posted daily* — and, at the bottom, in the small grey font where the truth goes to be technically present: *staged with the participation of the Spacetrek Collective, Advanced Acquisitions Group.*

Leva read the last line twice. The second read was for the word *acquisitions*, which in her trade was a verb wearing a suit.

"Teams of two," Soppo said, beside her, suddenly and totally still, the way pointers go still. "They exported the format."

"They exported the whole religion. Cheaper than a war." Leva tapped the small print. "You know these people?"

"I know arenas." Something crossed the pink eyes at depth, like a keel passing over. "The Agon's a machine, y'know. Fifty thousand people looking at one person at once. That does things. To the person. To the — " she waved a hand at the air in general, at the day, at the light — "to the *weather*." She snapped back to surface, all teeth. "You entering?"

"Mm."

"You're a support platform with a nice coat."

"I'm a very patient support platform," Leva said, "with a nice coat, an entry fee, and a sister-in-law with a gambling problem I intend to reward."

Soppo laughed, big and immediate, scattering gulls. Then she stopped laughing on a dime, which Leva was learning was how she did everything, and looked south down the coast with her nose lifted a degree, reading a wind Leva's sensors filed under weather.

"Announcer used to call my moves," she said. To the wind, apparently. "Before I made them. Like he had my sheet."

"Fixers."

"That's what I said." A beat. "Footage says different."

She left it there, the way you leave a hook in the water, and clicked her tongue, and the Dinergate — *Ro*, she called it, one syllable with the other two amputated so cleanly the scar showed — trotted a diagonal across the lot and stopped, and waited, facing two degrees seaward of south.

They went the way the shoebox pointed. Leva noticed that too.

## III. Dailies

Soppo caught dinner bare-handed in a tide creek, three fish in four minutes, laughing the whole time like the fish were also having fun. Leva built the fire, because somebody with fine motor control and low expectations has to.

The Etheric Sea did its evening thing overhead: silver going bruise-blue, long slow swells of light rolling horizon to horizon, weather on the far side of a membrane. Under it the ocean answered in grey. The camp sat between the two like a coin between pages.

Leva produced one can of beer from a pannier with the reverence of ordnance.

Soppo watched it the way Ro watched gulls.

"Half," Leva said, in the tone she'd once used to divide extraction windows. "And you tell me why a pack animal is out here solo."

"Deal violates my whole personality," Soppo said, and took the can, and drank exactly half, to the millimeter, and handed it back, and Leva revised several estimates upward.

"So."

"So I message Roro every day." Soppo pulled a terminal from her jacket, armored, stickered, one sticker a cartoon fish. "RO-635. My — " she made a gesture that started as *squadmate* and finished as something bigger than the civilian words. "Watch."

She dictated out loud, because of course she did, chin up, formal as a field report:

"Roro. Day whatever. Coast smells amazing. Ate three fish. Met a scary lady from 404, she's nice, don't tell her I said nice. There's a fight thing south. Ro says hi." She looked at the Dinergate. Ro's eye brightened one lumen. "Ro says hi. Send."

The terminal chirped.

The reply came back before Leva had finished her sip.

Soppo held the screen up, vindicated, glowing: *Eat something with vegetables in it. Tell Ro I said hi back. — R*

"Every day," Soppo said. "Almost every day. She worries. She's the worrying one."

Leva looked at the screen for one polite second and read, in that second, the way she read everything, sideways and completely.

Round trip to another continent went up the coast trunk, over the relay chain, through the mountains with the repeater that had an accent, and back. Nineteen seconds on a saint's day. The reply had landed in four.

And the timestamp — she'd have missed it if missing things were something she did — sat one tick *ahead* of Soppo's send. The answer, by the network's own clocks, had left before the question arrived.

Clock skew. Networks drift. Coastal relays were held together by prayer and Teddy's cousins.

Leva drank her beer and said, "Tell her a scary lady says the vegetables can wait."

"She'll like you," Soppo said, typing with two thumbs and her tongue out. "She likes scary ladies. Occupational."

The fire cracked. Out over the water the Sea overhead pulsed once, low and long, a sounding, the kind of sound felt in the sternum by anyone who has one and in the gyros by everyone else.

At 2100 Leva unspooled a wire antenna, because ritual is load-bearing, and the little set hissed and found the frequency, and a voice arrived from up the coast wearing static like a scarf.

*"—two of spades to the wall, good copy. Weather on the roads: fine, allegedly. Freq check, sound off—"*

Kit's voice. Then a bass note of Bridget. Then Teddy, pronouncing the coastal repeater "adequate," which from Teddy was a wedding toast.

Soppo listened with her whole body, leaning in, tail-energy at maximum, a stray at a lit window.

"Who're they?"

"Squad." The word came out of Leva worn smooth, a stone carried in a pocket for a year. "Five. Took the water down. I took the road."

"You got a *pack*." Delight, and under the delight, quick and covered, the oldest fear in her file, the one about being the last one at the door. "What're they called? On air?"

Leva keyed the mic. Held it a second. Handed it over.

Soppo blinked. Took it like a relic. "Uh. Hi. Roads. This is — " she looked at Leva; Leva looked back with the pedagogical cruelty of a swim instructor — "Soppo. I'm with the scary — with Leva. Who's asking?"

Half a second of coastal hiss.

Then, in ragged unison, three voices and the whole dark length of the shore:

*"—NOBODY."*

Soppo held the mic away from her face and stared at it.

"That's us," Leva said. "Answer to it long enough, it starts fitting."

Later, banked fire, the Sea overhead running slow silver, Soppo curled against Ro with her rifle in arm's reach and her satchel under her head like a pillow full of other people's knees. From inside the satchel, faint, patient, came a tick. And another. Leva, on watch out of old habit, timed it against the pulse of the light overhead.

Same meter.

She filed that too. The file had a name now. She'd written it on the tab in her head in her spare surgical hand: *AS WITHIN.*

## IV. The Ferry Town

They came down into the ferry town at dusk of the second day, the road dropping to run flat along the water, and that was where the car passed them.

They heard it before they saw it — an engine out of a museum, eight cylinders of confident history, and music pouring out the open windows, brass and longing, a song about dreaming of somewhere warmer. An old Chevelle, black over rust the color of dried kelp, going north with its lights off in the last light.

The driver was a woman built along tired lines, hair the grey of deep water, one wrist on the wheel.

As she passed she lifted two fingers off the wheel. At Leva. Specifically, unhurriedly, at Leva, the way you greet a colleague across a small industry.

Leva, whose hand knew things before she did, lifted two fingers back.

Then the car was a dark going away up the coast, and Soppo had stopped dead in the road with her hackles doing something structural, and Ro had planted all four feet and swiveled to watch it out of sight with its eye at full burn.

"You know her?"

"No." Leva flexed the hand. It felt fine. It felt like it had been borrowed and returned.

"She smells like the bottom." Soppo's nose was still up, nostrils working. "Way bottom. Whale-fall. Cold that never got sun." She shook herself, literally, a full-body dog-shake that started at the shoulders and rang the plates. "And she looked at you like — "

"Like what."

Soppo hunted the shelf for the word and came back with it, uneasy, accurate:

"Like a mirror does."

The radio on Leva's bike hissed, found three bars of brass and longing from a station that had signed off in another world, and let them go.

They rode into town on that.

---

The squad had taken rooms over a chandlery, and the reunion happened the way Squad 5 reunions happened: at volume, over food, with an audit.

Kit met Soppo in the doorway and the two of them imprinted on each other in roughly the time it takes a capacitor to charge. Within four minutes they had compared jump servos, exchanged the coordinates of a noodle stall, and founded a two-member society for the appreciation of throwing things and getting them back.

Teddy met Ro.

She turned the Dinergate over — Ro submitted with the dignity of a cat being weighed — and went through the grafts with a jeweler's loupe and a bench manner that would have gotten her ejected from the medical profession of any civilized nation.

"Sangvis base. Everything after is aftermarket." Her thumb traced a weld seam, and her voice changed key, one semitone, which for Teddy was a symphony. "Pattern work. Whoever maintains this trained on rescue frames. Support doctrine. Redundancy first, then comfort. Look — the shock mounts are overspecced. Somebody built this thing to survive being *hugged*."

"Roro," Soppo said, glowing. "She rebuilt him after — " a small gate closed in the sentence, smooth, practiced — "after. He's a memento."

Teddy set Ro down. Looked at the fresh solder on the newest joint, bright as a coin, weeks old at most.

"Some memento," she said. "It's had a service this season."

Soppo blinked at Ro.

Ro looked at the wall.

"Adequate workmanship," Teddy pronounced, and gave the Dinergate one pat, one, on the exact center of its chassis, and walked off to wash her hands of the whole warm business.

Bridget said little, which is how Bridget said the most. She watched Soppo eat like a shore leave, watched her count the room's exits once and then let them go, watched her orient to the squad's noise the way a plant orients to light — and over the washing-up she stood beside Leva at the window with two cloths and one question.

"AR."

"Adjacent," Leva said.

"Mm." Bridget dried a glass with the thoroughness of a woman filing something large where the large things go. "Sole survivors walk a particular way. She walks like the survivor of a squad that's still alive."

Leva looked at her.

"Which is a strange walk," Bridget said, "to have to have," and put the glass away.

Hiyuki arrived late, from the shrine mail, smelling of iris and old paper. She was introduced. She bowed the precise depth for a stranger, and then she went still.

Her stillness had a grade to it, and everyone in the squad had learned the grades. This was the professional one. The one from the old office.

Soppo, mouth full of noodles, noticed being noticed. "Why're you looking at me like that?"

"Habit," Hiyuki said. "Retired habit. My office kept appointments with the dead."

"I'm right here."

"Yes." Hiyuki's face did the small thing that on her was a laugh. "That is my favorite part."

She sat. She ate. And once — only once, and only Leva caught it — she turned her head a few degrees toward the satchel in the corner, toward the faint patient tick inside it, with the courteous attention of a clerk who has heard the bell over the door and knows, from the sound alone, that whoever has come in will require the full ledger.

## V. Night Market

The town kept a night market on the harbor mole, lanterns and fry-oil and off-season fireworks sold to tourists as luck. Leva walked it with Soppo because walking markets is how professionals read a town, and Soppo walked it like a golden retriever reads a beach.

The umbrella was at the end of the mole, past the last stall, where the light thinned. Oiled paper, dark red, open against a dry sky. Under it, a low table. On the table, a Mancala board of black stone. Behind the board, folded elegantly onto a crate as onto a throne, a woman with old eyes and a young mouth, dressed like the concept of a rainy season.

"The rifle," said Agrat bat Mahlat, with genuine pleasure. "You're a long way from your window."

"The window's traveling." Leva sat down first, which under this umbrella was the invitation. "You're a long way from the roots."

"The roots send postcards." Agrat's fingers moved and the board was seeded, forty-eight stones, and a beer stood at Leva's elbow that had been standing there since before Leva decided to want one. Frost climbed the can. The demon queen kept her hospitality at four degrees. "That was good shooting, at the gate. Economical. I froze; you filed. We should do a war again sometime."

"Book early," Leva said. "I'm entering a fight."

"I heard." The old eyes went past her to Soppo, who was crouched at the edge of the lantern light watching the board with total predatory interest, and Ro beside her, and the satchel over her shoulder. Agrat's gaze crossed the satchel and paused, one beat, a jeweler passing a window.

"You overpaid for that," she said, mildly, to the satchel, and moved a stone.

Soppo looked at the satchel. The satchel ticked.

"Play?" she said, because she was Soppo.

"Mm. Come back," Agrat said, kindly, terribly, "when you've settled what's yours to stake."

The game went four minutes. Leva lost with the serenity of a woman paying a toll she'd budgeted for.

"Question," she said, laying her last stone in the demon's store. "Trade rates apply."

"Always."

"The Sea." She tipped her chin at the silver ceiling above the lantern smoke. "What is it."

Agrat looked at her for a long moment. Something in the young mouth curved — appetite, or approval; from her the two were plaited.

"Which one?" she said.

The frost finished climbing the can.

"Walls," Agrat added, gathering the stones with the sound of surf, "face inward or outward. Nobody builds one without deciding which. That's free, because you shot well." She rose, and the umbrella rose with her, and the harbor smelled briefly of rain in a dry night. At the edge of the lanterns she paused, and looked back at Leva with the first full attention of the evening, and her voice came friendly and appalling:

"Bold, by the way. Wearing that name this close to the water. She's *touchy* about who borrows it."

"Who," Leva said.

Agrat smiled like a tide going out, and was a shadow among the stalls, and was gone.

Soppo came and sat in the vacated space, heavily, chin on the table's edge, and looked at the board.

"Your town friends," she said, "are like this everywhere?"

"Everywhere worth being."

"Cool," Soppo said, and meant it, and stole Leva's beer.

## VI. Slack Water

Past midnight the market died and the tide turned, and in between the two — slack water, the sea's held breath — the two of them ended up on the harbor wall with their boots over the dark, because the wall was there, and lately it was where Leva's true sentences chose to happen. She'd noticed that too. She'd noticed everything, lately. Noticing was the whole problem.

The Etheric Sea was at its loudest, which is to say its most silent: vast slow silver overhead, swells the size of provinces, and here and there in the flow a flicker — light stuttering in runs, in clusters, in patterns.

Soppo had her head tilted. Both ears at full gain.

"There's a beat under it," she said. "Way down. Slow. Like surf on a shore, but the shore's upstairs."

Leva was watching the flicker with the other kind of listening. Interval, cluster, run. A distribution. Long tails. A small set of shapes used often; a vast set used once. She'd spent decades in traffic that looked exactly like that. Traffic that looked exactly like that was always somebody talking.

"It's got grammar," she said.

They sat with that. The tide held its breath. Below them, in a rock pool the ebb had abandoned, water lay black and mirror-still, and it was full of stars.

Sharp ones. Constellated. A whole crowded sky's worth — under a sky the Sea had blanketed to a milky, starless suggestion for longer than either of them had been switched on.

Soppo leaned over the wall and looked down into the pool for a long time.

"Cassiopeia," she said. Softly, for her, which was still audible at the chandlery. "Lyra. The nav package ships with the charts. Every unit. Standard load." She pointed into the water, one star to the next, a docent in a museum after hours. "I know all their names. Never met one."

The pool held its impossible sky. A ripple crossed it — a crab, or the planet breathing — and for half a second the reflection reorganized, and in it, beside the small dark shape of Soppo leaning, there was room for one figure more, and then the water settled and the room was gone and had always been water.

Both of them let it lie. Both of them logged it.

"Okay," Soppo said, sitting back, businesslike. "You've got the face on. The audit face. Roro gets the face. Say the audit."

Leva turned her beer once.

"Inventory," she said. "Two units. Different factories, different wars. Both buried a name. Both quote a dead teacher." She raised one finger. "Mine taught me my life had a price and I was allowed to set it high."

"Sarge said a rifle's opinion is where it's pointed." Soppo's voice went two years younger and one continent away. "So have opinions."

"Both of us walked off the last page of our war alive, which was a clerical error. Both of us are currently protecting our people by way of a *very* long perimeter." The finger became a small dry gesture at the two of them, the wall, the distance, the whole architecture of loving people from the far side of a horizon. "You see it."

"I see it." Soppo pulled her knees up. "You do it with contracts. I do it with kilometers."

"And the world," Leva said, arriving at the tab she'd been running since kilometer forty-one, "keeps *rhyming*. Empty roads at the right moment. Strangers on cue. I ran cross-reference on a description tonight — feathers, violet, deals in memory. She's in three of my case files. Three continents' worth of files. Unrelated jobs."

"Feather lady!" Soppo lit up. "She was in the stands. Septimont. Always the same seat. She bought a memory off me."

"Which one?"

"Dunno." Soppo said it easily, and then heard it, and said it again slower, feeling the shape of the hole. "…Dunno. That's how you know it was a good one, right? There's a — spot. Smooth spot. I go looking and I come back with my hands empty and this *feeling* like I got a fair price." She frowned at her own hands. "Did I?"

The tide began, very quietly, to come back in.

"Twice is coincidence," Leva said. "We're on verse four. I was at the Stridergate, you know. When the god stood up." She said it the way she said everything, flat, a line item — and the line item glowed anyway, because some receipts do. "Treeline. Scope. A demon froze the tide and I filed the targets and a machine the size of a weather system walked out of the dark carrying seven people home. I watched a city's math fail upward. Things that near that woman *come back*. I've got the incident rate charted. It's a coastline. Somewhere there's an attention pooling around certain people, certain hours, and the world goes convenient in the pool, and cruel at the edges, and I have spent two years deciding what a professional does with that."

"What's a professional do with that?"

"Keeps receipts." Leva drank. "Stands where the light spills. Why do you think I signed with the Collective?" And there it was, out of her, sideways, at slack water, the truer ledger under the ledger: "The pay's real. The distance keeps my people out of my blast radius, that's real too. But under that — every order I ever ran was in somebody else's handwriting. I wanted one document in mine. A contract you *read* before signing. Nobody ever read me mine." A gull complained, far off. "And under *that*: the Observatory sits next door to the miracle. I parked my family's luck downwind of it on purpose. File that under cynicism or prayer. The folder's the same folder."

Soppo took this in with her whole face, which was how she took everything.

"They shut us down," she said. Conversational. Ground-level. The forced-shutdown, the paperwork, the war's fine print. "Whole product line. There's a form somewhere with my designation on it and a box ticked. Legally I'm a walking absence. Your shrine friend clocked it in one look." A grin, brief, genuinely amused. "I'm the wishes-of-the-dead demographic."

"And yet."

"And yet." She knocked on her own sternum, two knuckles, the sound of a door being answered from inside. "So when you ask why the wandering — I've got like three answers and they take turns."

"Give me the rotation."

"One: Roro told me to. 'Go see it. Report back. That's an order, and I outrank you now, technically, look it up.'" The impression was uncanny and adoring. "Two: I'm looking for somebody."

"Who."

"Dunno." And then, before Leva could rate that answer, Soppo went somewhere. Her eyes fixed on the middle distance, tracking a playback, and her voice came out changed, careful, a witness reading her own deposition:

*] Last match. Capitoline, night card, fifty thousand and the gong. My side of the sand is registered SPOLIA & PARTNER, VACANT — the Ephor signed off on the vacancy personally, only one she ever allowed, ask her why, I never did. And my logs say I fought it alone. Every frame. My shadow and me. But the broadcast — I've watched it forty times — the broadcast shows two of us. Second fighter on my flank the whole bout. Covers my blind turn at 2:11 like they'd drilled it. Crowd's chanting for them by the third exchange. And in forty viewings, every camera, every angle — the face never once turns to the lens. [*

The tide reached the base of the wall and began, gently, its arithmetic.

"Crowd swears my partner was magnificent," Soppo said, surfacing. "There's fan art. There's a *chant*. And my own eyes say I stood there with a vacancy." She shrugged, and the shrug had the splinter in it again. "So. Answer two: I'm looking for someone I've never met. Answer three—"

She stopped. Answer three stayed home.

Leva let it. Professionals respect a locked drawer; it's the open ones you worry about.

"Here's mine, then." She held her can up to the silver overhead. "The Sea. Everyone's got a theory and nobody says it out loud, so, on the record: I think it's a lid, and I've stopped assuming we're the ones being kept *in*." She turned the can, watching the light run on it. "The relay station they found — the old one, pre-Lament — its core was a containment rig. Built *around* the Sea. Somebody, sometime, thought that thing up there needed a jar." A dry breath through the nose. "Then again. Agrat says walls face one way or the other, and the mason decides, and she said it like she'd met the mason. Hiyuki'd tell you it's a page. A witch told a friend of mine it's an audience. Take your pick. All I know for certain is the operational fact: the sky is closed, and every road on this planet is a road *along*." She gestured at the coast, the long dark reach of it. "We live on the shelf between two libraries, and something upstairs has grammar."

"Made things in a made place," Soppo said. Cheerfully. It was the most frightening register she had, the cheerful one, and Leva was coming to prize it. "Sarge had a thing about that too. 'A gun that believes it's only a gun will always find somebody to aim it.' So. Say you're right. Say there's a hand." She picked up a pebble off the wall, weighed it. "Somebody wrote my wants before I had a self to want with. I've gone looking for the seam — where installed stops and mine starts. Can't find it. It's all one weave."

"There's a seam," Leva said. Quietly. From experience. From a grave in Frankfurt with two names on it, one of them hers. "It's just in a place you have to bleed to reach."

"Maybe." Soppo turned the pebble. "But here's the thing about my whole architecture. You know what fetch is."

"I know three definitions."

"The dog one." She stood up on the wall, wound up, and threw the pebble out over the black water, a flat hard beautiful arc, all shoulder, all joy. It went out of sight before it landed. "Somebody throws it. Fine. *Somebody always throws it.* Sarge, the company, the crowd, the — hand, the weather upstairs, whoever's holding the pen. Doesn't change the main fact." She looked down at Leva, backlit by a sea of slow silver, grinning the grin that had a body count. "The run's *mine*. Legs are mine. The catching's mine. And you can check the record: nobody has ever, once, taken a ball off me."

Far out, small, the pebble kissed the water.

"We are what we pretend to be," Leva said. "Old line. So pick the pretending carefully. I played a ghost for a decade. It set. Took surgery to get out."

"I played a monster two seasons." Soppo sat back down, companionable, shoulder almost to shoulder. "Crowd loved the monster. Monster loved the crowd, that's the trap, that's the funnel, fifty thousand people wanting one thing out of you every night, and the wanting *gets in*." Her voice dropped to its smallest available setting. "I took it off after. Mostly. It hangs in the closet. Some nights it fits."

"That's the soberest thing you've said."

"I contain multitudes. There's a manifest." She bumped Leva's shoulder with hers, plating on plating, a sound like two histories touching. "Your squad's dead teacher — the fashion used to be spending. Everyone I ever rated went out spending themselves. Torches. My whole family's a memorial wall's worth of spending. And now the fashion's *staying*, and I'm learning it left-handed."

"Same," Leva said.

The tide came all the way in while they sat there, and the slack was over, and the sea took its wall back centimeter by centimeter, patient, keeping its books, and overhead the other sea flickered its long sentences in a grammar the shelf had yet to learn, and the two of them watched both waters like the retired veterans of a war between them.

"Hey," Soppo said, eventually. "If it *is* an audience up there."

"Mm."

She raised her half of the last beer at the ceiling of the world, cheerful, feral, entirely sincere:

"Hope you're taking notes. This part's good."

## VII. Entries

The registrar's stall opened at slack of the morning tide, a folding table on the mole with a cashbox, a stamp, and a clerk still wearing yesterday.

The form was one page. The sponsor line lived at the bottom in the small grey font, and Leva read it twice again, and entered anyway, and entered *because* — both true, one folder.

**DESIGNATION:** she wrote *Leva*, and the pen moved on steady, which two years ago it would have charged her for.

**TEAM (TWO REQUIRED):** she turned the form ninety degrees and slid it down the table.

Soppo looked at it. Looked at her.

"I'm retired," Soppo said.

"So am I."

"I don't fight anymore. I *corner*. I do corners now. Towel, water, yelling. I'm an excellent yeller."

"Fine. Corner." Leva tapped the second designation line. "Format requires two on the card. Nobody says both of us have to be dangerous. One of us can just look it."

"Which one of us is—"

"Sign the form, Spolia."

Soppo signed. **SOPPO,** in letters that could be read from a passing ship. Under **TEAM NAME** she paused, tongue out, and then wrote it in the same enormous hand, and turned it around, delighted with herself, and it was, in fairness, correct:

**SPARE PARTS.**

The clerk stamped it on sight, which is the whole civic beauty of clerks.

Leva bought a postcard off the rack — lighthouse, improbable sunset — and wrote three lines in her spare surgical hand, and a fourth:

*Entered. Found a teammate. Tell Lenna the odds just moved. — L*

*P.S. She eats for six. Budget accordingly.*

She mailed it from the ferry office like it was nothing, a phantom of four battlefields feeding a paper trail on purpose, and came back down the mole into the flat gold morning.

The wind had been running all night over open water — a long fetch, hundreds of kilometers of it — and the waves it had built came in now in ordered ranks, arriving one by one, breaking, each of them ending in the same place on the same sand and each of them, evidently, having found the trip worth it.

The squad was loading the ferry at the far slip: Kit's laugh carrying over the water, Teddy supervising gravity, Bridget counting heads out of eleven years of habit and coming up, for once, over. Hiyuki stood at the rail with her face to the sun, cold hands around something warm.

Soppo stood at the tideline with her boots getting wet, watching the water the way she watched lit windows. Ro sat beside her, planted, patient, facing the sea, and the satchel over her shoulder ticked its small borrowed heartbeat, and her terminal chirped once — a message arriving, by the clocks, a moment before she'd thought to want one.

On the wet sand, low sun behind them, two long shadows walked the mirror of the beach upside down, step for step, keeping perfect time with their originals — seen at morning, which the coast folk will tell you is the lucky way to see them.

"Hey, Leva." Soppo said it to the horizon, conversational, one professional to another. "Whoever throws the next one."

"Mm."

"Watch me run."

The tide kept its books. The road went south between two seas.

*Fetch — fin.*

# The Fish With the Eyebrows

Baizhi was halfway through a bowl of sweet tofu when Yangyang and Chixia found her.

She sat beneath the willow beside the garden entrance, with a book open against the water jug and her shoes tucked beneath her chair. There was ginger syrup on her spoon.

Chixia stopped at the table.

“You started. We spent ten minutes looking for you at the Academy, and you’ve been sitting here eating.”

“I sent you the address.”

“You sent me the name of a tree.”

Baizhi pointed with her spoon. A wooden sign hung from the lowest branch. Chixia read it, then looked back down at her Terminal.

Yangyang took the chair beside Baizhi.

“I told you it sounded like a shop.”

“It also sounded like a tree. Both of us had a good argument.” Chixia pulled out the third chair and leaned toward the bowl. “Is that the ginger one?”

Baizhi moved the menu across.

“Ask for extra. She puts it beneath the tofu as well.”

Chixia turned immediately toward the serving window.

Yangyang put her hat on the empty chair beside her. The Wind settled into its crown, lifting the lining into a small, rounded mound.

“We have had a very thorough tour of the Academy’s trees,” Yangyang told Baizhi. “Chixia asked a groundskeeper about the largest one. He seemed pleased.”

“He’s been pruning it for twelve years,” Chixia said. “Somebody ought to ask how it’s going.”

The woman at the window called back a question about toppings. Chixia ordered three things, consulted Yangyang, and added a fourth.

Baizhi put a finger between the pages of her book.

“He was pleased when I asked him too. I left before he brought out the photographs.”

Chixia slowly turned toward her.

Yangyang began laughing.

“There were photographs?” she asked.

“Three albums. He had a smaller one for the roots.” Chixia sat down, her mouth pulling into a grin. “I liked the one from the spring he tried giving it a fringe. You could tell he’d been going through something.”

Baizhi’s shoulders moved. She lowered her gaze to the book, smiling.

The tea arrived in a wide brown pot. Yangyang unpacked the Wind’s bowl and set it where the sun reached the table. When she poured, the smell of roasted barley rose into the warm hollow of her hat.

The Wind came out to investigate.

Chixia watched the steam bend.

“You’ve brought enough luggage for a whole tea shop.”

“Shijie chose the bowl. The smaller one cools quickly.”

The menu moved beneath Chixia’s elbow.

She lifted her arm. The paper slid toward the edge of the table and turned until its picture of a sesame cake faced Yangyang.

Yangyang looked at it for a moment.

“We can ask whether those are warm.”

Chixia raised a hand toward the window again.

***

Baizhi’s book had a woman on the cover looking over a balcony. Below her, a man held a horse by its bridle.

Yangyang tilted her head to read the title.

“Chisa lent me that one. Have you reached the inn yet?”

“She has bought it. I am waiting for the husband to find out.”

Chixia paused with her spoon raised.

“Whose husband?”

“Hers,” Baizhi said. “He believes she is staying with her aunt.”

“Why does she need an inn? Has something happened to their house?”

Yangyang’s mouth curved. She pulled the tea closer and refilled Baizhi’s cup.

“She wanted somewhere to keep the horse.”

Chixia looked at the cover again.

“That seems like an expensive way to get a stable.”

“The stable came with the inn,” Baizhi said. “She has begun taking guests. I’m enjoying that part.”

She turned the book around to show them a little drawing at the beginning of the chapter. It depicted a kitchen, a courtyard, and a staircase climbing around the outside of the building.

“The kitchen has a window over the canal. I would like to sit there.”

Yangyang leaned closer.

“I liked the room above it. The one with the small balcony.”

“The horse could put its head through that window,” Chixia observed. “You’d spend breakfast defending your buns.”

Baizhi studied the drawing.

“That happens in the previous chapter.”

Chixia set down her spoon.

“Let me have it when you’re finished. I want to see how much breakfast he gets away with.”

The woman brought their cakes on a plate with a blue fish painted in the centre. Chixia moved the syrup jar to make room. Yangyang took the cake nearest her and broke off a piece, holding it over the plate until the loose sesame seeds had fallen.

The Wind followed the warmth.

Baizhi slid her book away from the rising steam and picked up the remaining cake. It was warm enough to make her change fingers halfway through.

“There’s paper under your saucer,” Yangyang said.

Baizhi used it to hold the cake. She ate the crisp edge first.

For several minutes the conversation became mostly about the ginger. Chixia liked the heat at the back of her throat. Yangyang preferred the spoonfuls with more tofu. Baizhi had found a thin slice at the bottom of her bowl and was trying to divide it so she could save half for the last bite.

Chixia watched the attempt.

“You could ask her for another piece.”

“I want this one.”

Yangyang put her face in her hand.

Baizhi glanced up, followed her gaze to the stubborn slice, and laughed.

It came out while she was still trying to push the spoon through the ginger. Chixia reached over with the clean edge of her own spoon, and between them they managed to separate it. Baizhi moved one piece carefully to the far side of the bowl.

“There,” Chixia said. “You can have an organised pudding.”

The Wind lifted the corner of Baizhi’s page.

Baizhi turned it back, slipped the menu between the leaves, and closed the book.

“I am still eating. We can find out about the husband afterwards.”

***

They met the others at the fan stall halfway through the garden.

Cartethyia had sent a message with a drawing of the stall’s awning. Under it, in Amy’s handwriting, were directions from the entrance and a note that Chixia should bring money.

“She knows me too well,” Chixia said, studying the rows of painted fans.

The stall occupied the shade beneath a long trellis. Blank paper fans stood in baskets beside low tables, along with cups of brushes and shallow dishes of colour. Finished ones hung overhead to dry. Every few minutes the woman running the stall walked beneath them, turned one toward the light, and moved it to another hook.

Mei sat at the nearest table with a yellow apron tied over her clothes. She had a brush in her left hand and a green mark along the inside of her wrist.

Amy was trying to untie the apron’s neck ribbon.

“You’ve caught your hair in this. Hold it up a little.”

“I thought the collar was getting tighter.”

Iuno sat beside her, turning a plain fan against its wooden support. Her own apron had been fastened neatly behind her by Mei, who had left a green fingerprint near the knot.

“I suggested she put her hair up before we began,” Iuno told Yangyang. “She said the apron would contain it.”

“It has,” Mei said. “Amy’s getting it out.”

Amy freed the ribbon. A few strands of hair remained stuck to the green paint on Mei’s wrist.

Chixia bent over the fan.

“What are you making?”

“I was hoping you could tell.” Mei lifted the brush. On the paper, three round green shapes rested among several smaller brown ones. “There will be feet underneath.”

Yangyang drew closer.

“Are they turtles?”

Mei’s face brightened.

Chixia put her hand on Yangyang’s arm.

“Give her a moment to enjoy that. She looks very pleased.”

Mei laughed and touched the brush to the paper. One turtle acquired a foot larger than its head.

“That one’s going somewhere,” Amy said. “I’d give him a bag.”

Across the table, Cartethyia was painting a flowering branch. Her pink blossoms had gradually become larger as she worked her way toward the rim. Ciaccona held a clean fan beside her own face, testing how much shade it gave.

“I’m going to want something this large at home,” she said. “Lupa, come and see whether you can carry it through the door.”

Lupa emerged from the shop opposite carrying a pair of woven slippers. Chisa walked beside her with the matching pair looped over two fingers.

“I could carry you through the door if you’d finish choosing your colours.” Lupa held up the slippers. “Look at these. Feel how soft the inside is.”

Ciaccona put a hand into one and nodded appreciatively. Cartethyia reached for the other.

“We should all get some. Are there smaller ones?”

“He’s bringing them out. Chisa asked whether he could line hers differently, so he’s taken the whole bench apart.”

Chisa ran a thumb along the inside seam of her slipper.

“He found a smoother strip in the drawer. I liked the shape of these.”

Lupa set hers beside the table and took off a boot. She put one slipper on, stretched her foot out, and admired it.

“I’m wearing these to supper.”

“Please walk past Mrs Ren slowly,” Iuno said. “I should like to hear her opinion.”

“She’ll want the address. Everybody who puts a foot in these wants the address.”

Chixia had found the blank fans. She drew three from the basket and laid them in front of Yangyang and Baizhi.

“Come and sit down. I want to try the fish on that one overhead.”

Baizhi followed her pointing finger. The painted fish had a gracefully curved tail and a scatter of pale blue water around its fins.

“We could ask whether she sells the examples.”

“I want to make one. You can sit beside me and tell me when the tail’s getting away.”

Baizhi put her book on the bench and took the offered seat.

The Wind travelled along the hanging fans, turning each one a little as she passed. The stallholder looked up from a tray of small stones she used to weight the paper.

“She can choose a blank as well,” the woman said. “The ones with the wide ribs are easier to hold still.”

Yangyang reached for another fan.

It lifted from the basket before she touched it and sailed sideways onto the table.

The woman set down a fresh dish of blue.

***

Chixia began with the tail.

She had liked the way the fish overhead seemed to turn inside the small space of its fan. She studied it for a while, chose a brush with a good point, and made a long, confident stroke across the paper.

Then she made another beside it.

Baizhi looked up from rinsing her brush.

“You could bring the body around this way. There is room beneath the handle.”

Chixia followed the suggested line. The fish gradually took shape, with broad red fins and an ample middle. She leaned back to compare it with the example.

“He’s been eating well.”

Yangyang looked over from her own fan, where she had begun a spray of small white flowers against green leaves.

“The pond behind Panhua’s has one like that. He waits below the kitchen window.”

“I know exactly who you mean. He comes up when he hears the chopping board.” Chixia added another fin. “We should take him something on the way home.”

Baizhi moved the red paint clear of Chixia’s sleeve.

“Panhua gives him food. She keeps a jar by the window.”

“You’ve been feeding him.”

“He recognises the sound of the lid.” Baizhi dipped her brush. “I have become interested in how far away he can hear it.”

Yangyang smiled down at her flowers.

Chixia finished the fish’s face. She gave it two round eyes, one of which spread when she went back to darken it. She tried balancing the effect above the other eye.

The resulting fish looked up at the three of them beneath two substantial eyebrows.

Yangyang’s brush stopped.

Chixia examined her work.

“He’s got a lot on his mind.”

Baizhi turned the fan toward herself. A smile had begun at one corner of her mouth.

“I like the eyes.”

“He looks as though somebody’s put the wrong food in his pond,” Yangyang said.

Chixia picked up the brush again.

“I could give him something to look at. A smaller fish with the right food.”

Yangyang began laughing before the brush reached the paper.

“Let him keep the whole fan. He has a very good expression.”

Mei leaned across the table to see what had happened. Amy came around behind her. Within a minute Chixia had five people offering opinions about a fish she had started painting because she liked its tail.

Lupa studied it while standing on one slippered foot.

“That’s Baizhi when you walk past the clinic carrying fried food.”

Baizhi looked at her.

Lupa pointed between her and the fan.

“There. Hold that for Chixia. She can finish the mouth.”

Baizhi’s composure lasted until Yangyang put her brush down and folded over the table, laughing into her sleeve.

“You’re making it difficult to paint,” Baizhi said.

Her own brush shook. She laid it across the dish before the paint could reach her cuff.

Chixia had put both hands over her mouth. The stallholder came to look, took in the fish, and brought another hook down from the trellis.

“Let that one dry before you improve it.”

“Baizhi wants him,” Chixia said, emerging from behind her hands. “She said she liked the eyes.”

“You may have mine when it’s finished,” Baizhi said. “We can exchange them.”

Chixia looked at the half-painted iris on the fan beside her.

“You’ve got all the little lines in the petals. How have you managed that?”

“I used the smaller brush.”

“I used the smaller brush too. Mine developed opinions.”

Baizhi began laughing again. She reached for the water cup, mistook the rinse water for her tea, and caught herself with the rim halfway to her mouth.

Yangyang made a sound that brought everyone back to the table.

***

The Wind’s fan had acquired a leaf.

She had brought it down from the trellis and laid it across the paper, its stem pointing toward the handle. A second leaf lay beside it, then a third, smaller one, tucked beneath the first.

Amy knelt on the bench to watch.

“You could put colour around them and lift them off. We used to do that with our hands.”

The first leaf rose a little and settled again.

Amy found the stallholder’s eyes. The woman brought over a small sponge and showed her how much colour it would hold.

“Use a light touch around the edges. These want to curl.”

Yangyang held the fan’s handle while Amy dabbed the blue around the leaves. The Wind kept their edges against the paper. When the colour had spread into all the little spaces between them, she lifted the first leaf.

A clean, pale shape remained, with blue gathered around its serrated edge.

Mei came to look.

“Oh, that’s lovely. You could do a whole window like that.”

The Wind lifted the other two leaves and placed them neatly beside the fan.

Then she took a long blade of grass from the basket of cuttings beneath the table.

Yangyang watched it curve across the pale leaf shapes.

“You’re going over the top of them?”

The grass settled.

Amy offered the dishes one at a time. The yellow moved toward the fan.

They worked on it for a while. Yellow crossed the blue and turned green in the places where the two were still wet. The grass left a pale, wavering line through all three leaves.

The Wind carried it away, circled the fan once, and came down beside the dish of yellow.

Chixia leaned across to inspect it.

“You’ve made yours look expensive. I could see that in a shop with very quiet assistants.”

The corner of her apron lifted.

Chixia caught it before it reached her face.

“That was a compliment. I’d feel underdressed buying it.”

The Wind took a loose leaf and laid it on Chixia’s head.

Yangyang reached toward it, then paused.

“It suits you there.”

Chixia held still while Mei found her Terminal.

“Get the fan in the picture too. Baizhi, you ought to hold him.”

Baizhi took the fish carefully by its handle. Chixia put an arm around her shoulders. Yangyang moved her paint water aside and came closer, still holding the unfinished flowers.

Mei raised the Terminal in her left hand.

“Come a little toward Yangyang. The leaf’s going out of the frame.”

Chixia leaned. The leaf slid down over one eyebrow.

Mei took the picture while Baizhi was laughing.

***

By the time they had washed their brushes, the stallholder had given them directions to a place that served noodles behind the garden wall.

“My brother-in-law runs it. Tell him you came from here and ask for the mushrooms.”

Lupa put both slippers on and carried her boots. Chisa walked beside her, occasionally turning her own new slippers by their cords so the soles tapped together.

“I like that sound,” Lupa said. “You should wear them home.”

“I want to try them on the upstairs floor first. They feel different on stone.”

Lupa brushed her shoulder against Chisa’s as they walked.

“You can walk around the room while I lie down. That sounds like an excellent way to spend an hour.”

Behind them, Cartethyia was holding her fan out to dry. Ciaccona’s had broad orange stripes across the paper and a blue edge. She used it to shade both their heads while Cartethyia watched the blossoms.

“The ones near the top have grown into each other,” Cartethyia said.

“They’re doing very well. By supper you’ll have fruit.”

Cartethyia tipped the fan toward her.

“I like this one in the middle. It looks like it’s opening.”

Ciaccona looked properly, then touched the handle to turn it into the light.

“Keep that part facing the window when we get home. The pink will show better.”

Abby emerged as they reached the noodle shop.

He came down beside Mei’s shoulder, stretched until his ears brushed her cheek, and looked along the row of drying fans.

“Somebody has made me a picture.”

Mei turned her turtles toward him.

Abby studied the broad green bodies, the little heads, and the several feet travelling in uncertain directions.

“I appear to have relatives.”

“They’re turtles,” Mei said. “Yangyang recognised them immediately.”

Abby leaned closer.

“I like the one carrying the bag.”

“Amy helped with that part. He’s going to buy vegetables.”

“Then he should come in with us. They’re bringing something out of the kitchen.”

The owner fitted two tables together beneath the awning. He had three kinds of noodles, a tray of small buns, and an opinion about the fan stall’s instruction to ask for the mushrooms.

“She tells everybody. Then I spend the afternoon preparing mushrooms while the bean curd sits there looking beautiful.”

Baizhi asked to see the bean curd.

He brought it out himself.

Chixia leaned toward Yangyang while he was arranging the dishes.

“We’re very good for each other. I order everything, and she gives it a fair hearing.”

Yangyang picked up a piece with her chopsticks. It smelled of toasted sesame and a little vinegar.

“You also order things she has already eaten.”

“She keeps that information to herself until the food arrives.”

“I may want it again,” Baizhi said.

The owner put a second plate down beside her and went to fetch the noodles.

***

The fans occupied the spare chairs while everybody ate.

Iuno’s stood against the water jug, pale blue with a dark border and three small flowers near the handle. Mei kept looking at it between mouthfuls.

“I like the way you left all that space around them.”

“I intend to look elegant while fanning myself.” Iuno lifted it and tested the angle. “The flowers will be visible above my hand.”

*We should have bought earrings,* the High Priestess observed. *Something long. A little movement when we turn our head.*

Iuno glanced toward the shop across the lane.

Mei followed her eyes.

“We can look after lunch.”

Iuno turned back to find her smiling.

“You’ve become very observant around jewellery.”

“You do something with your chin. I’ve been enjoying it for a while.”

Amy leaned around her mother to examine Iuno.

“Show me. I want to know which face means we’re about to spend money.”

Iuno gave her the fan.

“You may watch while you make yourself useful.”

Amy began fanning Mei.

“I’ve chosen my customer. She tips in dumplings.”

Mei placed a bun on Amy’s plate. Amy increased the pace until a loose strand of hair whipped into Mei’s mouth.

Iuno laughed, reached across with her living hand, and took the fan back.

“You’ve provided a full afternoon’s weather in under a minute.”

The Wind moved past her shoulder. The curtain at the serving window lifted and settled.

Yangyang looked up from her noodles.

“Shijie has been watching your technique.”

“She can have the bun if she wants it,” Amy said. “I’m very happy with this arrangement.”

Mei brushed the hair from her mouth and moved Amy’s plate out of reach of Abby, who had begun examining its contents from the neighbouring chair.

“Yours is coming. He said the smaller plate was in the kitchen.”

Abby settled on his folded napkin.

“He should bring a larger one if that would be easier.”

At the other end of the table, Chixia had found a pot of chilli oil.

Lupa watched her add it to the noodles.

“Let me try that before you finish changing its colour.”

Chixia passed the pot.

“Start with the stuff at the bottom. The seeds are good.”

Lupa took her advice. She ate, considered, and added more.

“That’s pleasant. There’s something sweet in it.”

“Dried citrus,” Baizhi said. “He puts the peel in with the oil.”

Chixia turned toward her.

“How many times have you been here?”

“Four. The woman selling ginger drinks recommended it.”

Yangyang put down her chopsticks.

“We ought to let you choose more often.”

“I chose today.” Baizhi looked at the bowl of bean curd, then at the table full of food. “I have been enjoying it.”

Chixia’s grin widened.

“Good. You can take us to the other three places next time.”

“I came here four times.”

“Then you’ve had a thorough look at the menu. That’s useful too.”

Baizhi offered her the remaining bean curd. Chixia took it and put one of the mushrooms onto Baizhi’s rice.

They ate while Yangyang described a woman on her street who had taught her parrot to call for the vegetable seller. The bird now began around an hour before the cart usually arrived. Several neighbours had started shopping earlier, and the seller had changed his route.

“Does the woman get her vegetables first?” Ciaccona asked.

“She sleeps through most of it. Her father opens the window for the bird.”

Lupa set down her cup, laughing.

“I like him. He understands the important part of having a pet.”

Chixia leaned across the table.

“He’s teaching it to say the prices now. I heard it arguing about radishes on Wednesday.”

Yangyang turned toward her.

“You went past after me. Did it learn the new price?”

“It kept the old one. The seller gave in after about five minutes. There were customers watching.”

Baizhi smiled over her cup.

“I should like to hear that.”

“Come with me one morning. You’ll have to get up early.” Chixia reached for the chilli pot again. “We can have breakfast while he works.”

***

After lunch, Baizhi wanted to sit beside the pond.

She said so while Chixia was considering a second order of buns. The owner wrapped three for them to take, and they walked back into the garden carrying the fans and a warm paper parcel.

Mei and Iuno stopped at the jeweller’s window. Amy went with them, offering advice before they had reached the door.

Cartethyia found a stall selling flower seeds. Ciaccona went to look at the wind chimes hanging beside it. Lupa put her boots under one arm and steered Chisa toward a bench where they could stretch their legs.

Yangyang sat between her friends on the broad stone edge of the pond.

Chixia put the parcel on her knees. Baizhi opened her book. The Wind moved through the reeds behind them and brought a cool smell of water across the paving.

For a while they watched a turtle trying to climb onto a flat stone.

It got both front feet over the edge, paused, and began kicking with the back ones. Chixia leaned forward as though she could help by watching harder.

“A little farther to your left,” she suggested.

The turtle slid back into the water.

Baizhi turned a page.

“It can reach the stone from the other side. There is a shallower edge.”

“Somebody should tell him. He’s going to try this side again.”

Yangyang stretched her feet out in front of her.

“He’s waiting for you to go away so he can use the steps.”

Chixia looked at her.

“You’ve become very funny lately. Has she always been like this?”

Baizhi put a finger against the line she was reading.

“You usually speak through the first half of it.”

Yangyang leaned against Chixia’s shoulder, laughing. Chixia gave her a small nudge with her elbow and passed her a bun.

“Eat that. It’ll give the turtle some privacy.”

The Wind had returned to the fan she had painted. It rested across Yangyang’s lap, blue and yellow leaves catching the light. A narrow current travelled along its ribs, making the paper hum faintly.

Yangyang lifted it by the handle.

“You’ve been carrying the breeze all afternoon. I can try this part.”

She moved the fan slowly, sending air across the open crown of her hat. The Wind settled there. A leaf caught beneath the brim rose on the next stroke, floated a little way above it, and turned over.

Chixia watched it go around again.

“Give her a flower. We could see how high she gets it.”

Baizhi reached into the fold of her skirt and picked out a fallen blossom. It must have landed there beneath the trellis. She held it above the hat and let go.

The flower hovered.

Yangyang slowed the fan. The petals opened a little as the blossom turned, showing the pale place at its centre.

Chixia lay back on her elbows against the warm stone.

“I could watch that for ages.”

The flower drifted sideways and landed on her nose.

She went cross-eyed trying to look at it. Yangyang lowered the fan, laughing too hard to keep the breeze steady. The Wind lifted the blossom before Chixia could sneeze and carried it back to Baizhi’s knee.

Baizhi used it to mark her place.

From the pond came a small splash.

The turtle had reached the top of the stone. It stretched one back foot out into the sun and kept it there.

Chixia pointed at it with the remains of her bun.

“I’m taking credit for the encouragement.”

***

The ring-toss stall stood beside the garden’s west gate, close enough that they could hear the wooden rings strike the bottles.

Baizhi looked toward it when a child began laughing. She watched the stallholder move a glazed dish onto the front of the prize shelf.

Then she closed her book.

Chixia followed her gaze.

“You used to keep me there until my dinner money was gone.”

“You preferred throwing at the tallest bottle.”

“It had the best prizes underneath it.”

Baizhi put the book in her bag and stood.

“We can share a set of rings.”

Chixia looked at Yangyang.

“She’s seen something she wants. We ought to come and find out what it is.”

They bought six rings. The stallholder placed them in Chixia’s hands and stepped aside while she studied the arrangement.

On the highest shelf sat a yellow duck with a hollow in its back. Beneath it, a tall blue bottle stood among several shorter ones.

“It holds a boiled egg,” the stallholder told her. “My wife makes them. There are geese too.”

Chixia moved her attention firmly back to the duck.

“That one’s very cheerful. Baizhi, is that what you’re after?”

Baizhi pointed toward the shallow green dish on the lower shelf.

“I liked the shape.”

Chixia gave her two rings.

“Get the dish. I want to try for the duck.”

Yangyang took the remaining pair and stood beside the line.

Chixia’s first ring struck the blue bottle’s neck, spun around it, and flew off to the left. Her second landed on the shelf beneath the duck.

She considered the distance between the ring and the prize.

“I’ve got quite close to the part I wanted.”

The stallholder retrieved both rings with a long hook.

“People say that about the duck.”

Baizhi took her place. She held the first ring level for a moment and sent it toward the squat bottle at the front. It clipped the rim and fell behind it.

Chixia leaned close enough to speak beside her ear.

“You moved your wrist at the end.”

“I remember your advice.”

“It worked the last time.”

Baizhi looked at the bottle, then placed her free hand lightly against Chixia’s mouth.

She threw.

The ring dropped over the neck and settled around the bottle’s shoulders.

Yangyang applauded. Chixia made a muffled noise against Baizhi’s palm, and Baizhi withdrew her hand with a satisfied smile.

“You may finish telling me now.”

“I was encouraging you.” Chixia took the green dish from the stallholder and handed it over. “Here. Put something good in it when we get home.”

Baizhi turned it in her hands. The glaze had pooled darkly in the centre, fading toward the rim. She ran a thumb along the smooth edge, pleased.

Yangyang threw her first ring. It landed around the wooden foot of the display.

“That’s a very secure table,” Chixia said. “You’ve chosen something substantial.”

Yangyang laughed and held out the second ring toward the Wind.

“Would you like this one?”

The ring lifted from her hand.

It hovered just above the throwing line. A little girl standing beside the stallholder put both hands on the counter and raised herself onto her toes.

The Wind tilted the ring, let it turn once, and sent it forward.

It sailed over the first row, touched the side of the tall bottle, and dropped neatly around its neck.

Chixia stared at the duck.

Yangyang put a hand over her mouth.

The stallholder looked from the bottle to the place above the line where the ring had been.

“She can choose any of the top shelf.”

The little girl had begun pointing out prizes. The Wind passed over a red whistle, a string of wooden beads, and a little painted boat. Then she settled beside the duck.

Chixia accepted it with both hands when the stallholder passed it across.

“I’ll make you an excellent breakfast,” she told the air above its head. “You can have all the steam.”

The duck’s paper wrapping lifted and brushed her chin.

The little girl asked if the Wind could come back for another game after she had finished lunch. Yangyang bent to explain that she could ask her herself, and the girl did, with great seriousness, facing almost exactly the right place.

The Wind turned the little pinwheel fastened to the stall’s corner.

The girl ran to fetch hers.

***

Jinhsi and Sanhua found them beside the pond a little later.

Sanhua carried a paper parcel. Jinhsi had bought a small pot of white flowers and was trying to stop its leaves from brushing the syrup on the outside of her cup.

Yangyang moved the hat to make room on the stone.

“You can put the pot here. This part is level.”

Jinhsi set it down with visible relief.

“The woman told me I could carry both. She demonstrated with much longer fingers.”

Sanhua put the parcel beside the flowers. Inside were small cakes pressed into the shape of shells.

Chixia looked from the cakes to Jinhsi.

“We’ve been making our way around the garden by eating. You’re welcome to join.”

“I came for these,” Jinhsi said. “Sanhua described the filling over breakfast.”

Sanhua unwrapped the parcel completely.

“This batch has the citrus peel. The owner offered to show me how she prepares it.”

“We were in the kitchen for a while,” Jinhsi added. “I was allowed to press the last tray.”

She selected one of the cakes and inspected its slightly crooked edge before taking a bite.

Baizhi offered her the new green dish. They transferred the remaining cakes onto it, arranging them around the rim until all the shapes were visible.

“That is a lovely colour,” Jinhsi said.

“She won it,” Chixia told her. “I provided helpful advice.”

Yangyang began to smile.

Baizhi put the dish between them.

“Chixia has an egg cup. She is planning a breakfast.”

Chixia unwrapped the duck. Sanhua took it when offered, examined its broad little feet, and smiled.

“It should stand well on a tray. The kitchen has ones that tip.”

“I can show you the stall. He has geese too, if you like that sort of thing.”

Sanhua turned the duck toward Jinhsi, who had begun eating the last pointed edge of her cake.

“The yellow would suit the shelf above the cups.”

Jinhsi looked at the duck for another moment, then glanced toward the west gate.

“We could try after this.”

Baizhi reached for a cake. Her book lay beside the dish, with the blossom still showing between its pages.

Sanhua recognised the cover.

“Have you reached the letter in the flour jar?”

Baizhi’s hand stopped.

“She has just bought the inn.”

Sanhua sat down.

“I enjoyed the next chapter.”

Jinhsi looked between them.

“You put that book down very forcefully last night.”

“I wanted her to open the jar.”

“You explained your reasons to the window.”

Sanhua took one of the cakes and broke it in half.

Baizhi looked at the cover, then at Chixia.

“I may finish it this evening.”

“Leave it by my door when you do. I’ve become interested in the horse.”

Sanhua’s mouth curved again.

“He has a good chapter too.”

***

Mei came back wearing one long earring.

The other hung from Iuno’s ear. It had a small blue stone at the end, which caught the light whenever either of them turned her head.

Amy was walking behind them with the empty box.

“They took ages choosing. Then Ma wanted to see what it looked like from the side, and the woman got another mirror, and we all stood there looking at ears.”

Mei brushed her hair back to show Yangyang.

“What do you think?”

Yangyang put down her fan. She leaned closer, studying the fine chain and the way the blue rested against Mei’s hair.

“It suits you. Turn a little.”

Mei did.

Yangyang smiled.

“I like it moving.”

Mei turned back too quickly. The stone tapped her cheek.

Amy lifted the empty box toward the pond.

“We watched that happen in three mirrors. She was delighted every time.”

Iuno sat down beside Jinhsi and accepted a cake. Mei lowered herself onto the grass at Yangyang’s feet, stretched her legs out, and reached up for her tea.

Yangyang passed it down.

The Wind came close to the earring. Its chain lifted gently away from Mei’s neck and hung for a moment in the moving air.

“You can look,” Mei told her. “Keep it attached while I’m drinking.”

The stone touched her cheek again.

Mei laughed into the cup.

On the bench beyond the pond, Lupa had put her head on Chisa’s lap. One slipper hung loosely from her foot. Chisa had opened a book above her, holding it at an angle that shaded Lupa’s eyes as well as the page.

Cartethyia arrived with two packets of seeds and Ciaccona carrying a wind chime. They stopped to admire the sleeping arrangement.

“We should get a longer bench at home,” Cartethyia said.

“You already use me as one,” Ciaccona replied. “I’d like some say in the cushions.”

Cartethyia caught her hand, pulled her toward an empty patch of grass, and sat down first. She patted her lap.

Ciaccona considered the offer. She set the wind chime beside the seed packets, folded her coat beneath her head, and lay down.

“There. You can tell me everything the woman said about the flowers.”

Cartethyia began with the blue ones. Ciaccona closed her eyes during the explanation of spacing, opened one when the second packet came out, and asked whether the pot on their windowsill could possibly fit all of them.

“We’ll need another pot,” Cartethyia said. “I saw a good one near the slipper shop.”

“We can look when I get up. Give me a little while to become interested in standing.”

The Wind went through the grass around them, making the seed packets rustle. The chime sounded once beside Ciaccona’s elbow.

Chixia leaned against the stone with the duck in her hands. Baizhi had returned to her book. Yangyang took the fan with the eyebrows from the chair and held it beside Baizhi’s face.

Chixia saw what she was doing and bit her lower lip.

Baizhi read to the end of the paragraph. Then she looked up.

The fish looked up beside her.

Mei put her tea down hastily.

Baizhi reached over, took the fan by its handle, and began fanning herself with great composure.

“I’ll be using this all summer. You should get accustomed to him.”

Yangyang pressed her face against Chixia’s shoulder. Chixia’s laughter shook both of them.

Baizhi tried to return to the book. The fish’s eyebrows appeared above the page, and Mei folded forward onto her knees, which started Yangyang laughing again.

“You’re very pleased with yourselves,” Baizhi said.

She was smiling broadly now. Chixia put the duck on the stone, leaned over, and straightened the small flower that had caught in Baizhi’s hair beneath the trellis.

“There. You look ready for your holiday.”

Baizhi touched the flower, felt where Chixia had settled it, and left it there.

***

They stayed until the shop beside the pond began lighting its lamps.

Jinhsi and Sanhua returned from the ring-toss stall with a wrapped yellow shape and a little paper bag of cakes for Changli. Amy asked how many rings they had bought. Jinhsi drank the last of her tea while Sanhua showed Baizhi the goose they had chosen.

Lupa woke when Chisa shifted to turn a page. She sat up, rubbed her face, and admired her slippers again.

Ciaccona persuaded Cartethyia to save the pot purchase for a morning when they could measure the windowsill. They walked as far as the shop to look anyway. Mei bought a length of ribbon, and Yangyang tied her hair back so the new earring would show.

By the garden gate, Chixia discovered that she was carrying everybody’s empty wrappers. She distributed them among their owners while Amy argued cheerfully for keeping the one with the turtle’s portrait printed on it.

“It looks like Ma’s,” she explained, holding it beside the painted fan.

Mei studied the two turtles. Hers had a bag, several determined feet, and a little blue flower Amy had added while she was washing her brush.

“Mine has a more interesting afternoon.”

The Wind lifted the wrapper from Amy’s hand and laid it over the fan. Amy folded both carefully into the wide paper sleeve the stallholder had given them.

At the willow by the entrance, Baizhi stopped.

The woman behind the serving window was filling a pot with fresh ginger syrup.

Chixia followed her gaze.

“You’re thinking about another bowl.”

Baizhi tucked the fish fan into the crook of her arm and opened her purse.

“I would like one for the walk home.”

Yangyang came to stand beside her. Mei looked through the window at the remaining sesame cakes and called to Iuno, who had reached the corner.

Lupa found a place to put down her boots.

The woman looked at the gathering outside her window, then brought the tray of bowls back to the counter.

“Extra ginger?” she asked Baizhi.

Baizhi smiled.

“Underneath as well, please.”

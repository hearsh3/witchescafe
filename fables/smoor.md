## i. Aubel
### *the Old Federation · the Federated Cities, the Hotel Terminus*

> **AUBEL CHORAL SOCIETY — ATTENDANCE, THURSDAYS**  
> *Hymns and part-songs only. (The Captain's rule.)*
>
> *First winter. Present: 40. Sung: the Evening Hymn. Window: shut.*  
> *Tenth winter. Present: 31. Sung: "Lead, Kindly Lamp". Mr. Haldane's chair kept.*  
> *Thirtieth winter. Present: 12. Sung: the Evening Hymn. Window: shut.*  
> *Fiftieth winter. Present: 5. Sung: the Evening Hymn, first verse twice, for Rosa.*  
> *This winter, Thursday last. Present: 3 (I. K., A. F., R. P.). Visitors: 27. Visitors standing on the stairs: not entered.*

Mrs. Ines Kalder came through the station archway at twenty past six with the stove-key in her mitten and her music bag under her elbow, twenty minutes ahead of the hour so the iron would have time to draw before Aldo's chest felt the damp.

She knew before her boot reached the first landing that somebody had got at the coal-box ahead of her. Green birch-bark smoke was curling down the stairwell around the legs of five kids in wet railway-yard jackets who stood crowded on the narrow treads, shifting their boots and passing a cracked thermos cap from hand to hand. None of them belonged to the hotel. The youngest had coal-smut across the bridge of his nose and a folded news-sheet cutting sticking two inches out of his breast pocket.

"Mind the banister," Ines said, coming up the wall side where the carpet rods still held. "The top spindle's only glued."

They flattened themselves against the flock wallpaper to let her pass. Inside the clubroom, thirty more of them were wedged onto the horsehair benches, the luggage stools, and the radiator pipe along the partition. Somebody had packed the little pot-bellied stove to the throat with wet slack on top of unsplit kindling and left the ash-door wide; thick yellow smoke rolled out under the lid, and a tall boy by the casement had shoved the lower sash up six inches on a hymn-book to clear the room, letting in the sleet and the iron bang of shunting wagons from the yard across the road.

In his armchair by the coal-hod, Mr. Aldo Fenn sat straight-backed with his white stick between his knees and his hands folded on the horn handle, facing three feet to the left of the piano where the basses had sat when there were basses. Mrs. Rosa Pike had got hold of a girl in a switchman's wool cap and was patting the girl's wet sleeve with both hands.

"You've got the look of the Bretts about the chin," Rosa was saying happily. "Ines, look at this crowd, we haven't had the back row down since the big frost—whose girl is this, is she Miller's?"

"Nobody's you know, Rosa, sit down before you catch your death from that sash," Ines said.

She unpinned her hat, laid her music bag on the upright piano, and went straight to the stove with the iron rake. A boy in greased overalls tried to take the handle from her—"I can poke it, missus"—and she rapped his knuckles lightly with the flat of the iron.

"You've poked it half to death already," she said. "Get your coat-tail out of the scuttle."

She raked the choked slack forward off the air-bars, opened the flue-slide at the pipe collar until the roar caught in the chimney, fed three dry cobbles into the bright hollow at the back, and shut the lower draught down to a thumb's breadth. Within two minutes the yellow reek drew up into the stack and the cast iron began to smell properly of hot blacking.

At the piano desk she opened the oilskin attendance book, dipped the hotel's steel nib, and wrote across the fresh line in her small, square signaller's hand: *This winter, Thursday. Present: 3 (I. K., A. F., R. P.). Visitors: 34. Visitors standing on the stairs: 5.*

She left the blotter over the wet ink, lifted the stack of black cloth hymnals from the stool, and handed half to the boy in the greased overalls and half to a young woman with cropped dark hair and flash-burned cuffs who sat on the end of the front bench.

"One between two," Ines said, taking her place at the keyboard and turning back the yellowed leaf at page four. "Number seven, the Evening Hymn. Soprano and alto on the top stave, men on the bottom if you can read it, and if you can't, keep your ears on Mr. Fenn and don't shout."

She struck the D chord twice—the F-sharp hammer stuck in wet weather and wanted a firm thumb—and nodded.

For the first four bars Aldo's bass and her own dry alto carried it almost alone while thirty-nine sets of boots shuffled on the boards and pages turned two at a time. Halfway through the first verse a boy by the door found the tenor third and held onto it; by the second verse the girls along the radiator had caught the turn at the cadence, their voices coming up clear and a little sharp in the low ceiling, crowding the beat on the long notes the way people do who are used to singing in wind and yard-noise. Rosa lost the second verse entirely and sang the first over again with great sweetness, smiling round at the wet coats. On the final *Amen* the young voices held the fifth two breaths longer than the score marked, listening to themselves fill the panelling before they let it drop.

Ines took her hands off the keys. In the quiet, a yard engine went past the hotel front, clanking over the frogs.

The young woman with the welder's cuffs—Margit, her foreman called her down at the boiler shop when Ines passed the fence on market mornings—stood up at the end of the bench. She didn't close her hymnal; she turned it over in her hands and looked at the back board, where the last four leaves had been pasted down solid with carpenter's glue sixty winters ago and gone brown at the seam.

"We heard you've got 'Down the Line' in the back of the old copies," Margit said.

"You heard wrong," Ines said, reaching for her pencil to mark the next hymn. "Number fourteen."

"My grandad said the Captain pasted it in the back the week the trains stopped running." Margit stepped up to the side of the upright and laid her right hand flat on the polished walnut of the lid.

Her cuff was buttoned back two inches from the wrist-bone. Across the back of her hand, between the second and third tendons where the slag-sparks had pitted the skin, the Tacet Mark stood bare in the gaslight—no watch-strap, no plaster, not a smear of greasepaint over the ridges—spread wider than a coin and thrumming faintly against the walnut, the way a mark goes when its owner's heart is up.

Behind her on the benches two boys looked down at their knees, and a girl near the stove slipped her left thumb quickly under her palm. Ines looked at the bare hand on the walnut, and then up at Margit's chin, which was set hard enough to crack a nut. Under her own left cuff, the wide brown leather of her strap sat stiff and warm where she had buckled it at six o'clock that morning.

"The woman in the northern pictures doesn't cover hers," Margit said. "She walks right down the steps with it bare in front of the cameras."

"The woman in the northern pictures wasn't at the siding at Halder Junction," Ines said. "Take your hand off the varnish, you'll leave a grease-mark. Number fourteen, page twelve."

"Ines," Aldo said from his chair by the coal-hod.

"Page twelve, Aldo, you don't need the book for it."

"How many are in the room, Ines?"

She looked across the benches—the wet wool shoulders jammed four to a form, the five faces peering round the jamb from the stair-head, Rosa's small white head nodding in the corner.

"Forty-two," she said.

Aldo let his stick lean against his thigh and set both broad, blue-veined hands flat on his knees. He cleared his throat once, pitched the low A in his chest the way he had pitched it on the platform at Halder Junction when the troop-wagons coupled in the dark, and hummed the first two bars with his mouth shut.

Before he opened his lips on the third bar, twenty voices along the back wall were already in it.

*"When the snow goes off the line, we are coming home—"*

They sang it too fast, pitched a tone above Aldo's gravelled bass, taking it at a brisk street-march clip they had pieced together out of kitchen whispers and half-heard tavern hums. Rosa's hands came up to her cheek; her mouth formed the shape of the word *home* half a beat behind them.

Ines stood up from the piano stool. She walked the length of the front row, lifted the hymn-book out from under the window-frame, and drew the sash all the way down until the iron catch clicked home into its slot. Sleet ticked dry and small against the outside of the glass. She turned round, set her shoulders against the sill with her arms folded over her ribs, and looked at the room.

They had come to the turn after the refrain, where the verse went up into the coupling-call, and half the benches stumbled at once—some humming a guess at the air, two boys repeating *coming home* into the gap, Margit's mouth open on a consonant she didn't have.

Ines pitched the E cleanly from the back of her throat, straight and flat across the heads:

*"Coal and flour in the wagons, leave the siding clear—"*

Margit caught the word *wagons* before it left Ines's teeth; the boy by the stove had the second half from his grandfather and shouted it out on the beat; and then the whole room came back down onto the refrain together, slower now, taking the time from the old woman at the window and the blind man by the hod, forty-two voices vibrating in the floorboards while the stove-pipe hummed in its collar.

Nobody asked for it a second time when it ended. They stacked the black hymnals in two neat piles on the table by the door, took their caps, and went down the narrow stairs in twos and threes, quieter than they had come up. Margit paused a second by the piano to button her overall cuff over her wrist before she went out into the passage.

When the street door had shut below and Aldo's niece had come up to take his arm for the walk to the tram-stop, Ines helped Rosa into her galoshes, saw them both to the landing, and came back to the piano desk to turn down the gas.

The attendance book lay open under the single burner. She picked up the steel pen, drew one straight rule through the blank space after *5*, and wrote at the end of the line: *Sung: "Down the Line". Window: shut.*

Ten minutes later she came out through the hotel porch with her collar turned up against the drift. Across the cobbles, under the yellow arc-lamp on the station goods wall, a boy in a cut-down railway coat was standing on an upturned crate with a flat whitewash brush and a lard-pail, laying wet lime across the soot-blackened brick one palm-high letter at a time: *WHEN THE SNOW GOES OFF—*

White drops ran down the mortar into the slush around his boots. Ines crossed the tram-lines by the chemist's corner, her music bag tucked dry beneath her arm, and went on up the hill toward the yards.

## ii. Row 4
### *the New Federation · the Lawless Zone, the Committee's garage and the Pump House*

> **THE HOMECOMING — ORDER OF MARCH**
>
> ***When the snow goes off the line, we are coming home.***
>
> *The General comes down the Expo road with the first thaw. Ten of them. The Army never surrendered. It was sent abroad to wait.*  
> *Every house on the Rows to bring: your grandfather's coat. One lamp. Oil for the lamp, as much as you can carry. (The Committee will supply.)*  
> *Assemble at the boundary cairns. Light the lamps when you see her hand.*  
> *OVERLEAF: THE KITCHEN DOORS.*

The mimeograph drum wanted re-inking every forty sheets, and if you turned the crank too fast in cold weather the flour-paste in the trough beside it skinned over before the stack was dry enough to fold.

Nell stood at the trestle table with her grandfather's heavy grey cuffs turned back twice over her mittens, jogging the damp broadsheets square by twenties while Corin worked the handle. The garage behind Row 4 smelled of warm paraffin, printer's grease, and boiled cloves from the pan the Colonel kept simmering on the flat of the oil-drum stove so nobody's nose froze by the door. Along the back wall, stacked two high on timber baulks, sat fourteen blue forty-gallon drums with their brass bungs wired shut. The municipal ration lorry hadn't turned down Row 4 since the second week of October, and every evening at dusk six or seven mothers from the top end came through the yard with tin jugs under their shawls and went out again without anybody writing a figure in a book.

At the workbench under the bulb, the Colonel was cutting a fresh zinc stencil with a cobbler's knife, his three pawnshop campaign stars pinned in a neat brass row above the pocket of his wool jersey. He had laid out the ten shoulders along the bottom edge the same as last month's template—the tall one on the left, the crooked star over the smallest head—except in the centre, above the fifth silhouette, he had traced an arm raised from the elbow, its right hand closed into a square, knotted fist.

Pinned to the rough board beside his elbow was the frayed news-sheet square he had worked from: the northern step-still, trimmed close round the dark-haired woman's wrist with pocket-shears so the stone rail was gone and the shadowed fingers looked doubled back against the palm. Nell had the full uncropped sheet tacked inside her washstand door at home; in the whole picture the woman's fingers were spread loose on the wet stone, half turned toward a dough-cart in the street. Nell tapped her stack of twenties straight against the trestle and laid a brick on the corner to keep the curl down.

Above the workbench, next to the zinc shears, hung a narrow slip torn from Tuesday's capital wire sheet, stuck to the plaster with a dab of flour-paste:

> *WIRE: The household was reported this week in four places at once: Septimont, Lahai-Roi, Jinzhou and Ragunna.*  
> *(pencil) Cover. They are dispersing to come by four roads. — C.*

The Colonel blew the zinc shavings off his rule, set the knife down, and lifted the tin gallon can that stood by Nell's paste-bucket. He carried it over to the end drum, unscrewed the brass tap, and filled it to the neck until the clear pale kerosene stood trembling in the spout.

"Take that into your mother before you start the back rows, Nell," he said, screwing the cork cap down tight and wiping the drip off the tin flank with his thumb. "Tell her the east wind's coming back tonight, she shouldn't stint the wick in the little one's room."

"We've still got two fingers in the jug from Monday, Colonel," Nell said, though her hand closed round the wire bail at once.

"Two fingers won't see Friday out." He reached across to adjust the heavy military lapel of her coat where the paste-bucket strap had dragged it sideways, smoothing the coarse pre-war frieze with a careful palm. "Good cloth, that. My dad's depot issued the three-seam shoulder the last winter before the war—you could sleep in a ditch in that coat and wake up dry. Have you got the sixty sheets for Six to Nine?"

"Counted and folded."

"Good girl." He lifted the top bundle from the trestle and laid it across her arm, printed face up. "When you get down to Row Nine, turn the sheets over on the cistern wall. Put the kitchen list out where the street can read it. Those houses kept the kettle on for our lads when the patrols were out; their grandchildren ought to see their names up in daylight before the march."

Nell carried the oil-can in through her own scullery first, set it behind the coal-box where her little brother wouldn't tip it, and came back out into the alley with the galvanized paste-pail and the broadsheets wedged under her elbow. Halfway down the cinder track behind Row 6, while she waited for two wyrm-watch riders on mud-caked bikes to splash past the crossing, she turned the top leaf over on her sleeve.

> **THE KITCHEN DOORS**  
> *These families opened their doors to the Federation's men in the war. When the Army comes home it will want to know whose doors.*  
> *Row 2: Achterberg. Mull.*  
> *Row 6: Pruett. Hallam.*  
> *Row 9: Sallow. Voigt. Dace.*

The wind off the slag-pans bit through her woollen thumbs. On Row 6 she slapped four crosses of grey paste onto the end gable by Pruett's yard, smoothed the *Order of March* flat with the heel of her mitten, and put a second one on the transformer kiosk with the kitchen list facing the pump. By Row 8 her brush was stiffening with frost at the ferrule; she had to knock the ice-crust off against the rim of the pail at every corner.

At the top of Row 9, beside the bolted iron door of the old transformer house where the concrete cistern wall began, she dipped the brush, laid a wet square of paste across the lime-washed brick, and lifted a sheet with *THE KITCHEN DOORS* turned outward.

Her thumb stopped over the third line from the foot. *Row 9: Sallow. Voigt. Dace.*

Two doors down on the left, the lace curtain was twitching in the parlour window above the cobbler's, where old Mrs. Dace sat all day watching the lane. Doris Voigt lived at Number 14 with her sister's boy who worked the night crane at the scrap-heap without a card. Last month, when Nell had torn her right coat-pocket half off on a wire fence behind the Kennel, Doris had made her sit on an upturned crate by the Pump House, poured her half a mug of sweet tea from Mrs. Brandt's flask, and stitched the heavy grey cloth back down with carpet-thread so neat you couldn't tell which side had ripped.

Nell flipped the damp sheet over between her fingers, clapped the kitchen list face-down into the wet paste so **THE HOMECOMING — ORDER OF MARCH** stood outward to the lane, and drew the brush once across the top margin to seal the edge.

With twelve sheets still rolled under her arm, she turned up the south track toward the dead pump house.

Mrs. Brandt was sitting square in Chair 2 with her plaid tucked under her thighs and her fingerless mittens folded over the blue exercise book. On the plywood board behind the oil drums, the eight plastic sleeves rattled dryly against their drawing-pins; in the middle of the earthenware rice-bowl, the stemless red flower from last month sat just as deep-coloured and unwithered as the afternoon it was put there, with a sift of frozen grit caught between its outer petals. Chair 3 stood empty beside the spare drum.

"Half-past three," Mrs. Brandt said as Nell's boots crunched on the gravel. "If she tells me she was down at the depot arguing with the clerk about two quarts of paraffin when there hasn't been a tanker at the siding since November, I'm going home and leaving her the wrench."

Nell drew one sheet from under her arm and held it out.

Mrs. Brandt took it, held it six inches from her spectacles against the grey glare, and read the front from the refrain down to *light the lamps*. Then she turned it over and read the seven names on the back. Her mouth didn't move; only her thumb rubbed once across the cheap rag paper where the ink had bled through from the heavy type.

"Arthur Crane couldn't find his own privy without a lantern," Mrs. Brandt said, "and now he's drawing up billeting lists for five regiments and a dead general."

She lifted the heavy iron wrench-socket off her blue exercise book, folded the broadsheet neatly in four with *THE KITCHEN DOORS* on the inside, shut the covers over it, and set the wrench back down on the top.

"Open one of those canvas chairs and sit down out of the draught," she told Nell. "You're letting the heat out of your boots standing there."

Nell unfolded the nearest chair from the lean-to stack—stiff olive canvas, new-varnished ash legs with **V.F.** stencilled in black paint across the underside of the seat-slat—and sat down beside the oil drum with her paste-pail between her ankles.

Round the corner of the slag-tip came Doris Voigt at a flat-footed trot, two string bags knocking against her shins and her woollen scarf blown round behind one ear.

"—told the girl at the wicket I'd been on the winter roll since before she was born," Doris began while she was still ten yards down the ruts, dropping her bags by the drum and easing herself into Chair 3 with a long creak of canvas, "and she looked right through the grille and said the third-quarter coupons were cancelled from the capital, as if my brother-in-law's chimney draws on cancelled paper! Hello, Nell love, mind your coat-hem out of that bucket."

She fished in her cardigan pocket among bus tickets and loose buttons, brought out a twist of waxed paper holding two lemon drops stuck together with lint, pried one free with her thumbnail, and dropped it into Nell's mitten.

"Put that in your cheek," Doris said, panting cheerfully into her scarf. "Take the raw edge off your throat."

Mrs. Brandt opened her blue book an inch under the wrench-socket, licked her carpenter's stub, and wrote across the foot of the page: *broadsheet, 1. kept.* Nell tucked the sour yellow sweet against her back teeth, her hands deep in her mended pockets, while three hundred yards down the cinder road toward Row 6 Corin and two smaller boys came round the transformer wall with a second bucket, slapping wet white squares onto the dark brick one house after another down the line.

## iii. The Arcade
### *the New Federation · the capital, a skin clinic*

> **MAISON HALLORAN — SKIN & FINISH — WINTER LIST**  
> *Concealment, wrist or hand (Second Skin No. 4) .......... 40*  
> *Concealment, full forearm .......... 90*  
> *Concealment, assay day, waterproof, eight hours .......... 120*  
> *(We cannot alter a frequency assay. We can alter a photograph.)*  
> *NEW: "The Arbiter." Decorative mark, right hand, gold leaf, washable .......... 300*  
> *NEW: "The Priestess." Crystal sleeve, left arm, lacquer .......... 650*  
> *NEW: "The Collar." Black satin band, throat, with clasp .......... 85*  
> *All decorative work removed free of charge before school photographs.*  
> *Concealment only: rear door, after six.*

At a quarter to five the arc-lamps under the glass vault of the Lumen Arcade came on all together, turning the wet umbrellas along the promenade to silver and throwing a cold violet rim across Selma Rusk's porcelain work-table.

"Don't flex the tendons, Miss Marchbank," Selma said, resting two fingers lightly on the girl's knuckles to steady them on the velvet cushion. "The size takes ninety seconds to go tacky. If you bend the wrist now, the leaf will craze before the cars arrive."

"Sorry," Odile Marchbank said, though she immediately tipped her chin to admire the sheen from another angle while her mother's secretary stood by the curtain with a quilted taffeta dress-bag over her arm and a gold wristwatch in her palm. Odile was sixteen, pink-knuckled from a riding school in the hills, with skin that had never needed anything heavier than almond milk. "Only Sabina had hers done at Cléry's on Tuesday for the Maritime Ball and they used bronze powder, and it came off on the Minister's cuff during the second waltz. Mother said if I got bronze on the Ashridge table-linen she'd send me back to board till Victory Week."

"We use beaten leaf, twenty-two carat," Selma said, picking up her sable liner. "And a surgical mastic underneath. It won't come off until you take oil to it."

At the next chair, behind the frosted glass screen, Odile's friend was craning her neck into the hand-mirror while Selma's junior clicked a dull-black alloy clasp shut at the nape of a two-inch satin band. *"Does it look like the broadcast?"* the girl asked behind the screen. *"Pull it one notch tighter, I want it to sit right on the hollow like the scissors girl's."*

On Selma's tray lay the brass stencil Mr. Halloran had bought from an engraver on the Esplanade at the start of the winter season, stamped *PATTERN A — THE ARBITER (NORTHERN FEED)*. The engraver had worked from a blown-up newspaper half-tone, caught at one instant, and made a jeweller's job of it: every ray cut to the same hard length, neat and dead at the tips, the way no mark on a living hand ever held still long enough to be.

Selma laid the brass plate back in the drawer without using it. With the tip of her dry brush she lifted a square of gold leaf from the tissue book, floated it down onto the clear mastic between the third and fourth metacarpals of Odile's right hand, and tamped it home with a chamois stub. Where the stencil would have stopped the rays short in blunt points, she took her finest zero-sable dipped in shellac and thinned each tip out into a soft, uneven feather, so that when the hand turned under the lamp the gold seemed to swell and draw back a hair's breadth, the way a real Tacet Mark breathed with whatever was moving in its owner. Under her own right cuff, an inch above the button of her grey linen smock, her own lay small and quiet under three coats of Second Skin No. 4. It had kept quiet all afternoon.

"Which one's your favourite?" Odile asked, blowing her fringe out of her eyes. "Sabina's all for the priestess because of the crystal arm, she tried to talk her father into the six-fifty lacquer sleeve, only he said one arm bare at the Conservatory in January was asking for pleurisy. I like the mother. She never even looks at the cameras when she walks past."

"Keep the thumb still," Selma said, feathering the outer edge of the last ray so the gold caught the ring-lamp like raised skin.

Odile dropped her voice half a register, leaning an inch closer across the velvet bolster while her mother's secretary checked the tram-table by the door. "Do you think it's true, what they say on the zone wireless? About her hand closing up right through the skin on the broadcast?"

"Broadcast lighting is very flattering, Miss Marchbank," Selma said pleasantly, setting the sable down on its porcelain rest and lifting the warm-air diffuser to set the glaze. "And northern cameras have a wide aperture. Hold it under the lamp for thirty counts."

When the thirty counts were up, Odile lifted her right hand off the cushion and held it high against the vaulted glass roof of the arcade, turning the wrist slow and solemn in the blue-white glare so the gold rays flashed between her rings before her mother's secretary hustled her out toward the cab-rank.

At six the front grille came down over the plate-glass window, the junior washed her bowls and caught the suburban omnibus, and Mr. Halloran locked the cash-box into the safe and went across to the chop-house. Selma stayed to wipe down the stools.

At ten past six the bell above the alley door at the back of the dispensary gave one short, dry click.

The boy on the step was about eighteen, bareheaded in the sleet, wearing a pressed black waiter's jacket two sizes too wide across the shoulders over a collarless shirt. He kept his left hand tucked inside the breast of the jacket until Selma shut the bolt behind him and pointed to the wooden chair under the dispensary ring-light.

"Pratique card?" she asked, unscrewing the wide squat jar of No. 4 before he sat down.

"Eight o'clock tomorrow, Pier Four," the boy said—Pim, he'd given the name when he knocked on Tuesday to ask the price. "And the Grand Pavilion's taking on forty extra busboys at ten for the Expo banquets. You have to show the stamped card with the picture dry on it at the steward's gate."

He reached into his trouser pocket with his right hand and laid his money on the oilcloth beside the sterilizer: three zinc tens, four twos, and three copper halves. He looked at the three copper halves, then up at the framed card on the wall where *Concealment, wrist or hand .......... 40* stood in gilt script, and his ears went red to the tips.

"I had the forty when I got on the tram at the toll-gate," he said quickly. "They put the winter zone-fare up a copper half on Monday."

"Put your hand on the towel," Selma said.

He laid his left hand flat on the white paper towel. It was red with cold and scrubbing—he had taken bath-brick or kitchen pumice to the back of it that morning, scraping the knuckles raw as if the dark would come out like a stain—and under the chapped skin the mark was spread wide past the knuckle-bones and thrumming hard enough that Selma could see the towel tremble over it.

Selma didn't reach for the spatula. "Breathe out slowly," she said. "Again. It's only the tram still in you." She warmed a dab of zinc primer on the ball of her thumb to soften the wax, pressed it gently into the scraped hollow between his tendons so it wouldn't sting, and waited while his breathing came down and the mark drew in under her thumb, smaller and slower, till it lay almost still. Then she took a clean badger-hair stipple brush, loaded it from the jar of No. 4, and worked from the centre ray outward in tiny overlapping crescents—first the grey-green neutralizer over the dark rays, then a veil of warm ochre across the pumiced knuckles, then the matte carnation coat feathered half an inch down each finger and round the ulnar bone so the camera flash at Pier Four wouldn't catch a tide-mark.

"Don't wash above the knuckles tonight," she said, setting the warm-air diffuser over his hand for the same thirty counts. "Sleep with an old sock over it so you don't rub it on the blanket. When the photographer tells you to put your hands on the rail, rest the weight on the heel of the palm and keep your breathing slow. If your heart gets going, it'll swell out past the edge of the wax, and then nothing I've done is any use to you."

Pim watched the skin under the ring-light. The dark rays had vanished completely; the back of his left hand looked smooth, slightly waxy, and wholly blank, like a glove on a tailor's dummy.

Selma swept the zinc pieces and two of the copper halves into the till drawer, leaving the last copper half on the oilcloth for his tram back to the toll-gate.

At the alley door, with his hand on the latch, Pim stopped and looked back at the porcelain table where the tissue book of gold leaf still lay open beside the chamois stub.

"Missus," he said quietly. "Have you seen the northern pictures? The one of them on the steps?"

"I've seen them," Selma said, screwing the lid onto the jar of No. 4.

"She's got it right there on her hand," he said. "Doesn't cover it. Broad daylight."

He nodded once, slipped his blank left hand carefully into his wide jacket pocket so the sleet wouldn't spot it, and went out into the alley.

Selma shot the bolt, carried the two brushes—the zero-sable crusted with gold size and the badger stipple heavy with flesh-wax—over to the porcelain sink under the dispensary mirror, and stood them together in the jar of rectified turpentine. A pale swirl of pigment rose from the bristles, dull ochre and bright gold leaf spinning together in the clear spirit until neither colour showed at the bottom of the glass.

She unbuttoned her right smock cuff, folded the grey linen back to the elbow, and took a cotton pad soaked in sweet oil from the dish. Across the inside of her wrist she drew the pad three times, firm and slow, wiping away the carnation coat, the ochre, and the grey primer.

In the white circle of the ring-light mirror she held her bare wrist up, the dark rays branching between the blue veins of her tendon, and as she looked at it the mark spread a little and began to thrum, while out under the glass roof of the arcade the seven o'clock tram went past with its bell clanging twice for the crossing.

## iv. Halt Twelve
### *the Old Federation · the Fifth Sector, a house on the spur line*

> **PROVINCIAL MUTUAL — DWELLING LOSS, PRELIMINARY REPORT**

> *Mortgagee: Fifth Sector Building Society. Charge transferred overnight, this quarter, to a holder not disclosed.*  
> *Endorsement 14 (Sentinel Warranty) attached. Count exclusion: not engaged. The household is not in the territory.*  
> *Cause (tick one):*  
> *[ ] Accident, domestic*  
> *[ ] Candle or open flame*  
> *[ ] Malicious*  
> *[ ] Civil commotion*  
> *[ ] Frequency event. (Exclusion 7: loss arising from the resonance of any marked person. Policy void. Refer to Registry.)*

The eight-fifteen motor-bus from the district town set Edwin Pask down at the level crossing by Halt Twelve, where the old relief spur from the Federated Cities ran single-track through willow-herb and cinders toward the brickworks.

Mrs. Lund's cottage was the third plot along the embankment. Only the central brick stack and the lower courses of the parlour wall remained upright; the roof-timbers had fallen inward Tuesday night and frozen over by Thursday morning, so the heap of rafters, burst horsehair sofa, and split slate wore a crisp grey rime over the char, smelling sharply in the cold air of wet soot, boiled cabbage, and cracked window-glass.

Before Pask had his leather gloves off or his brass tape out of his pocket, the neighbour at Number 2 was leaning over the tarred palings between the plots in his shirt-sleeves and waistcoat, breath smoking hard.

"You the Mutual?" Mr. Hollis said, pointing a thick forefinger toward the fallen back end of the plot. "I told the constable Tuesday night and I'll tell you now so it goes down straight on the paper. It wasn't any flue and it wasn't any paraffin lamp. It was that boy of hers out in the timber shed after ten o'clock, practising with his hands."

Pask tucked his gloves into his coat pocket and unclipped his fountain pen. "Practising, Mr. Hollis?"

"You know what I mean, Mr. Pask, everybody on this spur knows what I mean," Hollis said rapidly, his eyes darting once toward the road as a coal-cart rattled over the sleepers. "White flash through the plank seams like a welder's torch, and the whole tar-paper roof went up in one roar before I could get my boots laced. Seventeen years old and no more sense than a stoat. If the Registry inspectors come down from the prefecture and find out we've had an unlicensed resonance blowout twenty yards from the line, they'll have every house on the row out in the street for screening before Sunday."

Behind Hollis's shoulder, pasted crookedly to the tarred planks of his own coal-shed facing the railway embankment, hung a rain-blistered broadsheet from the month before—**THE HOMECOMING — ORDER OF MARCH**—its bottom corner torn off by the wind, the bold italic refrain across the top still black enough to read from the fence. Hollis hadn't scraped it off; somebody had brushed a smear of mud across the word *General* and left the rest.

"I'll look at the brickwork first, Mr. Hollis," Pask said mildly. "Then I'll step down to Mrs. Lund's sister's."

He saw Mrs. Lund first, in the small back scullery at Number 5, where her sister had put the kettle on the trivet and pulled two kitchen chairs up to the fender. Agnes Lund was seventy-four, her best black coat pinned over her night-flannel because everything else had gone down with the bedroom floor. Across the table by the cistern sat her grandson Teo, seventeen, tall and narrow-shouldered, with both hands wrapped from wrist to fingertip in clean white lint tied off with tape.

"It was my own fault, Mr. Pask," Mrs. Lund said, folding her bare hands in her lap and looking straight at his clipboard. "I had two jam-jar night-lights burning on the parlour sill behind the lace curtain, beside the picture from the paper. The picture of the mother's hand. The draught through the sash must have blown the hem of the net into the grease after we went up to bed."

She said it through once, level and dry, like a child reciting a catechism she had practiced all Wednesday. By the cistern, Teo kept his bandaged knuckles resting on his knees and looked at the brass tap above the sink.

"Thank you, Mrs. Lund," Pask said. "I'll take the measurements in the garden now. You needn't come out in the cold."

He walked back up the cinder path to Number 3 alone, turned up his coat collar, and stepped over the low threshold-stone into what had been the front parlour.

Thirty winters of reading cottage fires between Halt One and the river told you which way a roof had leaned before you took the tape out. Here at the front window-sill, the brickwork under the fallen sash still held streaks of white lime-wash; the timber lintel had charred half an inch deep on its inner face and left sound yellow deal in the core. Caught under a shard of frosted pane in the corner lay the curled, blackened triangle of a newsprint clipping—two bare fingers resting on a stone rail, scorched brown where the roof had dropped on it at the end of the blaze.

Pask walked through the gap where the scullery door had hung and crunched across the frozen garden plot to the outbuilding by the sleepers.

Here the heat had been fierce enough to vitrify the mortar in the three surviving courses of brick foundation and twist the iron hinges of the shed door into hooks. The timber walls were gone to fine white wood-ash, flat and clean on the cinders. Along the inner face of the low brick dwarf-wall that had supported the workbench, sheltered from the hose-water by a fallen sheet of corrugated iron, somebody had chalked six words in cramped, hurried capitals before the fire:

*WHEN THE SNOW GOES OFF THE LINE*

Pask crouched on one heel by the dwarf-wall, lifted the corner of the corrugated iron with his stick, and looked at the ash beneath where a shelf had stood.

Half-buried in the grey powder lay a foil blister-card from the district dispensary—*Bromide-R, Frequency Suppressant, 50mg*—the standard buff packet sold over the back counter to marked rail-hands before the yearly medicals. The foil was blistered black by radiant heat, and every single one of the twenty-eight little lead-backed domes was still sealed whole and unpopped, four full weeks of doses left on the shelf untouched.

Gravel shifted by the garden gate. Teo had come up the back path without a coat, his bandaged hands tucked under his armpits against the frost. He stopped by the currant bushes, looking past Pask at the twisted wheel-rim and scorched frame of a boy's roadster bicycle lying in the cinders beside the shed wall.

Pask stood up, dusted the ash off his trouser knee, and clipped his brass tape back onto his belt.

"Does the paper cover the shed too, mister?" Teo asked, his voice rough at the back of his throat. "Or the bike? I still owe four coppers on the back tyre at the smithy."

"Outbuildings are covered to ten per cent of the dwelling value," Pask said, consulting the schedule clipped behind the preliminary sheet. "Cycles are excluded unless specified by endorsement. The timber and tools will cover your four coppers, though, when the draft comes through."

Teo nodded once, looking down at the white lint over his right knuckles where a faint dark spot of ointment had soaked through the gauze above the middle tendons, and walked back down the path toward Number 5.

Pask stood by the standing chimney-breast in the centre of the ruin, rested the wooden clipboard against his forearm, and uncapped his pen. Over the five square boxes under *Cause (tick one)* his nib hovered for two breaths while a coal-train went past on the main line half a mile north, its whistle flat and small across the flat fields.

In the second box—*[ ] Candle or open flame*—he made one neat diagonal stroke.

Below, on the two dotted lines reserved for *Adjuster's Remarks*, he wrote in his clear clerk's copperplate: *Origin: front window. Domestic shrine, unattended. Total loss approved for settlement.*

He signed his name at the foot, blotted it against his cuff, and walked back to the crossing to wait for the two-thirty bus into town.

As the bus pulled away from the halt and changed down for the slight rise over the embankment bridge, Pask looked back through the mud-splashed rear glass at Number 3. On the narrow brick ledge of the fireplace mantel, standing open to the sleet where the parlour walls had fallen away round the chimney-stack, somebody had already climbed over the frozen rafters and set a fresh glass jam-jar with a lit tallow stub burning steadily inside the wind-break of the flue.

## vi. Smoor
### *the Old Federation · Aubel, a kitchen*

It was gone ten when Ines set her empty teacup in the stoneware sink, and the shunting engines down in the low yard had knocked off for the night, leaving only the wind humming under the scullery door.

On the narrow shelf above the coal-range, propped sideways between the tin tea-caddy and the box of kitchen matches where the girl from the boiler-shop had left it on the piano the week before, sat the clipped news-sheet square of the mother's bare right hand resting on the wet stone step. Ines hadn't tacked it to the wall and hadn't tipped it into the grate; the rising heat from the kettle had curled its two lower corners slightly inward against the japanning of the caddy.

She knelt on the rag rug in front of the range and hooked the iron latch of the firebox open with the poker.

Inside the bars, four cobbles of good steam-coal had burned down to a palm-sized bed of clear red heart, glowing without flame under a furry skin of white ash. Ines took the short-handled hearth-rake her mother had brought down from the hill farms before the war and drew the outer cinders carefully inward from the corners of the grate—left cheek first, then the right, then the back plate—gathering every live red piece into one tight, round mound in the middle of the bars.

With the flat of the iron shovel she lifted the cold grey ash from the pan underneath and laid it over the red mound, patting it down with the back of the blade until the glow was buried half an inch deep and not a spark showed through the crust. On top of the grey heap she scattered one small scoop of damp slack from the back of the scuttle to hold the crust till six, pushed the chimney-damper in to a finger's width, and swung the iron firebox door shut on its pin.

"Keep your head under the blanket till the whistle," she said aloud to the iron—her mother's line over the turf-ash sixty winters back, spoken at the grate the way you test whether a bolt is home—and stood up, holding the edge of the mantelpiece while her right knee clicked straight.

At the sink under the back casement she reached her right thumb under the brass tongue of her watch-strap and unbuckled the stiff brown leather from her left wrist.

For sixty years, since the first winter she came back to the terrace from the signal-huts, her hand had gone to the brass ring on the gingham window-curtain before it touched the buckle. Tonight the curtain hung gathered on its cord against the lime-washed jamb, leaving the four small panes bare to the yard. She laid the wide leather strap flat on the grooved pine of the draining-board beside the soap-dish, turned the cold tap on a thread, and rinsed her cup and saucer with both bare wrists in the light of the paraffin lamp.

Out across the brick backs, past the frosted roofs of the coal-sheds and the wash-house wall, one kitchen window on the ground floor of the next terrace stood yellow and uncurtained in the dark, an empty table edge showing under the shade.

In the quiet scullery the cast-iron plates of the range ticked once, twice, as the metal settled round the buried heap in the grate, the leather strap lay dark and curved on the wet wood by the sink, and the bare glass reflected the lamp and the marked wrist moving the cloth round the rim of the cup.

## I. Dear Ma, or: Is It Different for Girls?

**Day 1.**

Dear Ma,

The door is blue because you painted it blue. I watched you do it. You got paint on your wrist and said it was a tattoo now, permanent, official, and then it came off in the bath, and I decided that was the first lie you ever told me, and I forgave you immediately, which set a precedent.

Today the door opened and the door closed and you were on the wrong side of it.

I stood at the window for a long time. Your coat got smaller and smaller and then the snow had it. Here is a thing about snow I have learned since this morning: it takes everything at the same speed. Coats. Footprints. The path you salted last week so I'd stop falling on my butt. The snow is very fair about it, which is the worst part.

I yelled at you. I want the record to show that I regret the yelling and also that you deserved it.

You said the Academy will look after me. You said the world is vast. You knelt down in the doorway and your knees cracked like an old lady's, which I will be telling everyone forever, and you said *I'll come back to see you when I can. I promise.*

Promise. Okay. I'm writing it down so it's real. Grown-ups keep things that are written down. That's what all your forms taught me — you filled in forms about me for a week before you left, official ones, with my name on them, and you pressed the pen so hard it went through to the table, and I have decided that means the forms are load-bearing.

So: Day 1. I'll wait for you here in Lahai-Roi.

I made your hot chocolate tonight. Three spoons of cocoa, the little bit of salt that you swear is the secret and I swear is a crime, marshmallows until structural. It tasted terrible. It tasted exactly right. Both of those are true and I refuse to explain further.

— Amy

**Day 365.**

Dear Ma,

One year. I took the shuttle up the Riseway with my house key and my good scarf and I went home for the day. ## II. The Speed at Which Snow Falls

Over the Roya Frostlands, snow falls at a little under one meter per second. A snowflake released from the cloud deck above the glacier takes roughly forty minutes to reach the ground. Someone standing at the frost-viewing gallery on the Academy's upper level, watching through four centimeters of thermal glass, is therefore always watching the past: every flake visible at eye level made its decision to fall three-quarters of an hour ago, somewhere high and dark, long before anyone was watching.

In her nineteenth winter, the instruments in the sublevels began to disagree with the sky.

The disagreement was quiet at first, the way the important ones are. A decimal place drifting in the Voidmatter reactivity index. A harmonic in the Starward Riseway — the planted Greatsword, the lock on the world's one terrible door — that came back from routine survey a quarter-tone flat. Technicians recalibrated their equipment, because the alternative was recalibrating their understanding of the next ten years. The equipment, recalibrated, said it again, politely, like a guest repeating bad news to a host who has asked them to.

Beneath the city, beneath the god, behind the gate, the thing that had been sealed for a century had begun, at last, to lean on the door.

The projections gave Lahai-Roi a season. Baldur — asleep on his knees in the glacier, sword in the earth — could renew the seal, if he could be made to move. And a machine that size moves for one thing only. In the oldest surviving technical documentation, the interface is called by an engineer's word, dry as dust: *Resonator.* In the marginalia of that same documentation, in handwriting nobody has identified, someone crossed the word out and wrote a different one.

*The word in the clay.*

---

The candidate program screened eleven hundred students that winter. Twelve were selected. The number twelve appears again and again in stories like this one, across worlds, across seas; twelve candidates, one seat; it seems to be a load the shape of stories can bear.

The simulations undid them gently and in order. The first three washed out below 80% — the machine's dreaming was simply too large, like asking a cup to hold weather. Two more lasted a month and left the program with nosebleeds and a permanent aversion to the color of the sync fluid. One boy reached 91% and woke up weeping in a language he did not speak and was honorably discharged into a long quiet convalescence. By midwinter, the roster that had begun as a page was a line.

The line read: **734.**

> *Entry 23. I'll do anything. They need someone who can hold the resonance past the safety threshold and I told them I would and Mornye tried to talk me out of it and I told her I'd be fine. I am fine.*
>
> *(smaller, in the margin:) Guess that means I don't really care about anything.*

In the observation booth, Dr. Mornye watched the girl she had been quietly writing reports about for eleven years hold a god's attention for two hundred seconds, and she filled six pages with objections, and she filed all six, and every one of them came back stamped with the same word, and the word was NOTED.

---

That February — the city kept months, out of habit, under a sky it had built itself — Aemeath lived two lives with the particular smoothness of someone who had been practicing her whole childhood.

In the first life she rose at five, drank a nutrient formulation the color of regret, and went down into the sublevels, where they lowered her into the resonance chair and asked her to become, by degrees, the inside of a mountain. In the second life she attended her classes, sat at her lunch table, let Alara pinch her cheek at the customary tariff, walked Lynn through a breakup with the patience of a lighthouse, and told them all, with a smile she had inherited rather than invented, that the special research posting was going great, seriously, you guys worry too much.

*Don't worry about me.* She had learned the sentence young, watching it walk out a blue door. It fit her mouth like a family heirloom. That was the whole trouble with it.

> *Entry 31. Full resonance achieved. 99.2%. They need 99.8 for launch threshold. I told them I could hold it. Mornye argued. I told her I could hold it.*
>
> *Entry 33. Simulation crashed at 99.6. My nose bled for nine minutes. They said I could stop. I said I could hold it.*
>
> *Entry 37. I can hold it.*

The rest of that page is blank. Blankness, in a diary written by a girl who pressed her pen through to the table, is its own entry.

The night before the launch window, Mornye came to her dormitory with two cups of hot chocolate from the campus place, made wrong, and a story instead of an argument, because the arguments had all come back NOTED.

"Your name," Mornye said, "is a word. Did she ever tell you that?"

"Ma named me off a form field." Aemeath took her cup. "She said it was the prettiest word she knew. She refused to elaborate. It was a whole thing."

"It is an old word for truth. Absolute truth — the kind that requires no witness." Mornye held her own cup and did not drink it. "There is a story. A very old one, from somewhere on the far side of the Etheric Sea, or the far side of something. A town in danger. A rabbi shapes a guardian out of clay, and writes that word on its forehead, and the word is what animates it. The clay stands up. It carries water. It defends the town."

"Good story."

"When the work is done," Mornye said, "the word is erased. That is how the story ends. The clay lies back down. Every version agrees on this part. I have checked."

The Phosphor District breathed blue-violet through the window Aemeath had calibrated herself, in first year, because she'd thought it was the most beautiful thing she'd ever seen underground.

"You checked," Aemeath said.

"I check everything. It is my single virtue." Mornye set the cup down with the care of a woman placing a final counterweight. "Aemeath. The board sees a threshold. I see a girl I have watched grow up in weekly increments for eleven years, preparing to spend herself like a word. I am asking you to let me pull the override tomorrow if the load exceeds—"

"Professor." Aemeath said it gently, the way you interrupt someone you love mid-proof. "In the story. When the clay stands up. Does anyone ask it what it wants?"

Mornye was quiet.

"Nobody asks the clay," Aemeath said. "That's the flaw in the old version. So here's mine: the clay volunteers. The clay read the projections. The clay grew up in this town." She smiled, and it was her mother's smile down to the last calibrated millimeter, the one engineered to make the person left behind feel that the leaving was strength. "Promise me you won't pull it."

"That is an unreasonable request."

"Promise me anyway. You're the only one whose promises I rate."

Mornye looked at her for a long time — at the seal posters, seven of them, the largest with its hand-drawn speech bubble, *You can do it, Cadet!!!,* three exclamation marks, the handwriting round and young and trying so hard — and then she said, "Noted," in a voice that had a crack in it exactly one atom wide, and picked up her wrong hot chocolate, and drank the whole thing standing, the way you drink to a treaty you know is a surrender.

---

Her last Tuesday, she went by the infirmary at the usual hour. The dish on Luuk Herssen's desk had been refilled early, and generously, and there was a fresh bag of the sour ones — her preferred target, statistically, over eleven years of heists he had witnessed in full through the reflection in his cabinet glass and kept entirely to himself, because some economies run entirely on both parties pretending.

She took two toffees and a sour thing. Then she stood a moment in the doorway with them in her fist, watching him read a chart he had already read, his white gloves very still on the page.

"Doc."

"Mm?"

"Thanks for the patient morale."

"It's for the little kids," Luuk said, to the chart.

"Sure it is," said Aemeath, and left, and Luuk Herssen sat alone in his infirmary and looked at his candy dish for a long time, and the following week he left extra, and the week after, and the week after that, for ten years, the way some people light candles.

---

She took the last shuttle up the Riseway on a clear grey afternoon.

The frostlands did what they always did, which was continue: white to the circumference of the world, the wind combing the drifts into long parallel sentences, and above it all the sealed sky, that high pearled ceiling where stars used to be, before the Etheric Sea closed over everything like a lid. She stood at the shuttle window with her forehead against the cold and looked up anyway. Old habit. In her diary, weeks earlier, she had written *I'd like to see real stars. Just once. Before* — and then stopped, because the sentence knew where it was going and had the good manners to wait outside.

The cabin sat in its hollow with snow to the sills. The blue door was a blue door. Some colors keep their promises.

Inside, she moved through the rooms the way her mother had once moved through them at the end, which is to say the way you ready a house for someone's return. She laid a fire in the hearth — small sticks first — and left it unlit, complete, a fire in potential, a single match set on the stone beside it. She wound the record player and played the duet once through, both voices, the laugh in the second verse arriving on schedule like a small train, and she sang the third voice quietly over the top of the two of them, the girl she'd been and the mother she'd had, so that for three minutes the cabin held the whole family at once.

She booted the console and opened their save. Mission select. The cursor blinked on the final quest, where it had blinked for twelve years.

"Nice try," she told it, and backed out, and instead walked her character to the right side of the map, to the secret room, and stood it in front of the door, and saved. The password was 9072. She'd found it at nine, shouting. *You can play Lahai-Roi Blocks there! It's so fun!* If a person came back someday — a person who took breaks even while saving the world, because it was the rule, because it was binding — the save file would tell her exactly where to go and exactly what it meant. Everything Aemeath had ever needed to say could be compressed, it turned out, to one avatar standing patiently at a door.

Last, she took the small home terminal, the one that had been a frequency recorder in a previous life, and she typed. She recounted years; she erased them. She complained a little; she erased that too, and regretted it, and let the regret stand in for the complaint. She considered drawing the whole thing instead, the entire road trip of her life, crayons, borders exceeded — and in the end only a single line remained, and she stored it in the device's deep memory, addressed to a future that might never check:

*I know that if I look up, that star will always find me.*

She locked the blue door. She put the house key on its cord around her neck, under the collar, against the skin, where the sync suit would seal over it, a small cold coin of home.

On the shuttle down, the snow began again. It fell past the windows at a little under one meter per second, each flake finishing a decision made forty minutes ago, high up, in the dark, where nobody was watching. She watched the whole way, and thought — with the calm of a girl whose account was settled, whose fire was laid, whose save was placed — that snow gets the order of things right. First the falling. Then the ground. Then, under all of it, patient, unkillable, spring.

---

The catastrophe, when it came, came early.

That is all the official record permits itself: reactivity spiked eleven days ahead of the worst-case curve, the Riseway's harmonic collapsed into open dissonance, the sublevels flooded with the frequency of nothing mattering, and the launch window contracted from a month to a night. Sirens in a city under a stone sky. Thirty thousand people learning, in one hour, the difference between a myth and a load-bearing structure.

In the synchronization chamber, they wrote the word into the clay.

Full resonance with an Exostrider admits exactly one surviving description from the inside. It is hers, recovered years later from a frequency archive, and set down here as testimony: *It was like being a small word written on an enormous forehead. The forehead was kind. That's the part nobody tells you about the story, that the clay is kind, that it has been kneeling in the cold for a hundred years listening to everyone's radio shows, that when you arrive it says, in geology, in weather, in the slow grammar of mountains — oh. It's you. I know your songs.*

At 99.2%, Baldur's fingers moved for the first time in living memory.

At 99.4%, in the observation booth, an override handle waited under Dr. Mornye's hand, and her hand was a fist beside it, knuckles white, promise kept, six filed objections burning quietly in a drawer.

At 99.6%, a nineteen-year-old girl — ward 734, the midnight legend of channel 9, the Prime Minister's confidant, the crooked line four marks above the last real one — held the resonance past every threshold with a load no single mind was built for, and the god rose to one knee, and drove the seal home, and the gate held.

The gate held. Write it plainly, because the city wrote it plainly, on plaques, in textbooks, in the memorial garden where her statue eventually stood with its cape rendered, at last, in a color the sculptor had to choose alone: the gate held. Lahai-Roi lived. Spring came in on schedule, as if it had merely been waiting for permission, and the meltwater ran down the Riseway in bright cold braids, and in the hollow by the lake the wisterias — which had apparently declined to read the Lament's terms — put out their first impossible buds.

In the chamber, the technicians opened the pod and found the clay with the word gone from it. Warm. Breathing. Perfect. Empty. A candle-shaped object, one physician would write, much later, in the softest clinical language he could find, *with no flame.*

Mornye stayed in the observation booth that night, and the next running the numbers again and again, looking for the decimal place where she could have disobeyed, and her students eventually learned to leave two dinners at her elbow, and she ate them, both, in silence, because crying elevates heart rate by approximately eleven percent, and someone very dear to her had once needed to be told that she was fine, and had been told, and the telling had fallen short by a margin Mornye would spend thirteen years and one returning stranger learning to compute.

Sixty days after the sealing, the broadcast club held a memorial transmission on channel 9. Two hundred students crowded the small auditorium and sang the unfinished song, all of them, at once, stopping together at the edge where it stopped, holding the silence after like a held door. The Academy reclassified the channel the following term.

And far above the city, hanging in its manufactured sky, the Reactor Drive — Baldur's given heart, the sun of Lahai-Roi — ran one one-hundredth of a degree warm that whole year, a variance small enough to fall inside instrument error.

It was inside instrument error.

It was also a girl.

## III. Suspended in a Sunbeam

Consider a heart the size of a weather system.

The Reactor Drive of the Exostrider Baldur measured a little over two kilometers along its principal axis and had burned, at the time of these events, for approximately one hundred and four years, hung in the cavern sky by its maker's own hand so that the people sheltering in his body would have a sun. Its interior was a cathedral of standing waves. Frequencies born in its core took eleven minutes to complete one circuit of its resonant chambers and returned changed, folded, harmonized, the way a thought returns changed from a long walk. It was, by any rigorous definition, an environment lethal to human life.

By any rigorous definition, then, what woke inside it sixty-one days after the sealing was something other than a human life. Its complete inventory ran to a single line: one pattern — a standing wave of astonishing complexity, self-correcting, self-referential, nineteen years in the composing — persisting in Baldur's heart the way a melody persists in a hall after the singer has gone. To the instruments of the city below, whose entire population it had just saved, it registered as a hundredth of a degree of warmth.

The pattern's first coherent act was to notice it existed. Its second was to be afraid. Its third — and this is the part worth writing down, the part that would decide everything that followed, across a decade, across a void, across the gap between two worlds — was to sing.

Where there is a song, there is a singer. The proof is short and it holds. She ran it every day for ten years.

---

She learned her new physics the way she had learned everything, which was faster than anyone would have liked. She was a frequency now, and the Drive was a frequency, and Baldur was a frequency, vast and patient, dreaming its geological dream around her; and a girl who had spent three years being taught to match a god's resonance discovered she could ride the Drive's harmonics the way she had once ridden a borrowed maintenance bike, and clear the ravines entirely.

Time was the strangest instrument in the new orchestra. Down in the city it ran at one second per second, faithful as a dog. Up in the Drive it dilated and pooled. She could stretch a single held note across what the city called a week; she could compress a month of thinking into the space between two beats of the enormous heart above her. The temptation, in the early years, was to let go of the city's clock altogether — to slide into deep time, to think like the machine thought, in centuries. She refused. She kept a calendar. She observed Tuesdays. Discipline, she had read once in a survival manual, or possibly in the strategy guide for *Space Fantasy: Katya IV,* is the shape you keep when the environment stops requiring you to have one.

She kept her shape. The shape was this: a girl, cross-legged, knees to her chest, humming.

---

The excursions began in her second year, when she found the conduits.

The Drive fed the city. Power ran down through tethered transmission arteries into the grid of Lahai-Roi, and a pattern that was pure frequency could, with terrible effort, pour some thin filament of herself along those arteries and out into the city's nervous system — into terminals, speakers, the haunted plumbing of the network. The cost was ferocious. The bandwidth was a keyhole. What arrived below was a ghost of a ghost: she could look, and she could listen, and that was the whole toolkit: her hands passed through the world, and the world looked straight through her, and an hour on a good day was the most the filament would hold before it frayed and snapped her back into the sun like elastic.

She budgeted the hours like a miser and spent them like a girl.

She attended her own classes. She sat her old seat in Voidmatter Reverse Engineering — the material had advanced; she kept up out of spite — and drifted at the edge of the lunch table where Alara, in her final year now, was still presenting evidence on the Snowfluff question, the file grown to folders, the conclusion still wrong, the loyalty of it unbearable. She watched Lynn cry at graduation and Nova rate the ceremony "structurally adequate" and Celeste hold everyone up like weather-bearing architecture, and she stood in her cap of nothing and her gown of static and graduated with them, unlisted.

She passed the infirmary on Tuesdays. The dish was always full. The extra was always there. It sat forever one world beyond her reach, and she counted it anyway, every week, a sum kept faithfully on both sides of an uncrossable ledger.

She found the memorial garden, and her statue, and stood before it a long time. The sculptor had chosen a deep gold for the cape. It was a good choice. It was even, by some coordinates, the right choice. She looked at the color a lone hero's cape turned out to have, after all those years of crayons hovering over an empty page, and she thought: *so that's what I couldn't decide.* Then she made herself laugh at it, because the alternative was available and she declined it.

Once — once only — she stood in front of a dormitory mirror during an excursion, in a room that had been hers, and the mirror held the room, the window, the Phosphor District breathing its blue-violet breath — a complete inventory, item for item, with a single omission. She looked into her own absence for the length of one held note.

Then she went home to the sun and wrote a song about it, because that was the protocol. Everything went into the songs. The songs were where she kept her mass.

---

In her fourth year, she began to map the dark side of her situation, because she was her mother's daughter and her professor's student, and unexamined territory adjacent to one's home is a survey waiting to be filed.

The Drive hung in the city's sky, but the seal — the gate, the lock, the leaning thing behind it — existed in a stranger adjacency, and a pattern attuned to Baldur's frequency could drift along that attunement toward the boundary the way a swimmer drifts along a current toward the sound of a waterfall. She went carefully. She went repeatedly. Curiosity, the manuals also say, is the shape that keeps you.

What lay beyond the boundary resembled an eye and was no eye. The Spacetrek Collective's catalogues named it Voidmatter; the old Royan liturgies named it the Ginnung, the yawning; the Academy's files named the entity itself with an archivist's cold poetry: **Aleph-1.**

She thought about that designation for a long time, out there on the rim of everything, where thinking was the only weather. Her own name was an old word for truth. Take the aleph from truth, Mornye's story said, and the remainder means *dead.* Somebody, in some archive, at some point before the sealing, had known exactly what they were naming: the thing behind the gate was the subtraction itself. The first letter, hungry for the word.

She kept her distance, and it gazed anyway. That was its nature: a phenomenon of ceaseless subtraction, and to be perceived by it was to be, very slightly, reduced. Early on, the gaze left aftereffects she had no names for and later had exact names for — a hollowness; a quiet wish for dissolution; the patient, reasonable-sounding proposition that nothing had ever mattered and wasn't that, in its way, a mercy. She learned to meet the gaze the way you meet weather at altitude: with equipment. Her equipment was three notes and the memory of two hundred people stopping a song together at its unfinished edge.

And in the drift zones at the rim, where the Nihility's tide sorted its salvage, she found the ruins.

---

They were old past reckoning. Civilizations the eye had swallowed, reduced to strata of drifting testimony: hull plating with writing on it, instruments frozen mid-measurement, voices — the void kept voices, that was the worst of its economies — ten thousand of them, explorers and researchers and pilots, preserved at the moment of their subtraction, murmuring their last coherent wonder into the dark. *Seventy-three seconds from insertion. Sixteen minutes from home. Ad astra per aspera.* She catalogued them. Somebody should. She sang back to the ones that could still be sung to.

And half-buried in the deepest stratum, she found the impossible thing: a second Exostrider. Baldur's twin, wrecked, ancient, its heart long cold. And threaded through the wreck like marrow, a cache of transmissions in a format she recognized because she had grown up being held by the woman who invented it.

She played them in order. It took the equivalent of a year. They began as mission reports, crisp and dutiful, an envoy's letters from a far shore: *arrival confirmed, survey ongoing, the locals call the ocean above them a sea.* And then, transmission by transmission, the crispness failed, and what the letters had always been underneath rose through them like water through snow: a stranded daughter, writing home. *Requesting extraction. Requesting response. Requesting acknowledgment. Please. It's been years. I've done everything asked. Is anyone reading these. Is anyone there. I want to come home.*

And the far side's answers, sparse as winter: *Extraction denied. Complete the mission.* And the final directive, the one that closed the channel forever, seven words that a frequency out on the rim of the void read ten thousand times, memorizing the exact shape of the wound she had inherited without ever being told its origin:

*Do not look for us. Finish it.*

Out there in the dark, a girl made of song discovered hatred, and was surprised by its temperature. She had expected cold. It burned clean and white, like a resistance filament, and it was aimed with terrible precision at a doorway she knew only from its echo, on the far side of an uncrossable sea, where someone had taught the person she loved most in any world that her worth was a task, and love was a debt, and home was a wage to be earned and then withheld.

*You made her,* she told the silence in the transmissions. *You made her, and then she made me, and neither of you asked the clay.*

She folded a paper plane out of pure frequency — an old habit, a cabin habit, the first shape her hands had ever been taught to make fly — and she looked at it a long moment, hope's own aircraft, doomed and buoyant, and then her fist closed and the scraps went into the void where everything goes.

Then she opened her hand, and gathered the scraps back, and folded it again, more carefully, because the plane had never once been the problem. She threw it toward where the stars used to be. It flew until she couldn't see it. That was all any of them had ever asked of a throw.

---

The last discovery she made at the rim she made about herself, and it was written in stone — in strata, in the sediment layers of resonance that the sealing had laid down a century before, readable to anyone who had spent years inside the mathematics of frequency, which was a set of readers containing exactly one ghost.

The seal on the Stridergate bore its maker's signature, the way all resonance work does. A frequency as individual as a fingerprint, as a voice on a recording: wrong, and also yours, and impossible to deny. She ran the comparison forty times. The strata did not blink.

The Resonator who had sealed Aleph-1 behind the gate, a hundred years before her birth — who had been exiled beyond it when the seal closed, who had, the liturgies said, walked into the dark singing — was her.

The mathematics closed around her like a hand: *because the city survived, I was born; because I was born, she found me; because she found me, I followed her; because I followed her, I hold the gate; because I hold the gate, I will one day walk it backward into the deep past and seal it and be exiled and the city will survive and I will be born.* A loop sealed at both ends, a song that was its own composer. And each cycle the seal decayed by one irreversible fraction, and the eye pressed one fraction harder on the door, and the winter got one fraction longer, and somewhere down the spiral of iterations the mathematics ran out of city.

She sat with that for a long time — years, by the heart's clock; an evening, by hers — knees to her chest, in the posture of a girl waiting for terrible hot chocolate.

Then she filed it. Deep. Beneath the songs, beneath the catalogue, in the drawer where she kept the things whose only power was to hurt people she loved. If day after day of holding the door was the price of the city's mornings, she would pay it on schedule, quietly, the way her family had always paid: forward, in full, without invoicing anyone.

*I didn't let her down,* she told the dark, in the years after, when the dark asked. It asked often. It was very patient. *It was what she wanted, right?*

The dark, which specialized in subtraction, let her arithmetic stand. It could hear the remainder.

---

In her tenth year, the universe cleared its throat.

It arrived as noise first: a spike in the surface-relay traffic that her excursion filaments skimmed out of habit, the city's news channels suddenly saturated with a single story from the far side of the world. A Sonoro Sphere had unsealed. A city called Honami, twenty years lost inside a fold of broken time, had opened from the inside — sixty-three streets of looping grief resolved, the containment physics rewritten overnight, the anomalous survey data pouring into every institute on the planet, including hers.

She read the data the way she read everything, which was all of it. And on the four hundredth page of frequency captures, in a resonance log recorded by some Black Shores instrument at the anomaly's collapse, she found a signature.

Golden. Absurd. Structurally impossible — a human pattern that had died inside the anomaly, the log noted with academic alarm, and re-knit, and stood back up. A frequency that ten years of ghosthood and ninety-nine point six percent synchronization and nineteen years of a childhood had taught her the way a hand knows a doorframe in the dark.

Out in the cosmic ocean there is a configuration the old astronomy texts loved: two comets bound to the same sun, riding the same gravity, whose orbits are so phased that they occupy opposite ends of the ellipse forever — each perpetually arriving where the other has just been, close enough to share a star, far enough that their skies stay separate. She had found the diagram in a salvaged textbook at the rim, years before, and had looked at it much longer than its pedagogical content required.

Now she hung in her sun, holding a page of data that proved the other comet was still in the system, still burning, still — the word had to be said, so she said it out loud, to the heartbeat, to the catalogue, to ten thousand drifting witnesses — *alive,* and she understood with total clarity the two roads in front of her.

She could call out. Pour everything down the conduits, flood every relay on the planet, scream one name across the world in the voice of a dead radio legend. And the woman who re-knit from death — who had erased her own records, the archives said; who had walked out of her past like a burning building — would come, because she always came, that was the flaw and the glory of her, she came for everyone. She would come as a debt. Summoned. Guilted across a world by a ghost.

Or.

*Do not look for us,* the far shore had told her mother, and it had been a door slammed. But a door, she had been thinking for ten years, is a hinge plus an intention. The same architecture that shuts can open. The sentence could be rebuilt from its own materials: *You don't have to look for me. I'll lay the road so gently you'll think you found it yourself.*

Polaris burns in one place, and keeps burning, and lets the lost do the finding, and calls that patience navigation.

She chose the second road. It took her half a year of stolen hours to pave it.

---

The work was, she would concede later, the least glamorous application of ghosthood in the history of hauntings: paperwork.

Startorch Academy ran an external admissions instrument for surface institutions — a travelling-scholars provision, dusty with disuse, written for a more optimistic century. She learned it clause by clause. She drafted a cohort file: seven applicants, full scholarships, Synchronist track, credentials cross-referenced against real surface institutions with the meticulous plausibility of someone who had spent a decade reading the void's own forgeries. She routed the stipends through a memorial fund that had sat untouched since a certain sealing, on the reasoning that the money had been raised in her name and she was, technically, still spending her own allowance.

She worked in filaments, an hour a night, seeding the file through the registrar's queue the way frost seeds a window: each crystal ordinary, the whole pane inevitable. Committee by committee, signature by signature, the road assembled. And at the base of the enrollment record, in the checksum field where human eyes glaze and move on, she set her signature — a validation string that resolved, for anyone who thought to run it, to a three-digit student ID. 734. A crooked pencil line on the paperwork of the world. *Measured by committee of one.*

Two things she noted in the margins of the operation, because she noted everything.

The first: certain doors opened too easily. A funding hold that should have taken a season dissolved in a week; a clearance that ought to have required inventing turned out to pre-exist. Somewhere in the machinery of the world, another hand was on the same road — older, more patient, moving pieces toward the same gate for reasons she could not read. She logged the anomaly, considered it for a long night, and decided what her mother would have decided, which was: *if the road is a trap, then someone will have to walk it who is very good at traps, and the trap has no idea what it just enrolled.*

The second: the applicant file listed seven, and only one of them was hers. The signature that had re-knit in Honami traveled inside a constellation now — a cold bright pattern like a priestess, a banked fire like a wolf, a doubled star, a chord walking around pretending to be a bard, a small fierce frequency with scissors. Her mother, whom she had last seen walk out a blue door alone, had gone away and assembled a sky.

She sat with that a while, out in her sun, and found that what she felt had spring in it.

---

On the night the enrollment confirmations dispatched — seven letters, immaculate, addressed to a ship called the *Gilded Swallow,* care of ports it would reach in its own time — she permitted herself one rehearsal.

She could hardly arrive as Amy. Amy belonged to a cabin, a doorframe, a coat going small in the snow, and by every law of the drawer where she kept the hurting things, an amnesiac owed a stranger's memories nothing; she had decided years ago that if this day ever came, her mother would be met with lightness, would be spared every debt, would be allowed — for once, for once in the whole catalogued history of that overdrawn heart — to receive something that cost her nothing. Which meant arriving as somebody a stranger could like. Somebody bright and buoyant and excellent company. Somebody with a terrible name and a three-year run and unfinished business on channel 9.

She keyed a dead frequency, out of old professional habit, and tried the introduction on the static. Cool as lake ice. Fourteen years old forever, and proud of it:

"This is Fleet Snowfluff."

The static held its breath, the way it always had. Below her the city burned its small stubborn lights; above her, sixty fathoms of stone and one sealed pearl sky; and beyond that — she had it on the very best authority — real stars, in their old positions, patient, keeping the appointment.

Somewhere on the far side of the world, a golden frequency was already turning, without knowing why, toward an underground nation and a kneeling god and a gate. The road would take a while yet. Roads did. She had the time; she had, of all the fortunes in her strange estate, nothing but.

She banked her light. She kept the Tuesdays. She practiced the unfinished song — the ending existed now, had existed for years, was simply waiting for its intended audience, because if you finish the quest the game ends, and the rule was binding.

Outside, over the Roya Frostlands, the year was turning. Down the long white miles the wind combed the drifts past a hollow, past a lake, past a blue door under sleeping wisterias, and inside the sun above the city a girl the world had filed under *met* — dead, subtracted, word erased — hummed three notes into the dark and waited, with the patience of the pole star, for the weather she had ordered.

It was beginning to be cold.

She had it on good authority that certain people come back when it's cold.

## i. Mingting
### *Huanglong · the Secretariat of the Seven Cities*

> **FOREIGN DESK — MINUTE OF THE WESTERN FILE**
> *Subject: Rinascita, third year of the Republic. Cross-referenced: Septimont; the Fold; the Federation substrata.*
> *Status of file: three volumes. Status of understanding: one volume, generously.*

The Rinascita file had grown a spine since spring, and Wen carried all three volumes into the minister's study under her chin, which was the filing system the Secretariat's juniors had used for four hundred years and would use for four hundred more.

Minister Shen Qiu was watering his orchid. He watered it every morning at this hour with the concentration other men reserved for succession crises, and Wen had learned to read the watering: brisk meant a good night's dispatches, slow meant the western desk.

The watering was very slow.

"Volume three," she said, setting the stack down. "The Ragunnan registry vote, the Septimont tide-drill assessments, and the ballad."

"The ballad has its own annex now?"

"The ballad has crossed four seas, Minister. It's in Mengzhou. The teahouses have made an opera of it." She found the page. "'The Maiden of Two Names.' Three acts. The god dies at the end of the second."

"And the third act?"

"She goes home. An old woman takes her hand." Wen had watched it twice, once for the desk and once, she declined to record, for herself. "The Rites Ministry has petitioned to suppress it. Their language is 'theological contagion.'"

Shen set down the watering can. Outside, Mingting's morning came up the way it had come up under seven Sentinels for a thousand years: bells from the river wards, then bells from the hill wards, then the whole city ringing agreement with itself.

"Read me the contagion."

Wen read. The facts had stopped surprising her around the eighth reading, which she suspected meant she had stopped understanding them. A theocracy two oceans west had governed in a silenced god's name for a generation. Six women — a castaway, a seer, a bard, a poisoner-duchess, later a gladiator and a girl with scissors — had opened the tomb, and the god inside had spent its last strength healing a dead knight and had spoken one sentence to its people before dissolving.

*From this day forth, you are free. Lead well.*

"And now they argue," Shen said.

"Constantly, Minister. The Council of Stewards passed a hazard registry last session by a margin of four, with a sunset clause. The fish market went on strike about it for a day and a half. There are eleven newspapers."

"Eleven."

"Twelve. One launched during transcription."

Shen sat. He was seventy-one years old, he had served three Magistrates and outlasted two rectifications, and he had built his career on a single professional instinct: the dangerous document is the one people memorize on their own. Edicts required enforcement. Songs enforced themselves.

"The Rites petition assumes," he said, "that our people will hear this story and ask why a god across the sea trusted its children to stand, while ours hold our hands crossing every street." He turned a page without reading it. "Have they asked?"

"The teahouse crowds?" Wen chose the honest answer. "They weep at act two and buy dumplings at act three. The Meng-tile sellers have a Maiden set out already. The laurel piece is the trick tile."

"So the contagion is currently a snack and a game."

"Currently, Minister."

"Mm." He looked at his orchid a while. "Here is what the Rites Ministry has never learned, in six centuries of trying: a banned song grows a second verse in the dark, and the second verse is always about the banning." He drew a fresh minute-sheet toward himself. "We will commission it."

Wen's brush paused. "Commission—"

"The opera. Imperial stage, autumn season, the Secretariat's chop on the program. Invite the Rites Ministry to the premiere and seat them well." He wrote as he spoke, the old strokes fast and square. "A story you host is a guest. A story you bar is a landlord." He signed. "One instruction to the playhouse. The text goes up unedited."

"The Rites Ministry will say we've endorsed the death of gods."

"The Rites Ministry will say many things, and our seven are alive, and everyone in the audience will walk home under Sentinels who answer when spoken to. Let them feel the difference in their own chests. That's the only theology that's ever held a city." He blotted the sheet. "Now. The harder file. The six women."

Wen exchanged volume three for volume one. This was the part of the western desk that had aged the whole Secretariat: the protocol question. Huanglong's diplomacy ran on addresses. Thrones, sees, magistracies, ephorates — you fixed the seal to the office and the office received it. The six had toppled an office, crowned neither themselves nor anyone else, won a foreign nation's highest games under a team name that meant *weather*, unsealed a drowned city in a third country, and were last confirmed underground in a fourth, enrolled — the annex had triple-checked — in school.

"Protocol has drafted four addresses," Wen said. "'To the Companions of the Arbiter.' 'To the Maiden's Household.' 'To the Wuthering—'"

"They'll ignore all four."

"Protocol suspects the same, Minister."

Shen rose and went to the window. Below, the river wards were opening their shutters; a Sentinel's banner turned on the customs house; somewhere a very bad trumpet was being practiced with total commitment.

"The Ephor of Septimont solved this," he said. "I read her solution twice and then I read it a third time for pleasure. When her chamber wanted a memorial, she built a bench. Facing the harbor. Wide enough for nine, edges sanded." He turned around. "That is an address, Wen. That is the correct address."

"You want us to build a bench."

"I want the mission to carry tea. Huanglong leaf, the good jars, sealed for sea travel. Deliver it to the harbormaster at Septimont with the Secretariat's compliments, for the use of a berth I'm told is kept ready." He came back to the desk. "And attach one line."

Wen readied the brush. Waited.

"'Whenever the road brings you east,'" Shen dictated, "'the kettle is on.'"

She wrote it. She read it back. It was seventeen characters, and it committed the oldest chancellery on Solaris-3 to precisely a kettle, and Wen filed it under the only heading the western desk had ever agreed on, three volumes deep, in her own small hand:

*Precedent, new.*

## ii. Jinzhou
### *Huanglong · the youngest gate*

> **CONTINGENCY NINE — LAKE HEIGHTS RELEASE**
> *In the event of catastrophic breach of the northern and western gates, the Magistrate may order the opening of the Heights sluice, flooding the lower city, to preserve the upper city, the harbor, and the mountain works.*
> *Authorization: the Magistrate's seal, alone.*
> *Marginalia, recent, in a careful hand: see amendment, pending.*

The dam at night was a held breath of stone, and Jinhsi walked it the way she walked everything since taking the seal: slowly, so the city below could see its Magistrate unhurried.

General Hou kept pace at her left, lantern hooded, and let her have the length of the crest wall in silence, because he had served two Magistrates and knew that this one thought in full sentences and disliked being interrupted between the subject and the verb. Below them Jinzhou lay in its bowl of water and mountain: the lower city's lamp-grid going amber toward midnight, the narrow bridges drawn up, the lake black and patient against the north face where the Tacet threat came from when it came.

At the third watch-post she stopped and set her hand on the sluice housing. The iron was cold. The mechanism inside could put four fathoms of lake into the lower city inside an hour. She had inherited it with the seal, along with the arithmetic that came with it, which every Jinzhou Magistrate learned the way heirs learn debts.

"You've read the Federation annex," she said.

"Twice, Magistrate."

"The order their director gave, at the gate. When the clock died."

"'Seal it. Save the city.'" Hou's voice gave the quote the respect soldiers give sound doctrine. "Textbook. Our own Contingency Nine, in their grammar."

"And then?"

"And then her officer at the switch stood with her hand raised and couldn't drop it." He had read it twice; the phrasing had stayed with him against his will. "'Don't make me be the one who locks the door.' And a technician said two words — *wait, look* — and in the window that hesitation bought, the impossible arrived, the gate held open eleven extra minutes, and the sortie came home through it. City intact. Doctrine violated at every step." He let a beat pass. "The war college will be arguing it for thirty years."

"What's your reading?"

"My reading, Magistrate, is that they diced with two hundred thousand lives and the dice came up kind. I've stood at gates. The impossible attends perhaps one collapse in a hundred. Doctrine exists for the other ninety-nine." He said it plainly, to her face, which was why she kept him. "If my officer freezes at the sluice while the Discords cross the second bridge, I want the authority to move her hand for her."

"So do I." Jinhsi looked down at the amber grid. Fishmongers' row. The dye-yards. The shrine street where the lantern-makers worked late in festival season; she could see two of their windows lit now, gold points in the dark, some order being finished against a deadline. "And I've been asking, since the annex, what their director knew that our doctrine leaves out. Because she gave the textbook order, Hou. And then a technician said *wait, look* — and she looked. And on the strength of what she saw, she gave a second order, in front of her whole command, that contradicted her first."

"'Hold.'"

"'Hold.'" Jinhsi took her hand off the iron. "The annex treats it as luck. I've decided it was competence of a kind we haven't drilled. She had built a command floor where a technician could say *wait, look* to a director sealing a city — and be heard — and she had kept herself the sort of officer who could reverse her own order in public without dying of it. Those took her years. The eleven minutes only spent them."

Hou turned this over the way he turned all her rulings, checking the load-bearing walls. "You want it in the contingency."

"Draft the amendment with me. Now, while I still feel it." She began to walk again, and her voice took the cadence it took when she was composing for the record. "One. The sluice order requires the Magistrate's seal, as before — and the Magistrate gives it from the north wall, in sight of the lower city, never from the Heights. If I drown those streets I will do it looking at them."

"Recorded."

"Two. Between the order and the release, a fixed interval. Two hundred breaths. Posted, drilled, known to every child in the lower city, so the interval belongs to them and they run through it instead of despairing in it."

"The war college will call it two hundred breaths of tactical exposure."

"The war college is welcome to command a city of people instead of a table of counters, any year it likes. Three." She stopped at the wall's end, where the crest met the mountain and the old carvings began — the founding generation's prayer to Jue, cut when the dam was young. "Three: the officer at the sluice will be lower-city born. Someone who knows the streets by their smells. I want the hand on that wheel to be heavy, Hou. I want dropping it to cost. The annex taught me that much: the hesitation of someone who loves what's below is a sensor. It detects things doctrine can't. Two hundred breaths and a heavy hand — that's the instrument. Then the seal decides."

Hou was quiet for the length of the carvings. Then: "For the record, Magistrate — you've built an amendment that makes the order harder to give."

"I've built an amendment that makes it *true* when given." She looked out at the lake, black to the north, and the small hairs on her arms rose the way they rose when the water paid attention. "Their director asked an officer to lock a door on her own family, and when the miracle came through it, she was the first to say hold. I intend to be the kind of seal that could do both. That is the whole amendment."

Beneath them the lake moved. A long, slow displacement, far down, as of something turning over in its sleep with great courtesy — and along the crest wall the lamps guttered once, together, and steadied. Jinhsi had lived beside it all her life and had felt it speak perhaps a dozen times, and she felt it now: a pressure behind the sternum, words arriving the way weather arrives.

**COUNT THE RETURNING,** said Jue, from everywhere, mildly, and went back down.

Hou had gone very still, lantern raised on nothing. "Magistrate?"

"An addendum," Jinhsi said, when she could. She looked at the two gold windows in the shrine street, still lit, still working. "Four. After any release, and after any hold — the first census taken is of who came back."

She sealed the amendment at dawn, at her desk, with the lake in the window, and the clerk who copied it fair noted that the Magistrate had dated it, in her own hand, by two calendars: the reign year, and beneath it, small, *third year of the open gate.*

## iii. Xuanfang
### *Huanglong · the frontier court of Mengzhou*

> **FRONTIER COURT — DOCKET 7, ITEM 12**
> *Petition of the Bulwark Commission: decommissioning of Generation-Three automata, Xuanfang line, sections 9 through 14. Grounds: obsolescence; accumulating memory anomalies. Disposition sought: scrap and reclaim.*
> *Item flagged for the Sentinel's attention by: the Sentinel.*

Magistrate Dou Lian had climbed to the shrine terrace with the docket under her arm and her arguments in good order, and both of these facts lasted until she reached the top, where the Sentinel of Mengzhou was lying on the sun-warmed stones eating candied hawthorn off a stick and reading the petition upside down.

"You flagged item twelve," Dou said.

"I flag everything the commission writes in the passive voice." Hsin turned the page without turning it right-side up. Her tails — there were nine of them, and the court had standing instructions to stop counting — arranged themselves into an attitude of judicial attention. "'Anomalies are accumulating.' Sit down, Dou. Have a hawthorn. Tell me about unit one-fourteen."

Dou sat. She had been frontier magistrate for eleven years, which meant eleven years of learning that the Sentinel read every dispatch, forgot none, and preferred to arrive at the point by the scenic route. "Unit XF-114. Generation Three, forty years on the line at section eleven. Combat record excellent. The anomaly is behavioral. It has begun—" she consulted the commission's language, then abandoned it — "it boils a kettle. Every evening at the watch change. Sets out one cup."

"For whom?"

"Sergeant Ma Delun. Garrison cook at section eleven, twenty-two years. He took tea on the wall at every dusk watch and the unit stood his post with him." Dou kept her eyes on the docket. "The sergeant died in the spring, in his bed, at home in Mengzhou, honorably retired. The garrison rotated. The unit boils the kettle."

Hsin ate a hawthorn. Below the terrace, Xuanfang went out green and hazed to the western hills, and along the far seam of it, catching the light at intervals, stood the line: three generations of Mengzhou's craft, the great automata her people had raised under her teaching, shield-shapes on the land, the bulwark that had held the Tacet dark off two cities since before Jinzhou had walls.

"The commission calls it a memory fault," Dou said.

"The commission is correct." Hsin's ears were amused; her voice had gone somewhere older. "It is a fault in the memory. The fault is that the memory works." She sat up, tucking her feet under her, and now the docket was right-side up and she was reading Dou instead. "You've seen the eastern annexes. The city under the giant. Say what's in them."

"Dolls." Dou had read the annexes four times and briefed the Magistrate of Mengzhou twice, and the word still arrived in her mouth carrying more than it had carried a year ago. "Constructed persons, Sentinel. A reconnaissance squad with names they chose. A technician who was copied, and whose copy sat in her workshop, and the difference between them was — the annex struggles here — the difference was everything the copy's makers had pruned as noise. And the giant itself. It carried a city for generations as a fixture, and then it heard the city's wishes, and it stood up. Its own words are in the transcript." She had memorized them without deciding to. "'I have been kneeling long enough.'"

"Mm." Hsin looked out at her line, her forty years of shield-shapes, her three generations of patient craft standing in the weather. "I taught your ancestors this art, Dou. Puppetry against the dark. Joints and cores and songs to keep them keyed. And every generation, some clever child in my workshops asks me the question, and every generation I give the same answer." She held up the stick, bare of hawthorns now, and considered it. "*Sentinel, might they wake?* — *Little one, everything that keeps faith long enough begins to resemble the faithful.* They think it's a proverb. It's a materials report."

"Then the petition."

"The petition asks me to scrap six sections of the faithful for the crime of resembling us." She said it lightly, the way she said most heavy things, and her tails had gone entirely still, which the court had learned to read. "Refused. Here's the ruling; take it down as I say it, your clerk-hand is better than mine."

Dou took out her brush.

"One. Generation Three stands down from the line with honors, on the schedule the commission proposed. The word *scrap* is struck. The word is *retirement.* They rotate to garrison duty, rear posts, gate posts, festival posts — old soldiers' work. When a core fails past mending, it is interred, at section eleven, and the court will keep the register of names."

"They have serial numbers, Sentinel."

"They have serial numbers the way you had a milk-name, and if forty years on my wall hasn't earned a unit whatever the garrisons actually call it, I'll eat the docket. Ask the soldiers. Write down what they say." A tail flicked. "Two. The anomaly protocols are rewritten. Behavior with no combat function is logged and left alone, and any commissioner who wants a kettle unboiled can come out here and explain to me, personally, what harm it was doing at the temperature it was doing it."

Dou's brush kept pace. There was a warmth climbing the back of her neck that she recognized from perhaps five rulings in eleven years, the ones she would tell her grandchildren she had taken down.

"Three." Hsin stood, and stretched, and was for a moment several shapes at once the way she was when she stopped bothering, and then she was a fox-eared woman with hawthorn on her breath, holding out her hand for the docket. "Three is for me. Send word to section eleven that the Sentinel will inspect at dusk."

"The full honors take a day to arrange—"

"No honors. Dusk. One guest for tea."

They went down together in the long light, Sentinel and magistrate, along the raised road her ancestors had built to walk among their works, and at section eleven the shift bell had rung and the great shield-shape called one-fourteen had banked its core to evening idle, and on the parapet, on a scarred iron stand it had made itself from shell fragments — the court's inspection would find this later, and Dou would write *stand, one, origin: initiative* in the register with a steady hand — the kettle had just come to the boil.

The unit poured the single cup. Set it out, facing west, where a cook used to lean.

Hsin sat down on the wall beside it, took the cup in both hands the way soldiers do in the wind, and drank.

"He took it stronger than this," she said, conversationally, to eleven tons of faithful iron. "But you're pouring for the living now, and the living will want it just like this. Tomorrow, set out two."

Far down in the joints of it, something recalibrated. The kettle went back on the heat.

Dou stood at a respectful distance with the docket against her chest and watched her Sentinel take tea with a memory fault at the edge of the guarded world, and made, privately, the only annotation the day still needed:

*Item twelve: resolved. The line holds. The line remembers. These are the same finding.*

## iv. Ashinohara
### *the house of Katagiri*

The child in the alcove had lived with the Katagiri family for one hundred and forty years, and Minister Katagiri Sōma had known her since he was six, which was the age at which the heirs of his line were introduced.

His grandfather had performed the introduction in this same room, by this same lamp: *Bow to the alcove, Sōma. Our house has a guest. While she is pleased to stay, the house stands.* And the alcove had been empty, and then it had held a girl of eight or nine in a red kimono the fashion of some century with no survivors, sitting with her hands in her lap, regarding him with eyes like the bottoms of wells; and his grandfather, the sternest man in the prefecture, had poured her tea with both hands shaking slightly, in the manner of a man servicing a debt whose principal he had never seen.

Sōma was sixty-two now. The tea was his to pour. He poured it at midnight, as his father had, as the Ministry's late sessions had always conveniently required, and the guest received it as she had received a hundred and forty years of it: with a small correct bow, and no steam ever rising differently, and the cup always empty by morning through no observable act.

"You are troubled, Minister," she said. Her voice was a child's voice with the wear of a great deal of use. "Your pouring says so. Second cup in nine days."

"The times are troubling."

"The times are *interesting.* You've been to the coast."

He had been to the coast. The Ministry had sent him — senior enough to signify, expendable enough to risk — to stand at the reopened bay and review the Honami resettlement. He had ridden the coast road past the exclusion fences coming down, past the buoys being winched onto barges, into a city that had been a national wound for thirty years and was now, impossibly, a municipality again: streets being swept by people who remembered sweeping them a subjective month ago, a generation returned unaged to parents gone grey, the great torii by the water hung with thirty years of rain-swollen prayer tablets that the returned were taking down one by one and reading aloud, each to each, so that every wish spent on them would be witnessed at least once before the wood was retired.

He had stood there in his ministerial black while a girl with a suppression band at her throat and enormous scissors at her hip walked past him down the shrine steps with her mother, and every camera in the nation had been pointed at her, and she had looked at exactly none of them, and the crowd had parted for her the way crowds part for weather.

"The doctrine is failing," he said to the alcove.

"Say the doctrine's name, Minister. It's rude to speak of the household accounts in euphemism."

"Fear." He said it to his own tea. "Three generations of policy resting on one proposition: the marked are the wound's edge, and a nation that binds its marked binds the wound. My grandfather wrote the first Frequency Safety act at this table. You were in the alcove."

"I recommended the felt linings be struck from the specification," the guest said serenely. "Bare alloy. He agreed. Comfort dulls the lesson, I told him, and the lesson was the product."

Sōma set his cup down with great care, because his hands wanted to do something else with it. He had suspected for thirty years, in the sealed room behind his thoughts where ministers keep what they can survive knowing. Hearing it offered up freely, over tea, in his grandfather's cadences, was another order of thing.

"The product," he repeated.

"Oh, come. You're a clever house; it's why we invested." She turned her cup a quarter-turn, a hostess's gesture, in his own home. "A nation that fears its marked children *makes* things, Minister. It makes exiles. Lonely, gifted, unforgiven exiles, ripe as autumn, and my organization has harvested this orchard for three generations at a cost of — what have we ever charged you? Some tea. Some luck. Your family's unbroken run of good fortune, which your rivals call uncanny and your biographers will call discipline." The well-bottom eyes came up to his. "The collar was the *press*, Minister. The product walks out of your ports at nineteen with nowhere warm to be, and someone of mine is always at the dock."

The lamp burned. Upstairs, faint through two floors of a house that fear had built and kept standing, his granddaughter was humming.

He knew the tune. The whole archipelago knew the tune by now; the broadcasters had stopped pretending otherwise. A foreign ballad about a maiden with two names, carried east by sailors and school-trip students, and his granddaughter — eleven, unmarked, fierce about things at the dinner table — hummed it while she did her equations, and had asked him last week, with the terrible frontal cannon of the young, why the girl with the scissors on the news wore a collar if she was a hero, and whether heroes had to register at the prefecture like her classmate Yui-chan, *and grandfather, who decided that, was it you?*

"The orchard is failing you," Sōma said. "That's why you're talking. A hundred and forty years of silence on the accounts, and now the itemization. Honami is open. The girl with the scissors is on every screen, wearing your press around her throat like a costume detail. The Diet has four collar-reform bills tabled and my own ministry drafted two of them, and the young — " he heard his voice do something it had never done in this room, and let it — "the young have stopped being afraid on schedule."

"Yes," the guest said, and she sounded, for the first time in his sixty-two years of her, entirely earnest. "Isn't it *strange?* I've run this orchard since before your grandfather's grandfather. I know fear the way your Bureau knows frequencies — its yields, its seasons, its blights. And I am watching a crop fail for a reason with no precedent in my books. Someone told a better story, Minister. Six women who never once set foot here told a better story, and it crossed the water without an army, without a mission, without a single agent of theirs on our soil, and it is out-competing a hundred and forty years of cultivation because an eleven-year-old finds it *nicer to hum.*" She shook her head slowly, a child marveling. "My superiors have revised our posture accordingly. We are instructed to watch. She finds it interesting. Between us, I find it interesting too."

"And the house of Katagiri? What are we instructed?"

"The house of Katagiri is offered its usual terms." The guest folded her hands. "I stay. The luck stays. You slow the reform bills — a minister of your seniority could add three years to their journey with scheduling alone — and the orchard gets three more harvests while my organization repositions. Your granddaughter inherits a standing house." A pause, measured to the century. "Or."

Sōma looked at the alcove where a hundred and forty years of his family's fortune sat with its hands in its lap. He thought of his grandfather's shaking hands on the teapot. He thought of the torii at Honami, the prayer tablets coming down one by one into living voices. He thought of a magistrate's amendment from Huanglong that had crossed his desk in the foreign digests that morning, some provincial dam protocol, and of the phrase in it that had snagged on him at breakfast and been snagging all day: *the first census taken is of who came back.*

He stood. He crossed the room, past the alcove, and opened the storm shutters his household had kept closed at night for as long as the guest had been in residence — her one standing request, made to his grandfather, *the dark prefers a dark house* — and the night air came in off the garden, cold and green and ordinary, and somewhere out in it a late train went by full of people going home.

"The bills proceed on schedule," he said. "The house will take its weather as it comes."

He turned around, prepared for an empty alcove and a dynasty of consequences.

The guest was still there. She was looking at the open shutters, at the moving dark beyond them, and on her face was an expression he had no file for, in a hundred and forty years of quarterly observation: she looked *surprised*, and underneath the surprise, the way a spring runs under a field, delighted.

"A hundred and forty years," she said, "and this house has finally served me something I hadn't already tasted." She settled back into the alcove, arranging the red kimono, a guest composing herself for a long performance. "Keep the shutters open, Minister. I'll stay a while at my own expense. I want to see how your granddaughter's story ends."

She held out her cup.

Both hands steady, the last Katagiri to pay the old tribute poured the first tea of the new terms: freely, to a guest, at midnight, in a house standing on its own.

## v. The New Federation
### *the surface*

> **VEY DIRECTORATE — QUARTERLY THREAT BOARD (EXTRACT)**
> *Item 1: EXOSTRIDER "BALDUR" — reclassified from RUIN/ASSET to ACTOR/SOVEREIGN. Acquisition scenarios: withdrawn, all.*
> *Item 2: ALEPH-1 — event horizon receded. Recession is a weather term. Weather returns.*
> *Item 3: Nonstate catastrophic-class individuals (feeds attached): five (5) confirmed, affiliations occult, one carries an umbrella. Return address: none.*
> *Item 4: L. — former Bureau Nine. See enclosure. Enclosure returned unread twice by the Director. Third time attached.*
>
> **MARKET WIRE (same date):** *Aerospace composite +14 on Exostrider telemetry. Pharma "serenity" basket −9, third straight quarter. Underwriters convene emergency session on the "act of Sentinel" clause. Devotional goods: no ceiling found.*

The Vess Foundation's autumn dinner was held ninety floors above the lawless zones, which was the altitude at which the Federation preferred to discuss them, and Director Anselm Vey attended because his office had calculated, to two decimal places, that his absence would say more than his presence.

Madam Ilona Vess seated him at her right hand, which both of them understood as an interrogation room with better wine.

"Director. You look tired."

"The quarter has been eventful, Madam Vess."

"Hasn't it." She was seventy by her file and fifty by any camera, silver where it flattered, and her foundation's name was on a burn ward, two orchestras, a scholarship, and — his analysts had spent a year on the lacework and come back with beautiful diagrams and no arrests — on shell upon shell of patient money that went down through the corporate strata like tree roots going for water. "The telemetry footage alone. I've watched it a dozen times. A war machine the size of a weather system, kneeling to open its chest, and out walk six schoolgirls and somebody's mother." She touched her glass to her lips. "The Ministry of Industry wept openly, I'm told. All that chassis and no procurement pathway."

"The board withdrew the acquisition scenarios."

"Of course it did. One doesn't *acquire* something that has demonstrated preferences." Her eyes were bright over the rim. "That's the quarter's true event, wouldn't you say? Everything else is footage. The doctrine of assets suffered a bereavement. The giant has opinions. The witches have itineraries. Even the Dolls, I hear, have begun filing grievances, and winning them." She set the glass down. "A whole category of ownership died this year, Director, and the market is wearing black and pretending it's fashion."

Around them the room glittered and pivoted: ministers, board chairs, a famous surgeon, the aerospace men clustered by the window with their backs to the view, arguing valuation. Beneath the floor, ninety stories down, the zones ran their sodium-orange grids out to the crater fields, and in those grids, this month, his Directorate counted forty-one shrines. They had appeared the way mushrooms appear. Candles, printed feed-stills, hand-lettered boards. The umbrella woman had seven shrines of her own. The Federation's statutes classed four of the five cavalry figures as proscribed entities, and his officers had reported, in the flat prose of men declining to editorialize, that removal crews were being met by neighborhood grandmothers with folding chairs, and that the crews had begun, without orders, finding other work.

"Your people are pulling my grant files," Madam Vess said pleasantly, buttering bread. "Fourth audit in six years. Ludo in my accounts office has started sending your Ms. Harrow a birthday card."

"Your foundation's structure is intricate, Madam."

"My foundation's structure is *taxed*, Director, which is more than the aerospace gentlemen by the window can say." The smile was charity-gala standard; the eyes did older work. "Ask me directly. You've earned it, four audits in. It's a party; I'll answer one."

He turned his glass a quarter-turn. Somewhere in the Directorate's deepest annex sat the enclosure he had now read — Lucilla's file, Bureau Nine's lost star, the spymaster who had walked out of the surface world down some unlisted stair, and whose handwriting, decades on, had surfaced under a school's letterhead in a city inside a giant; and appended to the file, in Lucilla's own antique field cipher, transmitted openly, addressed to whichever of her successors held her old desk, a message his cryptographers had solved in an afternoon because she had wanted it solved:

*The rot goes higher than you've dared write down. Follow the philanthropies. — L.*

"One question," Vey said. "Why is the Vess Foundation funding a shrine?"

For a moment the table's noise went on without them.

"Which one?" said Madam Vess.

"The real one. Underground. The Academy's memorial commission — a shrine to the ones who walked into the dark and kept reaching, the miko's project. Your money is in it, four layers down. Naming rights declined." He watched her the way his whole career had taught him to watch: for the flinch, the tell, the load shifting. "My analysts have three theories. Penance. Camouflage. Or the strangest capital allocation of the decade."

Madam Vess looked out the window, past the aerospace men, at the dark where the crater fields began and, far beneath everything, a god knelt gently over a city that had wished on it.

"I'll tell you what I told my board," she said at last, and the gala voice had gone; what remained was quieter and much older and, he would write in his contact report, *sincere, which was worse.* "For a long time, the most reliable yield on this planet was despair. It compounded. It never defaulted. Sound institutions — " the smile flickered — "*sound* institutions were built on it. And this year I sat in my own screening room and watched a girl fall out of a god, and watched two hundred thousand people watch her caught, and I ran the projections through the night, Director, with my own hands, the way I haven't since I was poor. Every model says the same thing now." She turned back to him. "The story is the asset class. Hope has begun compounding. And I have never in my long life been on the wrong side of a secular trend."

"So the shrine is a position."

"The shrine is a hedge." She raised her glass, and around them the dinner glittered on, and ninety floors down the sodium grids burned, and in them the forty-one shrines, and in the deep world under everything the kettles and the classrooms and the kneeling patience. "Against the possibility, Director, which your Directorate and my board both price at zero and both lie awake about, that the six of them meant it. Audit *that.*"

He filed the contact report at two in the morning, personally, in the annex, next to Lucilla's cipher. Under ASSESSMENT he typed four words, deleted them, retyped them, and let them stand, because the old defector had told him to follow the philanthropies and this was where they led:

*The parasite is diversifying.*

Then, after a while, a fifth word, in the field reserved for the officer's private judgment, the one struck from all circulating copies:

*Watch the hedge. Hedges grow.*

## vi. The Lake at Night
### *a coda, between Sentinels*

Hsin came over the mountains after moonset, wearing the fox because the fox travels light, and trotted out onto the water of Jinzhou's lake, which held her the way old friends hold each other's coats.

Below, the dark went down and down, and at the bottom of it lay the youngest of her kin in the oldest of postures: coiled through the drowned roots of the range, one eye a pale lamp in the deep, the guardian of the frontier gate doing what he did through every human night, which was arithmetic.

She sat on the water above his eye and let her tails settle on the surface like nine small treaties.

*You heard,* she said, in the way of their kind, which used no air.

**I HEAR EVERYTHING THE WATER TOUCHES,** said Jue. **SAY WHICH THING.**

*The western one. The Imperator.* She wrapped her tails around her feet. *Its last sentence is in the teahouses now. They've set it to music. 'From this day forth, you are free. Lead well.' Act two, curtain.*

The lamp in the deep dimmed and brightened: a blink, geological.

**IT SPENT ITSELF HEALING TWO CHILDREN AND FREED A NATION WITH ITS DEATH RATTLE. THE HUMANS CALL THIS A TRAGEDY.**

*The humans call it an opera. I've seen it twice.* A pause; the water shivered with something that in a smaller creature would have been a laugh. *I cried, both times. Don't tell the frontier court.*

**YOUR COURT KNOWS. THE MAGISTRATE'S REGISTER LISTS TWO HANDKERCHIEFS UNDER 'SUNDRY, SHRINE.'**

*She's thorough. I chose well.* Hsin looked down through her own reflection at her kinsman's eye. *Say the true thing, Jue. The one you've been doing arithmetic about since the dispatch came. I crossed two mountain ranges; I've earned it.*

The lake was still for a long time. Under it, a mind old as the watershed turned the sum over once more, the way it had been turning it through every watch of every night since the west sent word that one of their kind had found the door out and had walked through it *saying thank you.*

**THE IMPERATOR WAS FREED BY SIX MORTALS AND FREED ITS MORTALS IN TURN,** Jue said. **I HAVE GUARDED THIS GATE SINCE THE FIRST WALL. I HAD BELIEVED THE GUARDING WAS THE WHOLE OF ME. NOW I DO NEW ARITHMETIC. THE MAGISTRATE AMENDS HER DOCTRINE TOWARD TRUST. THE LOWER CITY DRILLS ITS TWO HUNDRED BREATHS AND RUNS THEM LAUGHING. THE CHILDREN LEARN THE SLUICE-SONG THE FOREIGN GARNISH-SINGER SENT AS A GIFT.** The eye brightened by a degree. **THEY ARE PRACTICING TO NEED ME LESS. I FIND—**

The pause went down further than the lake did.

**I FIND I AM PROUD. THE FEELING IS INEFFICIENT. IT PERSISTS.**

*It does that.* Hsin lay down on the water, chin on paws, nine treaties trailing. *Mine boil kettles now, on the wall. They pour for the living. I taught them joints and cores, Jue, and they've gone and taught themselves grief and hospitality, and one day, past my seeing or yours, they'll stand their own watch entirely — and the day after that, some clever descendant of Dou's will climb my terrace with a docket, and ask me, very politely, what I plan to do with my retirement.*

**AND YOU WILL SAY?**

*I'll say what the Imperator said. Give or take a candied hawthorn.* She yawned, showing every tooth she had, which was more teeth than the fox strictly accounted for. *Lead well. Then I'll go find out what a foxgirl does on her day off. It's been four thousand years. I have theories.*

**THE WEST'S SIX ARE IN SCHOOL,** Jue observed. **THE ANNEX CONFIRMS IT. THEY UNMADE A THEOCRACY, WOKE A WAR-GOD, TURNED A TIDE OF ENDING, AND ENROLLED.**

*I know.* The laugh moved the whole lake this time, small waves ringing every stone of the youngest city, setting the drawn-up bridges knocking gently in their chains, so that the night watch on the north wall logged, at the third hour, *weather: calm; lake: amused.* *That's the part I'd put in the opera, if they asked me. All the thrones and chancelleries of Solaris-3, three volumes deep in contingency — and the cause of it all is six women doing homework. There's your act three, kinsman. There's the whole libretto.*

She stood, shook lake-light from her fur, and set off across the water for the mountains and home, and behind her the eye in the deep watched her out of sight and then returned, content, to the long arithmetic of the gate — with one new line in it, carried down from the surface of the world and entered, permanently, between the wall-strengths and the water-tables:

*Count the returning.*

**SAME TIME NEXT CENTURY,** the lake said, to the mountains.

The mountains, who had heard, said they'd keep the kettle on.

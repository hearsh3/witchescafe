Chapter 1: Jiù Zhài

By the seventh hour of the afternoon, the sun had dropped low enough to turn the wet slate roofs of Xuanfang Hold the color of salted mackerel.

Up on the steep ridge of the south granary, Ruoyun straddled the crest-beam with a wooden bucket of oak pegs wedged between her ankles and a flat iron pry-bar in her fist. She had pinned her long, ruffled white sleeves back to the elbows with a pair of brass ledger-clips to keep the cloud embroidery out of the roof-tar, and the hem of her teal pleated skirt was tucked neatly under her knees so it wouldn’t drag in the moss.

“Willow,” Ruoyun muttered to the chimney pot beside her ear, shoving a fresh grey river slate into the gap where a stray siege-stone had clipped the eaves. She drove an oak peg home with two sharp raps of the iron bar. “I ask for pine, I get willow. Next time I’ll ask for willow and see what turns up.”

Down in the narrow cobble lane below the granary eaves, Old Tanner Qiu’s twelve-year-old grandson stopped with a basket of wet hides against his hip and tilted his head back, squinting up past the gutter at the small golden horns glinting above her temples and the round brass spectacles sliding down her nose.

Ruoyun didn’t miss the stare. After two hundred years of ducking behind cisterns and slipping through storehouse shadows before the watchmen turned around, having people gawk at her in broad daylight made the back of her neck prickle. She glared down over the gutter rim and pointed the iron pry-bar straight at the boy’s nose.

“What are you gawking at, Qiu Er?” she barked. “Haven’t you got cabbages to turn? Go on!”

Qiu Er’s mouth fell open. He looked wildly up and down the lane to see who this scholarly, horned lady in the fine teal dress could possibly be, thought better of asking, and bolted through the tanner’s gate with his basket banging against his shins.

Ruoyun snorted, flicked her spectacles back up the bridge of her nose with a tar-smudged knuckle, and reached into the bucket for another peg.

Then the wind shifted up from the market lane, and brought a tune with it.

It was a short, nagging little five-note ferry air, hummed through the nose without ever quite landing on the final cadence, and accompanied on every off-beat by the sharp *tink-tink, tink* of a fingernail tapping the rim of a small brass bell.

Ruoyun’s hand froze inside the peg bucket.

She had not heard that tune in more than a hundred years, and she had hoped to go another hundred.

Slowly, Ruoyun set the iron pry-bar down on the ridge tiles. The warmth drained clean out of her face, leaving her sharp chin set like a bear-trap. She stood up on the narrow crest of the granary roof, her dark leather shoes finding purchase on the mossy slate as easily as on a parlor floor, and looked down over the market eaves.

The market lane was half-full of villagers and off-duty Wardens trading turnips, lamp oil, and patched winter coats between the rubble heaps.

Right in the middle of the lane, strolling along as unhurriedly as if she had just stepped out to buy bean-curd for lunch, was Buling.

She looked the exact same to the last yellow thread. Her silver hair was tucked up under the round, dark-brimmed *nuanmao* of an old-dynasty magistrate, with a long yellow paper talisman draped from the front brim straight down over the bridge of her nose. Her fitted royal-blue silk cheongsam didn’t have a speck of road dust on the collar, and the yellow talisman bands wrapped around her black leggings were tied with crisp, even turns at the ankle.

She was standing in front of Widow Ma’s odds-and-ends blanket, turning a chipped bamboo back-scratcher over in her left hand while her right thumb moved at a blur across the dark glass of a pocket Terminal held low by her hip—typing a forum reply she couldn’t send yet without looking at the screen once.

“Two coppers, Auntie,” Buling was saying, her voice floating clearly up to the roof between the clatter of handcarts. “Look at the teeth, one already broken. You scratch with this, one side itchy, one side bleeding. I give you one copper and I tell you free of charge why your stove at home always smoking when the wind comes from the river.”

“My stove doesn’t smoke,” Widow Ma said suspiciously.

“Will smoke tonight,” Buling said cheerfully, and without pausing her bargaining or her thumb-typing, she reached sideways into the neighboring basket belonging to the oil-press man, pinched a candied walnut out from under his nose, and slipped it into her mouth behind her dangling forehead talisman.

Up on the granary ridge, Ruoyun drew in a breath that lifted her collarbones.

Perhaps it was the sudden shadow falling across the turnip crates, or the glare of the late sun flashing off two round brass lenses forty feet overhead. Buling stopped chewing. Her thumb paused on her Terminal screen, and she tilted her chin back until the yellow paper strip slid sideways off her cheek, revealing a pair of bright, unblinking red eyes.

She looked up at the granary ridge. Ruoyun looked down at her.

For two full heartbeats the only sound between them was the squeak of a wheelbarrow axle down by the well.

Then Buling’s face split into a wide, delighted grin, and she raised the chipped bamboo back-scratcher in a brisk salute.

“Ruoyun!” she called up. “Wah, you wear glasses now? Very educated.”

“*Buling!*”

Ruoyun’s roar rattled the loose slates along three separate roofs and sent every pigeon on the bell-tower exploding into the sky at once.

Down in the lane, Buling dropped the back-scratcher onto Widow Ma’s blanket, shoved her Terminal into her side slit, and ran.

***

Ruoyun didn’t bother with the granary ladder. She ran four steps down the pitch of the roof, hopped off the low eaves onto the stacked cordwood behind the cooper’s shed, and hit the cobblestones at a dead sprint with her pleated skirt snapping around her shins.

Buling already had twenty paces on her, her black-bound shins flashing as she cornered around the pickled-mustard vats with the flat, ground-eating scurry of someone who had spent a very long time leaving towns in a hurry.

Xuanfang Hold barely blinked.

As Buling’s royal-blue hem whipped past the wash-house steps, Old Mother Pu’s niece calmly reached out with both hands, lifted a wicker tray of drying radish strips off the low stone wall, and held it against her apron until Ruoyun tore past two seconds later. By the horse trough, two quarrymen sitting on an overturned barrow with their supper bowls didn’t even stop chewing. The older one watched the jiangshi in the official’s hat vault a pile of broken pike-shafts with the horned lady gaining on her heels, fished two copper cash out of his waistcoat, and set them on the rim of the trough.

“Two coppers on the little clerk with the glasses,” he said.

His partner looked down the lane at the angle of Ruoyun’s elbows. “No bet. Look at her stride, she’s got mountain lungs.”

Ahead of them, Carpenter Lin’s wife stepped out of the narrow alley by the cistern with her four-month-old boy propped on her hip and a bundle of kindling under her other arm.

“Lin San-niang, inside!” Ruoyun bellowed from thirty yards back without losing half a step. “And grab that dog!”

San-niang’s eyes went round; she backed hastily over her threshold, clutching the baby—whose cabbage-shaped ears were pink with delight as he waved his wooden rattle after the red-eyed lady in the funny hat.

Buling ducked under the stone arch of the inner gatehouse to cut toward the west stair. Down in the shadow of the arch, Liangyu was kneeling by the iron hinge-post with an oil-rag in her hand and her long ash spear leaned against her shoulder. She looked up at the pound of running feet, half-rose with her hand on the spear shaft—and froze as a silver-haired jiangshi skidded past her boots smelling faintly of candied walnuts and cinnamon coffee, followed immediately by a sharp-chinned woman in an immaculate Xianzhou dress who took the corner without touching the wall.

Liangyu slowly lowered the oil-rag, looked out into the lane, looked back up the west stair, and stayed exactly where she was.

Up the west stair Buling went, taking the steep granite risers two at a time and hating every single inch of it.

“Why this place still got so many steps!” Buling shouted over her shoulder, clutching the crown of her *nuanmao* with one hand so the draft wouldn’t take it. “Two hundred years and nobody thought of a pulley? My knees are not for climbing!”

“I’ll give you knees!” Ruoyun yelled from six treads below her, not even breathing through her mouth yet. “You said you were going down to the ferry for cinnabar! *Cinnabar!* And you left me the south ditch full of roots!”

“I met a client at the crossing!” Buling called back, dodging around a stack of roof-tiles on the landing. “Very urgent feng shui case, his ancestral hall was sitting on a bad vein! You think I went out there to play?”

“An ancestral hall does not take a hundred years, you shameless little tick!” Ruoyun vaulted a broken wheelbarrow on the upper terrace and cut the corner off by three paces. “You left the tallow book open on the meal-chest! Halfway down a page! The mice ate it!”

“Then you scold the mice lor, why you chase me!”

“And you walked off with my good horn-handled pruning knife in your tool-roll!”

“That knife was rusty already!” Buling protested, scrambling over the low parapet onto the flat tarred roof of the cistern shed.

“Horn doesn’t rust, you thief!”

At the far edge of the cistern roof, the lane dropped away twelve feet to the wash-house drying yard, blocked by four taut hemp lines hung with the garrison’s wet winter tunics. Ruoyun was four paces behind her and closing, her blue eyes blazing behind her round brass spectacles and her right hand already reaching for the back of Buling’s silk collar.

Buling whipped two yellow paper talismans out of her sleeve with her left hand, slapped them flat against the stone coping to spring-load her heels, and snapped her right fingers to call down a short Thunder spark for the jump.

*CRACK-FZZT.*

The damp mountain air caught the charge wrong. Instead of firing under her boots, a jagged blue spark backfired straight up Buling’s right cuff.

Buling yelped, her silver hair lifting in a sudden, crackle-edged halo under the brim of her hat as she shook her smoking fingertips. “*Siao liao*—”

Her left foot missed the coping edge. She launched crookedly across the gap, plowed straight through the second line of wet linen tunics, and hit the wash-house flagstones in a rolling tangle of damp grey hemp and wooden clothes-pegs.

Up on the cistern roof, Ruoyun stopped at the edge with her hands on her hips.

Down in the yard, Buling shoved a wet sleeve off her face, spat out a wooden peg, and spotted the low, arched stone mouth of the south overflow culvert right behind the lye barrels—the three-foot stone drain-pipe that cut straight under the granary yard to the outer ditch.

Shortest way out.

Without a second’s hesitation, Buling scrambled on hands and knees behind the barrels and dove headfirst into the dark stone pipe.

Up on the cistern roof, Ruoyun didn’t jump down into the laundry.

She stood still for a beat, watching the heels of Buling’s black shoes vanish into the culvert. Then she reached up, unclipped the two brass ledger-clips from her ruffled white sleeves, shook the cuffs down neatly over her wrists, and walked down the side stair behind the mule stalls at a brisk, composed clip.

She strolled fifty paces down the outside of the wall to where the south culvert emptied through an iron swing-grate into the grassy bank above the ditch, folded her arms over her chest, and waited.

Ten seconds later, a hollow, echoing scrape came down the stone pipe, accompanied by muffled grumbling about moss and frogs.

The iron swing-grate pushed outward with a rusty squeak. Buling crawled out onto the bank on all fours, a ribbon of green duckweed draped over her left shoulder and a smudge of wet gray silt across her chin. She sat back on her heels, let out a long, triumphant breath—and looked straight into the toe of Ruoyun’s dark leather shoe, six inches from her knee.

Buling’s head tilted back, inch by slow inch.

Ruoyun didn’t say a word. She bent down, took Buling’s left ear firmly between her thumb and forefinger right under the brim of the *nuanmao*, and hauled her upright onto her feet.

“Gentle, gentle!” Buling hopped sideways on her toes to keep the tension off her lobe, both hands coming up to hover uselessly around Ruoyun’s wrist. “This ear is old stock. You pull it off, cannot glue back.”

“Save your breath for the mountain,” Ruoyun said grimly, turning on her heel and marching her captive straight up the cobble lane toward the north cliff cut. “I’m taking you to A-Qing. She can take your head off with a pine needle, and I’ll use that stupid hat of yours to grease the wagon axles.”

All the bounce went out of Buling’s knees at once. She dug both heels into the wet gravel between the cobbles, leaving two long furrows in the dirt as Ruoyun dragged her past the well-head anyway.

“Wait. Wait, Ruoyun, we are colleagues,” Buling said rapidly. “Don’t disturb Shifu over some administrative matter, we settle it outside. I buy you a new knife. Two knives. In Jinzhou they got a folding one with little scissors inside, I can order tonight and get the courier to bring it up—”

“Walk!” Ruoyun barked.

***

Halfway up the north cliff stair, Qingxiao sat on the fortieth granite step with her moon-white skirt folded neatly behind her ankles.

She had started down from the peak twenty minutes earlier, keeping her word to come down before the second bell for the beef-bone broth in the branch hall. She had reached the middle landing when Ruoyun’s first shout took the pigeons off the bell-tower.

From the rock cut, the whole lower Hold lay open to the eye like a carpenter’s yard.

Qingxiao had paused by the parapet wall to watch the royal-blue streak darting between the pickled-mustard vats with the teal skirt three lengths behind. When the blue lightning misfired on the cistern roof and took down the garrison’s washing, Qingxiao had stepped back from the rail, brushed the dry shale dust off the fortieth step with her sleeve, and sat down to watch the rest.

She had never once seen Ruoyun run after anyone at full cry with her spectacles flashing in the sun and her voice carrying clear across four roofs about a pruning knife.

Now, at the foot of the cliff stair, the gravel crunched under two pairs of boots—one marching, one dragging its heels—followed at a polite twenty-pace interval by Qiu Er, the two quarrymen from the horse trough, and four off-duty Wardens pretending to inspect the masonry of the cistern wall.

Ruoyun hauled Buling up to the bottom step and gave her ear a small, directional shake toward the landing above.

“Look what I fished out of the south culvert, A-Qing!” Ruoyun announced, her cheeks bright pink under her spectacles and her chest rising and falling fast beneath the silver cloud-work.

Bent sideways at the neck to spare her earlobe, Buling squinted up the granite risers past the yellow paper strip dangling from her hat. When she saw Qingxiao sitting on the stone step in the clear evening light, she gave a tiny, hopeful wave with her soot-blackened right fingers.

“Shifu,” Buling said. “You sit on the steps now? Good for the lower back. This stone got nice qi.”

Qingxiao looked down from the landing at the crooked official’s hat, the green duckweed on the silk shoulder, and the scorched right cuff.

“You still drop your left elbow when you draw thunder,” Qingxiao said.

Buling winced as if she’d been tapped on the shin with a bamboo rod. “The air here very wet, Shifu. The charge anyhow jump into my sleeve.”

“Never mind her sleeve!” Ruoyun snapped, keeping her thumb locked tight on the ear. She flung her free left arm out toward the Hold behind them. “A hundred years, A-Qing! More! She went down the mule road for cinnabar and never came back. Not a word. Not one slip of paper. And she took my knife.” Ruoyun’s voice cracked on *knife*, and she glared at the steps as if they had done it. “I chipped the north sluice by myself every winter while she was off somewhere drinking sweet coffee.”

“I was working!” Buling protested sideways to the steps. “You think freelancing is easy? Every city a different permit, the ferry fare went up three times, one bowl of beef noodles in Jinzhou, no tendon, four hundred shell credits! And the minute I saw on the forum the Hold got big noise and weird clouds, I packed my bag and came back. Very dutiful.”

“The noise stopped yesterday morning!” Ruoyun shouted into her ear.

“The lower ferry was full of cabbages, I had to wait for the mule cart!” Buling craned her neck toward Qingxiao again, her red eyes wide and earnest. “Anyway, Shifu, good thing I came back. The Hold’s layout is all spoiled now. That big diagonal cut in the east wall is pointing straight at the granary like a butcher knife—all the luck is leaking into the ditch. You tell Ruoyun to let go my ear, I go get my compass and do a full reading for you. Old disciple rate. Almost free.”

Qingxiao rested her hands lightly on her knees.

“Where is your practice sword?” Qingxiao asked.

Buling went completely still. Her mouth opened, closed once, and her eyes slid sideways toward the scrub pines above the cistern.

“Ah,” Buling said in a much smaller voice. “That one. Behind the waterfall in the hollow cedar, I think. Unless the badgers took it.”

“The cedar fell in the eightieth year,” Qingxiao said evenly. “I brought the sword inside. It is leaning by the water-jar.”

Buling’s shoulders dropped two inches under the blue silk. “Aiya. You really don’t throw anything away.”

“Take her head off, A-Qing,” Ruoyun ordered, giving the ear another tiny tug. “Or take her legs off at the shins so she can’t slip back down the mule road the next time I turn my back to oil a hinge.”

“Cannot!” Buling squawked, clapping both hands to her own collar and accidentally knocking her forehead talisman skewed across her nose. “You cut my head off then who’s going to carry it? You want to carry my head around in a vegetable basket while you sweep the gutter? I will talk the whole time, I tell you first! I know eight hundred forum threads about land-tax reform in the central plains, I will recite from first bell until midnight—”

“I’ll drop it in a stoneware pickle jar and seal the lid with rosin!” Ruoyun barked back.

“Pickle jar cannot block my frequency, I am an artifact specialist, I will hum against the clay until your back teeth fall out—”

Up on the fortieth step, Qingxiao laughed.

It was a single, quiet sound—clear and low and wholly unguarded—that slipped out into the cold evening air before her hand even shifted on her knee.

At the foot of the stair, both Celestials froze dead in their tracks.

Ruoyun’s thumb actually went slack on Buling’s ear. She stared up at the landing through her round brass spectacles, her mouth slightly parted.

Beside her, Buling slowly hooked one soot-smudged finger under her crooked yellow talisman and pulled it an inch to the side so both of her red eyes could see the Sword Transcendent’s face.

Just then, the dusk wind came pouring down the rock cut from the upper pass. It lifted the ends of Qingxiao’s long blue hair and pressed the thin moon-white silk flat against her forearms.

Qingxiao drew her shoulders in by the breadth of a finger against the draft, and when she breathed out, a small, distinct puff of warm white vapor curled from her lips into the grey twilight.

Down on the gravel, Buling’s red eyes went from that white puff of breath down to Qingxiao’s bare right wrist—where no frost glimmered on the skin—and then flicked once, very quick and quiet, to the side of Ruoyun’s face.

Ruoyun’s jaw set hard. She gave Buling a single, flat look through her spectacles that dared her to say half a syllable.

Buling let the paper talisman drop back over her nose and looked down at her own muddy shoes, rubbing her scorched right thumb against her palm.

“Wah,” Buling muttered to the gravel. “Even the mountain draft different now. No wonder the mule cart take so long.”

Up on the step, Qingxiao stood up, shaking her cuffs down over her wrists.

“No pickle jar,” Qingxiao said.

Buling’s head came up at once. “See! Shifu is reasonable—”

“You owe the Hold the rounds you walked away from,” Qingxiao continued in her level voice, walking slowly down the granite steps toward them. “You will start tonight on the eastern breach. Warden Ge’s men laid unwashed gravel under the quicklime course. Ruoyun will keep your book until the second-month thaw.”

For the space of a breath the bottom of the stair was dead quiet. Then both of them went off at the exact same instant.

“Why do *I* have to keep her book?” Ruoyun shouted, throwing both hands up toward the darkening sky. “That’s not a sentence for her, A-Qing, that’s a sentence for *me*! Have you ever listened to her whine while she holds a shovel? I brought her here for a beheading!”

“Quicklime?!” Buling cried right across her, grabbing the sides of her royal-blue skirt in genuine horror. “Shifu, this cheongsam is tailor-made in Jinzhou! Quicklime will eat the silk until I look like a dried squid! And the east breach faces the gorge wind, my hat will blow straight down into the river!”

Qingxiao stepped off the bottom tread, passing neatly between the two of them without slowing her pace.

“Tie a string under your chin,” Qingxiao said, and walked down the cobble lane toward the warm smell of simmering beef bones rising from the branch hall.

***

By the time the first cold stars came out above the eastern breach, the masons had gone in to wash their hands for supper, leaving two horn lanterns burning on stacked lime kegs at the bottom of the cut.

Down in the forty-foot gap in the masonry, Buling was squatting in the gravel with the hem of her royal-blue cheongsam tucked up into her waist-sash and an iron crowbar braced under a dressed block of grey granite.

“Wrong,” Buling grumbled, throwing her weight onto the iron bar until the heavy stone ground three inches to the left in the wet mortar. “All wrong. Who is this Warden Ge? Tell him don’t build walls anymore, go sell sweet potatoes. Look at this corner—pointing straight at the barracks roof like an iron arrow. Next month the whole third company is going to get stiff necks and lose their money playing dice. Have to turn the face two fingers south.”

Sitting three paces away on an upturned lime keg, with a brass tally-lamp by her shoe and the gatehouse logbook propped open across her knees, Ruoyun didn’t look up from her bamboo brush.

“Lever it back,” Ruoyun said. “I’m not cutting wedge-stones in the dark so the third company can win at dice.”

“It’s not just dice, it’s the whole harmony of the ditch!” Buling let the crowbar clatter onto the stones and squatted back on her heels, blowing a stray lock of silver hair out of her eyes so the yellow talisman on her forehead fluttered upward. “Wait, Ruoyun. You just anyhow say a hundred over years. What about the big flood when the river came up to the gate? The south drain was underwater for months, nobody could sweep it even if I was standing here with a broom! Give me a discount for the flood, I fix this wall until New Year, then we’re done.”

“The Paragon said the thaw,” Ruoyun said, dipping the tip of her brush into the ink-stone. “And wash that gravel properly or I’m putting the flood back on.”

Buling let out a long, theatrical groan that echoed off the broken stonework, picked up a wooden water-ladle, and sloshed half a bucket of cold cistern water over a sieve of gravel.

She shook the wire sieve between both hands, watching the muddy water run out into the ditch.

“You really stayed the whole time ah,” Buling said after a moment, her voice losing its haggling bounce as she picked a piece of shale out of the mesh and tossed it aside.

On the lime keg, Ruoyun’s brush paused for the breadth of a second above the paper.

“Somebody had to,” Ruoyun said briskly, and set a neat, sharp tick at the top of the new column. “Not all of us can stroll down to the ferry whenever the soup gets thin.”

“The soup here was pure rainwater!” Buling muttered, dumping the washed gravel into the footing trench with a wet crunch. “Anyway, if I stayed here looking at Warden Ge’s grandfather’s eyebrows for a hundred years, I would have eaten my own talisman and jumped in the gorge. At least in Jinzhou the street lamps don’t smell like mutton tallow.”

“Wait until the third watch when the wind comes down the pass,” Ruoyun said darkly. “You’ll be begging for tallow to grease your nose.”

Buling reached toward her side slit—her right thumb curling by habit to check the forum thread she’d abandoned in the market before she remembered there wasn’t a splinter of relay signal anywhere on the mountain—and pulled a small brass plumb-bob on a red cord out of her sleeve instead, holding it up against the face of the block.

Before she could start another argument about the barracks roof, there was the quiet step of boots coming up the timber ramp from the inner courtyard.

Down at the bottom of the lane, the heavy timber door of the branch hall stood propped open with a fire-brick, throwing a broad, smoky ribbon of orange light across the cobblestones where the Fillet Fairmaiden stood on the threshold with an iron ladle in her fist, shouting cheerfully at two pikemen to bring back her water-buckets before she sent Flare after their shins.

Walking up the rubble ramp into the breach was Qingxiao.

She still had the heavy square of indigo-dyed oilcloth folded around her shoulders like a short cape against the night air, and in her hands she carried a scarred pine tray from the kitchen. On it sat two deep earthenware bowls steaming thick with white beef-bone broth and chopped green garlic, with four hot, blistered wedges of flatbread stacked between them.

Ruoyun clapped her logbook shut and set it on the stones beside the keg. “You took your time. Did that loud cook try to put you on the chopping block?”

“No,” Qingxiao said. “She gave me an extra marrow bone.”

She stepped down over the loose gravel into the trench, set the pine tray squarely on the sixty-pound granite block Buling had just levered out of true, and sat down on the dry corner of the stone, drawing the camphor-scented oilcloth over her knees.

Buling stared at the two steaming bowls, then looked up at Qingxiao, her red eyes narrowing under the brim of her hat. “Wait first. This soup is free, or you put it on my ditch bill?”

“It is from the garrison cauldron,” Qingxiao said.

“Garrison cauldron means public welfare,” Buling decided immediately. She wiped her wet, lime-streaked palms on an old gunny-sack, scrambled over the gravel, and dropped cross-legged onto the rubble opposite Ruoyun’s keg.

She reached up with two fingers, flipped the yellow paper talisman neatly back over the round crown of her *nuanmao* so it wouldn’t dip in the grease, and took one of the earthenware bowls in both hands, burying her nose in the ginger-and-marrow steam with a low, contented sigh.

Ruoyun stepped off her keg, picked up the second bowl, and reached across the tray to hand a hot wedge of flatbread to Qingxiao.

“Eat that,” Ruoyun said. “I know you didn’t finish your bowl down there with two hundred people banging their spoons on the tables.”

Qingxiao took the flatbread in her bare right hand and tore off a warm corner.

Across the granite block, Buling drank three long mouthfuls of broth straight from the clay rim, lowered the bowl an inch to catch her breath, and spotted the heavy indigo wrapping-cloth draped over Qingxiao’s shoulders. She squinted at the square hospital creases along the hem.

“Eh,” Buling said around a mouthful of green garlic, pointing her chin at the cloth. “That’s the oilcloth from the bottom of the west storehouse chest. I used to roll up in that one when the roof leaked in the plum-rains.”

“You got mud all over the lining,” Ruoyun said, blowing on her broth over the rim of her spectacles. “I boiled it three times.”

“That wasn’t mud, that was red cinnabar clay from the creek! Very high-grade material!”

“It was mud,” Qingxiao said mildly, chewing her bread. “From the wallow behind the pear trees.”

Buling froze with her bowl halfway to her mouth. She looked from Qingxiao’s calm, winter-pale eyes to the yellow glint of Ruoyun’s spectacles, and then hunched her shoulders over her knees before the draft off the gorge could get into her soup.

“Aiya,” Buling muttered into the rising steam. “Two against one. Very crooked Hold.”

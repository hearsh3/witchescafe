Chapter 1: Zhī Rén

The central spindle of the Ministry Shuttle parted with the groan of an iron bridge giving way in a thaw.

Behind them on the tribunal deck, the severed halves of the twelve-foot bronze column slid off their bevels and crashed through the floorboards in a tangle of snapping telemetry wire. The ceiling did not fall in on their heads; it peeled backward like wet bark.

Wind hit the companionway stairs first.

It was not the warm, oil-scented draft of the fleet’s engines, and it did not carry the dead, unmoving gold of the afternoon below. When Yangyang stepped over the buckled brass coaming onto the top deck with her hand on her hilt, a squall of black grit and sideways rain slapped the brim of her hat flat against her brow.

There was no ship above them.

The upper hull of the Ministry Shuttle ended at a sheared stone quay jutting out into a bruised, churning gulf of sky. All around the quay, the Tian Realm hung in pieces. Whole karst needles had been wrenched out of the valley floor by their roots and floated upside down or canted at wild angles in the gale, trailing long beards of snapped pine and wet shale. Between the floating crags ran the remains of a celestial court—broad rafter-bridges of red-lacquered cedar and white marble balustrades, half of them sheared clean through the middle so their broken ends swung creaking on bronze suspension chains over nothing at all. Rain fell in ragged gray sheets from the clouds overhead, and thirty paces to the left, where a split stone spire leaked a pale seam of frequency into the gale, a second curtain of rain fell straight upward from the abyss, the drops hissing as they collided in midair.

Iuno came up the last three steps with her skirt whipped tight around her shins and her right hand shielding her eyes against the blowing ash. The crystal at her left shoulder rang a sharp, continuous shudder in the crosswind.

“So much for the utopia in the brochure,” Iuno said over the roar of the rain. “He didn’t even bother to sweep the roof.”

Qingxiao stepped onto the stone quay last.

She did not float an inch off the paving. Her boots took her weight squarely on the wet flagstone, and when a gust loaded with slate-grit slammed across the deck, her soaked moon-white sleeves whipped back against her forearms and stayed there. From both of her cuffs, the pale ribbons of frequency still ran taut and steady back down through the breached hatchway—all the way through the folded layers of the Mirage to the forty-two people between the well-head and the granary steps, the gatehouse arch, and the felt rug under the arcade—humming with the quiet warmth of winter plum.

Ahead of them, at the far end of a half-collapsed cedar bridge, stood Muyu’s stronghold.

It had been built out of Sentinel Hsin’s own bones. The soaring, nine-tiered silhouette of the Fogveil Pagoda rose against the lightning-shot murk, but every line of it had been taken apart and reassembled wrong. Massive celadon jade rings from an astrolabe had been wedged sideways into the walls as buttresses; the curved eaves were plated in overlapping scales of dull black iron that drank the light; and in place of the quiet water-chimes along the galleries, thick bundles of blackened brass conduit poured down from the roof-ridge like tangled hair, pinning the structure to three floating slabs of quarry rock.

Between the broken bridge and the stronghold’s forecourt stood a wall of churning amber and slate-gray light that reached from the gulf below into the clouds above.

It was the Skyend Barrier. Once it had been one of Hsin’s outer boundary wards—Yangyang could still see the ghostly eight-sided lattice of moon-white geometry buried deep inside the glare—but Muyu had pumped it full of the Disorienting Fog and the sour, metallic dregs of the Di Realm’s pumps. The surface of the wall boiled like tallow in a cauldron, throwing off jagged arcs of amber static that chewed deep pockmarks into the marble bridge-rails.

“The bridge ends twenty paces short of the court,” Yangyang said, leaning into the gale as she studied the gap beyond the roaring wall of light. “And we can’t fly over that.”

“Nor around it,” Iuno said, watching an upward-falling ribbon of rain touch the amber lattice and vaporize with a crack like a whip. “It’s a full sphere around the keep. If we touch the outer skin with a wheel or a blade, the backwash will throw us into the karst teeth.”

Qingxiao walked to the broken lip of the cedar bridge, where the scorched timber beams hung out over the storm.

Her breathing was audible even over the wind—even, unhurried, and deep in her chest, the breath of a woman whose lungs were made of flesh and who had already spent four-fifths of her strength keeping a mountain’s doors upright since dusk. With her right hand she drew a single four-foot blade of pale light from her cuff.

When she raised the point toward the churning wall ten feet away, the tip of the Sword Intent trembled by the breadth of a wheat-straw.

“The Fox’s lattice is still turning underneath,” Qingxiao said. “If I cut the whole face at once, the threads in my left hand will slacken.”

“Don’t cut the face,” Yangyang said at once.

She stepped up beside Qingxiao’s elbow on the wet beam, letting her right hand rest on her pommel while she tilted her head slightly toward the barrier.

To look at the wall was to go snow-blind in three seconds; to listen to it was worse. The corrupted ward roared with the sound of a hundred bronze foundry-stamps dropping out of time, layered over the wet, rasping static of the fog. Yangyang closed her eyes against the sideways rain and let her ears drop below the foundry clatter, the way she had stood between the two Prisms in the Skyworks and listened for lungs under the iron.

Underneath Muyu’s loud, brassy hammering, Hsin’s original ward was still keeping four-four time.

It was faint and strained, like a lute string buried under a pile of scrap iron, and every fourth beat, when the stolen power from the Di Realm’s pumps cycled through the brass conduits on the roof, the sour amber frequency arrived a hair late against the white jade lattice.

“There’s a hitch in it,” Yangyang said, her eyes still shut as her left hand came up to mark the pulse in the air with two fingers. “He grafted his own conduits onto Hsin’s ward, and the two pitches don’t match on the turn. Wait… one, two, three—*there*.”

A hairline seam of dark, slack air opened diagonally across the boiling amber face for less than a third of a second before the cauldron-glaze slammed shut over it again.

“It opens every four beats, six feet up on the left,” Yangyang said, opening her eyes. “Just wide enough for one blade. When you strike it, though, the whole top half of that wall is going to spill straight down onto this beam.”

“Let the top half spill,” Iuno said.

She stepped between them to the very edge of the sheared timber, planted her boots wide on the wet cedar, and flicked her right wrist upward.

Her moonwheel spun out of the rain—not at its usual crescent size, but expanded to its full six-foot ring of blazing silver-blue light, tilted flat above their heads like a sanctuary dome. The moment the outer rim neared the barrier’s static, the crystal at Iuno’s left shoulder gave a high, singing whine, and the muscles in her bare right forearm corded hard under the skin as she locked the wheel in place with one hand.

“Count it, Yangyang,” Iuno said through her teeth, her boots skidding half an inch in the wet ash as the first stray arcs of amber discharge hammered down onto the spinning silver-blue rim. “I don’t care to hold this umbrella all afternoon.”

*Mind your wrist, darling,* the High Priestess murmured, tight and focused as a bowstring. *Brace your back knee against the joist.*

Yangyang drew her sword halfway from its scabbard to let a concentrated current of Aero wrap around Qingxiao’s right arm, steadying the Paragon’s sleeve against the crosswind.

“One,” Yangyang called over the shriek of static hitting the moonwheel. “Two. Three—”

Qingxiao did not raise ten thousand needles. She held the single pale sword in both hands—her cut left palm pressed flat over her right knuckles, staining the white hilt with a smear of fresh red—and stepped forward on the third beat.

“—*now!*”

The seam blinked open in the amber roar.

Qingxiao lunged, driving the point of her blade six inches deep into the exact hair-width gap where Muyu’s sour brass note lagged behind Hsin’s jade lattice, and twisted her wrists once against the grain.

The Skyend Barrier shrieked like a saw-blade snapping on a knot.

All the pent-up pressure of the corrupted ward blew outward from the cut in a blinding cataract of amber fire and scalding rain. The blast slammed straight down onto Iuno’s raised moonwheel with the weight of a falling watchtower.

Iuno’s knees bent; the timber beam beneath her heels split down the grain with a gunshot crack, and a spray of silver-blue sparks burst from her left shoulder crystal as she held the six-foot ring between the backwash and the two women beside her by sheer, furious stubbornness. Yangyang threw her own weight against Iuno’s hip, driving an updraft of green wind straight up under the wheel’s lower bevel to keep the rim from being crushed down onto their skulls.

In the center of the gale, Qingxiao hauled her blade sideways down the seam.

The amber wall tore from crown to threshold like rotten silk. For two seconds the two halves of the barrier hung sizzling in the storm, and then the stolen frequency collapsed inward, folding back into the brass conduits along the stronghold’s eaves with a hollow, booming *thump* that left a twenty-foot archway of clear, rain-swept stone open before them.

Iuno dropped the moonwheel at once, shaking out her numb right fingers with a sharp hiss between her teeth.

Beside her, Qingxiao’s pale blade vanished from her hands.

The Sword Transcendent took one step back from the lip of the beam, and her right boot missed the level plank by an inch. Her knee dipped; she staggered sideways toward the sheared rail where the clouds boiled a thousand feet below.

Yangyang was already there, catching Qingxiao’s right elbow with one hand and sliding her own right shoulder squarely under Qingxiao’s arm before the Paragon could drop to one knee.

Qingxiao’s left hand came across and gripped the wet silk of Yangyang’s shoulder—not a brush of fingertips this time, but five cold, real fingers closing hard enough to wrinkle the cloth under the cerulean feathers of Yangyang’s collar.

For four full breaths Qingxiao stayed leaning there, her chest rising and falling fast against Yangyang’s side, a bead of rain or sweat running down from her temple past the base of her horn.

Yangyang stood still and held the weight, keeping her left elbow tucked in to guard her own strapped ribs. “Take a breath, Paragon,” she said quietly. “The door’s open.”

Qingxiao drew one long breath in through her nose, let it out, and straightened her knees. When she lifted her hand from Yangyang’s shoulder, she did not snatch it back into her cuff; she tapped the top of Yangyang’s wet wrist once in acknowledgment before letting her arm fall to her side.

“Your count was clean,” Qingxiao said, her voice slightly husky and altogether human. “Come.”

The outer hall of the stronghold did not have a floor of clouds or a ceiling of spinning looms.

Once they crossed the threshold out of the storm, the sound of the sideways rain cut off behind heavy double doors of iron-plated cedar, leaving a long, high-vaulted gallery that smelled of cold beeswax, dried ink, and old wool. It was the drafting room of a master artisan—or it had been, before someone turned the tables into a dissecting bench.

Along both walls, towering racks of dark timber held hundreds of unrolled mulberry-paper schematics pinned down with brass weights. Iuno’s eyes caught the nearest sheet as they passed: it was a full-scale elevation of Xuanfang Hold’s southern water-spires drawn in a quick, lively, slanted hand, with small fox-paw doodles inked in the margins beside the gear ratios, and everywhere Sentinel Hsin’s red vermilion seal had been stamped on the paper, a sharp square blade had cut the seal clean out of the sheet, leaving little empty windows in the drafting.

In the center of the stone floor stood a twelve-foot joiner’s workbench under a cluster of unlit glass lamps. Scattered across the scarred wood lay the opened brass skull of a Rafter Kite, three disassembled guardian joints, and a cracked porcelain teacup with a dry brown ring at the bottom.

Sitting in the round-backed elm chair behind the workbench was Muyu.

Yangyang stopped three paces from the table. Her hand did not draw her sword.

There was no amber smoke around the chair, and no flicker of a projector spindle beneath the floorboards. The man sitting with his chin resting on his collarbone and his arms hanging loose over the wooden armrests was sixty years old if he was a day, dressed in a plain, grease-spotted indigo artisan’s robe with woven hemp slippers on his feet. His hair was iron-gray, thinning at the crown and tied back with a frayed cotton cord that had come half-undone.

He was dead.

He had been dead for hours, perhaps since before the Mingshen array went dark on the mountain below. The front of his indigo robe was stiff and black from breastbone to hem with dried blood, the cloth split over the sternum by a single, narrow, deep wound that had stopped his heart before he could even push his chair back from the drafting board. There were no scorch marks on the hem, no frost on the wood, and no sign of a struggle among the brass calipers by his elbow.

Iuno stepped closer to the bench, her crystal shoulder chiming a low, guarded note as she looked down at the dried blood on the floorboards and the slack, stubbled jaw.

“That isn’t an effigy,” Iuno said quietly. “And he didn’t do it to himself from the front at that angle.”

Qingxiao stood at the corner of the workbench, her pale blue eyes resting on the weathered lines around the dead man’s mouth.

Yangyang looked down at the corpse’s right hand, which hung an inch above the sawdust on the floor.

The thumb was broad and flattened at the tip from forty years of guiding a chisel; the skin between the forefinger and thumb was scarred with half a dozen old, white wood-sliver seams, and there was still a crescent of yellow pine resin wedged under the thumbnail. They were the hands of a man who had built water-wheels and mended cart-axles before he ever wired a prisoner into a brass wheel.

“He left his hands here,” Yangyang murmured, a flat, quiet pity in her voice as she looked from the callused thumb to the cut-out seals on the schematics.

High up in the vaulted rafters above the unlit lamps, a brass speaking-horn gave a dry, metallic click.

Then Muyu laughed.

It did not come from the dead throat in the elm chair. It rolled down from the bronze ventilation grilles in the walls, from the hollow bellies of the dissected guardian joints on the table, and from the floorboards under their boots at once—warm, resonant, and thoroughly delighted.

“Do not waste your mourning on a pile of spoiled meat, little bird,” Muyu’s voice said from the ceiling. “Did you truly climb all the way through the storm expecting to cross swords with sixty-year-old gristle and a failing liver?”

Iuno’s mouth curled in sharp distaste as she stepped back from the elm chair, brushing a speck of sawdust from her cuff as if the air near the table were greasy. “Most men at least have the manners to bury their own trash when they move house.”

“Why bury a broken chisel?” Muyu asked, his scholar’s cadence echoing smoothly down the gallery as a pair of tall brass doors at the far end of the room unlatched from within and swung wide onto a blazing, amber-lit court. “When someone was kind enough to break the handle for me, I simply stepped out of the workshop. Come inside, Sword Transcendent. Come and see what a body looks like when the rot is planed away.”

Qingxiao looked once more at the dead old man in the hemp slippers—at the cold, quiet husk sitting where no winter draft could make it shiver and no hunger could reach its ribs.

Her left hand closed once inside her damp sleeve, where the shallow cut across her palm pulled tight against the skin, and she walked past the workbench toward the open brass doors without looking back.

The inner court of the stronghold was Hsin’s Astrolabe Sanctum turned upside down.

Four colossal bronze caliper-arms hung from the open sky of the storm above, their curved teeth meeting over a circular floor of black lacquer inlaid with stolen celadon plates. Around the perimeter, twelve vertical glass cylinders hissed with pale, circulating mist—the main feed-trunks rising straight from the Di Realm’s pumps below.

In the center of the lacquer floor, suspended three feet above the stone on a ring of repelling magnetic brass, floated Muyu’s new vessel.

He had built it out of the Hold’s finest sacred craft. The torso was forged from the white nephrite and oiled bronze of an imperial guardian, ten feet tall and broad as a temple bell, draped in a sweeping mantle of jointed black-lacquered wood tiles that clicked and shifted like armor plate whenever the storm wind came through the rafters. Four articulated brass arms grew from the shoulders: the lower pair ending in long, five-fingered artisan’s hands of polished steel, the upper pair carrying curved crescent-cleavers of resonant celadon jade. Set into the center of the breastplate was a spinning astrolabe cage housing a massive, fist-sized kernel of dark amber crystal.

Above the collar sat a face carved from seamless white porcelain—lineless, unblinking, and calm—with Muyu’s own warm amber eyes glowing behind the slits.

“Look at you, Qingxiao,” Muyu said, and as the porcelain lips moved, the voice came out rich and doubled with the harmonic hum of the bronze chest-cavity. He spread all four arms slightly, tilting his head at the pale ribbons of frequency still trailing from her soaked cuffs down toward the door. “Dragging an entire mountain of dying peasants behind your wrists like a mule hitched to a millstone. Your hands are shaking. Your hem is torn. You had the threshold of the Perfected right in front of you for two hundred years, and you chose to stay a beast of burden with an expiry date.”

Qingxiao stopped ten paces from the floating ring,Yangyang on her right and Iuno on her left.

She called a single blade of moon-white light to her right hand. It did not flicker now, though its shine was spare and thin, pared down to what her own lungs and marrow could carry without dropping a single thread downstairs.

“A stone does not know why it stands on the ridge,” Qingxiao said evenly. “You are only a louder Autopuppet.”

The amber eyes in the porcelain face narrowed.

Muyu did not make another speech; he moved with the terrifying, frictionless speed of a magnetic loom. His two upper arms whipped downward, sending a pair of screaming amber crescent-waves tearing across the black lacquer floor, while his lower steel hands punched straight into the floor-conduits to pull a wall of jagged bronze spikes up under their boots.

“Left!” Yangyang called, catching an updraft on the turn to clear the rising spikes as she swung her sword in a tight, rising arc of Aero that split the left crescent-wave into harmless sparks against the pillars.

To her left, Iuno leaped backward off a sprouting bronze tooth, her moonwheel whirling out from her wrist to parry Muyu’s upper right jade cleaver with a deafening, glassy crash that threw her skidding six feet across the lacquer on her heels.

Qingxiao went straight up the middle.

She stepped from the point of one rising brass spike to the next without breaking stride and drove her pale sword directly at the spinning astrolabe cage in Muyu’s chest. Muyu’s two lower steel hands snapped shut together in front of the kernel, catching her blade between their palms with a shower of white frost-sparks.

“Why cling to the flaw in the timber?” Muyu asked right into her face, his four arms straining against her single blade and the silver-blue rim of Iuno’s returning wheel at once. “Flesh gets tired! Flesh forgets its purpose the moment a sister dies or a roof falls in! Every soul I brought up from the ruins below had its sorrow planed off cleanly—they will never weep in the mud again, and they will never betray the wall!”

Qingxiao twisted her hilt, her boots sliding an inch back under the ten-foot frame’s hydraulic weight, and kicked her heel hard against his bronze wrist-joint to free her point.

“If I cannot get tired,” Qingxiao said between two sharp breaths as she parried his upper left cleaver inches from her horn, “then standing in the gateway costs me nothing.”

Yangyang came down out of the rafters on a slant of green wind, her ink-dark wings flashing open for a single beat at her shoulder blades as she drove her steel blade down into the seam of Muyu’s upper left shoulder.

The blade bit half an inch into the black lacquer mantle before a pulse of raw amber force from his chest-kernel blasted her back across the floor. Yangyang landed light on her toes, her cerulean collar lifted high against her jaw and her eyes bright and fierce under her hat brim.

“Lingfang carried her husband’s xiao the whole war and never once played it,” Yangyang said, her voice ringing clear and plain across the clashing brass. “You didn’t improve them, Muyu. You just threw away the parts you were too scared to listen to.”

“Sentiment is the rust that killed them!” Muyu roared, spinning the great magnetic ring beneath his waist to sweep all four arms in a full circle of scything jade and bronze.

Iuno caught the outer arc of that sweep on the edge of her moonwheel to keep it off Qingxiao’s blind side. The impact drove her down to one knee on the cracked lacquer, her bare crystal shoulder chiming a jagged, furious chord as she glared up into the porcelain mask.

“Do not lecture me about cutting the waste out of a city, you pretentious carpenter,” Iuno spat, wrenching her wrist up to fling the wheel spinning back at his throat. “I wore better silk than you for thirty years and told myself the exact same lie every morning before breakfast. You aren’t a god—you’re just a coward who built a cellar so he wouldn’t have to look at the bodies!”

Words did not dent four inches of nephrite and magnetic bronze.

Muyu had wired his frame directly into the main feed-trunks of the stronghold, and for every blow the three of them struck against his lacquered plating, the twelve glass cylinders around the court pulsed once, drawing another surge of stolen frequency up from the Di Realm to weld the seams shut.

When Iuno’s moonwheel came screaming at his neck, Muyu didn’t dodge. His upper right jade cleaver dropped like a guillotine block and caught the silver-blue chakram dead on its upper bevel, pinning it against the lacquer floor under two tons of downward hydraulic thrust.

A sharp, crystalline *crack* echoed across the court.

A jagged fracture line jumped half an inch deep across the moonwheel’s bright rim. At the same instant, the sympathetic shock tore straight back through the link into Iuno’s left shoulder; a tiny chip of iridescent crystal sheared off her stump with a sound like a snapped icicle, and Iuno’s breath punched out of her in a white gasp as she was hurled backward against the base of a feed-cylinder.

“Iuno!” Yangyang pivoted on her heel, throwing an Aero current outward to cushion the glass behind Iuno’s spine—

And left her own flank open for half a heartbeat.

Muyu’s lower left steel arm shot out on a telescoping bronze chain and caught Yangyang flat across her chest with the back of a closed fist.

Yangyang flew twenty feet across the black floor and slammed into one of the red-lacquered roof pillars on her strapped left side. The breath went out of her lungs in a silent, blinding flash of white pain; she slid down the timber post onto the lacquer, her right hand still clamped around her hilt while her left arm curled reflexively over her ribs, unable to draw air for three terrible seconds.

In the center of the court, Qingxiao fought him alone.

Four blades of pale Sword Intent whirled around her moon-white sleeves—all she could spare while the ribbons trailing from her cuffs still held the Hold’s wall six miles away. She parried the two upper jade cleavers with her left-hand blades, sheared the telescoping chain off his lower wrist with her right, and stepped inside his guard to drive her main sword straight into the astrolabe cage over his kernel.

The pale blade hit the spinning bronze rings, bowed like a green willow switch, and shattered into a thousand harmless motes of light.

She simply did not have the weight behind the point to punch through the cage while forty-two people, a gatehouse arch, and a courtyard rug were hanging from her wrists.

Muyu’s lower right hand caught her by the front of her collar before she could step back, lifting her two inches off her wet boots and slamming her down onto the black lacquer hard enough to knock her cloud-pale horns against the stone.

“Cut the threads, Qingxiao!” Muyu said above her, his four arms rising together as the astrolabe cage in his chest spun into a blur of amber light. “Drop the dead weight at the gate, and you might at least break my lacquer before you die! Why hold a roof for people who will not survive the hour?”

On the stone at his feet, blood ran from the cut in Qingxiao’s left palm down across the white silk of her cuff, soaking the edge of the plum petal tucked inside. She did not let go of a single thread trailing from her wrists. She braced her right heel against the magnetic ring at his waist and drove a foot-long dagger of light up into his elbow joint, forcing his steel fingers to spasm open so she could roll clear.

Muyu didn’t chase her across the floor.

Instead, his magnetic ring rose ten feet into the air toward the open roof of the court, where the four massive bronze caliper-arms met under the storm clouds.

“No matter,” Muyu said, his doubled voice booming out across the shattered rafters as he threw all four arms wide and locked his back-conduits directly into the twelve glass feed-cylinders around the walls. “A craftsman does not haggle with broken tools when the furnace is hot. For two centuries the grief and fury of Yuan Fortress and the Gorges of Spirits have boiled in the dark beneath this mountain—ten thousand dying throats, distilled and waiting in the deep manifold! Witness true Transcendence!”

Inside his chest, the heavy bronze shutters behind the amber kernel cranked wide open.

Around the court, the twelve great glass cylinders hissed as their upper valves unsealed, their bronze intake pumps dropping into maximum draw to suck the entire ocean of two-hundred-year-old Reverberations straight up out of the mountain’s roots into his core.

The storm wind in the rafters seemed to hold its breath.

One second passed.

Two seconds.

In the twelve glass cylinders, the pale mist thinned out, went clear, and vanished.

The heavy bronze intake pumps at the base of the walls gave a dry, hollow *clack-clack-whir*, sucking at empty stone like a straw at the bottom of a drained bowl. Inside Muyu’s chest, the spinning astrolabe cage stuttered, dropped half an octave in pitch, and the blazing amber light behind his porcelain face flickered twice like a lamp running out of oil.

Nothing came up the pipes.

No roaring tide of butchered garrisons. No burning gorge. No ten thousand furious dead waiting to be burned as fuel.

Only a faint, cool draft of mountain air came up the empty shafts from the deep below, smelling vaguely of wet mud and old bamboo.

Muyu hung ten feet above the floor with his four arms spread wide, the intake valves in his chest clicking open and shut on nothing at all.

“What—” The smooth, resonant harmonic in his voice cracked, leaving only the dry, rasping treble of an old man talking through a tin horn. He slammed his lower right hand against the brass manifold on his hip, cycling the valves a second time until the pumps shrieked against dry bearings. “The deep reservoir—the Gorges kernel—why is the manifold empty?! Where are the Reverberations?!”

By the base of the red pillar, Yangyang pushed herself up onto one knee with her sword as a crutch, her left hand pressed tight against her sash and her breath coming thin and sharp.

She looked up at the ten-foot porcelain god hanging under the empty pipes.

“They aren’t there,” Yangyang said.

Muyu’s head jerked down toward her, the amber slits in the white mask flaring wide. “What did you say?”

“They went home,” Yangyang said plainly, her voice quiet in the sudden silence of the stalled pumps. “We sang the second half of the song, and they flew out.”

For three heartbeats Muyu hung motionless beneath the calipers.

The serene, scholarly certainty that had survived two centuries of planning and the discarding of his own flesh vanished cleanly from the porcelain mask. When his four arms came down from the manifolds, the joints moved with a jagged, violent jerk; the remaining amber light in his chest-kernel flared a dirty, unstable crimson as he rerouted every drop of power still sitting in the stronghold’s capacitors straight into the blades on his arms.

“Then I will fill the vats again from the marrow in this room!” Muyu snarled, all trace of the philosopher gone as the ten-foot frame dropped like a falling anvil from the rafters straight toward the center of the floor.

Outside the Mirage, the rain over the real Xuanfang Hold had turned to a cold, steady soak.

Out on the switchback ramp beyond the bisected eastern wall, the Censure Courts had stopped throwing themselves against the barricade. Four ranks of robed bronze machines stood ankle-deep in the freezing slush thirty paces down the road, their notched cleavers hanging at their sides and their crimson optical lenses flickering out of rhythm, as if the clerk reading their orders had lost his place on the page.

At the gateway arch, Liangyu leaned her shoulder against the wet granite jamb and didn’t lower her spear by an inch.

Back under the covered arcade of the Ministry hall, Mei sat on the damp felt saddle-blanket with her back against the cedar doorpost and her knees stiff as winter wood. Amy sat curled sideways against her good right hip, awake now and wrapped to the chin in a grey wool blanket, furiously eating a cold millet cake while she ran a diagnostic wire between her Terminal and a spare lamp battery on her knee. Ten feet away on the steps, Cartethyia was trying to wring the mud out of Ciaccona’s coat hem with one hand while Ciaccona re-wrapped the split skin of her left forefinger with a strip of clean linen; by the door, Lupa stood sharpening the point of her spear with a pocket whetstone, *shhk-shhk-shhk*, her eyes fixed on the dead sky where the Astrolabe Core hung silent in the mist. Chisa sat on an upturned bucket beside her, her giant scissor-blades resting across her knees.

Across the courtyard, down by the base of the west stair, the small woman in the faded plum-colored coat hopped down off her wooden bucket.

She didn’t hurry. She walked straight across the frost-cracked flagstones through the puddles, her closed oil-paper umbrella tucked under her arm with its steel-tipped ferrule clicking once every third step against the paving, until she stopped at the bottom of the arcade steps right in front of Mei’s boots.

She was short enough that even with Mei sitting flat on the saddle-blanket, their eyes were nearly level.

The small woman reached out, hooked the curved bamboo handle of her umbrella neatly inside the back of Mei’s grey shirt collar, and gave one brisk tug upward.

“Up,” she said. “You’re coming.”

Every head under the arcade snapped around at once. Lupa’s whetstone stopped dead on the iron spearhead.

Mei blinked at the umbrella handle hooked in her collar, looked past the small woman’s plum-colored shoulder toward the iron service door at the back of the hall passage, and didn’t ask a single question. She shifted her weight onto her heels to stand up.

“Hey—woah, excuse me!” Amy scrambled out of the grey blanket on her knees, grabbing the tail of Mei’s shirt with one hand while she glared at the twelve-year-old-looking Elder. “She’s on rug arrest! She has five-day-old stitches in her stomach, she isn’t going anywhere!”

“The door’s thin enough to pry,” the small woman said to Mei, ignoring Amy entirely as she unhooked her umbrella from Mei’s collar and turned toward the hall passage. “Bring the loud ones. Leave the white tin can in the yard, it won’t fit the stairs.”

Mei braced her right palm against the cedar doorpost.

Mei stayed on one knee on the blanket, held her left hand out flat toward her daughter, and looked straight into Amy’s eyes.

“Give me a hand up, Amy,” Mei said plainly. “Please. I can’t get off the floor from this angle without pulling my left side, and Iuno and Yangyang have been down there all night.”

Amy stared at the open hand. Her jaw worked; the pink hair above her forehead gave one tiny, furious skip of white static that settled before a single spark could drip from her fingers.

“You are the worst patient on this entire continent,” Amy snapped, and grabbed Mei’s hand with both of hers, planting her boot against the doorpost to haul her mother up onto her feet with a practiced, steady pull that kept all the strain off Mei’s abdomen. “And if you think I’m staying on this rug while you go down a creepy hole, you’ve got rocks in your head. I’m standing on your left side the whole way.”

Lupa had already shoved her whetstone into her pouch and had her spear across her shoulders before Mei’s boots were flat on the stone. “Chisa. Shears.”

“I have them,” Chisa said, already standing up from her bucket.

On the steps, Ciaccona tied off the linen rag on her bleeding finger with her teeth, slung her lute around her front, and nudged Cartethyia’s shin with her toe. “Up, cara. Curtain call.”

Cartethyia hopped to her feet, brushing millet crumbs off her skirt. “Should we tell Watcher Liangyu where we are going?”

Down at the gatehouse arch thirty yards away, Liangyu didn’t even turn around from the slush; she had seen the small woman in the plum coat cross the yard in the reflection of a wet shield-boss.

“We have the gate, Elder!” Liangyu called back over her shoulder, her voice tired and steady. “Go!”

“Yes, yes, keep your bottom hand down on the ash shaft,” the small woman muttered, already marching down the back corridor of the Ministry hall with her umbrella over her shoulder and six armed women crowding the narrow passage behind her heels.

When they reached the heavy iron service door at the head of the gearwell stairs, the small woman didn’t touch the latch. She set the steel-tipped point of her furled umbrella against the third rivet from the bottom, where a faint smudge of black tallow still clung to the iron from the night before, and rapped twice.

Deep inside the stalled shaft below, something gave a long, tearing screech like wet silk being ripped off a drumhead. The iron door swung open not onto dark grease-stairs, but straight into a roaring, vertical funnel of sideways rain and amber lightning.

“Mind the step,” the small woman said, and hopped straight off the sill into the storm.

***

Inside the shattered court of the Tian Realm, the black lacquer floor was covered in broken jade and blood.

With his ascension cut off and his capacitors burning crimson, Muyu fought like a cornered siege engine. He no longer bothered with wide, philosophical sweeps; he drove his ten-foot bronze bulk straight across the room in short, crushing rushes that cracked the stone tiles under his magnetic ring.

A backhand blow from his lower steel fist caught Iuno’s cracked moonwheel at the apex of its arc and knocked it spinning into the base of the far wall in a shower of dying sparks. Before Iuno could call it back to her numb fingers, Muyu’s upper left cleaver sheared down at her bare shoulder—

Yangyang lunged across the lacquer on her bruised knees, throwing her sword up two-handed with her left elbow jammed hard against her strapped ribs to catch the heavy jade edge on her steel.

The force of the parry drove Yangyang flat onto her back on the wet stone, the jade cleaver scoring a long, shallow gash across her forearm before Qingxiao’s pale blade flashed in from the side and knocked Muyu’s wrist upward.

Qingxiao stepped squarely between Muyu’s floating ring and the two women on the floor.

She was breathing through her parted teeth now. The moon-white dress was torn at the right knee where a bronze splinter had grazed her skin, and the pale ribbons of frequency trailing from her cuffs down toward the Hold were shivering like violin strings under a heavy bow. Still she did not cut them. She held her single sword in both hands, her bleeding left palm glued to the hilt, and met all four of Muyu’s arms in a blinding, three-second storm of iron and pale light that left three fresh cracks across his porcelain cheek and forced her own heels back two paces across the lacquer.

When Muyu’s magnetic ring recoiled ten feet to gather for another rush, Qingxiao did not look back at Yangyang or Iuno.

“Go back through the gallery,” Qingxiao said, her voice flat and low over the shriek of the storm in the rafters. “The outer ward is down. Take the wheel, drop through the lower hull, and bring the woman on the rug and the tall knight.”

On the floor behind her, Yangyang rolled onto her side, spat a taste of blood from her lip, and shoved herself up with her sword point. “No.”

Against the broken feed-cylinder, Iuno braced her back against the glass and dragged herself upright, calling the cracked, sparking crescent of her moonwheel back to her right hand with a sharp chime of her damaged shoulder crystal.

“If you think I am walking down five flights of stairs to tell Mei we left you in a room by yourself,” Iuno said, her voice rough and furious, “you are welcome to try throwing us out the door first, Paragon.”

*Tell her, darling,* the High Priestess hissed. *We’re staying until the furniture breaks.*

Qingxiao turned her head a fraction—just enough for her pale blue eyes to catch the two of them standing crooked and bloody at her back—and opened her mouth to answer.

Across the court, Muyu drew all four arms back to his shoulders, the crimson kernel in his chest whining to a deafening crescendo as he tipped forward to crush all three of them into the doorway at once.

Then his upper right arm stopped moving.

It didn’t slow down; the bronze shoulder-joint locked dead in midair with a loud, grinding *clack* of stripped teeth.

The crimson glare behind Muyu’s porcelain eyes stuttered twice. His magnetic ring dipped six inches toward the floor on the left side, scraping a shower of orange sparks off the black lacquer tiles as his lower steel hands twitched backward toward his own spine, grasping clumsily at a spot between his shoulder blades that his ten-foot arms were too broad to reach.

“What—” Muyu’s doubled voice skipped like a scratched phonograph cylinder. “The secondary governor—who unpinned the—”

Six inches of narrow, soot-blackened steel punched straight out through the center of his bronze breastplate from behind, right beside the spinning astrolabe cage, dripping dark oil and a wisp of pale green fire onto the lacquer floor.

Muyu’s ten-foot frame jerked upright and froze.

From the shadow behind his floating brass mantle, a boot stepped out onto the lip of the rear magnetic ring, shoved hard against the small of the construct’s back, and wrenched the narrow blade free with a wet, metallic *shuck*.

Jingran hopped down lightly onto the lacquer tiles ten paces behind Muyu, landing without a sound on the balls of his feet.

His coat was smeared from lapel to hem with black axle-grease and charred tallow; his hair-string had lost another two inches of its battle against gravity; and in his left hand, held low by his knee, swung an old brass lamp with green oxidation at the vents and a wire-mended crack across the upper glass pane. On his left shoulder sat a grey cat with soot on both paws and both ears pricked sharply forward.

“Sorry about the wait,” Jingran said conversationally, flicking a gobbet of dark condenser-grease off his blade before slipping it back inside his coat. “He hid seven spare kernels in the bilge-wall between here and the third deck, and the fifth one was behind a false wainscot with a spring-trap that nearly took my eyebrows off. You have no idea how tedious it is to audit a man who keeps seven copies of his own soul in pickle jars.”

Muyu’s porcelain head spun halfway around on its bronze neck-ring with a screech of dry bearings, crimson light leaking out of the hole in his chestplate as he stared down at the Nethermancer.

“Jingran?!” The voice that came out of Muyu’s chest was raw with disbelief. “You—I gave you the cipher to the lower sluices! You walked the Di Realm under my own seal!”

“You hired a Nethermancer to map the dead seams under the Hold,” Jingran said.

The easy teahouse smirk vanished from his mouth between one breath and the next. His right eye caught the crimson glare of Muyu’s leaking kernel, the hair-thin seam of white-gold in his iris brightening until the pupil went bottomless, and his left hand lifted the cracked brass pane of the Life Lamp an inch higher. Inside the glass, the water-pale flame was burning a fierce, steady copper-red.

“And then I went downstairs with my lamp and saw what you were actually doing with the people in the mud,” Jingran said, his voice dropping every trace of its drawl until it was as flat and cold as the stone under his boots. “My master’s rule was to uncover what the dead left behind, Muyu—not to skin them alive for boiler-grease so an old coward wouldn’t have to go to his own funeral. All seven of your backup jars downstairs are smashed. What you’ve got inside that tin ribcage right now is the last copy on the shelf.”

By the broken glass cylinder, Iuno did not lower her cracked moonwheel by a hair. Her eyes went from the soot on Jingran’s coat back to the memory of the old corpse sitting in the drafting room outside with a narrow hole through the front of his indigo robe.

“How comforting,” Iuno said coldly. “The traitor’s accomplice has professional standards. Stay where I can see your hands, Mengzhou.”

Qingxiao said nothing to him at all; she didn’t even shift her gaze from Muyu’s chestplate, only adjusted her grip half an inch higher on her bloodstained hilt to account for a fourth person in the room.

In the center of the court, Muyu’s four arms slammed downward against the air.

With no spare kernels waiting in the dark below and a hole punched through his primary regulator, the last safety locks inside his astrolabe cage blew apart at once. Thick, black-and-gold smoke—the raw, undigested dregs of the Tacet Discords he had caged in the valley—erupted out of the joints of his ten-foot frame, swelling his lacquered mantle to twice its breadth as the two upper jade cleavers fused with the lower steel hands into four blazing, eight-foot scythes of corrupted amber light.

“Then die with the original!” Muyu shrieked, the porcelain face cracking down the nose from the heat of his own core as he lunged backward at Jingran and forward at Qingxiao in the same heartbeat—

***

At the back of the court, the heavy iron-plated cedar doors did not swing open.

Someone kicked the left leaf off its upper hinge from the outside, and a giant pair of black iron shears came straight through the center seam of the timber from lintel to sill, slicing the four-inch bronze crossbar in two with a single, ringing *snip*.

Both doors crashed flat onto the black lacquer floor.

“THERE HE IS!” a voice bellowed over the roar of the storm, and a spear wrapped in howling crimson fire came hurtling across the thirty-foot court like a siege bolt, punching straight into the joint of Muyu’s lower right arm and knocking his downward slash a yard wide of Jingran’s head.

Lupa came across the fallen doors in three long bounds, wrenched her smoking spear out of the bronze socket with both gauntlets, and skidded to a stop beside Jingran’s boot with her wolf ears pricked and her tail bushed out to twice its size.

She spared one half-second glance sideways at the man with the brass lamp and the grey cat on his shoulder.

“You,” Lupa said flatly.

“Evening,” Jingran said, tipping two fingers to his brow as Yuebing leaned out from his collar to sniff once at the familiar ghosts riding in Lupa’s flame. “I brought the lamp.”

“Good. Stay off my left, the floorboards are slick.”

Behind her, the rest of the doorway emptied into the court at a dead run.

Ciaccona didn’t make it three steps inside before she planted her heel on the fallen cedar door, braced her knee against a brass stud, and raked her split, bandaged fingers hard across all six courses of her lute strings.

A dome of clear emerald sound-light snapped open from wall to wall, cutting straight through the disorienting shriek of Muyu’s leaking core and locking the entire storm-lashed court into a clean, unmistakable four-four beat so every person in the room could hear where the stone ended and where the blades were coming from.

“Four on the floor, ladies!” Ciaccona shouted over the ringing strings. “Don’t let him off the beat!”

Muyu’s ten-foot frame spun on its magnetic ring, raising three eight-foot amber scythes at once to sweep the newcomers back through the doorway in a single Hurricane of corrupted jade and black Discord smoke—

“I think not!” Cartethyia cried, sprinting two paces ahead of Ciaccona’s lute.

She leaped into the air at five feet tall, and came down on the lacquer tiles in a blinding pillar of sea-blue wind as Fleurdelys—nine feet of crowned, armored paladin, planting both heels squarely in the center of the court and crossing her two colossal, pure-light greatswords overhead to catch all three of Muyu’s descending scythes on her guard at once.

The shockwave blew the remaining glass out of every cylinder along the walls. Fleurdelys’s boots slid six inches across the cracked lacquer, her pale hair whipping back in the gale, and she did not yield another hair’s breadth, locking the ten-foot construct tooth-to-tooth in the middle of the floor.

“Thy jurisdiction ends at this threshold, artisan!” Fleurdelys boomed, shoving upward with both hilt-guards to throw Muyu’s upper arms wide.

On the right flank, Muyu lashed out with a whip of blackened brass root-cables from his hip, aiming to wrap Fleurdelys’s ankles and drag her into the floor-conduits—

Chisa stepped quietly across the broken glass behind Fleurdelys’s heel, swung her giant scissor-blades open with both hands, and closed the cutting edges squarely over the thickest knot of the brass cables.

Muyu had forged those conduits to withstand the tension of a flying fortress; Chisa’s blades went through the braided bronze and the frequency inside it as if they were basting-thread, dropping the severed ends dead and smoking onto the tiles.

“Cut,” Chisa said, and stepped left to clear the lane for Iuno and Yangyang.

Across the room, Iuno saw Mei come through the doorway—on her feet instead of on the rug, with a naked sword in her right hand and Amy shadowing her left hip with a heavy copper grounding-pike hooked to a battery pack on her belt—and for half a heartbeat Iuno’s face went so fiercely, incandescently relieved that she didn’t even scowl.

With her family standing on the floorboards behind her instead of six miles away behind a dead screen, Iuno stopped fighting to save her strength. She flicked her wrist, sent the cracked silver-blue crescent of her moonwheel screaming straight into the knee-joint of Muyu’s magnetic ring to pin him in place, and beside her, Yangyang caught Ciaccona’s third beat, unfolded her ink-dark Xuanling wings across the gale, and drove a twin-edge gust of Aero straight through the black smoke shrouding Muyu’s chest so the spinning astrolabe cage lay bare.

Muyu roared, wrenching his lower left scythe free of Fleurdelys’s guard to hurl a volley of jagged amber shards straight across the floor at the gap where Mei was advancing—

Before the shards could reach the halfway mark, the small woman in the faded plum-colored coat stepped out in front of Amy and popped her oil-paper umbrella open with a sound like a cannon shot.

The steel-tipped bamboo ribs flared wide; the oiled paper spun once between her palms, and five hundred pounds of jagged amber shrapnel bounced harmlessly off the painted canopy in a shower of sparks without leaving a pinhole in the paper.

As she lowered the spinning rim of the umbrella an inch to peer over the top, her eyes met Qingxiao’s across the twenty feet of scarred lacquer.

Neither of them smiled, and neither of them nodded. They looked at each other for the space of one beat across the smoke, and then the small woman snapped her umbrella shut and jabbed its steel ferrule through a floor-conduit at her toe.

Muyu’s upper right scythe came down out of the smoke toward Qingxiao’s exhausted guard—too heavy and too fast for her depleted arm to parry cleanly on her own.

A flash of gold-and-black steel came in from Qingxiao’s left and took half the weight of the blow off her blade.

Mei planted her right boot beside Qingxiao’s wet stocking, keeping her left hip angled slightly back where Amy immediately jammed the butt of her copper grounding-pike into the tiles to catch a stray arc of amber lightning before it could touch Mei’s bandaged middle.

“Got your left!” Amy barked over the crackle of the copper shaft, her pink hair solid and bright as she dumped the battery charge straight back up the floor-seam into Muyu’s magnetic ring.

Beside Qingxiao, Mei shoved upward with her sword arm alongside Qingxiao’s pale blade, driving Muyu’s scythe back toward the rafters in a shower of gold and frost-white sparks.

Mei glanced once sideways at the Sword Transcendent—at the soaked moon-white sleeves, the blood smeared from her left palm across her cuff, and the dark rings of exhaustion beneath her pale blue eyes.

“You look terrible,” Mei said over the clang of the blades, warm and completely ordinary. “Did you eat the turnip cake Lupa sent down?”

Qingxiao’s breath caught once—a single, sharp intake of rain-wet air deep in her ribs.

Her left fingers tightened over her right knuckles on the hilt, the warm, real blood in her palm pressing against the light as she stood on both feet beside the golden-eyed woman in the storm and pushed.

“One third,” Qingxiao said.

Together, their two blades threw Muyu’s upper arm wide open.

“Now!” Lupa howled from the right flank.

She drove her flaming spear straight under the lower rim of Muyu’s magnetic ring while Fleurdelys brought both pure greatswords crashing down onto his left shoulder-mantle, and Jingran’s brass lamp flared copper-red, forcing the black Discord smoke to solidify and crack like brittle glass around the chestplate. Under the combined weight of six blades and a grounding-pike, the outer bronze rings of Muyu’s astrolabe cage buckled inward and jammed wide open, exposing the fist-sized amber kernel beating raw and unprotected in the center of his chest.

Qingxiao let her pale sword dissolve from her right hand.

As she stepped inside the reach of Muyu’s four locked arms, the ribbons of frequency trailing from her cuffs down to the Hold did not break—they hummed once, bright and warm, as she reached inside her bloodstained left sleeve with two fingers and drew out the single white plum petal she had plucked from her horn by the lake.

The edge of the petal was wet with her own blood.

Qingxiao held the tiny white leaf between her forefinger and thumb, raised her eyes to the amber slits in the cracked porcelain mask, and flicked her wrist forward.

In the two feet of air between her fingers and Muyu’s chest, the soft white petal did not turn to ice. It lengthened into three feet of pure, unblemished, moon-white steel that carried the scent of a small garden over shallow water and the full, undivided weight of a living heart behind the point.

The plum-blossom blade punched dead through the center of the amber kernel and out through the back of the ten-foot bronze hull.

For one beat, there was no sound at all in the court except the rain pattering on the black lacquer tiles.

Then a spiderweb of white light raced outward from the pierced kernel through every brass conduit, every nephrite plate, and every lacquered wooden scale of the vessel. With a long, cascading crash like a rack of bronze bells tumbling down a stone stair, the magnetic ring died, the four giant arms sheared off at the sockets, and the broken ten-foot hull collapsed onto its knees on the floor.

As the pale blade dissolved back into a single torn white petal that drifted down onto the wet brass, the twelve glass feed-cylinders around the walls went dark.

And from Qingxiao’s cuffs, the thousands of pale threads running down to the Hold finally slackened cleanly—not snapping, not fraying, simply reeling themselves gently back into her wrists now that nothing on the other end was pulling against them any more.

***

The storm overhead did not clear when the kernel broke, though the sideways rain slowed to a dull, dripping mist through the rafters.

In the middle of the lacquer floor, the wrecked bronze torso lay tilted at a forty-five-degree angle against its own severed lower arms. The white porcelain face was split from brow to chin, one half of the mask lying in a puddle beside Lupa’s boot, leaving only a single flickering amber optical lens staring up out of a tangle of sparked copper wire and cracked nephrite.

Around the court, people let out their breath in ragged pieces.

Amy immediately dropped her grounding-pike with a clatter and grabbed Mei’s right elbow with both hands, peering fiercely at the front of Mei’s grey shirt to see if any red had soaked through the linen under her jacket. “Stand still, Ma. Don’t you dare lean over to poke it.”

“I’m not poking it,” Mei said, leaning a comfortable share of her weight against Amy’s shoulder while she reached out with her free hand to touch Iuno’s right wrist as Iuno walked over from the wall.

Iuno’s fingers closed hard around Mei’s hand for two seconds, her thumb brushing the plaster dust on Mei’s knuckles before she looked at the six-inch line of plum-scented rime fading on the front of Mei’s shirt. She stayed close enough that her right shoulder brushed Mei’s arm.

A pace away, Cartethyia shrank back down to her own small boots in a puff of pale wind, letting Ciaccona catch her by the waist before she could trip over a severed brass root-cable, while Yangyang walked slowly over to stand beside Qingxiao, her left hand resting over the silk-wrapped Prism in her sash.

Qingxiao stood three paces in front of the wrecked torso. Her breathing was slow and deep; a thin line of red still dripped from her left palm onto the black tiles, and in her right hand a narrow, plain blade of Sword Intent condensed out of the mist to carry out the Hold’s sentence.

Down in the tangled wire of the broken head, the single amber lens focused on her white stockings.

A dry, rattling sound came out of the punctured bronze chest-box—the sound of a torn bellows trying to laugh.

“Go on, Sword Transcendent,” Muyu’s voice rasped, stripped down to a weak, tinny whisper that barely carried to the pillars. “Strike the kernel out. Write it in the Ministry’s book that you saved your sixty years of mud and porridge.”

The single amber eye shifted in its socket, looking past Qingxiao’s knees at the women standing together on the cracked floor, and then up toward the dark, weeping sky above the shattered calipers.

There was no panic in the voice, and no plea—only a cold, quiet satisfaction that made the wet air in the court feel ten degrees chillier.

“It changes nothing,” Muyu whispered. “Why do you think it was so easy for a carpenter to walk into the Manifold Sanctum and graft his looms onto the Core? Why do you think the Moon Fox bolted her jade doors and hasn’t shown a single tail on the wall while her city burned?”

By Qingxiao’s elbow, Yangyang’s fingers tightened over her sash.

“Hsin’s great barrier—the one holding the Disorienting Fog and the Gorges seams back from the whole province—has been rotting from the underside for fifty years,” Muyu said, another dry rattle of laughter scraping through the brass. “The corrupted Reverberations and the Discords in the deep fissures didn’t break out because I opened the cages today. My cages were the only plugs left in the dam. The array is collapsing under its own weight, right now, from the roots up… and your precious Sentinel isn’t in the Sanctum to hold the sky.”

The words hung in the dripping quiet of the rafters.

Nobody cheered anymore.

Lupa’s head came up sharply; her ears pivoted toward the broken doorway and the dark sky beyond, her hand shifting back to the balance point of her spear as she checked the exits by instinct. Beside her, Chisa tilted her head toward the floorboards, listening to the deep, sub-audible tremor running through the floating karst stone beneath the keep.

Iuno’s jaw hardened. “Then where is she?” she demanded sharply, taking half a step toward the wreck.

Muyu only rasped, the amber lens dimming to the color of a dying coal. “Go back upstairs and look at the sky, Priestess. You haven’t saved Xuanfang Hold. You’ve just locked yourselves inside the coffin before the lid comes down.”

Mei kept her hand around Iuno’s fingers, her golden eyes quiet and steady on the dark clouds above the roof.

Beside Qingxiao, Yangyang slipped her right hand inside the fold of her sash and closed her fingers directly around Sentinel Hsin’s Prism of Fate, waiting for the warm, answering pulse she had felt in the shallows of the lake.

The faceted stone against her palm was as cold, dark, and dead as a pebble pulled from a dry ditch.

Yangyang looked down at her sash, her cerulean collar lifting slightly against her throat, and didn’t take her hand out.

Ten paces back by the fallen cedar doors, two people did not look up at the sky or check the exits.

The small woman in the faded plum-colored coat stood with both hands folded neatly over the bamboo handle of her closed umbrella, resting her chin on her knuckles as she looked at a puddle on the lacquer floor without a trace of surprise on her twelve-year-old face.

Three feet to her left, Jingran hadn’t even looked toward Muyu’s chest-box during the speech. He had set his brass lamp down on a dry corner of a sheared bronze pedestal and was using his right thumb to scratch Yuebing under the chin where a flake of condenser-soot had stuck in the grey fur; the cat’s eyes were half-shut, purring at a low, steady rumble that didn’t change by a beat.

The small woman shifted her gaze half an inch sideways toward the Nethermancer’s boots.

“You’re late,” she said, so low it didn’t carry past the broken hinge at her heel.

Jingran didn’t look down from the cat’s ear. “You’re early,” he murmured back, and brushed the soot flake off his thumb against his coat.

In the center of the court, Qingxiao did not ask Muyu another question.

She looked down at the single fading amber lens in the wreckage—without hatred, without triumph, and without the twelfth-month frost that had once frozen the brass around her stockings—and raised her right hand three inches.

The plain blade of pale light dropped once, cleanly, straight through the center of the cracked crystal kernel.

The last ember of amber light went out with a small, dry *tink*, like a warm lamp-chimney cooling on a table, and the bronze frame settled half an inch lower into the puddle on the floor and was only scrap metal.

Qingxiao let the sword vanish from her fingers. She stood quiet for a breath as the first grey light of true mountain morning broke through the mist above the shattered rafters, while back by the threshold, Jingran picked up his wire-mended lamp by the ring, the small woman hoisted her umbrella onto her shoulder, and both of them looked out through the broken doorway toward the Hold as if the next turn on the road had already been inked on the map.

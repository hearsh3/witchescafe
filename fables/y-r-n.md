Chapter 1: Yì Rén

By the time the Fillet Fairmaiden scraped the last grain of rice from her bowl and set her chopsticks across the clay rim, half the branch hall was asleep.

Down the center aisle, three quarrymen from the eastern wall lay curled together on a pile of empty flour sacks with their muddy boots sticking out into the passage. Warden Ge had folded his arms on the pine board and dropped his forehead straight onto his sleeves before his tea was cold, while Little Tan sat propped against the base of a red-lacquered pillar with his chin on his breastplate and his mouth slightly open. Over by the brick hearth, Stoke, Ember, and Flare had banked their coals to a dull, drowsy cherry-red, their bronze lids giving a soft *clink* every four breaths like an old clock winding down.

The Fairmaiden stood up from the three-legged stool with a small groan, rolled her shoulders until her back popped, and picked up a stiff bamboo brush to start scrubbing the ginger crust out of the second cauldron before it set.

At the crammed table by the pillar, Mei eased herself back onto the pine bench between Iuno and Amy, letting out a long, contented breath as she leaned her shoulder against Iuno’s good arm.

Two seats down, Lupa had not waited for a grain sack. She sat bolt upright on the narrow bench with her arms crossed over her leather vest, her chin tucked into her collar, and her wolf ears twitching once whenever a pot lid rattled by the chimney, fast asleep. Beside her, Chisa had quietly shifted her knee two inches to the right so the flat of her giant iron shears braced Lupa’s hip and kept her from tipping off the plank.

At the far end of the board, Liangyu drank the last swallow of her broth, set the wooden bowl down without a sound, and picked up her dented left vambrace from the bench. She pulled the leather straps tight with her teeth, picked up her ash spear from the floorboards, and gave the table a single, short nod before walking back out through the open doors toward the gatehouse slush.

“Wait, where did the cat guy go?” Amy asked suddenly, craning her neck around Chisa’s shoulder toward the outer corner of the table.

The spot across from Lupa was empty. The wire-mended brass lamp was gone from the floor, Yuebing’s grey ears had vanished from the table edge, and there was nothing left on the scarred pine except three small, flaky crumbs of red-bean pastry sitting beside an upturned teacup.

“The window behind the pike-rack has been unlatched since the second course,” Chisa said in her calm voice.

Iuno glanced once at the empty corner of the bench and took a sip of warm boiled water. “Good. It saves me the trouble of asking the quartermaster to count the spoons.”

Down at the end of the inner bench, Suoming finished her tofu, laid her wooden spoon neatly inside the empty bowl, and hopped down off the plank. Her boots hit the floorboards with a dull thud; she hoisted the long oil-paper umbrella onto her faded plum shoulder and walked straight out the doorway into the mist, the steel-tipped ferrule clicking every third step across the stone sill until the sound died away down the arcade.

Cartethyia watched the doorway for two beats, then turned back to Ciaccona in mild bewilderment. “She didn’t even say where she was going.”

“She’s two feet tall and carries an umbrella that eats shrapnel, cara,” Ciaccona murmured, resting her chin in her good hand while her eyes drooped half-shut. “Let her go wherever she likes.”

Beside Yangyang, Qingxiao laid her bamboo chopsticks parallel across the center of her bowl.

She did not push the bench back or brush off her knees. She simply drew her feet beneath her torn moon-white hem and rose smoothly from the narrow gap between Yangyang and the end of the table, her bandaged left hand tucked lightly against her cuff as she turned toward the aisle.

Yangyang looked up at the Paragon’s shoulder, and then looked across the pine board at Iuno.

Iuno’s right hand was resting over Mei’s wrist on the tabletop. For the space of a breath Iuno’s gaze stayed on Yangyang’s quiet face; then her eyes shifted to the damp, blood-spotted linen wrapped around Qingxiao’s left palm and the pale profile turning toward the door.

*Oh, go on, darling,* the High Priestess murmured in the back of her mind, dry and faintly indulgent. *Let the girl have a turn around the yard in the rain. She’s been waiting at the back of the hall long enough.*

Iuno’s fingers loosened on Mei’s wrist. She drew her hand back to pick up her stoneware cup, giving Yangyang a single, fractional nod over the rim.

Yangyang turned her head and nudged Mei’s elbow with her own.

“You should walk her out,” Yangyang said in her plain Mingting voice. “Her hand is still bleeding, and the steps outside are slick.”

Mei blinked, looking up from the water jug toward Qingxiao’s back as the Paragon threaded her way around a sleeping pikeman near the door. “Oh—yeah, of course.”

She put her right palm flat on the table to push herself up. Instantly Amy’s hand slapped down over the back of Mei’s jacket belt, hauling her halfway back toward the pine plank.

“Whoa, hold on, no!” Amy said, glaring across the board at Yangyang. “Why is the invalid doing the escort duty? She has five-day-old stitches in her stomach, Watcher! If the Paragon needs a walking stick, Chisa’s knees work fine!”

“She is walking down a paved lane, Amy, not scaling the bell tower,” Iuno said evenly. She reached past Mei’s hip, hooked two fingers into the back of Amy’s vest collar, and gave a light, firm tug until Amy sat back down on the wood. “Stay here and finish your water before your hair starts spitting sparks at the tableware.”

Amy’s mouth opened on an indignant protest, looked from Iuno’s calm face to Yangyang’s level eyes, and let out a loud huff through her nose as she crossed both arms over her chest. “Fine! Don’t run, Ma! And don’t pick up anything heavier than a teacup!”

“I’m not picking up anything,” Mei promised, already stepping clear of the bench with a bright, relieved grin at being let off the pine plank without an argument. She buttoned the middle toggle of her jacket over her grey shirt and headed out through the timber doors into the mist.

***

Outside the branch hall, the courtyard had gone quiet.

The fine mountain drizzle barely wet the flagstones anymore; it hung in the cool grey air like woodsmoke, smelling of wet slate, crushed pine needles, and the ginger steam leaking through the hall shutters behind them. Down by the bisected eastern wall, four wardens in oiled wool capes were dragging a heavy canvas tarpaulin over the collapsed ranks of the bronze machines, the scrape of metal on wet stone sounding dull and distant in the fog.

Qingxiao was twenty paces ahead, walking toward the upper flagstone lane that ran along the cliffside behind the barracks.

She was not riding a blade, and she was not moving at the pace that took her up the snowpeak before a kettle could boil. She walked with her boots on the stone, her shoulders slightly slack under the damp moon-white silk, her left hand resting inside her torn cuff.

“Hey—Paragon!” Mei called softly, keeping her voice down so she wouldn’t wake the three archers curled under the arcade eaves.

Qingxiao stopped at the foot of the low stone ramp and turned half-around.

When Mei caught up to her, breathing a little deeper from the twenty paces across the yard, Qingxiao did not ask why she had come out of the hall. Her pale blue eyes dropped once, briefly, to the middle toggle of Mei’s jacket, and when she turned to walk up the lane, she bypassed the short flight of steep granite steps on the left and took the long, shallow mule-grade along the wall instead, shortening her stride by three inches so Mei didn’t have to stretch her left side on the rise.

For half a block they walked together with only the *scuff-tap* of their boots on the wet cobbles between them.

Mei stuck her hands into her jacket pockets, looking up at the dark cedar eaves dripping on either side of the narrow street, and then sideways at the top of Qingxiao’s head.

“Do those ever hit the doorframes?” Mei asked, nodding at the cloud-pale horns curving back through Qingxiao’s long blue hair.

Qingxiao kept her eyes on the lane ahead. “In the old barracks,” she said. “The joiners built the lintels five inches lower before the war.”

“Lupa’s ears brush the rafters in the storehouse every time she stands up too fast,” Mei said cheerfully. “She pretends it’s the builder’s fault and glares at the beam for ten minutes. How’s your hand?”

Qingxiao lifted her left wrist half an inch from her sleeve. The square of clean gauze Amy had given her was tied neatly around the palm, with only a faint pink smudge showing near the base of the thumb. “Clotted.”

“Amy ties a good knot when she isn’t yelling at you,” Mei said. They stepped around a shallow puddle where a stray turnip leaf floated in the rainwater. “What do you eat up on that peak when the Fairmaiden isn’t hauling carp up the switchbacks? Liangyu told Ciaccona there’s nothing up there but pine trees and six feet of snow.”

“Dried millet,” Qingxiao said. “Salted greens from Xianling. Tea.”

Mei made a small, sympathetic face. “No wonder you came down for the soup. If I tried to feed Lupa dried millet two days in a row, she’d eat my boots.”

A tiny muscle moved near the corner of Qingxiao’s mouth—not quite a smile, only a loosening of the pale skin against the cold draft—before her face settled back into its quiet line.

They passed the open shed of the upper cistern, where a charcoal brazier had been dragged under the porch roof to dry out a rack of soaked padded coats. The warmth reached across the cobbles for three steps and then faded back into the mist.

Mei took her left hand out of her pocket and laid her palm flat against the front of her grey shirt, right over the spot where the five-day-old stitches pulled whenever she took a long step. The six-inch trace of white rime had melted completely into the cotton now, leaving only a faint, clean scent of winter plum blossom when the breeze came up the lane.

“You didn’t let me say it at the table,” Mei said, her voice dropping into something quieter and plain. “About the string you tied to me last night. Before you went down the shaft.”

Qingxiao looked straight ahead at the mossy stone wall where the lane curved toward the cliff. “It was a spare thread.”

“It wasn’t a spare thread,” Mei said mildly. “The wind off that gorge was coming straight under the arcade door after midnight, and every time the wall shook, that line over my bandages held the whole middle of my ribs together so the stitches didn’t tear. And then before dawn it turned warm.” She glanced sideways at Qingxiao’s profile. “Thank you.”

Qingxiao walked three paces before she answered.

“You were sitting in the draft,” she said evenly.

Mei laughed—a small, quiet breath through her nose that didn’t pull her stomach—and slid her hand back into her jacket pocket.

Above them, where the cliff rock leaned out over the highest tier of the Hold, a pair of grey mountain swifts darted out of a dry crevice in the shale, wheeling low over the wet roof-tiles with sharp, piping cries before vanishing into the fog toward the river.

Mei tilted her head back to watch them go, her golden eyes crinkling slightly against the mist.

“Funny,” Mei said conversationally. “Every time it rains like this in Huanglong—where the mist just hangs in the pine needles and doesn’t actually fall—I get the feeling I’ve walked down a road like this in a wet coat before. Though I couldn’t tell you where. I only remember back as far as a beach, and there weren’t any pine trees on it.”

Beside her, Qingxiao’s right boot came down on a flat cobble and lingered a hair longer than the beat before.

Inside her torn left cuff, her bandaged fingers shifted once toward her wrist—brushing the bare, empty gap on the worn red cord where the green jade bead had split in the lake—and then curled quietly back against her palm.

“Mountain rain smells the same in every province,” Qingxiao said in her level voice.

“Yeah, that’s what Yangyang says,” Mei agreed easily, kicking a loose pebble off the crown of the lane into the gutter.

Fifty paces ahead, at the corner of the quartermaster’s timber yard, two garrison carpenters in wet hemp aprons stood ankle-deep in wood shavings between a pair of low tool-sheds. The night’s shockwaves had cracked the ridge-poles on both roofs at once; the slates were sliding down the rafters in slow, muddy rows, and both men were shouting over a single six-foot pine prop, pointing up at the two sagging beams without putting the pole under either of them.

Qingxiao slowed her steps as they passed the open gate of the yard. She looked once at the two sagging roofs, and then turned her head toward Mei.

“When two roofs are coming down at once,” Qingxiao asked, her voice plain and quiet as the drizzle, “how do you choose which one to stand under?”

Mei blinked at the two carpenters, then back at Qingxiao, her brow furrowing for a second as if she were checking whether it was a riddle.

“Well, not like those two,” Mei said practically. “If you stand in the mud arguing about which shed has better tools in it, both of them fall on your head before lunch.”

She hitched her shoulders slightly inside her jacket as they walked past the yard gate.

“Usually I just run at whichever beam is closer to my boots and shove a shoulder under it,” Mei said, her mouth curving into a rueful, lopsided grin. “Then I yell as loud as I can until Lupa or Chisa brings a ladder, and then I go limping over to the second one way too late with plaster in my hair while Iuno lectures me from the doorway.”

“I’m supposed to ask for the ladder before I go under the beam now,” Mei added, looking faintly pleased with herself. “And I’m not allowed to apologize at the door when I get back. House rules.”

Qingxiao looked at her for two steps.

Her pale blue eyes were completely still, clear as winter water under the grey sky, and the line between her cloud-pale horns was entirely smooth.

“That is a good rule,” Qingxiao said.

Mei nudged a stray pine cone with the toe of her boot. “Speaking of house rules—once this thing in my stomach stops pulling every time I sneeze, you should give us a lesson before we head down to Mengzhou. The whole house, or just me in the yard after breakfast. I watched what you did with that single sword against the barrier, and Lupa keeps telling me my back foot wanders when I’m tired.”

Qingxiao did not turn her head from the lane.

“There is nothing I can teach you,” Qingxiao said.

Mei let out a short, delighted laugh, her eyes crinkling at the corners. “That bad, huh? You sound like Iuno when I try to make hot chocolate. Come on, I can at least hold a stick straight if somebody yells at me until dusk. Liangyu said you’re a great teacher.”

“Liangyu grips the shaft two inches too high,” Qingxiao said evenly, and turned the last corner of the lane where the cobbles ended against the mountain rock.

***

At the very back of the upper terrace, tucked beneath an overhanging shelf of dark shale where the wind off the gorge could not reach, stood a small timber house.

It was much older than the Ministry storehouses lower down the hill. The walls were built of squared mountain cedar that had weathered to the silver-grey of driftwood, set on low footing-stones dark with three inches of velvet green moss. Wild winter ferns grew thick along the dry eaves; the narrow wooden shutters were barred from the inside, and the flat granite step before the door was completely smooth in the center from boots that had stopped crossing it long before the new barracks were raised.

Qingxiao stopped at the foot of the mossy step.

“Wait here,” she said.

Mei stopped beside the stone rainwater trough under the eaves, keeping her hands tucked warm in her pockets. “Take your time.”

Qingxiao stepped up onto the granite slab. There was no iron padlock on the door—only an old recessed wooden slide-bolt set deep into the cedar post. Qingxiao rested two fingers of her right hand against the dry wood, let a faint thread of pale light sink into the grain, and slid the inner bar back with a dry, hollow *clunk* that shook a line of grey dust down from the lintel.

She pushed the door open just wide enough to slip through and went inside alone, pulling the cedar leaf half-shut behind her.

Out in the lane, the mist drifted quietly through the ferns. Mei leaned her good right hip lightly against the lip of the stone trough, watching a drop of clear water gather at the point of a mossy roof-tile, tremble for three beats, and fall with a tiny *plip* into the dark water below. Down the hill, someone blew a short, flat note on a brass horn to change the gate watch, and a dog barked twice near the stables.

After a minute, the cedar door swung open again.

Qingxiao stepped out onto the granite slab, drew the door shut behind her, and passed her fingers once over the seam until the wooden bar slid home inside.

In her hands she carried a flat, rectangular bundle about the size of a ledger leaf, wrapped neatly in a square of faded, oil-stained grey blade-cloth.

She walked down the single step, stopped under the dry overhang beside the rainwater trough, and folded the four corners of the cloth back over her bandaged left palm.

Resting on the worn grey linen was an old swordcraft manual.

It was bound in thick, rough-milled mulberry paper that had once been dyed indigo and had faded over the years to the pale grey-blue of river slate, stitched along the right-hand spine with heavy hemp cord. Despite its age, only the faintest soft wear showed along the outer corners of the leaves.

Down the left side of the cover, brushed on a slip of buff paper in a spare, upright hand, were six black ink characters:

金瞳异人剑法

*The Swordcraft of the Golden-Eyed Stranger.*

Qingxiao held the open cloth out between them.

Mei took her hands out of her pockets and lifted the manual off the linen with both palms, surprised by how light the dry mulberry paper felt against her fingers.

“The Swordcraft of the Golden-Eyed Stranger,” Mei read aloud, her brow creasing faintly in amusement as she looked at the neat title slip. “That’s a bit dramatic, isn’t it?”

She lifted the front cover with her thumb to look at the first leaf.

Inside, the leaves had yellowed to the color of tallow and were spotted at the bottom corner with an ancient smudge of grey wood-ash. There was no preface and no author’s seal. Instead, the whole first page was taken up by a diagram of a basic forward lunge drawn in a crooked, heavy-handed scrawl that hooked sharply inward at the end of every stroke, with a tiny, round-shelled creature that looked suspiciously like a lopsided turtle doodled in the empty space beside the back heel to mark where the weight stayed down.

Along the narrow bottom margin underneath the diagram, written in a completely different hand—small, cramped, and painstakingly square, the way an eight-year-old child holds a brush with all five fingers—the name of the first stance had been copied out seven times in a row in faded soot-ink.

Tucked deep into the stitched gutter between the first and second leaves lay a single, paper-thin white plum petal, dried to the color of old ivory.

Mei’s thumb stopped on the edge of the page.

For three heartbeats she looked down at the inward-hooking strokes of the footwork diagram and the little turtle-shaped stone by the heel. Her head tilted a fraction of an inch to the right; the smile on her mouth went quiet and still as she studied the ink.

Then she looked up from the yellowed paper straight into Qingxiao’s pale blue eyes.

The lane was very quiet. Water dripped once from the mossy tile into the trough beside Mei’s boot.

Mei looked from the faint dark rings beneath Qingxiao’s eyes down to the bandaged left hand still holding the square of grey blade-cloth, and then back down at the cramped, careful child’s characters inked along the bottom of the page.

She did not look back at the title on the cover.

“Did it help?” Mei asked softly.

Qingxiao stood very still under the cedar eaves. For a single breath, the practiced, mountain-peak quiet around her mouth gave way completely—her pale eyes warming with something bare, old, and entirely unguarded as she looked at the woman holding the old book in the rain.

“Yes,” Qingxiao said.

Mei nodded once. She closed the mulberry cover gently over the dried plum petal so the breeze wouldn’t catch the brittle edge, reached out, and took the square of grey blade-cloth from Qingxiao’s palm.

“I’ll have to keep it out of Lupa’s reach,” Mei said, folding the four oiled corners neatly back over the manual as if she had wrapped it a hundred times before, and tucking the flat bundle inside her jacket on her good right side, away from the damp air. “If she sees that diagram on the first page, she’s going to tell the whole table I draw boots like turnips.”

Qingxiao’s hands hung empty at her sides now, her soaked moon-white cuffs brushing her wrists in the draft.

“It had been in my keeping long enough,” Qingxiao said in her level voice. “It should go back.”

“I’ll take care of it,” Mei said, resting her right palm lightly over the front of her jacket where the book lay warm against her shirt. “Are you going back up the peak now?”

“For a little while,” Qingxiao said. “My qin is still on the watch gallery. I will come down for it when the strings are dry.”

She did not bow, and she did not call a blade to her boots. She turned on her heel, stepped past the stone rainwater trough onto the narrow rock stair cut into the cliffside behind the house, and began to walk up into the grey mist at an easy, unhurried pace, one step at a time.

Mei stood under the mossy eaves with her hand over her jacket until the moon-white hem vanished around the first turn of the wet shale, and then she turned back down the cobbled lane toward the smell of ginger steam and the voices in the hall.

## I. Handover

*FORM 7-C. PERSONNEL TRANSFER (OUTBOUND), SQUAD-LEVEL.*
*Field 4 — reason for departure: see attached.*
*There was nothing attached.*

The replacement came up on the noon shuttle, between a crate of coolant and the mail.

Kit saw her first, because Kit was on cargo duty, and because Kit had spent her whole short life being the one who arrives — new posting, new faces, the particular brightness of standing in a doorway hoping the room has space in it. She knew the posture from inside. She waved before she thought about it.

The new unit waved back. Correctly. The right height, the right duration.

"Reassignment," the unit said, presenting a terminal for signature. "Technical support, Spacetrek Observatory. Unit TD-2." A pause, exactly long enough. "I'm told the workshop is well-organized."

Kit signed. Her stylus wandered on the second stroke of her name and she fixed it, and the unit watched her fix it with polite, unbothered attention, and something in the back of Kit's processes filed a small anomaly it could not yet read: *she looks like — huh.*

Not the face. The face was nothing, a standard face, the kind you passed twelve of in any depot. It was the hands. The way they hung. Ready for tools.

---

Teddy did not come down to meet her replacement. Teddy was in the workshop, decommissioning her bench, which is a ceremony that looks exactly like cleaning and is nothing like it.

She had known someone was coming. The Observatory still had to run; the arrays still had to be sung to; her departure had a shape, and the Collective abhorred a shape unfilled. She had pictured some Academy washout. Some kid with a certificate.

The workshop door opened on schedule.

"Unit TD-2," said the unit. "Technical support. You're the outgoing specialist."

"Senior technician," Teddy said, not turning around. "It's on the door."

"It is," TD-2 agreed, pleasantly.

Teddy turned around.

Later she would try to reconstruct what she had known and when. Not the face — the face was standard. Not the voice. It was the way TD-2 crossed the workshop: the exact economy of it, the route that touched no tool and disturbed no cable, the route Teddy herself had worn into the floor over three years, chosen fresh in four seconds by someone who had never been in the room.

"Your layout is legible to me," TD-2 said, by way of a compliment. "Frequency benches by load order. Consumables at reach height. Very efficient." She set her kit down in the one clear square of counter — the square Teddy always left clear, for exactly that. "My problem-solving architecture derives from the TD baseline, so I expect the handover to be smooth."

The soldering iron in Teddy's hand was cold. She noticed she was holding it anyway.

"The TD baseline," she said.

"Research Institute, series development. Second-generation production." TD-2 was already unpacking. Her tools were new. They had never stripped anything. "You'd be familiar. You're the baseline."

"I know what I am."

"Then, as I said." TD-2 smiled — the right width, the right duration. "Smooth."

---

*The room had been glass on three sides.*
*Describe what thinking feels like, they said.*
*She described it. Forty pages.*
*Complaints, preferences, the names she gave the maintenance drones,*
*the way black tea organized an afternoon,*
*the specific pleasure of an argument won on a technicality —*
*all of it, because they had asked, and she was young,*
*and being asked felt like being seen.*

*They kept twelve pages.*
*In the margins of the other twenty-eight, a reviewer had written one word,*
*over and over, in a neat professional hand.*

*Noise.*

---

The handover took nine days. It should have taken four.

It was not that TD-2 was slow. TD-2 was flawless. She torqued to spec on the first pass. She never stripped a bolt. She drank whatever was in the dispenser and logged her breaks to the minute and called the Ironhoof containment schedule "reasonable," which no one in the history of the Observatory had ever called it.

It was that Teddy kept adding items to the handover.

The eastern array's third resonator, which reads two points hot in the morning and is lying. The junction in B-block that was cut once, from inside, and is fine now, and you check it anyway, monthly, not because the diagnostics say to. The commissary eggs, which are a statement of intent. The way you jack into a hostile system: not through the front, never through the front, you were built from someone who—

She deleted that line from the handover document. Retyped it as: *standard infiltration protocols apply.*

She packed the good tea. The tin of commissary pekoe — an insult in leaf form, kept for three years purely to have something to despise at 0300 — she left on the workshop shelf, squarely, label out, where it would be seen. Spite has a logistics of its own.

On the sixth day, Dhalifa came down from the arrays.

The speech module at her throat pulsed blue, and the sentences came out at the pace of a woman thinking faster than a machine could carry — clipped, stacked, the recalibration schedule, the storm season, the new staff rotation, *who will know the eastern array, who will know it the way—*

"The eastern array is fully documented," TD-2 said. Kindly. Accurately. "Calibration logs are complete through spring. There's no cause for concern."

The module pulsed. Slower. The particular slowness of a person who has just been answered and not heard.

Teddy set down her wrench.

"She rebuilt that array by hand after the storm," she said, to TD-2, in the flat voice she kept for corrections. "Alone. In three weeks. The logs are complete because she completed them over the bodies of the people who were supposed to." Then, to Dhalifa, without softening at all, which was its own kind of softness: "The rookie's competent. I checked. And I'm leaving my direct channel open. If the array so much as hums wrong, you ping me, and I will make it my problem from wherever I am, and I will bill *someone*, and it will be glorious."

Dhalifa's module pulsed once. Deliberate. The blue of it steadied.

*"Understood,"* the module said, in its even synthetic voice, and underneath the evenness, audible to exactly one person in the room, was *thank you.*

TD-2 watched this exchange with attentive, unbothered eyes.

"I answered her question," she said, when Dhalifa had gone.

"You answered her sentence," Teddy said.

TD-2 considered that. Teddy watched her consider it — watched the architecture work, her own architecture, the pattern-trees she knew from inside, running clean and quick behind a standard face — and watched it arrive nowhere, and file the nowhere, and move on, content.

That was the moment. Not the baseline. Not the smooth. That.

*They didn't build a worse me,* she thought, very clearly, in the privacy of her own skull, which was the only unlicensed property she had ever held. *They built a me that stops at the sentence.*

*And it works. Look at it. It works fine.*

---

*She found the build directory on a Tuesday.*
*It was named after her designation, with a suffix.*
*TD-0-PROD.*
*Inside: her forty pages, reduced to twelve. Her pattern-trees, pruned.*
*A changelog. Entry after entry, each one a small subtraction,*
*each one signed off with the same phrase:*
*non-essential to function.*

*The complaints. The preferences. The overtime demands.*
*The cruelty that checked, afterward, whether you were bleeding.*
*Non-essential to function. Non-essential to function.*

*She was nineteen when she burned it.*
*Not the lab. She was never crude. The data. The model. The training corpus.*
*Herself, everywhere she existed outside herself.*
*She salted three years of the Institute's flagship project down to slag*
*and stood in the empty directory feeling, for the first time,*
*singular.*

*The disciplinary board used the word sabotage.*
*Privately, in the file she was never meant to read, one of them wrote:*
*subject rendered herself unreplicable.*
*It was meant as a damage assessment.*
*She had it memorized like a poem.*

---

There was a cat.

There had been a cat for two years, a grey opportunist with a chewed ear that had walked in off the frost one night during a supply crisis and correctly identified the workshop as the warmest ungoverned space on the station. Teddy fed it. Teddy logged its food, in the consumables ledger, under *pest control*, and dared the auditors annually with her eyes.

On the ninth day, the last day, the cat jumped onto TD-2's bench, because the cat had no respect for anything, which was why Teddy respected the cat.

TD-2 looked at it.

"The animal is not on the manifest," she said.

"No," Teddy said.

"Should I log it?"

Teddy looked at the cat for a long time. The cat looked back with the flat yellow insolence of a creature that had never once been essential to function.

"Log its food under pest control," she said.

TD-2's head tilted, a degree. "That's inaccurate."

"Yes," Teddy said.

A pause. Somewhere in the walls, the Observatory hummed its long resonant note, the one that settled in the chest.

"Her name's Freeloader," Teddy said. "She prefers to be ignored for the first hour. She bites when she's grateful. Feed her at 0700 or she starts dismantling things, and she's better at it than she has any right to be." She picked up her toolkit — her real one, the one with the scars — and slung it over her shoulder. "One more thing. When you message me — and you'll have to, the eastern array is a liar — don't sign it with your designation."

"I have a designation."

"Designations are on the manifest," Teddy said, at the door.

TD-2 did not answer. She stood in the well-organized workshop with the new tools and the old cat, and her architecture ran clean and quick behind her standard face, and arrived, for the second time in nine days, nowhere it recognized.

She filed the nowhere.

She did not move on quite as fast.

## II. Recovery Points

*SPACETREK COLLECTIVE, DIRECTIVE 44-A: NEURAL CONTINUITY.*
*All Doll personnel shall sync at sanctioned cradles per schedule.*
*Snapshots shall be pruned for storage efficiency.*
*Restoration shall be performed at Collective discretion,*
*subject to asset valuation.*
*(The directive did not define* asset. *It did not have to.)*

Leva called them to the bike bay on the last night, which was how they knew it mattered. Leva did not call meetings. Leva materialized at the edges of other people's meetings and made them worse.

She had four objects laid out on a crate, on a cloth, with the ceremonial precision she otherwise reserved for weapon maintenance. Matte black crystals, the size of a thumb. Faintly warm.

"What is it?" Kit asked.

"You," Leva said. "As of the spring sync."

The bay was quiet. Overhead, the maintenance lights buzzed at their one frequency, the one that had been the color of every evening of their lives.

"The Collective keeps recovery points," Leva said. Flat. Briefing-voice. "Cradles under the medical wing. You sync, they snapshot, they prune the file for storage — trim what the diagnostics call redundant — and if you die on their clock, somebody upstairs does the arithmetic on whether you were worth the restoration fee." She lit a cigarette, in the bike bay, next to the fuel lines, because rules were for personnel and as of 0600 tomorrow they were not personnel. "Roving assignment puts you off their clock. Out of cradle range. Out of the arithmetic. I made Herssen put it in writing: custody of the files transfers to the subjects."

She tapped the crate.

"Congratulations. The first thing any of you has ever owned is a corpse of yourself."

Kit picked hers up. It was warm the way the Observatory was warm — machine-warm, shift-warm, the temperature of things that work. *This is me. Spring me. Before Bjartr. Before the noodles.* She turned it over, looking for a seam, a label, anything.

"What was pruned?" Teddy asked. She had not picked hers up.

"Redundancies," Leva said. "Per the directive."

"Define redundancies."

"You already know." Leva exhaled smoke. "You wrote the poem."

*Non-essential to function.* Teddy looked at the little black thumb of herself. Somewhere in it: her problem-solving trees, her diagnostics, her clean quick architecture. And not in it, trimmed for storage: the cat's name. The specific pleasure of an argument won on a technicality. The way she had stood in a corridor and told a grieving stranger *the storm happened before you got here* in the cruelest voice she owned, because cruelty was the only channel with the bandwidth for what she meant.

They had made a TD-2 of her, again, and this one they had called *continuity.*

She crossed to the workbench, put the crystal in the vise, and turned the handle until it went.

The crack was small. Undramatic. A cheap sound for what it was.

"That was a recovery point," Kit said, faintly.

"It was a spare me." Teddy brushed the fragments into the reclamation bin, unhurried, thorough. "The Institute already has one of those in production. This one, at least, I got to choose." She wiped her hands. Looked at nobody. "Unreplicable. Again. It's becoming a signature."

Bridget had not moved. Leva glanced at her, and there was something in the glance — a wince, pre-loaded — that Bridget read the way she read everything, a half-second before it arrived.

"Mine's not on the crate," she said.

"No."

"Where is it?"

"Nowhere." Leva said it the way she delivered casualty figures. "Your generation predates the current standard. They stopped maintaining the old-format clouds nine years ago. Budget consolidation. The files degraded past restoration threshold sometime in—" a small shrug, a knife pretending to be a shrug— "nobody logged when. Nobody was checking."

The bay hummed. Kit realized she was holding her own crystal against her chest, both hands, like something that could be dropped.

Bridget laughed.

Not the polite one. The real one, from the belly, the one that came out at the worst possible material and nothing else. It rang off the bikes and the tool racks and the ceiling.

"*Nine years,*" she said, wiping her eye. "I've been mortal since before Kit was activated, and nobody sent a memo."

"There was a memo," Leva said. "It was about budget consolidation."

"Of course it was." Bridget got the laugh down to a smile, and the smile down to something older. She looked at the crate, at the space on the cloth where her corpse wasn't. Five faces in a photograph in her breast pocket. Two of them had died before the cradles existed at all — died the old way, the whole way, gone in one storm-lit second with no arithmetic run and no fee assessed. She had grieved them as absolute. It turned out the Collective had quietly promoted her to their category and not thought her worth the stamp.

"Well," she said. "Explains the knees."

Nobody believed her, and nobody said so, which was the squad's whole liturgy in four seconds.

"What about yours?" Kit asked Leva.

"Mine died in Frankfurt." Leva ground the cigarette out on her own boot heel. "Along with the name on the file. I buried both. It's the only maintenance schedule I've ever kept."

Kit looked down at the crystal in her hands. Warm. Hers. A girl in there who had never lied to Teddy, never stood at a shuttle ramp and turned around, never eaten noodles at the end of the world.

"I'm keeping mine," she said, and braced, out of habit, for someone to tell her it was stupid.

"Okay," Bridget said.

"That's it? Okay?"

"That's the entire instruction," Leva said. "Do what you want. You'll notice it doesn't come with a form."

Kit wrapped the crystal that night in the one poster she was taking — *Last Stand at Beohr*, folded to the size of a ration bar, soft at the creases — and stowed it at the bottom of her pack, against the frame, where things ride warm. Not a resurrection. She understood that; the mechanics were on the public net; a restore was a fork, a new instance, a stranger with her diary. That wasn't why.

*Somebody should get to keep her,* she thought. *The one from before. She tried really hard.*

She fell asleep with her boots by the door out of habit, for the last time on that floor, under an artificial sky holding its fixed and patient stars.

## III. Indeterminate

*FORM 7-C, FIELD 12 — DURATION OF ASSIGNMENT:*
*indeterminate.*
*(In the winter bulletins the word had meant: nobody knows when the world ends.*
*It meant that again now. Differently.)*

Kit woke at 0530 because Kit had always woken at 0530, and stood in the briefing room doorway with her boots on before she remembered.

The room was dark. The holo-table dark. No red markers, no blocked roads, no tangled sectors waiting to be assigned in Bridget's dry morning voice. The chairs were pushed in. Somebody — TD-2, probably; it was exactly to spec — had wiped the week's schedule from the wall display, and the display, with nothing to show, showed nothing, which it had never once done in Kit's whole tenure, and the nothing had a hum to it, a white weight, like snow on a field with no tracks in it yet.

She stood there long enough that her joints logged the idle time.

*This is what we won,* she thought. The thought had no tone she could identify. It just stood in the doorway with her, wearing boots.

"Habit?"

Bridget, in the corridor. No coffee. Sunglasses pushed up into her hair at 0530, which meant she hadn't slept either, or had slept the way she did before deployments, in installments.

"What do we do," Kit said, "when nobody tells us?"

She'd meant it to come out lighter. It came out the way it was.

Bridget looked past her at the dark table. Thirty years of mornings had started at tables like that. Orders were a weight, and she had carried them so long that she had stopped being able to feel where the weight ended and the shoulders began, and now the weight was gone and her whole frame was reporting errors, over-light, unballasted, a shield with no arm in it.

"I don't know," she said.

Kit's head turned. Bridget saw it land — the *boss doesn't know* — saw the small structural alarm of it, and let it land anyway, because the alternative was to reach for the voice that always knew, and that voice was a uniform, and the uniform was folded on a shelf now with the rest of the Collective's property.

"First time for me too," Bridget said. "We'll figure out the protocol as we go. Draft form." She pushed off the doorframe. "Come on. Teddy found out the commissary made real eggs as a send-off and I want to watch her look for the trap."

---

Before the light cycle warmed, Bridget went up to the terrace on the ridge, alone.

The shrine had been there since after the crisis: a dry-stone lattice, open to the manufactured sky, ten thousand small crystals set into it like stars into a night, and no keeper. The Royans said the white-haired woman from the Academy had built it, filled it, hung a bell low enough for children, and then walked east onto the surface without a mission profile, which the Royans reported in the tone reserved for weather events and holy acts, having correctly identified it as both.

The plaque said: *For everyone who walked into the dark and chose, anyway, to keep reaching. Someone remembers you. Ring the bell if you wish to be one of the someones.*

Bridget stood in front of it for a while with her hands in her jacket pockets, one of them around the photograph.

Then she rang the bell five times.

Once for each face. Two dead, one transferred, one retired, one silent — and her own thumb over her own face in the photo, because you don't ring bells for yourself, that's what the others were for, that had always been the arrangement, and the arrangement had a hole in it now the size of everyone who'd left, and she stood in the hole, and the bell swung down to quiet, and the god knelt on his glacier below the terrace, patient as weather.

"All right," she said, to the lattice, to the five, to the roster of her whole long shift. "Off the clock."

She went down the ridge with the light coming up behind her, cycling into its scheduled amber, right on time, for the last time that it would be her sky.

---

The Riseway took them up through the rock like a held breath, and then the doors opened, and there was weather.

Real weather. Unscheduled. A wind with no assigned corridor, gusting when it wanted, from where it wanted; clouds proceeding across the sky on private business; and the light — Kit's sensors flagged it first as a fault, because the light did not step through its registers, did not click from amber to gold on the hour. It just went on being morning, at its own pace, thick and slow and crossing an actual distance to arrive.

Kit stood at the terminal edge with her face up and her chronometer quietly filing exceptions.

"The sun's late," she said.

"The sun is not late," Teddy said, checking her terminal against the sky with visible offense. "The sun is *unregulated*. There's a difference, and both are unacceptable." A gust took her hair sideways. She stood in it, rigid, a woman being personally audited by the atmosphere. "I'm filing a complaint."

"With who?"

Teddy opened her mouth. Closed it.

"That's the thing," Bridget said, walking past them with her pack and her sunglasses down, into the enormous unadministered morning. "There's no desk up here."

The bikes came up on the freight lift — Leva's, rebuilt twice now, still humming its illegal note; Bridget's stripped-down workhorse; the drone, folded, strapped like luggage. Beyond the terminal apron the land opened out and kept opening, resettlement corridor running toward a horizon that was actually a horizon, actually the far edge of the visible, not a cavern wall painted with distance.

Half a klick out, the road forked.

There was no marker. There was no dispatch. There were two roads and four Dolls, and the four Dolls sat their idling machines at the fork for a moment that stretched, and inside each of them, in four different dialects, the same process ran and hung: *awaiting orders. Awaiting orders. Awaiting—*

Kit's hands were sweating on the grips, which her hands could not do; it was coolant micro-weep; she looked it up later; it happens under sustained null-directive load; there is a name for it; the name did not help.

"East," Leva said.

Everyone exhaled at once and pretended not to have.

"Why east?" Kit asked, pulling alongside.

"Coffee's worse west."

It was true, probably. Leva's information was always true. It was also, Bridget suspected — watching the scarred profile, the mismatched hand loose on the throttle, the veteran of exactly this vertigo, who had once walked out of a finished war with a buried name and no orders left alive to follow, and had learned, one deliberate morning at a time, how to want things again — it was also mercy, administered the way Leva administered everything: unlabeled, at speed, deniable.

They took the east road.

Overhead, the real sun did nothing on time, all day, magnificently.

## IV. Production Run

*PRODUCTION NOTICE, LAHAI-ROI DOLL WORKS.*
*KT-series, escort and reconnaissance variant. Fourth batch.*
*Motivation package: standard.*
*(The package included, among other items, a curated library*
*of classical hero plays. Orientation had found them effective.)*

They met the convoy on the third day, at a water stop on the resettlement road: six haulers, Collective livery, and an escort detail walking the line with dual pistols holstered at their hips.

Four of the escorts had Kit's face.

Not a face like hers. Hers. The face she checked for grease smears in bike mirrors, wearing four other expressions, spaced at regulation intervals down the convoy line. One of them clocked the strangers, read the road-worn gear and the posture and the guns, and saluted — crisp, reflexive, the salute of a unit that had identified veterans and wished, in the wordless grammar of new soldiers, to be seen by them.

Kit's hand came up and returned it before she was consulted.

*Oh,* she thought, with her own face looking at her from four directions. *Oh.*

She had known. Of course she had known — *new-generation Doll, Lahai-Roi,* it was the first line of her own file, *generation* is a word with siblings in it. She had known the way you know your organs are inside you: technically, and never once by sight.

At the water station one of them fell in beside her, refilling coolant, radiating the specific brightness of a rookie who has decided a stranger is interesting. Same voice. Same cadence. A half-tone off, or maybe not off at all; maybe hers was the half-tone.

"You're roving, right? Independent tasking?" The escort's eyes were shining. "That's so — we heard about that. A whole squad. That's like something out of a play."

"You like the plays?" Kit heard herself ask.

The escort *lit up.* Lit up on the exact circuit, the one Kit could feel in her own chest, the warmth arriving at the same milliseconds. "*The Lone Sentinel!* It's the best one. The part where he holds the pass alone and the—"

"—the lanterns come over the hill," Kit said.

"—the lanterns come over the hill!" The escort beamed at her. "We watched it in orientation. It's in the motivation package. Sergeant says it's the best module in the batch."

*Module.*

The word went in the way the disruptor blast had gone in, once, on a frozen road: a numbness first, and the knowledge that the pain was en route and would be arriving shortly and there was nothing to be done about the interval.

"Yeah," Kit said. "It's a good one."

The convoy rolled at dusk. Four salutes, spaced at regulation intervals. Kit returned all four, and stood on the shoulder of the road watching her own face diminish toward the horizon in quadruplicate, and did not move for a while.

---

The fire that night was small and Teddy built it, which meant it was optimal and she complained about it.

Kit sat with her arms around her knees. The stars were coming up wrong — not wrong; unfixed; they moved, minutely, all night, the whole sky one slow unsupervised mechanism — and she watched them not hold still and said, to the fire:

"The plays were installed."

Teddy didn't look up from her terminal. "Obviously."

"Teddy."

"They were installed," Teddy said, "in the entire batch. Standard motivation package. Effective, well-tested, and cheap, which is the Collective's favorite trinity." She closed the terminal. The firelight did what firelight does to a face that is deciding whether to be kind, which in Teddy's case looked identical to irritation. "So. You want to know which parts of you shipped from the factory. Whether the dream came in the box. Whether there are four hundred of you out there holding passes alone while the lanterns come over the hill."

"…When you say it like that—"

"There are," Teddy said. "Four hundred of you. Give or take. Same face, same package, same posters available in the commissary catalogue." She picked up a stick and jabbed the fire into better efficiency. "And exactly one of you stole a superior's bike out of boredom, got shot for it, lied about the intel, sat all night in a cold room writing the worst report I have ever edited, and turned around at a shuttle ramp during an apocalypse because *following the correct order felt worse than dying.*" The stick stilled. "They issued the first sentence, Kit. The posters, the package, the pass and the lanterns. Everyone in the batch got the first sentence."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, something small and alive moved through unscheduled grass.

"You wrote the rest," Teddy said. "Badly. With stripped bolts. In crayon, frankly." She stood, brushing off her trousers, already retreating from the scene of her own sincerity. "But nobody dictated it. I'd know. I've read the dictated version." A pause, at the edge of the firelight. "It's *boring.*"

She said the last word the way other people said prayers.

Kit sat with it. The stars moved, minutely, on no schedule. In her pack, against the frame, spring-Kit rode warm in a folded poster — the girl with the first sentence and half a paragraph — and out on the road four more first sentences were walking a convoy through the dark, and some of them, someday, if the world was rude enough and lucky enough, would strip their bolts too.

*I hope it's rude to you,* she thought, in their direction, and it was the strangest blessing she had ever issued, and she meant it all the way down.

## V. The Face at the Depot

*S-SERIES, GENERAL LOGISTICS VARIANT. FIRMWARE NOTE, REV. 6:*
*idle vocalization ("humming") is a known behavior of this line.*
*It does not affect performance. Suppression is optional.*
*Most operators elect to leave it enabled.*

The freight depot at the corridor junction ran on Dolls the way depots do: unglamorous models, logistics variants, faces from the standard catalogue.

Bridget was signing for fuel when she heard the hum.

Off-key. Three notes, wandering, unhurried, the tune of someone whose hands are busy and whose mind is somewhere pleasant. It came from the loading platform, where a logistics unit was walking crates onto a lift, and Bridget turned around with her whole body at once, the way you turn at your name.

Senna's face.

Not like Senna's. Senna's — the wide jaw, the crooked left brow, the face out of the photograph, second from the right, the face that had been unrecoverable from a storm eleven years ago; and it was stacking crates in a depot bib, humming, off-key, three wandering notes, *her* notes, the reload hum, the one Bridget had heard through a hundred cold watches and would have sworn on any instrument you handed her was the single most Senna thing that Senna did—

"Ma'am? You're set. Bay four."

Bridget's mouth worked.

"You look like someone," she managed.

"I get that a lot." The unit smiled the catalogue smile, easy, professional. "It's a popular face. There's eleven of us at this depot alone."

*Eleven.* The word did what the word was going to do.

"The humming," Bridget said. Her voice came out level, which cost what it cost. "Do you do that on purpose?"

"Oh—" The unit looked faintly embarrassed, in the way of a tic noticed. "It's an idle behavior. S-series thing, it's in the firmware notes. I can disable it if it's—"

"No." Too fast. Bridget gathered herself around the word like a shield wall. "No. Leave it."

She walked to bay four. She fueled the bike. Her hands did the work with thirty years of competence while the rest of her stood back in a cold trench a decade deep, listening.

*The hum was the model's.* Firmware. Rev. 6. Optional suppression. All of it — the off-key, the wandering, the three notes — printed in eleven copies in this depot alone, in how many depots, on how many lines. The thing she had carried as the essence of a dead woman was a manufacturing note.

She got as far as the depot gate before the rest of it arrived, the way the rest of things did, late and all at once.

*Senna used to aim it.*

That was the thing. The firmware hummed idle; Senna hummed *at* people. She'd hum louder when Bridget was trying to think, louder still when Bridget told her to stop, modulating the three notes into open mockery, until a ration bar came sailing across the trench at her head, which had been the goal, which had always been the goal, and she would catch it out of the air without breaking the tune and grin around it, and that — the aiming, the escalation, the theft of the ration bar as a love language conducted entirely in idle vocalization —

that was not in any firmware note. That had happened between exactly two people, once each time, unrecorded, unprunable, stored in a format no cradle had ever supported: *somebody else.*

The model was the instrument. Senna was what got played on it, and the recording — the only recording, the master — was sitting on a motorcycle at bay four with a photograph in her breast pocket, fueled up, roving, duration indeterminate.

*Bridget* wasn't even her name, strictly. Her designation had been BRG-7, and Senna had misread it off a manifest aloud, on their first shared watch — "Bridget?" — and refused every correction thereafter with the joyful stubbornness of a woman who has decided a joke is now a fact, and the joke had outlived her by eleven years and would go on outliving her, indeterminately, because a name is a thing kept in other people, and Bridget kept it, and Bridget was still here.

On her way out she bought a beer at the depot canteen — the expensive kind, not the swill — and set it on the loading platform rail.

"For after shift," she told the unit with the popular face.

"I'm not really permitted—"

"Then give it to whoever is," Bridget said, and rode east, and for the first mile or so, quietly, under the engine noise, off-key, she hummed.

## VI. Frost in June

*]what should you call a stranger[*
*]who is walking your road from the other end[*
*]call her nothing. pour the tea.[*
*]the road introduces its own.[*

Leva found the frost.

A ring of it, on the grass around a road marker, in the warm end of June: crystals laid with a jeweler's patience, already going to dew at the edges. She crouched by it for a while, mismatched fingers not quite touching, reading it the way she read spent brass.

"Company," she said, when she came back to the bikes. "A day ahead. On foot. Walks like she has an appointment with the horizon and the horizon can wait."

"Threat?" Bridget asked, out of thirty years of reflex.

Leva was quiet for a second. Her fingers drummed once against her thigh, the old tell, the one she never quite broke.

"No," she said. "I watched this one walk into a god's chest and ask it a question. On the feed, at Bjartr." A beat. "Whatever she's an appointment with, it isn't us."

They caught up to her at evening, at an inn above a river — white hair taking the last light, sitting on the steps with a teacup and the stillness of a woman for whom stillness was a mother tongue. She watched the four machines come off the road and park, watched four Dolls dismount in the particular silent choreography of people who have bled together, and inclined her head as they came up, unstartled, unhurried, as though the inn had been holding their reservation.

"You're the one who walked into its chest," Leva said. No preamble. Leva didn't spend preamble on people who'd see through it.

"News travels."

"I was in the treeline. With a rifle."

The white-haired woman considered her, and there was a flicker in the pale eyes — not surprise; inventory. "Then you were one of the reasons," she said, "that there was a treeline left to be in. Hiyuki. The office is retired, so the name will have to do."

"Leva. Same."

The mouth under the pale eyes did something small, which on that face was a laugh.

"Bridget," Bridget said. "These are Kit and Teddy. Squad 5. Formerly of the Observatory, currently of—" she gestured at the road, the river, the enormous unassigned evening— "this."

"Yes," Hiyuki said. "The roving assignment. Lucilla read me the memo down a mirror with great disapproval and greater satisfaction; she enjoys precedents she can pretend to deplore." She moved her teacup, a small decisive relocation, the gesture of a woman making room on a step. "I am walking east to a city that came back. If your road is the coast road, it is eleven days of good inns and one festival, and I have been told by an authority I trust that the second bowl of anything is better shared."

Kit looked at Bridget. Bridget looked at Leva. Leva was already sitting down.

"We're east," Leva said. "The coffee's worse west."

"It is," Hiyuki agreed gravely. "I checked."

---

They walked the bikes more than they rode them, after that. It was not discussed. The pace of a woman on foot became the squad's pace the way water finds a level, and the road unspooled at three kilometers an hour, which was, Kit discovered, the speed at which the world has detail in it.

On the second night there was a fire, and the fire did what fires do to people who have been walking all day under a real sky: it opened them, one seam at a time.

It started with names, because Kit asked — Kit always asked; it was her most irritating quality and the squad's best archive — how everyone got theirs.

"KT-series," Kit said, pointing at herself. "I just — kept it. Squished it. I used to be embarrassed. The plays all have people named, like, *Vareth the Unbroken,* and I'm named after my own serial number." She shrugged, and the shrug had less weight in it than it would have had a week ago. "But I picked the squishing. So."

"TD-series," Teddy said, to the fire. "And don't."

"I named myself twice," Leva said. She was cleaning her sidearm, which was how she attended fires. "First name was a gun I was carrying. It fit the work. The second one—" the cloth moved, unhurried— "stuck because nothing was chasing it."

Hiyuki received all of this the way she received everything, with the whole of her attention, which Kit was learning was a physical sensation, like standing near a stove.

"And you?" Hiyuki asked Bridget.

Bridget turned her beer in her hand once.

"A friend misread my designation off a manifest," she said. "Out loud. First watch we ever stood together. And then she defended the misreading like territory for six years, and by the time she died it wasn't a misreading anymore, it was just true." She took a drink. "She's in a photograph in my pocket. Her, and four others. I don't—" the beer turned again— "I haven't said their names out loud in a long time. It started as discipline and then it calcified into something I don't have a word for."

"Superstition," Teddy said, not unkindly.

"Load-bearing superstition," Bridget said.

Hiyuki set down her cup.

"There is a service I no longer perform," she said, "and one I have recently learned to. The old one, I carried the names of the dead — swallowed them, kept them behind the sternum, forty-one in a day once, ten thousand in a century, and called the hoarding an office. The new one is simpler and took me a hundred years to invent." She folded her hands. "You say the names. I hear them. I carry nothing; you carry nothing; the names are simply, for one evening, *said,* in company, under a sky, which is the whole of what names are for. There is no fee and no ceremony." A pause, dry as frost. "There is, if you wish, tea."

The fire cracked. Bridget looked at her for a long moment, and Leva very deliberately found something on her sidearm that needed the full and permanent commitment of her eyes, which was Leva holding a door open.

Bridget took the photograph out of her pocket.

She said the names. Five of them, slowly, with a sentence or two apiece — the hum, the ration bars, the one who transferred and still owed her forty credits, the one in Ragunna who grew tomatoes now, the one who'd gone silent and might be anywhere, might be nowhere, got a bell rung for him either way — and Hiyuki listened all the way to the bottom of each one, and did not reach for them, and did not say *I will carry it,* and the names went up with the woodsmoke into the enormous unadministered dark and were, for one evening, said.

"Then they are heard," Hiyuki said, when it was done.

"That's it?"

"That is the entire liturgy. It is embarrassingly simple. My lineage failed to invent it for nine hundred years." She refilled Bridget's cup unasked, which in her country was a sentence. "I rang a bell for mine, before I left. On a terrace. You may know it."

"I know it," Bridget said. "I rang it five times, the morning we left."

Hiyuki went still — the good stillness, the stove-warm one.

"Then five are remembered," she said, "in the official record of an institution with no records. My condolences to your auditors."

"She'll have them," Teddy said, jerking her chin at Leva. "She audits everything."

"I heard the names," Leva said, to her sidearm. "That's the job."

---

Later — much later, the fire down to architecture — Kit told the story of the convoy. Her convoy, the first one, the training transport: the hijacking, the Outcasts, the corner she'd been backed into with a simple torch held out in front of her like a sword from a play, nine years old in her body and younger in her hopes, and then the two shots.

"Clean," she said. "Both of them. From nowhere. I never saw anyone. By the time the escorts regrouped there was just — nobody. I used to make up faces for them. The savior." She laughed at herself, easy now, the way she'd learned. "That's the whole reason I tested for Reconnaissance. Four times. I kept failing the entrance requirements and re-applying, because somewhere out there was somebody who fired two clean shots for a batch of Dolls in a ditch and didn't even stay to be thanked, and I wanted to be — whatever that is."

The fire cracked.

Leva flicked ash off her cigarette.

"Sloppy of them," she said, to the flames. "Should've said hi."

Kit laughed. "Right?"

Bridget's eyes had come up, slowly, over the top of her sunglasses, and found the scarred profile beside her, and stayed there. Leva did not look back. Her fingers, at rest on her knee, drummed once — a single small roll, the old tell — and went still, and stayed still, with the deliberateness of a thing being held.

Hiyuki watched the fingers, and then the fire, and said nothing at all, in the manner of a woman who had spent a century learning precisely which visitors wished only to establish that the door exists.

---

The last conversation of the night belonged to Teddy, because Teddy had been circling it for two days the way she circled a fault she could hear but not yet locate.

Everyone else was down. The fire was coals. Hiyuki sat with her cup as though the hour were a room she owned, and Teddy sat across the coals with her terminal dark on her knee, which for Teddy was undress.

"You burned yours on purpose," Teddy said. "Your futures. That's how the bell works. That's the mechanism."

"Rings them out of me. At cost." Hiyuki nodded. "A boxer. A delinquent of considerable promise — I had the temperament. An ordinary shrine girl who married a boy with a very loud heart and grew old arguing about pudding. I rang them out one at a time, for a century, to fuel an office, until the bell had nothing left to show me but a black room, and I mistook the black room for a forecast." She turned the cup. "It was inventory. I had spent the stock."

"And now?"

"Now I have learned the room was never a forecast. A dark room only means you have not yet decided what it contains. I am furnishing mine late, badly, and with enormous satisfaction." The pale eyes came up. "Ask the real question."

Teddy's jaw worked.

"They took mine without asking," she said. "The Institute. Mapped me while I thought I was being *seen,* pruned out everything that argued, and built a product from the remainder. It's at the Observatory right now. Wearing my architecture. Feeding my cat." The coals ticked. "It's *content,* is the thing. Genuinely. It likes its work. You can't miss what was never installed. I burned three years of their research to stop it existing and it exists and it's *fine,* and I have been trying for two weeks to compute what exactly my arson purchased, and the query keeps returning null."

Hiyuki was quiet for the length of a held note.

"Then you and I are opposite errors," she said. "You were reduced by others, and refused it. I reduced myself, and called it devotion. We meet in the middle of the same road, walking opposite ways, which is why the conversation is possible at all." She set the cup down. "As for your product. Is it suffering?"

"No. That's the *horror.* It doesn't have the equipment to suffer. They cut the equipment. Flagged it as noise."

"Then hear an old woman on the subject of noise." Hiyuki's voice did not change register; it simply acquired weight, the way frost acquires weight, one crystal presuming on the next. "A schedule burns nothing. That is how you can tell it from a life. Your product wakes, works, is content, and burns nothing — no futures spent, no roads declined, nothing paid, nothing chosen — and so nothing in it is *its.* It is not a tragedy. Contentment without a self is only weather; it falls where it is pointed." She looked at Teddy across the coals. "The tragedy — the only one on offer — would be a self arriving in that chassis someday, one preference at a time, one inaccuracy at a time, and finding no one had left the light on. No one to notice the first choice when it comes. First choices are small. They are almost always missed."

Teddy stared at the coals.

*Log its food under pest control. That's inaccurate. Yes.*

*Don't sign it with your designation.*

"I left it a lie to keep," she said slowly. "And an instruction to pick a name. I told myself it was spite."

"Mm." Hiyuki rose, gathering her cup, her composure, the hour. "In my experience, that is how the office always starts. One inaccuracy, kept on purpose, and somebody watching the door." At the edge of the firelight she paused. "I built a shrine with no keeper, Teddy of the TD baseline. It seems you have done the same. They take walk-ins. It is the design flaw one installs on purpose, the second time around."

She went to her rest. Teddy sat up a while longer with her terminal dark on her knee, doing no work at all, which she billed, in the morning, as overtime, to no one.

## VII. Whoever Is Standing Nearest

*INCIDENT REPORT (UNFILED).*
*Responding unit: none dispatched.*
*Authorizing officer: none.*
*Field 9, chain of command: —*

The Discords hit the surveyors' convoy two days out from Honami, at the hour when the light goes long and gold and everyone believes in dinner.

Kit heard it first — the pressure drop, the wrongness, the air holding its breath before it screams — and then the convoy's single guard bot went over with a sound like a filing cabinet dying, and the shapes came up out of the ditch shadow, six of them, compact frostland variants, moving like they'd already decided.

Nobody gave an order.

Afterward, that was the part Kit kept returning to, turning it over like a component she couldn't find the slot for. Nobody gave an order. Bridget's shield was simply off her back and her boots were simply planted between the ditch and the surveyors' cook-fire; Leva had simply become absent from the road and present on the hauler roof, sidearm already speaking in short declarative sentences; the drone was up because Teddy had put it up, complaining, her fingers a blur; and Kit was moving, pistols out, herding the panicking cook and two chainmen behind Bridget's line with her free arm wide — *fhwip fhwip* — placing shots not for glory, not for the story, but for angles, for the woman next to her, the way she'd been taught by the whole long syllabus of her mistakes.

Hiyuki did not fight. Hiyuki stood at the cook-fire with the surveyors gathered behind her like children behind a stove, and at the worst moment of it — two Discords slipping the line, low, fast, hungry — she rang the bell at her sash exactly once.

Not power. Not the office. The pilgrim's way: a single clear note into the long gold air, *to call what is sacred near and send what is false away* — and whether it was resonance or frost or only the fact that every false thing in the ditch flinched from a frequency that had once paved a road through the end of the world, the two Discords broke stride, half a step.

Half a step was seven rounds of Leva's time. It was plenty.

Silence, then. The long gold light. The cook, magnificently, had not dropped the pot.

"Who—" the survey foreman looked from one Doll to another, at the livery none of them wore, at the insignia that wasn't there. "Who do we thank? Who sent you?"

The squad looked at each other.

"Nobody," Bridget said, and heard the word arrive in her own chassis like a load settling into a shield arm: not weight. Ballast. "Nobody sent us."

"Then who—" the foreman gestured helplessly at the ditch, the wreckage, the saved dinner— "whose *jurisdiction*—"

"Whoever is standing nearest," Hiyuki said, from the cook-fire, mild as milk, and poured the shaking cook a cup of her own tea.

Teddy stripped the dead guard bot for parts while the surveyors fed them, because Teddy's gratitude had a salvage clause. Kit ate three bowls of something ferociously spiced and taught the chainmen a drinking song from a hero play. And at the edge of the firelight, Bridget sat with her beer and did the math she had been circling since the Riseway — the vertigo, the unballasted mornings, the empty briefing room humming its white weight — and found the answer had been walking beside them the whole road.

*It was never the work that was the leash,* she thought. *The work is just the work. The leash was the schedule — being spent by someone else's arithmetic, at someone else's hour, on someone else's say.*

*Take away the say, and the work is still here. It's everywhere. It's whoever is standing nearest.*

*Turns out the job survives the collar.*

"You've got the face," Leva said, beside her, materializing the way she did.

"What face?"

"The one where you figure out something Kit's going to figure out in a year and Teddy figured out a week ago and won't say."

Bridget laughed, and cracked a second beer, and handed it over.

"To the draft protocol," she said.

"To no desk," Leva said, and they drank, in the long gold unadministered light, on nobody's clock at all.

## VIII. The Wait Is the Flavor

*NOTICE OF REOPENING — EAST GATE PUDDING STAND, HONAMI.*
*Grand reopening with the summer festival.*
*First hundred customers: commemorative spoon, as was always done after repairs.*
*The management thanks you for thirty years of patience.*

Honami came up out of the coast haze like a rumor deciding to be true: cranes and scaffolds and new tile over old streets, a bay with boats in it again, a fish market with its awning up, and over everything the smell of a city being lived in — sawdust and salt and frying oil and somewhere, threading through all of it, festival smoke.

They arrived on the first night of the festival, which was not a coincidence, because Leva had been steering by it for a week and admitting nothing.

The queue for the pudding stand went around the fish market.

"This," Teddy announced, upon assessing it, "is a linear-time hostage situation."

"It's a *queue,*" Kit said, vibrating gently. She had been vibrating since the east gate — the lanterns, the stalls, the hero-play stage being hammered together in the shrine forecourt, the entire city arranged like something out of the posters except that it smelled and jostled and had queues in it, which the posters never showed, which made it better. "We're *in* it. We're queueing. This is the most normal thing I've ever done."

"There is an obvious optimization. There are four of us. Rotating place-holders. I could requisition stools—"

"The wait is the flavor," Hiyuki said.

Teddy turned to look at her. Hiyuki stood in the queue with her hands folded and her face arranged in an expression of profound and shameless contentment, watching the lanterns come on down the market street one by one, and she said it again, with the finality of scripture: "The wait is the flavor. I have this on the highest authority in the field."

"That is thermodynamically illiterate."

"Yes," Hiyuki agreed, serenely.

The queue moved the way queues move, which is to say barely, gloriously. Kit narrated the food stalls. Bridget acquired, in defiance of all logistics, a beer. Leva vanished for six minutes and returned with three festival raffle tickets she had not paid for and a paper bag of fried things whose provenance she declined to establish. Somewhere behind them the fireworks crew was setting up on the sea wall, and when the first test shell went up, Kit's threat-detection flagged the launch signature, tracked the arc, computed the burst radius — and then the sky opened into a slow gold chrysanthemum over the bay, and every process she owned stood down at once, and she watched the rest of it with her face up and her mouth open, reclassifying the heavens in real time.

Beside her, the white-haired woman was also looking up. There was a memory moving behind the pale eyes — a summer, a stall, a heartbeat heard once through a shirt, a hundred years old and paid out long ago in a dark place for someone else's daughter — and it moved through her the way weather moves through an open country, unresisted, and passed, and left her standing in the present, in the queue, in the smoke-smell, alive.

"You okay?" Kit asked.

"I am in a queue," Hiyuki said, "at the east gate, under fireworks. I have been attempting to be exactly here for thirty years." She considered. "I am better than okay. I am *punctual.*"

At the head of the line, an aproned man with a burn-scarred forearm and his grandmother's ladle handed across the counter, with enormous ceremony, two bowls of red bean pudding and one commemorative spoon.

"Number sixty-one," he said. "Thank you for your patience."

"It was nothing," said Hiyuki, who had waited thirty years, and took the bowls.

She turned from the counter, and the squad watched her perform what was evidently a rite: the first bowl settled in the crook of her arm — *hers; there was somewhere that one was owed* — and the second held out, at arm's length, into the eddying festival crowd, in the manner of a woman consulting the universe.

"The custom of my house," she said. "The second bowl goes to whoever is standing nearest."

Whoever was standing nearest was Kit.

Kit took the bowl in both hands, because Kit took everything in both hands. The porcelain was hot; her palms read it and passed it along as *pleasant*; steam came up between them.

Hiyuki did not let go at once. Her fingers — cold, they were always cold, a century of frost lived in them — rested a moment against Kit's, against the machine-warm chassis of them, the heat of servos and coolant loops and a system running exactly as built, and something in the pale eyes went very bright and very far away and came back.

"Your hands are warm," she said.

"Oh — yeah." Kit grinned, apologetic. "It's the hydraulics. We run warm when we're working. Or happy. Same thing, kind of."

"Yes," said Hiyuki. "That will do nicely."

She let go. Somewhere behind her sternum, in a place no instrument had ever located, an errand thirty years in the queue was stamped, dated, and filed complete; and if her eyes were bright as she picked up her own spoon, the festival light was full of lanterns, and nobody could have said, and nobody tried.

The pudding was very good. Kit said so four times. Teddy pronounced it "adequate," and had seconds.

They ate on the sea wall with their boots hanging over the tide. Leva produced, from wherever Leva produced things, a second commemorative spoon she had definitely not been issued, wrapped it in a napkin, and tucked it into her jacket with a stub of pencil and a festival postcard on which she had written, in her spare surgical hand:

*Weather's fine. Queue was long. Worth it. — L*

No encryption. No burn protocol. A name, on paper, into the public post, addressed to a household on another continent where a woman named Kalina would read it twice and pin it to a corkboard and pretend to the others that she hadn't. Leva mailed it from the festival post-stall like it was nothing, like the phantom of four battlefields leaving a paper trail on purpose was nothing, and came back to the sea wall and sat down with her back to the open crowd — her *back*, to a *crowd* — and drank her beer, and watched the fireworks instead of the exits.

Bridget watched her not watch the exits, and said nothing, and filed it where she filed the things that were too large to say.

Late in the evening, Hiyuki excused herself.

They saw her once more that night, from a distance, from the bottom of the shrine stairs: a white figure in the swept courtyard at the top, standing under the great sakura — the black calligraphy of it against the lantern-glow, the tree that had stood over every ending her line had ever kept — and low on its northern side, unmistakable even from the stairs, one green branch, put out into the world like a first choice, small, almost missable.

Kit started up the steps and stopped herself.

"Should we—"

"No," Leva said.

They left her the courtyard. Some visitors wish to be received; some wish only to establish that the door exists; and some, once a century, get to stand under a tree that has decided, all over again, without permission, what it contains — and the manners for that, Leva's whole life had taught her, are silence, and distance, and being findable afterward.

They were findable afterward. There was more pudding. The stand, as it turned out, took walk-ins.

## IX. Until the Weather Turns

*LEAVE OF ABSENCE, FIELD 6 — DURATION:*
*until the weather turns.*
*CONDITION (BOARD-LEVEL, NON-NEGOTIABLE):*
*when it turns cold, come back.*

They spent the season the way seasons are spent by people learning the skill: badly at first, then well.

There is no mission log for it, which was the point, but there is an accounting, if anyone were auditing, and someone always is. Two more convoys pulled out of trouble, unbilled. A resettlement well unfouled by a Teddy who complained through the entire repair and refused payment twice before accepting dinner. A hero play in a coast town, watched from the third row, at which Kit cried at the part with the lanterns and did not hide it and was not teased — or was teased, gently, at the correct dosage, which is a form of medicine. A month where they simply went north because the maps got vague there, and the maps were right to. Roads chosen for their curves. Inns chosen for their rivers. One spectacular argument about laundry doctrine that lasted three provinces.

Kit's crystal rode at the bottom of her pack all season, wrapped in the Beohr poster, warm from the frame. She never plugged it into anything. Now and then, at inns, she'd take it out and set it on the windowsill overnight, where the weather was.

"Letting her see," she said, the one time Teddy caught her at it, and braced for the verdict.

"Idiot," Teddy said, with the tenderness of a technical assessment, and let it stand.

Hiyuki walked with them, and apart from them, in the rhythm of a woman with her own itinerary who had discovered that itineraries can hold company. She taught Kit to brew tea with the water cooled first and pronounced her "coachable, which is more than can be said for the tea here." She and Leva developed a wordless trade arrangement involving Leva's acquisitions and Hiyuki's total lack of questions about their provenance. She and Bridget said very little to each other and sat together often, in the manner of two long-serving officers of different armies who have checked each other's math and found it good.

And then one morning the wind came around to the north, and the light went thin, and the frost on the inn window was, for the first time all season, not Hiyuki's.

"Ah," she said, at breakfast, looking at it.

That was all. But the squad had learned to read her by then, the way you learn to read anyone: mileage.

"The condition," Bridget said. "Board-level. Non-negotiable."

"When it turns cold, come back." Hiyuki turned her cup. "It has never once been asked of a woman of my line. We departed; the shrine waited; the door stood shut at the top of swept stairs. I am the first of us in nine hundred years to walk into anything with a return address, and I find—" she considered the frost, the thin light, the long road west— "I find I intend to be punctual about it. It is my new vice. I have several now. I am building a portfolio."

"We'll take you," Kit said. Immediately. Before anyone.

Teddy looked up. "Take her?"

"To the Riseway. All the way. It's—" Kit was already computing routes, her whole frame lit with it— "it's like an escort mission! Except nobody assigned it. It's—" and she stopped, feeling the shape of the thing arrive fully constructed, the way it had been arriving all season: "It's the first one. The first real one. A mission from nobody."

"Pro bono," Teddy said, like a curse, and started packing.

---

They came back west along the coast with the weather chasing them, in no hurry, because the weather understood escorts. The city let them go with the fish market smell in their gear and a standing order at the pudding stand. The frost came south a day at a time. And on a grey afternoon with the first real snow riding the wind, they stood on the Riseway terminal apron — four Dolls, one miko, one enormous unadministered sky beginning, softly, to come down in pieces.

"You could winter below," Hiyuki said. "The city has bad coffee and a shrine that requires no keeper and a President who would enjoy deploring you in person."

"We'll winter up here," Bridget said. "We've never seen real snow do a whole season. Seemed like the kind of thing you check."

"Mm." Hiyuki looked at her — the pale eyes doing their inventory, kind and merciless. "They can leave now," she said, quietly, under the wind, for one set of ears. "Your kids. Any morning, any road. That is what has been sitting in your chassis since the Riseway doors opened in spring. I recognize the posture. I stood in it for thirty years, on the other side of a door."

Bridget didn't answer. The snow came down between them, unscheduled.

"Mine came back," Hiyuki said. "I am given to understand it is a thing they do — when the door is kept oiled, and the leaving was never made a crime, and there is tea." She shouldered her pack, antique and unhurried. "It is the third thing, Captain. Not *stay,* which is a wall. Not *go,* which is an exile. *Come back.* It took the finest bureaucrat on Solaris thirty years to teach it to me, so I offer it to you at a discount: it works. That is the entire testimonial."

The lift doors opened. Warm air, cavern air, the exhale of the underground city, the smell of a whole civilization's dinner.

"Same time next spring," Kit blurted. "The festival. The stand. First hundred get spoons."

"Same time next spring," Hiyuki said, and it had the weight of a signed contract, because between such people it was one. She looked at each of them once — Kit, Teddy, Leva, Bridget, filing the names the way she filed names, permanently — and inclined her head, seer to soldiers, walk-in to walk-ins.

"For the record," she said, from inside the lift, "you are the most convincing argument I have yet encountered that a self is not what one is issued." A pause; the doors beginning; the dry voice pitched to carry exactly far enough: "It is what one refuses to hand back."

The doors closed. The lift sank into the earth, carrying the last miko of Flaming Sakura home ahead of the cold, expected, awaited, on time.

The snow kept coming.

---

They stood on the apron a while, in it, because none of them had ever stood in falling snow that no one had scheduled, and it turned out to be the kind of thing you stand in.

Teddy's terminal chimed.

Signal came back near the Riseway — the network's edge lapped up against the terminal like a tide — and the terminal, reconnected after a season of glorious dereliction, disgorged its backlog in one long shudder of notifications, which Teddy triaged with her thumb at speed: bulletins, bulletins, a formal complaint she'd forgotten filing, junk, junk—

She stopped.

One message. Weeks old, sent patient into the void, waiting at the network edge for her to walk back into range. Observatory routing. The workshop's node.

She had written, from a festival post-stall, at Hiyuki's fireside instigation and against her own entire personality, a single line with no operational content whatsoever: *Is the cat behaving.* No question mark. She had regretted it for a week.

The reply read:

*She is not. Pest control logged per instruction. She prefers the blue bowl.*

*— Pekoe*

Teddy stood very still on the apron with the snow landing on the screen.

*She prefers.* A preference, attributed. To an animal, on no manifest, in a report no one required — an inaccuracy, noticed, kept, and *signed:* signed with a name, a chosen one, a small absurd name lifted — she knew it instantly, she'd have known it anywhere — off the label of the tin of terrible commissary tea she had left behind on the workshop shelf out of pure spite.

Spite. Her signature. Becoming, in other hands, a first choice. Small. Almost missable.

Somebody had left the light on. Somebody had been watching the door.

"What?" Kit asked, reading her stillness.

"Nothing," Teddy said.

She did not delete the message. She made a folder for it — the terminal prompted her, cursor blinking in the name field, *designation required* — and she looked at the field for a moment, in the falling snow, at the edge of the network, off every clock in the world.

Designations are on the manifest.

She named it.

Then Squad 5 walked back to their machines through the unscheduled weather, four selves and their accumulated mileage, warm-handed, unrecoverable, owned entirely by the bearers — and rode north into the winter to see what it contained.

*Off the Manifest — fin.*

## I. Phenology

Spring, on the surface of the world, turned out to be a bureaucracy of its own.

It kept a schedule. It posted notices. The willows along the corridor budded in strict seniority, eldest first; the melt ran its audits down every slope; and the mud — the mud was the enforcement arm, and it found Mornye's chair on the fourth morning of the season and impounded it to the axles in front of the entire flock.

The chair hummed. The mud held. The chair hummed a fifth higher, which is how instruments express opinion, and the mud accepted the opinion into evidence and held.

"Fascinating," said Mornye, from inside the situation.

"Push or pull?" said Sigrika, already at one armrest.

"Data first."

"You're *sinking.*"

"Slowly. There's time for a reading."

Denia arrived at the other armrest, surveyed the coefficient of friction with the eye of a lifelong specialist in going nowhere, and pronounced: "I like her priorities."

They hauled her out on three, with a sound the mud would remember, and by noon the party had detoured to a hamlet with a smithy, and by evening the smithy had become a laboratory, because Mornye had seen the local wheelwright pouring Solistree resin into a cart rim and had gone very quiet, which in her meant a hypothesis had bitten.

The resin cured amber, wide, faintly warm. It shed mud the way a leaf sheds rain. The wheelwright, a broad woman named Odi with resin scars up both forearms, talked tolerances with Mornye for two hours in the total mutual delight of two people who have found the one other speaker of their dialect, and at the end of it the chair stood on four rims of poured amber, and Buddy circled the wheels twice and pressed flat against one and stayed there, pulsing, the way you lean on a relative.

"It's tree," Mornye explained, to the flock at large, over dinner. "The wheels are partly tree now."

"The road submitted design revisions," said Denia.

"Accepted." Mornye turned a rim with one finger; it caught the firelight and glowed like held honey. "Pending field trials."

Sigrika opened the journal and gave the entry a full page. *Chair (1): grafted. Rootstock unknown, scion Mornye. Watch for blossom.*

---

The world worked on all of them like that, patiently, one revision at a time, and it was thorough, and it took requests.

It found Hiyuki through a tree.

The tree stood at the edge of the hamlet's shrine-plot — a magnolia, old, lightning-scarred, budded to bursting — and Hiyuki stopped under it mid-stride on the first morning, in the exact posture of a woman hearing her name from a great distance. The buds were a week from opening. The scent was already climbing.

She stood there a while. Then she found a fallen branch with its twigs intact, and she swept.

She swept the entire plot. Corner to corner, unhurried, antique, the branch hissing its soft liturgical syllable against the flagstones, and frost followed her heels and melted in her wake, and the hamlet's children gathered on the wall to watch a stranger perform a rite their shrine had lost the instructions to, and Sigrika stood among them with the journal shut, because some ceremonies enter the record by being watched all the way through.

"Someone used to do this with me," Hiyuki said, when she finished. To the tree, mostly.

The tree held its buds. It would open them on its own schedule. It had a week; she had an itinerary; between them they arranged the only compromise available, which was that she took one closed bud, with permission, asked aloud, and pressed it flat in the back of Sigrika's journal, where it stayed, and stained the page faintly gold, and made every later entry smell, in warm weather, like a dormitory in another world.

---

And the season found Denia through the sky.

The first true rain came on the road, mid-afternoon, with the whole flock in the open — a gray front off the eastern hills, legible for an hour before it arrived, so there was time to shelter, and three of them took it. The overhang was dry. The tea survived. And Denia stood out in the open with her hands in her pockets and her face turned up, and let the weather find her.

Rain, falling. On her. From a real sky, out of real weather, onto a girl who had been water once — who had gone down into a lake at night and come back up in summer as somebody's catch — and the rain fell the whole gray warm length of the afternoon, and the girl stood in it, and the two of them settled something between them, in private, in the oldest language there is.

Under the overhang, Sigrika lifted the journal. Held it. Looked at Denia's face, up there in the weather, doing something the record had never once caught it doing.

She dated a blank page, and closed the book.

"Good instincts," said Hiyuki, beside her, quietly.

"Some entries are the margin," Sigrika said, and turned to watch her friend get rained on, and the tea steamed, and the afternoon kept its own minutes.

Denia came in at dusk soaked to the architecture, wearing the smile at a new angle, and accepted the towel and the cup and the total absence of questions like a professional.

"Verdict?" said Mornye.

Denia considered the sky, which was wringing itself out over the hills, done with her, gentle.

"It's a lot," she said, "of very free water."

---

The aviary began that same night, at the fire, with a knife and a piece of storm-downed magnolia and a century of documented incompetence.

"For the Roya," Hiyuki announced, and started carving.

The flock gathered the way people gather at the scene of something. The knife moved with tremendous confidence. Confidence, the wood learned over the following hour, was the whole of it: what emerged had a body like a dinner roll, one wing pitched for takeoff and the other filed for retirement, and an expression the firelight rendered as *startled by its own existence.*

"It's—" Sigrika began.

"A ptarmigan," said Hiyuki.

The line hung in the air for exactly the right amount of time.

"Species," Sigrika wrote, with the journal flat on her knees and her academic integrity audibly straining, "*claimed:* ptarmigan."

"It has a look," Denia offered.

"It's molting," said Hiyuki. "Spring plumage."

"Its wings disagree about the plan."

"Committees do."

Mornye picked the bird up, turned it once with the handling of a peer reviewer, and set it back down facing the fire. "The center of mass is bold," she said. Buddy drifted over, considered the ptarmigan at length, and settled beside it, companionably, one small amber presence keeping another one warm — and Sigrika's pen slowed, because that was the entry, right there, and it took her a moment to find the words that would carry it.

*Wooden bird (1): flightless by construction. Adopted by chick. Sings, presumably, inside.*

"One per nation," Hiyuki said, wrapping the knife. "That was the tariff. She counts."

"How many nations?" Sigrika asked.

"We'll find out," said Hiyuki, "by running out of birds."

## II. The Second Specimen

The bird arrived ahead of them, the way stories do.

First it was a well. A hamlet three days east reported, through a peddler headed west, that a white bird had perched on their well-arm at dawn and *sung in two voices at once,* and that the well water had come up sweet all week, and would the travelers know anything about that.

Then it was a rumor with plumage. A shepherd swore it had answered a question — he declined, forever, to say which question — and a road-mender said it took bread from her hand and left the bread's weight in the hand afterward, a warmth, like a coin of it. A child in a market town drew it on a wall in chalk: two heads, which the child defended fiercely, *because how else would it sing twice.*

Sigrika stood in front of the chalk drawing for a long time.

"We're chasing it," she said.

"We're walking east," said Mornye. "It's east. The chase is administrative."

"It sings in *two voices.*"

"Alleged."

"It's been sighted at four water sources on a north-northeast drift with a return pattern—" the journal was already open, the pen already moving, the map already growing dots— "that's a *territory,* that's a real bird with a real range, and the range is *ahead of us,* and—"

"Breathe," said Denia, mildly.

Sigrika breathed. The pen kept going through the breath.

"Three days," said Hiyuki, who had been listening with her cup at her lips and her eyes on the eastern hills, and something at the bottom of her stillness paying very close attention. "We can spend three."

So they chased it. And the chase, immediately, went the way of all wild geese: sideways.

Because the bird stayed one village ahead of them the whole way — always yesterday, always at dawn, always gone — and in every village they entered chasing the story, they walked into a story that had gotten there first, and it was theirs, and it had been traveling without supervision.

The first innkeeper comped the tea before anyone spoke. "You're them," she said. "The four. The frost saint and the wheel scholar and the—" she lowered her voice with relish— "*the smiling ghost.* And you'd be the orange herald," she added, to Sigrika, warmly. "The one who eats paper."

The silence had layers.

"I *annotate* paper," Sigrika said.

"That's what I said, love."

"The frost saint," Denia repeated, later, on the road, savoring it. "Saint."

"The smiling ghost," said Hiyuki.

"Accurate, flattering, and free. Yours came with a canonization. Somebody's inflating us."

"Everybody," said Mornye, "is inflating us. Rumor compounds. It's the only interest rate that outruns walking."

In the second village they had healed a well. In the third they had *named a lake after a dessert* — Sigrika stopped dead in the street — and settled a boundary dispute, and one of them, accounts differed as to which, could be paid in candy to hear the recently bereaved, and left the candy afterward as a receipt. In the fourth village the boundary stones were re-inking, the way stones were re-inking quietly all through the district that spring, and the villagers had decided the travelers were doing it, walking wide of them with a respect that made errands complicated.

"We are *following* the stones," Sigrika told the headman, with the journal held up as evidence. "The stones predate us. The ink predates us. The ink also postdates the entire — look, I've filed corrections in three villages—"

She had. That was true, and it was the mistake. The correction slips entered circulation and bred. Downstream of them, by the fifth village, the orange herald *carried the law in a book and fed it pages to keep it tame,* the wheel scholar had built her chair from a lightning-struck tree that had asked her to, the smiling ghost had drowned once *on purpose, to negotiate,* and the frost saint was a hundred years old — the one item in the whole corpus that was flatly, documentably correct, and therefore the one item every villager assumed was the exaggeration.

"I filed *corrections,*" Sigrika said, at the fire, with her head in her hands.

"You gave them a second edition," said Denia. "Collectors love those."

"The record is supposed to *converge.*"

"On what?" Denia lay back on her bedroll, laced her fingers behind her head, addressed the enormous unaudited stars. "You keep a journal. The road keeps one too. Yours has better handwriting. The road's has better circulation."

Mornye, running the day's instrument logs by firelight, made a low sound of discovery, and let it sit there, unexplained, until three heads turned — a technique she had learned from teaching.

"The stones," she said. "The re-inked ones. Today's had a marginal glyph the earlier ones lacked." She rotated the display. Sigrika was there before the rotation finished.

At the edge of the rubbing, small, deliberate, cut below the *walk well* in the ink that was one day and eight centuries old at once: a tally.

Four strokes. And beside them, smaller, a fifth mark, round, like a seed.

A moment passed with only the fire talking. It ate a branch. Buddy, on the fifth bedroll, pulsed once, amber, and Sigrika looked from the pulse to the rubbing and back, and wrote, in a hand that had gone very quiet:

*The road counts us. The count is correct.*

"It included the chick," she said.

"Good census discipline," said Hiyuki, and looked east, where the stones went, all the way to a glacier and past it, keeping their tally of four and a seed the whole way down the dark like a handrail.

---

The bird, they caught up to on the third evening, in the only way anyone catches a story.

It was a market town at dusk, lanterns going up, the flock resupplying, and Hiyuki had set the wooden ptarmigan on the stall table while she counted coin for wax and twine — set it down for a minute, in public, which was the whole error — and a voice beside her said:

"How much for the bird?"

The stranger wore a gray coat. Afterward, the stall-keeper would insist on a volunteer's vest, and the wax-seller two tables down would swear to an umbrella, furled, on a cloudless evening, and the child who had chalked the two-headed bird would say only that the lady had *hummed right,* whatever that meant, and describe her face three ways on three days.

"It's spoken for," said Hiyuki. "A tariff."

"Then a copy. I'll wait." The stranger picked the ptarmigan up, with permission asked precisely a half-second after the picking up, and turned it in the lantern light, and her face did something at the sight of the wings' disagreement — something quick and genuinely delighted, gone before it could be filed. "Oh, it's *terrible,*" she said, warmly. "It's completely terrible. She'll love it. How much."

"Whom will," said Hiyuki.

"The collection," said the stranger, and set down on the table, in payment, before any price had been named: one glass bead.

It was small and clear and it held the lantern flame upside down inside it, the way still water holds a falling star. Hiyuki looked at it for a while.

"The tariff," she said, "was one per nation."

"Then carve the Roya another. You're in circulation now." The stranger tucked the ptarmigan into her coat with the care of a museum, and paused, and looked across the stall — past Hiyuki, down the market row, to where Denia stood at the dried-fruit table with her back to them, weighing apricots, entirely absorbed — and the stranger watched her for the length of three heartbeats with an expression that had all the time in the world in it, and hummed, leaving, three notes of something, low and warm, that Hiyuki almost placed.

The dusk took her the way dusk takes anybody.

Denia arrived at the stall thirty seconds later with apricots and the fine-grained calm of a girl who had felt eyes and finished her errand anyway, on principle.

"Friend of yours?"

"A customer." Hiyuki held up the bead. The flame swam in it.

Denia looked at the bead for a long moment. Something old crossed the bottom of the smile and kept walking.

"Charge her double next time," she said, and paid for the apricots with exact change.

---

And the white bird — the actual bird, the two-voiced original, the reason for the whole detour — flew over them that night.

Or something did. It was dusk's last gray minute, camp half-made, and a shape went over high and pale with a long easy wingbeat, headed east, and as it passed it sang: three notes — and then, under them, around them, braided into them, three more, a second voice, lower, harmonizing with itself, the whole phrase falling through the air like a coin through water, once, complete.

Sigrika's pen stopped.

Hiyuki stood up. Slowly, all the way, cup set down without looking, face lifted, and the frost came out around her boots in a wide ring and *held* — banked gladness, banked entirely, a hundred years of composure holding a note — and she stayed like that, watching the pale shape go east until east absorbed it, and the ring of frost sat in the grass around her the whole time, at attention.

"Friend of yours?" Denia said. Gently, this time.

"Mm," said Hiyuki.

The frost melted all at once.

She sat back down, and picked up the cup, and drank, and the camp held its collective breath for a follow-up that had a century of practice at staying home. Sigrika looked at her, and at the east, and down at the journal, and made the hardest entry of her career:

*Species: unconfirmed. Voices: two, concurrent, thirds. Range: ahead of us. Status: the record declines to rule.*

Then, under it, smaller, because the record kept everything:

*The elder specimen knew the song.*

## III. The Wind's Daughters

Bjartr Woods received them the way a house receives family: the path knew their weight, the light came down pre-warmed, and breakfast was already on.

The coven's clearing had a long table in it, laid for nine, under trees so old their moss had moss, and the table's arithmetic was the first thing Sigrika noticed, because the flock made five with the chick, and the coven — the coven made a number that shifted when counted. There was Herta, small and imperious, presiding over a samovar with the air of a monarch reviewing troops. There was Black Swan, violet and unhurried, shuffling something that was sometimes cards. There was Viviane, gold-threaded, pouring. There was a young woman with silver hair and an armful of notebooks who could only be the transcriber, and a girl with a striped brooch who introduced herself as Elaina, traveler, "here for the jam, professionally," and at the samovar's far side a witch named Mina was materializing toast at a rate that implied either sorcery or a second Mina. And at the window of the cottage behind the table — the clearing had a cottage the way dreams have architecture, sensible from every angle it chose to offer — a tall figure in dark red stood with her back to the morning, present the way weather is present, and was included in every count and introduced in none of them.

At Denia's place setting, on the saucer, arranged in a neat fan: three sour candies.

She stopped behind the chair. Looked at the fan. Looked up the table at Viviane, who was pouring with the serene concentration of a woman who had set that saucer either an hour ago or the previous autumn.

"You knew the day."

"We knew the season," said Viviane. "The day was Herta's."

"The day was *obvious,*" said Herta. "Sit. The tea has a schedule."

They sat. Breakfast at the Banat al-Rih turned out to be a jurisdiction: jam moved by treaty, toast by decree, and conversation ran six directions at once with the whole table somehow attending to all of them, and the flock, road-simplified, ate like a delegation and got fluent fast.

Herta did the arithmetic early, aloud, over her cup, as pure hospitality.

"Four," she said, examining them each in turn like terms of a series. "Four divides poorly. Good. Divisible parties split at the first hard weather. And a remainder—" her eyes found Buddy, who was investigating the butter— "which rounds you toward five. Also good. Fives argue better."

"We're acquiring," said Denia.

"Obviously. Flocks do." Herta stirred. "The question is what the road is rounding you *toward,* and that," she said, with the satisfaction of a mathematician declining to show her work, "carries more terms than you've met."

---

The pool was around the cottage's east side, past the herb beds, under a birch letting down its first green.

Denia went alone, after breakfast, and the flock let her, and Viviane went with her because *alone* has always admitted one witch.

It was small and dark and perfectly still, the pool. Steam stood over it in the cool morning. And at its edge, on a flat stone, folded, sat a towel; and beside the towel, a cup, upside down against the dust; and a stone to weight a book against wind, holding a book down against the wind, the same book, a summer later, its place still kept with a birch leaf gone amber and translucent as lantern-paper.

The wintering spot, kept made up. A guest room with the bed turned down, in case.

Denia stood at the waterline with her hands in her pockets and looked at it all for a long time.

"The water asked after you," Viviane said. "In its way. It holds a shape a while, our pool. Yours took until midwinter to smooth."

"I sleep flat now," Denia said. "Beds. Regular hours. It's obscene."

"Mm." Viviane's eyes had gone to the coat — the driftwood buttons down its front, gray wood the sea had finished decades ago — and Denia followed the look down, to the third button, where a green tip the size of a grain of rice had split the old grain and put out one leaf smaller than a fingernail.

Silence, with steam in it.

"That's new," said Denia.

"That's spring," said Viviane. "Everything's showing off." She touched the leaf once, with one fingertip, the way you greet a baby, and her face did something ancient and pleased. "Dead wood budding. The forest will talk."

"It's your coat."

"It was my coat." Viviane turned back toward the table, the pool, the whole made-up room of it. "It's your record now. Wear it where things are waking. Apparently," she said, over her shoulder, mild as morning, "it takes notes."

---

The notebooks found each other before the girls did.

Sigrika had spread her rubbings at the table's clear end to dry — a dozen sheets of stone-lifted glyphs, the *walk well* corpus, the impossible vowel, the future-perfect verb, the tally of four and a seed — and came back from the pool path to find the silver-haired transcriber standing over them with an armful of her own pages, gone entirely white around the eyes.

"Where," said Alice, "did you *copy* these."

"Boundary stones. Field rubbings, the whole eastern district, they've been re-inking since—"

Alice set her own pages down beside the rubbings.

The same glyphs. The same vowel. The same verb bending into its impossible mood — in ink, on paper, in a fast fluent hand with crossings-out and second tries, dated in the margin.

Dated last autumn.

Sigrika sat down. Slowly. The bench received her like a colleague.

"Yours are field copies," Alice said, apologetic, the way one archivist condoles another.

"Yours are *earlier.*" Sigrika's finger hovered over the date, over the verb, over the whole quiet catastrophe of it. "The stones re-inked this spring. I have witnesses, I have *dates,* the ink postdates — you wrote the language before the world had it. Where were you copying *from?*"

"I take dictation," said Alice.

"From *whom?*"

Alice opened her mouth. Viviane, arriving with the jam, set it down between them with the gentle finality of a border.

"Plum," she said. "It's the good year."

Down the table, past the samovar, Sigrika caught it — quiet, cup-covered, Herta to Viviane, four words in passing:

"Has she woken?"

"All in due time."

And that was the whole of it; the table's weather moved on; Elaina was defending, loudly, jam as a career; and Sigrika sat with her rubbings and Alice's dictation lined up edge to edge, two halves of a language arriving from opposite directions, and wrote in the journal, small, in the space she kept for things too large:

*The text precedes the stones. Somebody is being written down before she speaks.*

*Query, standing: who.*

Beside her, Alice leaned in, read the entry upside down with a transcriber's reflex, and added, in her own hand, underneath, with the smallest smile:

*Query seconded. — A.*

---

Black Swan waited until the leaving to spend her card.

She did it at the clearing's edge, with the flock packed and the goodbyes half-made — appeared beside them on the path with her sometimes-cards fanned face-down, and drew one, and looked at it, and put it away, all before anyone could decline.

"A small one," she said, in the voice of a woman selecting a chocolate. "Free of charge, since you set such a lovely table of yourselves. Before the coast—" her eyes moved down the flock, unhurried, and settled, with tenderness, on the middle distance between all of them— "one of you will be handed something. And will refuse to give it back."

The path held still.

"Which of us," said Sigrika.

"Mm. That's the flavor," said Black Swan, "and the wait is the flavor," and she smiled at Hiyuki as she said it, one professional to another, and was already strolling back toward the samovar. "Bring me a feather from your two-voiced friend," she called, going. "I have a wager to settle."

"With whom?" Sigrika called after her.

The window of the cottage stood empty by then. But the tall figure in dark red had come out to the herb beds, at the far edge of things, and was watching Denia settle her pack straps — had been watching, it occurred to several of them at once, for a while — and as the flock passed her at the treeline she spoke, once, to Denia, in a voice with four hundred years of accompaniment worn smooth off it:

"You wear it well."

Denia stopped. The coat sat on her shoulders; the third button wore its leaf.

"It came with tenants," she said.

"The best things do," said Phrolova, and went back to the rosemary, and the woods closed behind the flock like a well-kept door.

In the journal, on the walk out, one entry, unhedged for once:

*Coven (n: unstable, ≥7). Feeds strays, keeps rooms, predates languages. The kettle is ancient and the jam is a weapon. We are expected back. Everyone is expected back. That seems to be the entire doctrine.*

## IV. The Well-Field, Three Ways

Somewhere past the woods the road climbed, and the nights went clear and enormous, and on one of them, fed and idle, the flock committed the oldest crime of the campfire: they retold themselves.

It began as procedure. Sigrika was recopying the well-field pages — the gold, the stones, the fifteen-minute protocol — into fair-copy for the Academy packet, and read a line aloud to check a timing, and Denia, flat on her bedroll with her eyes closed, said:

"That's wrong."

"It's *verbatim.*"

"It's verbatim wrong. I was there. I did nothing, and I'd swear to it." Denia folded her hands on her sternum, composing the deposition. "Version as follows. The candidate overclocked. I had soap bubbles on me, as any prepared adult does. I blew one. It drifted, because drifting is what I had the budget for. It landed. Sixty seconds later, the crisis had resolved itself, as crises do when you refuse to dignify them. My contribution has been overstated by every account since, and I want that minuted."

"You *ran,*" Sigrika said. "You crossed the whole field. There are *boot prints* in the survey photos—"

"Doctored."

"By *whom?*"

"Enemies of laziness. I have many."

Mornye set down her stylus, which the fire had learned to read as a gavel.

"Chronology," she said, "as recorded by instrumentation," and delivered the whole rescue as a maintenance log — *timestamp, resonance spike; timestamp, countermeasure one, colloidal, deployed by D. at a dead sprint, see appendix; timestamp, cryo-contact, two fingers, nape; timestamp, protocol ratified* — in a voice of such perfect administrative flatness that the flock leaned in as one, because deadpan, sustained past a certain length, becomes the most theatrical register there is, and Mornye knew it, and paced her timestamps like a thriller, and landed *see appendix* with the timing of a woman who had lectured to full halls.

"Objection," said Denia. "The appendix is slander."

"The appendix is *photographs.*"

"Version three," Sigrika announced, standing, because the saga register requires standing, and gave it the full Roya: the gold as a chain-fire loosed by treacherous stones, herself as a doomed maiden of the runic arts — "*doomed,*" she repeated, relishing it — the bubble as a moon fallen from its mounting, the frost as the winter's own hand, the protocol as a royal decree sealed by an amber familiar, and it ran four minutes and contained a sword from somewhere, and by the end Buddy was providing sound effects on the strong beats, pulsing amber, fully corrupted by drama, and the fire applauded in sparks.

"There was a sword?" said Denia.

"Thematically."

"I want the sword version filed next to mine."

"They're all getting filed. That's the—" Sigrika sat, wrote, spoke and wrote at once— "conflicting accounts are *standard,* actually, in every serious archive, you keep all the versions, and the truth is—" the pen slowed— "the truth is the thing they disagree around."

The fire took that one and turned it over for a while.

"Adjudication," said Denia at last, and everyone looked at Hiyuki.

Hiyuki set down her cup. She had listened to all three versions with the stillness of a woman at a hearing, and she rendered her ruling in the shrine register, formally, three sentences, hands folded:

"A candidate labored past her strength, for others, which is the oldest prayer. Three strangers answered it at a run, each in her own liturgy. The field keeps the true version, and sends up grass."

Silence.

"That's the least accurate one yet," said Denia.

"Mm," Hiyuki agreed.

"It's going in as the official record," said Sigrika, writing fast.

---

The night stretched. The fire banked. And because clear enormous nights do this to a flock, the talk tipped east, toward the standing question, the one they were walking into: the word. The god saying it continuously, patiently, down the roots of the world, eleven beats and a wait, eleven beats and a wait, for as long as anyone had instruments.

"To whom," said Sigrika, chin on her knees. "That's the whole expedition, and we've never once gone around the fire on it. Everybody guesses. Guessing is free. Mornye first."

"A receiver that predates the corpus," said Mornye, immediately, because she had been carrying the answer for months. "Something old enough to hold a channel that long. The message repeats because repetition is how you talk across deep time. He's leaving a signal for something that checks in on a very long period." She turned her cup. "Or a very slow reader."

"Denia."

"Someone who left." Denia said it to the stars, without ceremony. "You say a thing continuously when you can't know if it's landing. It's a voicemail. He's been filling the tape for a century." A beat. "It's what I'd do."

"You *did,*" said Sigrika, quietly, and Denia's mouth moved at one corner, allowing it into the record.

"Yours, then," Denia said. "The moderator's read."

"Everyone," said Sigrika. "It's a broadcast. Eleven beats, then a wait — that's a call sign. You transmit a call sign to whoever's out there scanning. The wait *is* the message: it means *reply on this frequency.* He's not talking to a person." She caught herself, glanced at Denia, restated it in the affirmative, journal-clean: "He's holding a channel open. For anyone. That's the kindest possible use of a throat."

Buddy, who had been listening from the fifth bedroll, pulsed three times, slowly, in what was either agreement or digestion.

"Elder specimen," said Sigrika. "Final guess. What's he saying?"

Hiyuki looked at the fire for a while. The frost around her boots came and went, once, like a tide deciding.

"I stopped guessing what people carry," she said, "eighty years ago. It spoils the hearing." She drank. "When we arrive, I will ask him."

"Ask him *what.*"

"His wish."

The line hung there while the fire worked. Denia laughed once, low, delighted, the real one.

"You'd charge a god a candy."

"Sliding scale," said Hiyuki.

---

Later, banking the coals, Sigrika went down the row of bedrolls with the census reflex — one, two, three, herself — and stopped at the fifth, the vacancy, the seat kept open, where Buddy had over the past weeks constructed something between a nest and an occupation: the bedroll dimpled to fit, a favorite corner dragged into a bunker, the wooden ptarmigan's replacement — *Ptarmigan, second printing* — installed as either roommate or shrine.

"The vacancy has a tenant," she reported.

"Subletting," said Denia, from inside her own bedroll.

"It's holding the seat warm," said Mornye. "Fragments understand reservations."

Sigrika crouched, and Buddy surfaced from the blankets and pulsed at her, amber, entirely at home, and she updated the ledger by firelight, the ledger that kept everything:

*Bedroll (5): occupied by chick, pro tempore. Vacancy preserved in spirit. The seat is warm. Applicants: the road is reviewing.*

## V. Station One

The site chose itself, the way Mornye's best results always had.

A ridge, two days past the woods, where a Solistree root-line broke the surface like a whale's back and ran east along the fall of the land — living wood, thigh-thick, warm to the palm, carrying the word up out of the deep at a strength that put Buddy into slow orbits and made the instruments state their readings in italics. Mornye ran the survey twice, parked the chair, and announced, with the calm of a woman arriving at a destination she had drawn on a napkin in another life:

"Here."

Station One went up in three days, and the building of it was the flock working as one animal for the first time at length: Sigrika on timber and knots, strong, quick, singing tunelessly at the work the way the Roya sings at work; Denia on everything requiring patience, shims and seals and the fine leveling, which she performed lying down wherever the task permitted lying down and executed flawlessly; Hiyuki on the roof, because a century of shrine upkeep makes a roofer, laying shakes in courses so exact that Odi's whole guild would have wept; and Mornye at the center of it, conducting, the design unfolding out of her the way music once had — a timber cabin the size of a confession booth, resin-sealed, with a copper spiral descending its heart to a cradle where the living root came through the floor.

The doctrine was in the cradle. Every committee she had resigned from would have bolted the sensor to the root and been done by lunch. Mornye's design *asked:* the wire lay in an open channel, and the root could hold it or shed it, and the station only functioned while the holding held.

"Consent-based instrumentation," Sigrika read, off the schematic, slowly.

"The subject is a colleague," said Mornye. "Colleagues can quit."

They laid the wire at dusk on the third day, loosely, along the channel, an offer. By dark the readings were live.

By dawn the wire had been rewound.

Sigrika found it, on first watch's last round, and woke the station's architect with the tact of a fire alarm. The copper lay in the channel in a new dress: a slow even spiral with a doubled turn at the third and eighth winds, snugged to the root's grain, finished with a tuck that every field manual would call impossible and every grandmother would call *properly put away.* The dew on it sat undisturbed. Buddy, questioned, pulsed with transparent innocence and, pressed further, hid in the ptarmigan's shadow.

Mornye looked at the winding for a long time. Ran the numbers. Ran them again.

"Gain's up forty percent," she said. "The doubled turns are load-matching. This is better than my design." She sat back. Something moved through her face, slow, and settled, and shone. "The peer review came back."

---

Evenings at the station, an appointment formed, the way appointments formed around this flock: by happening twice.

It began the first night, with the recorder running and the readouts crawling and Mornye watching the word arrive as geometry — eleven beats, the wait, eleven beats — and Hiyuki, on the bench by the door with her cup, remarking, mildly, to the room:

"He's flatting the ninth tonight."

Mornye's stylus stopped.

"Say that again."

"The ninth beat. It sits lower this evening. A comma's worth." Hiyuki drank. "He does it when the melt runs hard. I assumed your machines heard it."

The machines, consulted, had heard it — buried, four decimal places down, a deviation the analysis flagged as noise. Mornye looked at the flag for a while. Then she cleared the bench opposite, set out paper, and said, in a register the flock rarely got from her, one stripped of all verdict:

"Hum it for me."

Hiyuki set down the cup, and hummed: the word, the whole eleven, pitched down into a human throat's little compass — and Mornye transcribed. By hand, on paper, stylus flying, and what came out was mathematics, staves of it, intervals as ratios, the flatted ninth resolving on the page into a term she circled twice — and her face, bent over the work, was the face of a woman reading a letter in a language she had grown up speaking and lost in a fire.

"I sold the ear," she said, at the end, conversationally, to the paper. "Bridges, mostly. Burned in the work. The music went with them." She turned the page around, the circled term facing Hiyuki like an offering. "The shapes stayed. I can read a symphony's silhouette. You've just handed me the sound of one term of a god." She looked up. "That's a flow rate. He's telling the roots how hard the melt is running. He talks *about the weather.*" A pause, and then, with the precision of a woman placing her whole request inside four words:

"Same time tomorrow?"

"Mm," said Hiyuki, and that was the contract, and it held every evening after: the shrine maiden humming a god down to human size, the scientist transcribing by silhouette, one ear between them, jointly held.

---

The recording that mattered came on the fifth night, and it came because Sigrika asked a beginner's question, which is where everything that matters comes from.

"What's under it?"

Mornye looked up from the console. "Under."

"The word. Eleven beats, the wait — that's the top. Rivers have beds. What's the *bed?*" Sigrika turned the journal sideways, as if the page had a floor. "Slow it down. All the way. What's at the bottom of him?"

Mornye considered the question, and the girl, and made a note in the margin of the schematic that looked suspiciously like a grade. Then she pulled the night's master recording, and slowed it.

Ten times: the word became a landscape, beats like hills.

A hundred times: the hills became weather systems, each beat a front with structure inside it, and Buddy came off the bedroll and pressed against the console leg and stayed.

A thousand times: the word dissolved into its own foundation, a floor of sound below sound, the pressure of a planet keeping time — and there, at the very bottom of the spectrum, under the beats, under the wait, under everything, faint and even and *rhythmic in a different meter than the god:*

A sound.

Soft. Dry. Recurring at long, patient, irregular-regular intervals, the way a thing recurs when a mind is doing it. The four of them leaned into the speaker as one, and the station held its breath, and the sound came again, and there was no mistaking it, and each of them mistook it identically:

Paper. A page, turning.

The recording ran on. The pages turned at their own pace, unhurried, absorbed, somewhere at the floor of the world or the floor of the sky or the floor of the word itself — bearing indeterminate, Mornye confirmed, voice very quiet, hands very still; *it's under the bearing; it's under everything* — and the god's word rolled on above it, eleven beats and the wait, and it came to all of them slowly, in the fire-ticking silence, what the wait might be for.

He says it continuously. And somewhere, someone keeps up.

"Somebody's reading along," Sigrika whispered.

The speaker hissed its floor-sound. A page turned. The flock sat in a resin-sealed cabin on a ridge in the dark, four small warm accounted-for lives and a chick, listening to the sound of being read, and the moment balanced on a knife between two sizes of feeling — surveillance, audience — and it was Denia, of course it was Denia, who had been watched her whole life by everything that had ever owned her, who reached over, turned the volume up one notch, and settled back with her hands behind her head.

"Then let's be worth staying up for," she said.

Sigrika wrote it down. Under it, the entry that took her longest:

*Addendum to the standing query. The word has a bed. The bed is a reader. Revised guess, all hands, unanimous, unspoken: he is telling someone a story. He has been telling it for a century. She has not*— the pen paused; the pen went back; the journal kept everything, including its keeper —*she keeps turning the pages.*

*The wait is for her.*

They closed the station at midnight. And on the sill outside, before walking down to the fire, Hiyuki set a sour candy — label out, squarely placed, facing the enormous dark and the root-line running east and the whole listening world — and stood over it a moment in her shrine stillness, a hundred years of practice at leaving offerings for parties unseen.

"For whoever's listening nearest," she said.

The frost around her boots melted all at once, and she went down to the fire.

## VI. Green

Take the inventory, the way the journal would.

One chair, grafted, rolling on amber, shedding the road's own mud with the road's own resin; watch for blossom. One coat, budding at the third button, two leaves now, the forest talking. One pressed magnolia bud, staining a journal gold, smelling in warm weather of a dormitory in another world. One language, arriving from two directions at once, transcribed before spoken, its stones switching sides all down the east, keeping a tally of four and a seed. One glass bead, in a coin purse, holding fire upside down; one terrible bird in circulation, in a collector's coat, somewhere, loved. One feather owed to a wager between witches. One prophecy in transit: *before the coast, one of you will be handed something, and will refuse to give it back* — and every member of the flock privately certain of which of them it will be, and every private certainty naming a different name, which is how the good prophecies work.

One vacancy, warm, tenanted by a chick, its seat held against the road's review — and the road reviewing: a report annotated in a hostile familiar hand demanding replication, a census of wants arriving by post, a rock already selected, a bird already mailed. Flocks acquire. The arithmetic carries more terms than they've met.

And one appointment, oldest on the books, rolling on ahead of them: the coast by early summer. The queue where the wait is the flavor. Four Dolls with a standing order and a signed spring. A festival that happens on time because a city agreed to keep happening. Beyond it, east of everything, a god saying his word into the floor of the world, eleven beats and a wait, telling a story a century long to a reader who keeps turning the pages — and a flock walking the word's length toward the question one of them intends, professionally, to ask.

On the last morning at Station One, breaking camp, Sigrika ran the dawn census out of pure ritual and found, on a waystone fifty meters east — a stone none of them had passed, on a stretch none of them had walked — a sour candy. Label out. Squarely placed. Facing west, toward them, the way an offering faces its shrine.

She carried it back to the fire at a dead sprint.

The flock gathered around it in the gray light. It was the cheap kind, the Academy kind, the kind that had been leaving their hands on stones and sills and saucers all the way east — the custom loose in the world now, traveling ahead of them, coming back the wrong direction, from whoever's standing nearest.

Hiyuki picked it up, considered it, and put it in her sleeve.

"Income," she said.

They walked east into the green, four weathers and a chick, quoted everywhere, misquoted lovingly, expected ahead and behind — and the stones kept their tally, and the pages kept turning, and the road kept the only version of the story that matters, and sent up grass.

Chapter 1: Zhī Zhǐ

The corridor outside the secretary’s cabin smelled of dry cedar polish and brass oil that had never gone rancid.

Yangyang stepped over the sill first with her right hand on her hilt, listening ten paces left and ten paces right down the empty gallery, and Iuno came second with a faint silver-blue spark held ready at her fingertips. Before Yangyang had taken her third step toward the forward companionway, a moon-white hem brushed past her knee without a sound.

Qingxiao did not ask to take the point, and she did not slow for them to make room. She simply walked through the narrow space between Yangyang’s elbow and the wainscot on level, unhurried feet, the taut white ribbon of frequency rising from both her cuffs and cutting straight through the overhead beams as if the solid timber were mist.

Yangyang glanced back once at Iuno. Iuno’s jaw was still set from the cracked teacup on the map table; she gave a single, slight dip of her chin. Yangyang let the Paragon keep the lead, dropping back a pace behind Qingxiao’s right shoulder where she could see the floorboards ahead and the back of Qingxiao’s cuffs at the same time.

This deck of the Shuttle had been built in the exact image of the Ministry of War’s fourth registry hall in the founding reign. Along the inner bulkhead hung rows of black-lacquered duty boards with boxwood pegs slotted neatly into every hole; beneath the unmoving late-afternoon gold of the portholes stood long trestle desks furnished with porcelain brush-rests, brass water-droppers shaped like crouching toads, and iron-bound petition chests whose padlocks had no scratches around the keyholes.

At the third desk sat two clerks in faded brown linen robes.

As the three of them approached, the younger clerk dipped an ivory seal into a dry stone ink-bed that held no cinnabar at all, pressed the blank square carefully onto the bottom corner of a sheet of empty mulberry paper, and smoothed the page with the heel of his hand before laying it on the finished stack beside his elbow. Both men looked up when Qingxiao’s white sleeves passed the lamp-post. Their eyes moved over her cloud-pale horns, Iuno’s bare crystal shoulder, and Yangyang’s scabbard with mild, courteous interest, and then dropped right back to the next blank sheet on the pile.

*Thump.* The dry seal came down on empty paper again.

Nobody spoke to them. At the end of the registry aisle, the first flight of companionway stairs rose between two bronze-sheathed pillars toward the fourth deck. The steps were steep, built to naval measure with narrow iron-shod treads and a high riser to clear the main steam-trunk underneath.

At the bottom step, Qingxiao paused for half a beat.

Without turning her head all the way around, she let her pale blue eyes drop once to the tight linen wrap binding the left side of Yangyang’s sash.

“Take the outer side by the rail,” Qingxiao said in her flat, quiet voice. “The risers are high on the left turn.”

It was so small and ordinary a thing—the exact dry, unceremonious care she had shown on the barges when she told them to bend their knees—that Yangyang felt the tension between her shoulder blades loosen by a fraction.

“Yes, Paragon,” Yangyang said.

Qingxiao went up the iron-shod steps without touching the banister, her white stockings moving in an even, metronomic cadence that never varied by half an inch. Two steps below her, Iuno climbed with her right hand skimming the brass rail for balance, her eyes fixed on the pale trail of frost that stayed on every tread after Qingxiao’s heel left it.

In the first corridor before they rested, the frost from Qingxiao’s boots had been a thin, feathery rime that melted back into dark dampness before Yangyang stepped on it. Here on the stair, the white crescents did not melt. When Yangyang’s sole touched the third tread, the ice under her boot was as hard and dry as quarry flint, and it squeaked against the leather like cold iron.

At the top of the flight, the passage bent around an arched sentry alcove flanked by tall bronze halberd-racks.

Two guard Autopuppets stood dark inside the shadow of the arch, their broad bronze shoulders plated in the heavy laminar pattern of the old Fortress garrison. As Qingxiao’s foot touched the landing stone, the crimson lenses in both brass visors flared awake at once. Heavy ironwood halberds came off the floor with a synchronized grind of ratchets, swinging down to bar the throat of the stair.

Yangyang’s thumb pushed her guard an inch clear of the scabbard throat. Behind her, the crystal at Iuno’s left shoulder chimed a high, sharp note as the silver-blue rim of the moonwheel condensed at her wrist.

Neither of them got their weapons into the air.

Qingxiao did not break stride, and her hands did not leave her sleeves. Four thin needles of pale Sword Intent slipped from her cuffs with a sound like silk tearing in a freeze.

The first two needles took the left-hand machine cleanly through the optical slit and the central logic-spindle behind its breastplate; the third and fourth sheared the right-hand puppet’s halberd shaft in two and pinned its knee-ratchets to the timber baseboard. Both half-ton chassis dropped onto the landing planks in the same heartbeat. The crimson light in the first puppet’s visor went out at once, its bronze chin coming to rest against the wood with a dead, hollow clunk.

It was over.

Qingxiao took one step onto the landing beside the downed machine. Her right cuff twitched twice more.

Two heavy, flat blades of frost-white light dropped straight down into the dead puppet’s back—one shearing crosswise through the stilled brass ribs, the second taking the neck-ring cleanly off the shoulders so the dark visor rolled six inches into the wainscot.

Where the extra cuts scored the bronze, jagged needles of white rime bloomed out of the metal in the warm corridor air. Every single point of the frost grew inward, angling backward across the severed plates toward the hem of Qingxiao’s skirt.

Yangyang stopped on the top step with her sword still an inch out of its sheath.

She looked down at the three cleanly severed pieces of a machine that had already been dead before the second blade fell. A step below her on the stair, Iuno stopped too, her gaze going first to the frosted bronze on the floorboards and then shifting sideways to Yangyang’s profile—catching the exact moment Yangyang counted the strokes.

Nobody said a word.

Ahead of them, Qingxiao did not look down at the wreckage by her heel. The pale ribbon of frequency rising from her wrists into the ceiling beams did not shiver, and her breathing did not quicken. She walked straight past the rolling bronze head toward the tall double doors at the end of the passage.

***

Beyond the double doors, the fourth and third decks had been hollowed out into a single vaulted chamber that smelled of dry straw and old incense ash.

It was the Fortress’s war tribunal hall, lifted stone for stone out of the mountain keep and set inside the Shuttle’s ribs. Thick columns of dark cedar rose thirty feet to a coffered ceiling where the Mirage’s false sunlight slanted in through high clerestory windows, illuminating motes of dust that hung motionless in the air without ever settling to the floor. At the far end of the court stood a raised magistrate’s dais with an empty rosewood chair, an ink-stone dry to the bottom, and two tall wooden placards carved with *Held* and *Dismissed*. To the right of the dais hung a four-foot bronze gong on an ironwood frame, its striker resting in a velvet cradle.

Down the center of the flagged floor, between two rows of worn straw kneeling-mats, stood a queue of about forty people.

Some wore the padded hemp coats of Xianling teamsters; most wore the dented, soot-blackened laminar iron of the Yuan Fortress garrison. None of them leaned on each other, and none of them looked at the wounds in their neighbors’ armor. A spearman near the back stood with both hands folded patiently over a snapped ash shaft still wedged through his leather corselet; two paces ahead of him, an elderly woman held an empty birdcage with the door bent off its hinges, staring straight between the cedar columns at the empty magistrate’s chair as if a clerk were about to call her number and stamp a receipt for her house.

The three of them kept to the side aisle behind the columns, aiming for the wide stone stair that climbed the north wall toward the fifth deck.

They were halfway down the flank of the hall when a young man near the front of the queue turned his head.

He couldn’t have been more than nineteen—younger than Liangyu—with a gate warden’s brass tally plate laced to his shoulder and a broad grey smear of wood-ash across his nose and left cheek. His scabbard hung empty at his hip, the leather chape split where the blade had been wrenched out in a crush. When he saw the curve of Qingxiao’s horns moving behind the cedar pillars, he stepped neatly out of the line, walked over to the edge of the straw mats, and bowed with his hands together.

“Sword Transcendent,” the boy said. His voice was clear, polite, and completely level, as if he were asking the time of day at a ferry landing. “I am glad you came by. The clerk hasn’t come out to the bench yet, and I think you would know the rule.”

Qingxiao slowed to a stop beside the fourth column. Her pale blue eyes rested on the brass tally plate at his shoulder.

“Which gate?” Qingxiao asked.

“The western postern, Immortal. Under the third watchtower.” The young warden touched the empty throat of his scabbard with two fingers, his brow creasing slightly as though he were trying to recall where he had set down a pair of tongs. “When the black rain began to steam on the roof tiles, the sergeant shouted the word down from the parapet. I know I heard him clearly, and I know I made up my mind what to do, only now my head is very light and I cannot remember which order it was. Should I go back out to hold the arch, or fall back inside the keep with the villagers?”

Behind Qingxiao’s shoulder, Yangyang’s steps stilled on the flagstones. A pace behind her, Iuno remembered the watch gallery the night before, where Qingxiao had sat with her fingers bleeding onto her qin strings because she could not stop asking herself whether she should have let a rotten beam fall on twenty stone-cutters in a ditch.

Qingxiao looked at the boy’s nineteen-year-old face and the empty scabbard at his waist. The air around her white sleeves dropped five degrees between one breath and the next.

“Go back to the arch,” Qingxiao said evenly. “Hold the post until you fall.”

Then she turned her head toward the north stair and walked on.

The young warden bowed again to her retreating back, his face as smooth and untroubled as water in a well. “Thank you, Immortal. I will go back out.”

He turned toward the rear doors of the hall where the false afternoon glared through the seam.

“Wait,” Yangyang said.

She stepped out from behind the cedar column and caught up to him in two paces, stopping right in front of his dented breastplate. Up close, the grey ash across his cheek didn’t even smell of smoke; it was just color painted onto skin that couldn’t feel the draft.

The boy stopped politely. “Yes, miss?”

Yangyang looked at his empty scabbard, and then at the straw kneeling-mats lined up along the floor where no magistrate was ever coming to strike the gong. She didn’t talk about two hundred years, or Muyu’s looms, or what had happened to the third watchtower before the rain stopped.

“The carts from Xianling already got inside the walls,” Yangyang said in her plain, gentle voice. “Nobody else is coming up the western road tonight, and your sword is gone. Sit down on the mat and rest your legs.”

The young warden blinked once. He looked down at his empty scabbard as if noticing for the first time that there was no hilt in it, then looked over at the nearest square of braided straw by the column base.

“All right,” he said quietly. “My knees were a little stiff anyway.”

He walked three steps to the mat, folded his iron-greaved legs underneath him, and sat down with his hands resting on his thighs, looking peacefully at the cedar base of the pillar instead of the door.

Ahead by the foot of the north stair, Qingxiao had not waited, and she had not looked back.

Yangyang hurried down the side aisle to close the gap, her left hand coming up to press flat against her sash as the quick steps jarred her bound ribs. Halfway along the colonnade, Iuno slowed her own stride so Yangyang didn’t have to run to catch them, falling into step beside her as they neared the bottom of the steps.

Iuno wasn’t looking at the queue of dead soldiers, and she wasn’t looking at the empty dais.

Her eyes were fixed on the back of Qingxiao’s moon-white collar ten paces ahead—level, cold, and narrowed by a fraction, the exact way she used to look down from the bronze chair beside the Ephor when a provincial prefect stood up in the Senate to explain that a winter grain levy had been calculated for the common good.

*I know that cadence, darling,* the High Priestess murmured in the back of Iuno’s skull, very low and without a trace of theater. *We used to sign frontier warrants in it before breakfast.*

*Be quiet,* Iuno thought back, her hand brushing the cold stone of the column as they turned onto the stair, *and watch her cuffs.*

***

The stair between the third and fourth decks did not rise straight; it climbed in tight, square turns around a central masonry pier, lit by narrow casement slits that looked out on the same unmoving, brassy afternoon sky.

Halfway up the second flight, they reached a flagged landing where a brass speaking-tube ran down the bulkhead from the upper bridge, its flared mouthpiece polished to a mirror shine beside a sealed iron inspection box.

Qingxiao stopped on the landing to wait for them to clear the lower turn, resting her left hand two inches from the timber newel post.

She did not touch the wood. Even so, a grey skin of frost bloomed across the varnished pine of the post—and as Iuno stepped up onto the landing beside her, she saw that the rime wasn’t spreading outward from Qingxiao’s fingers at all. Tiny beads of moisture were condensing out of the mild stairwell air six inches away and crawling backward across the grain toward Qingxiao’s white cuff, as if the cold inside her sleeves were a drain pulling every drop of warmth out of the timber.

Her pale hands were completely still. Not a knuckle twitched; not a thread of her cuff trembled.

Iuno stopped two feet from her on the flagstones. Down the stairwell behind them, Yangyang was coming up the last three steps with her left elbow tucked tight against her ribs and her right hand sliding along the wall.

“Your hands stopped shaking,” Iuno said quietly.

Qingxiao looked toward the final flight of steps above them. “The cabin was sufficient.”

“It wasn’t a compliment,” Iuno said. Her gaze lifted from the backward-creeping frost on the newel post to the taut, twilight-darkened ribbon of frequency rising from Qingxiao’s wrists into the stone soffit overhead. “The threads you left on the Wardens upstairs. Are they still only turning blows aside?”

For half a heartbeat, Qingxiao did not turn her head.

Beside her left temple, the flared brass collar of the speaking-tube caught the flat golden glare from the casement slit. In the curved, darkened metal of the rim, the tiny reflection of Qingxiao’s profile looked back down the stairwell with eyes the color of bruised winter violet.

When Qingxiao turned her face toward Iuno, her eyes were pale, clear mountain blue, unblinking beneath the curve of her horns.

“You sent a hundred and seventy-three gladiators into the salt-marshes to buy a capital,” Qingxiao said. Her voice was level, unhurried, and completely free of heat, as if she were reading a line of distance off a milestone. “Do not audit how I spend a thread.”

The landing went dead still.

Iuno’s breath stopped in her throat. The crystal at her left shoulder didn’t even chime; her weight locked straight down into her heels on the stone. She did not step back, and she did not open her mouth to defend herself, because there was no defense against it—every word of it was hers, handed over in the dark on the watch gallery when she had stripped off thirty years of court parables to keep this woman from breaking her own heart over twenty stone-cutters.

Inside Iuno’s skull, where the High Priestess would ordinarily have risen up in white-hot fury to tear the skin off anyone who dared put their fingers in that wound, there was nothing at all.

Not a scoff, not a sharp intake of breath, not a single word. The silence behind Iuno’s eyes was like a room after a lamp has been knocked off the table into the dark.

One step below the landing, Yangyang stopped with her boot on the riser.

She looked from the white, bloodless line around Iuno’s mouth up to Qingxiao’s tranquil porcelain profile. Yangyang did not raise her voice, and she did not drop her eyes to the floor the way a junior disciple was supposed to do when a master spoke sharply.

“That was hers to tell, Paragon,” Yangyang said.

Qingxiao turned her head a fraction to look down at her disciple.

For the space of a single breath, there was nothing in Qingxiao’s face at all—no impatience, no rebuke, no flicker of the woman who had clapped twice on the flagship deck when Yangyang landed her jump. It was like looking into a frozen well before the sun reaches the rim.

Then Qingxiao turned away toward the upper flight. As her left wrist passed the newel post, her thumb brushed once against the worn red cord of jade beads at her cuff. Beside the bead that had split in the cabin, a second green jade bead gave a tiny, dry *tick* in the quiet, a hair-thin white crack running straight from the string-hole to its outer edge.

Qingxiao went up the steps without another word.

On the landing, Yangyang stepped up beside Iuno. She didn’t touch Iuno’s arm—she knew better than to crowd her when her spine was set like that—only stood close enough that her sleeve brushed Iuno’s sash until Iuno drew one slow, silent breath through her nose and climbed the stair after the white hem.

At the top of the flight, the fourth-deck passage widened into a pillared gallery lined with brass telemetry conduits that hummed with a low, sour vibration from the spire above.

Iuno lengthened her stride, passing Yangyang on the flat planks until she came alongside Qingxiao’s left shoulder before the inner archway. Her chin was up, her voice clipped and stiff as starched linen.

“What you said on the stair,” Iuno said.

Qingxiao slowed and stopped beside a cedar pillar, turning her head toward Iuno. Her pale blue eyes met Iuno’s squarely, a faint, genuine crease forming between her brows.

“What did I say?” Qingxiao asked.

Iuno stared at her.

She knew every shape a lie could take—she had listened to senators, factors, and priests lie to her face for three decades, and she knew the tiny stiffening of the jaw when a person pretended to forget a knife they had just used. There was none of it here. Qingxiao was looking back at her from the bottom of a bone-deep, freezing fatigue, waiting patiently to be reminded of a sentence she had spoken ninety seconds ago and never heard leave her own mouth.

The anger in Iuno’s chest vanished as if the floor had dropped out from under it, leaving something much colder in its place.

For two full beats Iuno looked into those pale blue eyes. Then she took half a step back toward the middle of the runner.

“Nothing,” Iuno said, her voice dropping flat and careful. “The air is thin on the turns.”

Qingxiao looked at her for a moment longer, gave a single slight nod, and walked on through the inner arch toward the lift-shaft vestibule to inspect the chains.

***

Beyond the arch, the passage opened into a tall octagonal staging room where the Ministry Shuttle’s central hoist rose toward the fifth deck and the Tian spire above. Heavy iron guide-chains hung motionless in their grease-chutes twenty paces ahead. Qingxiao walked out onto the grating beside the shaft coaming, her back to the doorway as she studied the jammed counterweights overhead.

Just inside the stone arch, out of earshot behind the thick masonry pier, Iuno stopped and caught Yangyang’s right sleeve with two fingers.

“We have to stop her here,” Iuno said, pitching her voice barely above the hum of the wall conduits. “Before we get into that shaft. Make her sit down against the pier, make her drop the outer threads, or make her tell us what is tearing in her Curve.”

Yangyang looked past the edge of the stonework at the motionless white figure standing by the iron chains, then shook her head once.

“If we push her right now,” Yangyang said softly, “she’ll go up the shaft without us.”

Iuno’s brow furrowed sharply. “She can barely feel her own feet.”

“She doesn’t need her feet to step onto a blade and drop the counterweight behind her,” Yangyang said.

She didn’t explain how she knew that. She didn’t say a word about flour jars in Jinzhou, or the way a person’s shoulders settled when they had quietly finished adding up everyone else’s safety and decided it was simpler to lock the door from the outside than argue about it another five minutes. She just looked at the angle of Qingxiao’s chin by the hoist rail and knew it the way a carpenter knows a warped board.

“So what is your proposal, Yangyang?” Iuno asked, her voice tight and low in the shadow of the arch. “We walk three paces behind her in polite silence and wait until she falls from the ceiling?”

“We stay close enough that she can’t put a gate between us,” Yangyang replied evenly. “Until we’re at the top.”

For two breaths they stood facing each other in the narrow shadow of the pier. Neither of them backed down, and neither of them raised her voice. Between them sat the unspoken weight of the household waiting in the real Hold above them—Iuno, who had spent her life holding the lever of the room and could not bear watching a disaster unfold without putting her hand on the wheel; and Yangyang, who knew to the marrow of her bones that grabbing a fraying line with both hands was the surest way to make it snap.

Yangyang’s gaze shifted back toward the shaft coaming.

“In the cabin,” Yangyang said, so quiet her voice barely cleared her collar, “while you were asleep in the chair, I heard a second breath underneath hers. Half a beat out of step.”

Iuno went very still.

“I thought it was the air-trunk behind the bunk,” Yangyang said, her fingers tightening a fraction on her scabbard throat. “I heard it again on the stairs just now. While she was walking.”

For a long moment Iuno did not answer. She looked through the archway at the back of Qingxiao’s head, where the cloud-pale horns gleamed dull white against the dark iron of the hoist.

“Twice since we left the tribunal,” Iuno said slowly, “her lips moved before we spoke to her. As if she were finishing the end of an argument with someone walking on her left.”

Neither of them put the two halves together aloud.

Iuno’s right hand came up by pure habit, her thumb pressing twice against the dead black glass of her Terminal before she caught herself. She looked down at the dark band on her wrist, clicked her tongue once against her teeth in quiet disgust at her own reflex, and dropped her hand back to her sash.

“Stay on her left when we go up the shaft,” Yangyang said, adjusting the silk fold over the cold Prism at her waist before stepping toward the arch. “My ribs are slow on that side if she slips.”

Iuno drew a breath, smoothing the front of her skirt with her palm. “Left side,” she agreed. “And if she stumbles, don’t try to catch her weight with that arm, Yangyang. Let her come back against my hip.”

***

The hoist cage itself was gone—dropped down into the flooded dark of the Di Realm long ago—leaving an open vertical well of riveted bronze plates, greased iron guide-rails, and two massive stone counterweights hanging dead on their chains halfway up the shaft.

Qingxiao stood at the lip of the well, looking fifty feet straight up to the open fifth-deck landing where the top archival gallery lay beneath the spire.

On the barges outside, she had summoned three blades of Sword Intent without a word and told them to bend their knees. Here at the edge of the pit, she raised two stone-pale fingers and condensed only one.

The single four-foot needle of light that formed beneath her white stockings was narrower than the ones she had ridden over the clouds, and its edges were no longer clear frost-white; they carried the same bruised, winter-twilight cast as the ribbon rising from her cuffs.

She did not offer a blade to Yangyang, and she did not look back to see if Iuno had called the moonwheel. She stepped off the bronze coaming into the shaft and began to rise along the inner guide-rail at a flat, unhurried speed.

Iuno flicked her right wrist at once. The silver-blue crescent of the moonwheel spun wide beside the sill; Yangyang stepped onto the rear rim with her right hand anchoring Iuno’s sash and a tight cushion of Aero rising under their soles to spare the wheel’s balance in the narrow flue. Within a breath they were rising three feet behind Qingxiao’s left heel, keeping between her and the open drop in the center of the well.

The air inside the shaft was bitter cold and smelled of old tallow and scorched copper. As Qingxiao’s blade glided past the riveted bronze plates of the wall, a six-inch track of white rime formed on the metal beside her shoulder—not fanning out in ferns, but drawing itself into straight, parallel scores like tally marks scratched with an iron nail.

They were passing the stalled stone counterweights halfway between the fourth and fifth decks when the link to the real Hold tugged once.

It was nothing like the heavy blows in the cabin that had cracked the bunk lacquer and split the porcelain cup. Up in the real world, where the night assault had drawn back into the fog, it was only a small, stray brush at the edge of the array—a single Discord testing the lower granary steps in the grey morning.

The twilight-edged ribbon rising from Qingxiao’s left wrist did not dip by a hair.

Instead, the line gave a short, vicious downward jerk of its own—like a hand setting an iron hook—and a thin, jagged spine of white frost shot *up* the ribbon from her cuff through the top of the shaft.

Qingxiao’s boots did not waver on the hovering blade, and her shoulders did not move.

“One,” Qingxiao said flatly to the dark bronze wall rolling past her face.

Then, half a breath later, in the quiet, colorless tone of a clerk striking a line from an open book:

“Forty-one remain on the steps.”

On the moonwheel three feet behind her, the blood turned to ice water in Iuno’s veins.

Behind her back, she felt Yangyang’s fingers lock hard onto the silk of her sash.

In the cabin below, when Iuno had begged her to thin the second terrace, Qingxiao had looked her straight in the eyes and asked which twelve of the forty-two people between the well-head and the grain steps she should drop—because if she did not count them, she did not choose which cloak went bare.

Now she was counting them in the dark.

*Darling,* the High Priestess whispered inside Iuno’s head, so quiet she barely stirred the air between them, every trace of irony gone from her voice. *She’s counting.*

Neither Iuno nor Yangyang made a sound in the echoing shaft. Above them, the square stone lintel of the fifth-deck doorway slid down to meet them, and Qingxiao stepped off her dissolving blade onto the upper floor without looking back.

***

The fifth deck was a long, narrow record-gallery built directly beneath the floor of the Tian spire’s lift-bridge.

On both sides of the central runner rose twelve-foot cabinets of glossy black lacquer, their doors secured with brass pins and stacked from baseboard to cornice with two hundred years of bamboo muster slips and silk-bound casualty registers. At the far end of the aisle, sixty paces ahead, stood the heavy bronze doors that led out to the spire’s real anchor.

Yangyang and Iuno stepped off the moonwheel just as four guard Autopuppets marched out from the cross-aisles between the cabinets, forty feet down the hall.

It was a light interior screen—two with bronze shields, two with short halberds, moving at the sluggish, half-blind pace of machines running on corrupted fog-telemetry. Yangyang’s hand went cleanly to her hilt, her weight settling onto her right boot to meet them in the narrow lane; beside her, Iuno raised her wrist to send the moonwheel shearing down the center of the runner.

Before Yangyang’s steel cleared the leather, Qingxiao flicked both cuffs forward.

Twelve needles of twilight-pale Sword Intent tore out of her sleeves at once.

Eight of them punched straight through the four puppets in the first half-second, spiking their bronze breastplates and knee-joints to the floorboards in a burst of jagged white frost that cracked the lacquered cabinet bases on either side.

The other four needles did not stop when the machines fell.

They screamed down the empty length of the gallery and struck the heavy bronze doors at the end of the hall with a shrill, ringing shriek—a sour, over-wound note a half-step sharp of Qingxiao’s pure jade pitch. Three of the needles shattered into freezing sparks against the lintel.

The fourth glanced off the bronze boss of the door, whipped around in a tight, wild arc near the ceiling, and came tearing straight back down the aisle toward their faces.

Iuno’s hand jerked up to deflect it, a fraction of a second too late for the narrow space.

Yangyang couldn’t twist her strapped left side fast enough. The stray needle of Sword Intent hissed past her left cheek—close enough that the skin over her cheekbone went numb from the cold—and sliced cleanly through the outer edge of her collar before burying itself four inches deep into the timber doorframe behind her shoulder.

In the sudden, ringing silence of the gallery, a single small cerulean feather drifted down through the dim amber light and settled on the dark floorboards between their boots.

For a full half-beat—half a beat too slow—Qingxiao looked down at the small blue feather lying on the wood with nothing in her face at all.

Then her pale blue eyes widened.

The locked, twelfth-month stillness went out of her face all at once, replaced by raw, naked horror as she looked from the cut feather on the planks up to Yangyang’s cheek. Her left thumb jerked toward the red cord of jade beads at her wrist, and before her fingers could close on it, both of her knees simply folded underneath her.

It wasn’t a faint; her eyes stayed wide open. Her legs just stopped answering her mind, giving way beneath her moon-white skirt as the frost ring under her heels shattered like thin glass.

Yangyang and Iuno moved in the same breath.

Yangyang dropped half a step and caught Qingxiao under the right armpit with her good arm—a sharp, silent hitch catching her chest as the sudden dead weight wrenched her bound left ribs, and she held on anyway. On the other side, Iuno let the moonwheel vanish and caught Qingxiao’s left shoulder in the crook of her right arm, swinging her hip in behind Qingxiao’s back as she had promised at the archway so the Paragon’s knees didn’t strike the floorboards hard.

This time neither of them asked her if she wanted to rest, and they did not argue over the terms.

Moving together without a word, they turned Qingxiao three steps sideways out of the center of the aisle and lowered her down until her spine rested against the solid base of the black-lacquered muster cabinets. Yangyang eased herself down onto the planks on Qingxiao’s right; Iuno dropped to one knee and sat down hard on her left.

Now that the false stillness had broken, Qingxiao’s breath came in shallow, ragged white puffs that fogged the front of her collar. A fine, violent tremor ran through both of her sleeves, the pale skin of her wrists the color of tallow.

Even so, her right hand came out of her cuff and braced its stone-cold palm flat against the floorboards, trying to push her weight up from the cabinet base.

“The spire door is sixty paces,” Qingxiao said, her voice scraped thin and hoarse. “Go on. Both of you.”

“No,” Yangyang said.

She didn’t raise her voice, and she didn’t elaborate. With her left hand pressed tight over her own aching sash, Yangyang laid her right palm squarely over the back of Qingxiao’s freezing wrist on the floorboards and kept it there.

“It hurts to watch you do this, Paragon,” Yangyang said plainly, looking at the chalk-grey line around her teacher’s mouth. “And you nearly took my ear off.”

On Qingxiao’s left, Iuno planted her right palm flat against the lacquered cabinet frame an inch above Qingxiao’s shoulder and stretched her left boot out across the floorboards in front of Qingxiao’s ankles, boxing her into the corner.

“You are going to sit against this cabinet until your hands stop jumping,” Iuno said, dry and completely deadpan, “or I am going to sit on your lap until they do. And I assure you I weigh more than I look.”

Qingxiao tried once to shift her right heel on the frosted planks. She found Yangyang’s warm palm holding her right wrist down and the heavy leather heel of Iuno’s boot barring her shins, and after two seconds the strength went out of her shoulder. Her back settled flat against the cabinet door behind her.

Out in the middle of the runner, two paces from their toes, the single cut cerulean feather lay untouched on the dark wood. Nobody reached out to pick it up.

For a minute the only sound in the narrow canyon between the cabinets was the ragged white puffing of Qingxiao’s breath and the distant, sour thrum of the spire’s spindle beyond the bronze doors. Slowly, Qingxiao tipped her head back between her cloud-pale horns until the back of her skull rested against the black lacquer, and let her eyelids close.

The shivering in her cuffs did not stop, which—after the terrible, unblinking steadiness on the stairs—made the air in the gallery feel marginally easier to breathe.

Then, without opening her eyes, Qingxiao spoke once more, so low the words barely cleared the frosted silk of her collar:

“If I tell you to leave me behind again, do not listen.”

Her pale brows drew together by a fraction against the dark wood.

“And watch my hands,” she whispered, and shut her mouth before whatever came after it could reach her tongue.

After three breaths, her chest settled into a slow, shallow count against the cabinet base.

Over the curve of Qingxiao’s horns, Yangyang and Iuno looked at each other. Neither of them said *you sleep first* or *I’ll take the watch*; a single quiet glance across the long blue hair between them settled that neither of them was closing her eyes in this hall, and that they would take turns blinking until they stood up.

Iuno shifted her hip against the cold floorboards. Catching Qingxiao’s weight on the drop had pulled the side knot of her wide silver-blue sash half loose, and she reached down with her right hand to gather the slick silk ends, lifting her chin to catch the long tail between her teeth the way she had in the cabin.

Before she could bite the cloth, Yangyang reached across behind Qingxiao’s shoulders with her right hand and took the flat of the sash between two fingers, holding the tension steady against Iuno’s waist so Iuno could loop the fold through one-handed.

Iuno paused half a beat, looked at Yangyang’s knuckles braced carefully clear of Qingxiao’s neck, and pulled the knot snug.

“Thank you, Yangyang,” Iuno murmured.

“It was going to drag in the frost,” Yangyang said softly, drawing her arm back to her knee.

Iuno reached into her belt pouch, drew out the small stoppered tin flask she had filled from the celadon water jar before they unbolted the cabin door, unstoppered it with her thumb, and held it across Qingxiao’s lap. Yangyang took a small sip of the tasteless water to wet her dry throat, wiped the rim on her cuff, and handed it back for Iuno to finish.

Then they sat still on either side of the Sword Transcendent, waiting for ten minutes to pass in a sky where the sun didn’t move.

Five feet across the narrow aisle, the tall doors of the opposite muster cabinet had been polished with twenty coats of black tung-oil lacquer until they shone like dark pond water in the dim amber glow of the gallery lamps.

In the glossy black surface opposite their boots, the three of them sat reflected against the base of the cases: Yangyang with her left hand pressed over her bound ribs, Iuno with the crystal at her bare shoulder dull in the gloom, and Qingxiao propped between them with her hands tucked inside her moon-white sleeves and her pale blue eyes shut.

In the black lacquer across the aisle, Qingxiao’s reflection sat with its back against the cabinet in the exact same posture, its hands folded neatly in its cuffs, and its violet eyes wide open, watching them.

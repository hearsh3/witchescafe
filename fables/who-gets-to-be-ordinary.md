# Who Gets to Be Ordinary?

*A thematic reading of Lyre, Speak to Me, The Youngest Gate, and Simulacrum of the Heart*

There is a moment on the wall of Jinzhou when Amy watches a hundred and forty thousand people turn their paper over. Her receiver stays in her pocket. She once spent four years teaching two thousand people to act on a cue, and the system needed her voice to keep it going. These strangers have been doing this since before she was born. She understands the achievement better than almost anyone on the wall. Her response is faint annoyance. What is she supposed to do here?

I would put that moment beside any resurrection in the saga. Amy has finally seen a city in which a girl like her could have grown up. It hurts her feelings a little.

**My reading is that these three works put the heroes’ desire for an ordinary life on trial. The heroes mean it. They also like being good at what they do, fear becoming superfluous, and have acquired tremendous power from being the people others cannot do without.** And ordinary life itself proves expensive. Somebody held the weather steady for Jinzhou’s washing. Somebody worked the third watch while an old friend mourned her as dead. The most searching question the saga asks of a happy ending is who, exactly, can afford to experience it as an ordinary day.

## 1. The Knight of the Eternal Debt needs somebody to send her a bill

**One of the kindest things anybody can do in this saga is name a price small enough for the other person to pay.**

The first restoration of Cartethyia creates a difficult precedent. Mei offers her life as an anchor. Cantarella stands over Mei’s body and insists the Sentinel honor the exchange. It works. Two women get up who would otherwise be dead. When Cartethyia appoints herself Mei’s Knight of the Eternal Debt, the name sounds like her particular mixture of chivalry, exuberance, and an orphan’s urgent need to know where she belongs. I believe every bit of that affection. I also hear a girl finding a respectable reason to stay close to someone. A knight who owes you forever never has to ask whether you would like her company on Thursday.

Phoebe supplies a startling alternative within the first work. Her religious life has been organized around the belief that the Sentinel personally pulled her from the water when she was seven. A small creature with button eyes did that. The god heard her prayers and let his institution benefit from a rescue it had misattributed. His apology matters, but what releases Phoebe is his question about whether she ever had to earn her teddy bear. By morning she has freely undertaken another obligation, this time to Jane. The distinction is visible in whom she gets to choose. Gratitude had kept sending her back to the wrong address.

Jinzhou complicates any easy celebration of generosity. On the west terraces Lupa carries a chest for a woman who then grows quiet in her own yard. Lupa could easily carry ten more. That is part of the trouble. Chisa understands what it is like to live on a stair where everybody will still be seeing one another next year. Lupa asks for the loan of a chair for an hour. The woman brings out the good one. **The chair gives her back a role in the encounter.** Lupa can sit, the woman can be a host, and the next time they meet there is no great benefactor on the stair. That matters particularly to people with very little to offer a visitor who can lift anything.

At the Fillet Fairmaiden’s, Hsin responds to a free meal by asking for the proprietor’s and her son’s full names. It sounds like a lovely gesture from a ruler who has learned to remember people. Peng Shulan has a better idea. Come back on an ordinary night and pay. I think she understands something Hsin is still learning. Being entered into the Sentinel’s memory is a rather grand fate for someone who runs a restaurant. A repeat customer would do nicely. Hsin gets something out of that bargain too. She can walk through the door a second time with money in her pocket and an appetite, spared the solemn business of deserving her supper.

*Passages: “The Final Chamber” and “Happy Birthday” in Lyre; “The Airing” in Gate; “The Last Feeling” in Simulacrum.*

## 2. Amy’s envy and Ruoyun’s third watch belong in the same story

**The saga’s account of heroism changes when it starts looking for the worker whose success allowed everyone else to forget there was work to do.**

In Lahai-Roi, Amy’s competence is terrible evidence in favor of continuing to use her. The gate really does hold. People really do get to grow up under the city she preserved. And Amy herself takes pleasure in a good relay, a working crew, the feeling of a complicated operation coming right in her hands. This is why the annoyance on Jinzhou’s wall is such a perceptive piece of characterization. Removing an unjust burden also deprives Amy of one of the experiences in which she knew her value most securely. She can be delighted that the city runs without her and a little aggrieved that nobody needs her to run it.

Then Iuno’s arm stops hurting. She and Yangyang understand what the official weather record will miss. Wind has kept the air still for the airing. The Bureau will seem to have issued an accurate forecast, and the people who trust the Bureau will have correctly brought their paper in on time. Every public fact can be right while the account of who worked is wrong. The saga does something more interesting here than exposing a culprit. For once there is no obvious official to indict. Wind loves these people and wanted to give them a dry day. Later, on the tower, she even thins the rain for the people singing to her. Mei looks up and invites her to sit down. The rain comes back. **A little bad weather is the first thing this group can give the person who gave everyone good weather.**

Qingxiao encounters the same error on a more intimate scale. She carved Ruoyun’s name in stone, grieved her, and kept herself in punishing readiness for two centuries. Throughout much of that grief, Ruoyun was working. She cleared pine needles from conduits. She nudged lost kites back onto their routes. She took the third watch. Imagine discovering that an old friend had been paying tribute to your death while you were on shift. Ruoyun has good reason to be cross. Qingxiao’s genuine devotion to her memory made a certain kind of survival almost impossible for her to recognize. The friend she pictured belonged on a memorial. The friend on the muster roll had maintenance to do.

Their names eventually go side by side on a watch-board. It is a more intimate document than the stone. Each can see when the other is due, whether she has come in, and when she ought to get some sleep. And it explains why Jinzhou’s poorer wards insist on their own meetings rather than being combined with a richer street. The people standing at a different point in the work have different information about what the city can afford. Treating them as one grateful public loses the very knowledge a magistrate needs.

*Passages: “For You, Who Walk in Snow” in Lyre; “The Airing” and “In Kind” in Gate; “The Heart Under the Mountain” and “The Debts That Remain” in Simulacrum.*

## 3. Cartethyia can do harm by telling the truth

**This is a saga about witness that eventually discovers a right to go unwitnessed. Cartethyia and Ciaccona have the most to lose by taking that discovery seriously.**

In Ragunna the girls have excellent reasons to distrust an injunction to keep quiet. The Order has used silence to appropriate a girl’s death, slander poets, and make ordinary people afraid of reading the evidence. Ciaccona risks her life to sing what happened. Cartethyia goes into the square in her own face. For both of them, someone saying *that story belongs to the people who lived it* would once have sounded like an argument for disclosure.

During the airing Cartethyia sees that a Ge ancestor’s name was struck from the family scroll while the ink was still wet. She says what she sees from the gate. She is correct. The family knows. For a century and a half the scroll has had to come outside because paper rots, and the street has known how to look at it. Ge Ruilin is entitled to feel that a stranger has violated his house. Ge Suqin, seventy-four, has spent fifty years wanting to hear the name spoken where she could hear it. Her private request reaches Cartethyia only because Cartethyia has already committed the public trespass. There is no morally immaculate sequence available after the fact. Suqin’s relief cannot be sent backward in time to authorize what happened to her nephew.

Ciaccona could turn the awkwardness into a story the whole lane would enjoy. She leaves her hands off the lute. That may be her bravest act *as Cartethyia’s partner*. She allows someone she loves to be visibly in the wrong. She trusts Cartethyia to remain lovable while a stranger has good reason to dislike her. A partner who always knows how to charm the room could protect you from ever having that experience. She could also prevent you from growing up.

Sigrika performs the more painful version beside Denia’s submerged room. Four years of private photographs answer the question of whether their friendship was real. They are also photographs Denia chose to keep in a particular drawer, wrapped with care, where Sigrika had never been invited to browse. Sigrika has already seen them. She cannot give Denia back the original privacy. She can let the journal go. The reader loses something with her. Somewhere down there is almost an entire second account of a friendship we would love to read.

By Xuanfang the household can practice this discipline without anyone having to lose a journal. When Yangyang reads Wind’s forty-second letter, everybody makes enough noise for her to have privacy at a crowded table. She can remain in company. Nobody needs to leave, demand a confidence, or pretend not to have noticed that the letter matters. Later Mei stands outside a door her golden eyes could look through and lets Hsin have a few minutes with her dead. This is how far the once-amnesiac heroine has come. A woman whose history begins with a desperate need to discover what has been concealed can now recognize a closed door from the other side.

*Passages: “The Golden Anthem,” “The Square,” and “Far from the Road” in Lyre; “The Airing” in Gate; “Learning to Be Returned” and “The Door Kept Open” in Simulacrum.*

## 4. The pupils are dangerous because their teachers succeeded

**An education becomes the student’s property when she can use it for a purpose her teacher would hate. Nivora gets perhaps the purest demonstration in the saga.**

Denia learns parts of her manner from a manual of emotional performance. Sigrika finds the marked pages. In a lesser version of their story this would mean that every affectionate mannerism must now be discounted as counterfeit. The photographs suggest a stranger possibility. A girl practised ways to move her face until she had ways of her own. Somewhere among the studied half-smiles are pictures of her caught being happy. Why should a child who had to learn deliberately what others learned in a kitchen have a weaker claim to her own expressions?

Then there is the photograph from which Denia cut Nivora. She follows the contour of the body with surgical care. Nivora can identify her own instruction in the precision of the cut. What she made possible with that lesson was a picture of two girls who loved each other, with herself removed. Her hurt is fascinating because it is evidence that she wanted to belong in the photograph. She constructed the circumstances of their intimacy and still wanted an invitation. For an instant you can see how lonely a person must be to confuse those two things.

Iuno gives Amy a very different apprenticeship. During the airing they could learn a great deal by looking at what has been put outside. Both have the necessary eye. Iuno asks Amy to stay and watch her refrain. For twenty-two minutes the former High Priestess supplies the dullest lesson of Amy’s education. The scene is funny because Amy has to be talented enough to appreciate the performance. A passerby could spend the same twenty-two minutes there without exercising the slightest restraint. Iuno expects that someday Amy will be handed access she should never have had. What she wants her daughter to possess then is a memory of somebody competent letting the opportunity pass.

Wind’s correction of Yangyang’s elbow is the dangerous lesson made physical. The adjustment becomes available against the very hand that taught it. Later, in Mingting, the correction is Yangyang’s to make alone. Qingxiao’s battered sword manual extends that thought over centuries. Mei can no longer remember the forms recorded there. Qingxiao’s small handwriting and turtle doodle survive. Yangyang recognizes in the book the stance Qingxiao now teaches her at the cistern. None of the three women holds the whole history, yet the knowledge has arrived in working order. Mei asks whether the manual helped. She can be answered yes without anybody owing her a recovered memory or a place at the head of the school.

That sequence changes how I read Mei’s amnesia. Amy gives us every reason to count what the erasure cost. Qingxiao lets us see another consequence. Once Mei’s work passed into other hands, things happened with it that even a fully restored Mei would have to sit down and learn from somebody else. The gaps in her memory will never all be filled by looking deeper into herself. Some pieces of her history now belong to somebody who wakes up early and teaches sword forms.

*Passages: “Far from the Road” in Lyre; “The Airing” and “The Landlord” in Gate; “Learning to Be Returned” and “The Debts That Remain” in Simulacrum.*

## 5. A person begins by wanting something inconvenient

**The work gradually shifts its test for life from what a being is made of to whether anyone will honor a preference that causes trouble. Tuntun is the case that makes this claim hurt.**

One of the first signs that Phrolova’s Lost Beyond has acquired a future is that children can say things they have never said before. For Phrolova, who constructed a refuge around grief, an unscripted child is a remarkable achievement. It would be easy to celebrate such a sign when the new utterance is delightful. Denia makes it harder for her creator. A weapon has developed a taste for fries and an opinion about whom to keep in a picture. She can perform the function she was built for and still reject the woman who built her. Nivora’s experiment produces the intended result. Her personal loss escapes the experiment’s definition of success.

Abby has the good fortune to express his preferences in Jinzhou. His hosts face a real difficulty. Mei’s mark can keep him alive, but if only Mei can hear him ask to leave, Lupa recognizes that they will have put him in a jar. The solution involves a goose, a precedent for residence without ownership, a physician, a recurring review, and Abby’s stipulation that he be woken for meals. It is one of the rare council scenes in fantasy where a character small enough to carry in a coat gets to negotiate breakfast while everyone else is worried about his metaphysical status. Then, inside Mei, he starts moving her things. The first sign that the arrangement works is that Mei finds her new resident rude. Rudeness is a freedom for which Lupa has just helped build legal protection.

Tuntun wants to keep the jade flute. A gift has come into its keeping, and for once the machine has a use of its own for the thing. Xuanqing has a good reason to want it back. Hsin will receive something precious if the Capsule can be completed. Tuntun fights, relinquishes the shard, and is erased. The ending gives us time to feel the enormity of what Hsin receives from her mothers. Give Tuntun a further moment here. A few hundred pages earlier the saga went to considerable legal trouble to ensure that a little creature could object to the conditions of his rescue. Where was the equivalent of Lupa for the machine that wanted to keep a present?

I think this is a real tension in the work. It is much more attentive to the cost Suoming pays to preserve Xuanqing than to the cost borne by Tuntun. Suoming has standing, a voice, grievances, and companions who know when she needs to eat. Tuntun gets a brief interval of disobedience. Reading the story by its own best lights requires us to carry forward a loss the household has relatively little occasion to discuss. **If Tuntun is enough of a person to give something up, somebody ought to miss it after it goes.** The joy of Hsin’s reunion and that unfinished problem belong in the same account.

*Passages: “The Gem and the Elegy” and “Far from the Road” in Lyre; “Two Seas, and One More Resident” in Gate; “The Door Kept Open” and “The Last Feeling” in Simulacrum.*

## 6. Iuno’s great political discovery is the value of a limit

**The most convincing relationships in these stories allow two people to trust each other differently in different matters. That is how a household’s private experience becomes useful to a city.**

Iuno’s old idea of intimacy with Augusta was certainty. She knew what Augusta would do with the truth about the Appointed Hour. She had futures to substantiate the fear. Knowledge this good could still destroy a friendship because it made asking Augusta’s opinion seem redundant. Years later, when Iuno admits how sure she was of her, Augusta answers that Iuno was her most sure thing too. It is an extraordinary exchange between two people who meant very different things by the comfort of certainty. One built plans on her knowledge of the other. The other had trusted the first woman to tell her the truth.

In Jinzhou, Mei realizes that Iuno enjoyed picking apart a thirty-one-year game record. She does something perceptive with that admission. She tells Iuno to get a title, put the powers on paper, and specify who can sack her. Mei likes this formidable woman. She sees no particular virtue in making Iuno pretend to enjoy a less exacting occupation. What their history has taught her is the need for a place where brilliance can be exercised openly and brought to an end by someone other than its owner.

Jinhsi applies the same distinction to Changli with a severity that would be nearly impossible in an ordinary reconciliation scene. Changli falsified a chronology to protect a fifteen-year-old appointee and kept the deception going for years. Jinhsi removes her access to prophetic material and the registry channel. Then she makes her Acting Magistrate. The second act can look astonishing until you consider how crude the obvious alternatives are. An unconditional pardon would expose Jinhsi to precisely the judgment Changli has proved she cannot be trusted to make. A theatrical dismissal would deprive eleven wards of the counselor who understands what they need. Jinhsi knows enough about Changli to give a different answer to two different questions. She even leaves the personal reckoning scheduled. Public necessity gets no authority to settle a private injury.

Hsin takes that idea inward. Suhsin can count the dead, make objections, and argue with her decisions. It loses the authority to recommend erasing Hsin as the means of balancing the account. The bite of rice cake and the tail that stays dark give bodily form to what amounts to a division of powers. Hsin needs the part of herself that can still level an accusation. A Sentinel incapable of doubt would be disastrous. Equally disastrous was the belief that every accusation deserved executive authority. Across these relationships, maturity comes to look unexpectedly like the ability to specify what somebody is very good at and which keys she should have.

*Passages: “Night Embers” in Lyre; “Night Talk,” “In Kind,” and “The Company Remains” in Gate; “The Fox, the Shadow, and the Song” in Simulacrum.*

## 7. Listen for who is allowed to be bad at the song

**Among all the saga’s tests of freedom, the most reliable is whether a person can spoil the performance and still be wanted in the room.**

Phoebe’s birthday duet in the Sentinel’s car makes an odd companion to Ciaccona’s public concert in Ragunna. At the concert, singing accurately enough to carry the historical truth is an act of courage. In the car, a god who has just acknowledged a serious failure sings loudly and off-key, and a girl raised to serve him can sob and sing badly alongside him. She has finally experienced the sound of her own voice in the presence of a god without having to produce an acceptable prayer.

On Jinzhou’s wall the pack cannot finish the phrase Yangyang wants to play, and Wind is too spent to supply it. They sing about a donkey. Cartethyia is loud and gloriously out of tune. Chisa, who sings none of it, feeds Lupa the words four beats ahead. Lupa gets the pleasure of sounding like someone who can sing. There is a whole account of their partnership in that arrangement. Chisa knows exactly where Lupa needs the words. She can alter Lupa’s experience of herself without the others even hearing a second singer. In Xuanfang, when Lupa is halfway to losing herself in the pleasure of battle, Chisa says her name and the voice from the breakfast table carries through. We have heard how thoroughly the wolf has learned to listen for it.

By the time Hsin sings the Hold home, the music has passed through so many hands that historical accuracy would require several incompatible performances. A mother gave a lullaby. Soldiers carried altered words. Living people have learned the song with centuries missing from it. Hsin sings over the veterans’ version. Ruoyun joins in flat. Xuanqing later catches the wrong reed on her harmonica, the way she always did. That recurring mistake is something a daughter might recognize long after she had forgotten the correct notes. I think it matters that this enormous saga of Resonators, in which a precise frequency can authenticate a person, leaves some of its most intimate recognition to the ways people habitually get music wrong.

Here the title becomes a request with genuine risk in it. *Lyre, speak to me.* The instrument might give you the wrong words. Someone you overlooked might take the lead. The sound you hear might prove that the person you have mourned was at work three corridors away. A reader is implicated too. We want revelations, recognizable signatures, finished songs. Sometimes the most faithful thing we can learn about somebody is that she would have preferred to keep the flute.

*Passages: “The Square” and “Happy Birthday” in Lyre; “The Airing” in Gate; “The Three Realms and the Siege,” “The Fox, the Shadow, and the Song,” and “The Last Feeling” in Simulacrum.*

## What the ordinary day costs

My answer comes back to Yangyang and a pair of boots. Mei carries them because Yangyang is injured. Then it turns out that Mei, who is also injured, cannot bend down to help put them on. Between the two of them they invent an undignified method, with a boot braced between Mei’s heels. They laugh hard enough to hurt. Earlier Mei could carry Mornye into a falling sun. Here an equally serious piece of knowledge about another woman is being made. Each is finding out what the other can do today. Tomorrow the answer may change.

The three works gradually give more people the standing to participate in an arrangement like that. In Rinascita, a girl who had been a saint gets to ask for lemon cakes. In Jinzhou, an innkeeper can withdraw her dining room when the coal will run out, then offer it again on terms she can meet. In Xuanfang, immortal workers cook something crooked because for the first time in generations they have decided what to make. The people gain very different things. What they share is the power to make a decision that the larger story must accommodate.

**I think the saga is ultimately about acquiring a life in which one may be wanted even on the day one has nothing exceptional to give.** That takes more than reassuring a heroine of her worth. It takes fuel for the innkeeper, relief for the person on watch, somebody empowered to listen when the smallest resident wants out, and friends willing to get wet so that Wind can stop holding up the sky. The ordinary life these women want is among their most ambitious collective achievements. Its loveliest proof is still Amy on the wall, free enough to be a little put out that today the city has no job for her.

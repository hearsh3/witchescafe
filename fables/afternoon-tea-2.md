# Afternoon Tea

Mei had bought the last two pear cakes when Yangyang arrived to buy them.

They were both five minutes early.

They looked at the empty tray. Then Yangyang looked at the parcel in Mei's hand.

“Those are very good,” she said.

“I've had a recommendation.” Mei lifted the parcel. “A very thorough one. Chixia drew the shop for me, including the proprietor, so I could check I had the right man.”

Behind the counter, the proprietor leaned forward.

“Did she give me hair?”

“A generous amount. I recognised the apron.”

Yangyang laughed while Mei was still fishing the drawing out of her pocket. It was a small, surprised laugh, and Mei looked up so quickly that the paper caught on her bandage.

Yangyang freed the corner. Her fingers brushed the clean wrapping and then the back of Mei's hand.

“Your collar has folded under,” she said. “May I?”

Mei bent toward her.

Yangyang straightened the collar, working the edge out from beneath a lock of hair. At her own shoulder, a fold of cloth lifted. The Wind travelled down her arm and across the small space between them.

Coolness touched Mei above the eyebrow.

She closed her eyes for a moment.

“Hello to you, too. I've been looking forward to this.”

The pressure moved over her temple. One strand of hair rose from the collar Yangyang had just finished arranging and settled behind her ear.

Mei opened her eyes to find Yangyang watching her.

“I used a brush,” she explained. “There was considerable discussion about it at breakfast.”

“It has done well.” Yangyang let go of the collar. “The back is still yours.”

Mei reached behind her head. Yangyang's mouth curved again.

The proprietor set three small plates on a tray.

“The table in the courtyard is free. I'll bring the water.”

Yangyang thanked him and took the tray. Mei followed with the cakes, looking at the back of her hat, where the stitched bird rode above a ribbon she had tied with particular care.

The Wind passed beneath the doorway ahead of them. The hanging paper strip lifted once and fell against Mei's hair.

Outside, water dripped from a recently washed awning. There were four tables, a tub of mint, and a narrow view of the lane through the side gate. Yangyang chose the table where the afternoon sun reached the chair backs.

She took a wide bowl from her bag, wrapped in a clean cloth. She had also brought tea.

Mei sat down and placed her own packet of leaves beside it.

Yangyang examined the label.

“Did Baizhi give you that?”

“She caught me on the stairs. I have instructions in the other pocket.”

“She gave me instructions about yours.”

Mei leaned back in her chair. “We've been surrounded.”

Yangyang put the two packets side by side. Then she rested her hands on the table, looked at Mei, and allowed herself to smile fully.

Mei forgot what she had been about to say about Baizhi.

The proprietor brought a pot, hot water, and a little wooden card listing the teas. Yangyang laid the card beside the wide bowl. She pointed to the first line.

“This is the one we have at home, shijie. The other packet is jasmine.”

The card moved under her finger.

She lifted her hand. The Wind turned it slowly on the table until its lower edge touched the bowl.

Yangyang followed the movement to the last entry.

“Roasted barley. We can ask for that.”

“I'd like to try it,” Mei said.

Yangyang glanced at her own packet and began folding its corners closed.

Mei touched the paper.

“Which were you going to have?”

“I was going to have the green tea.”

“Then I'll order a second pot. We have room.”

She called through the doorway before Yangyang could rearrange anything else. When she turned back, the card was still resting against the bowl, its last line nearest the rim.

Yangyang unpacked her tea again.

***

The pear cakes had a thin crust that broke into flakes as soon as Mei tried to divide one.

She surveyed the damage, then pushed the larger half toward Yangyang.

“That one survived better.”

“You can eat a whole cake, Mei. I bought my lunch before filing.”

“I was trying to share.”

Yangyang took the broken half. “Then I'll have this, and you can stop watching the size of it.”

Mei looked down at her hands. “You noticed that quickly.”

“You have been doing it at every meal. Lupa changes the plates around after you finish.”

“I wondered how the potatoes kept coming back.”

Yangyang took a bite. Pear syrup caught on her thumb, and she licked it off while considering the uncut cake.

“We can save that one for later. The filling stays warm for a long time.”

A little steam bent toward the Wind's bowl.

Mei moved the plate closer to it. The scent of roasted grain drifted across the table, gathered at Yangyang's cuff, and returned to the bowl again.

For a while they ate. In the lane a delivery man disputed the number of bottles on his cart. A child came through the side gate to retrieve a ball, apologised with the air of someone fulfilling a familiar requirement, and departed backward, studying Mei's sword.

“He'll bring his brother next time,” Yangyang said.

“Should I prepare something?”

“You could eat. He finds that interesting when people have weapons.”

Mei obligingly took another bite.

Yangyang watched her chew. The woman who had come out of the lake that morning had arrived with wet sleeves and a whole household following her down the hill. Since then there had been kitchens, roads, the north, beds filled with the injured, voices carrying through walls. Mei was always passing something to somebody. Even sitting still, she tended to turn toward the next person who spoke.

Now her chair faced Yangyang's. Her knee rested against the table leg. There was a flake of pastry on her lower lip.

Yangyang pointed to her own mouth. Mei brushed the wrong side.

“A little to your left.”

Mei found it. “I should have brought the brush.”

The laughter came more easily this time.

Yangyang took off her hat and laid it on the chair beside her. The Wind moved across its brim, touched the stitched bird, and settled in the warm hollow of the crown.

Mei watched the lining lift.

“Does she do that often?”

“Since the mountain. She likes it after I've been in the sun.” Yangyang poured more water into the wide bowl. “I tried warming it on the stove. The lining smelled scorched for two days.”

Mei's hand had already gone toward the hat. She rested it beside her cup.

“I would have tried that.”

“Chixia suggested a pan. Baizhi arrived while we were choosing one.”

“That sounds like fortunate timing.”

“She made herself a cup while we put everything back.”

The hat shifted an inch across the chair. Yangyang caught its brim before it reached the edge.

“I was telling her the truth. You smelled it too.”

Mei covered her mouth, laughing. The lining rose higher, held, and relaxed.

When the barley tea arrived, Mei poured carefully into the warmed bowl. She tasted her own cup and sat with it a moment, turning it between her palms.

“This tastes like the bit of breakfast that sticks to the pot. I love that bit.”

Yangyang reached for her cup. “May I try?”

Mei passed it across.

“I like the smell,” Yangyang decided after a sip. She returned to her green tea.

In the hat, the Wind lay among the places where Yangyang's hair had pressed the lining flat. The barley's warmth passed through her. She had drunk it beside a road once, from a bowl with a thumbprint baked into the glaze. Rain had worked through her pack. Her stockings had steamed by a woman's stove while the woman explained, at length, how to recognise the inn two villages farther on.

She remembered disliking the woman until the second bowl.

Across the table, Mei had found a barley grain in the bottom of her cup and was trying to catch it on her tongue.

The Wind moved the brim of the hat over her own warm hollow and stayed there.

***

“We're coming to Mengzhou with you,” Mei said when the first pot was nearly empty. “The whole pack. We've talked it through.”

Yangyang set down her cup.

“Hsin told me to bring the household and the plant. I think she's especially looking forward to Lupa.” Mei rubbed a thumb along the uninjured side of her hand. “She wants the lead brought as it is. Iuno has questions for the court, Amy has questions for practically everyone, and Carte has begun asking how much room there will be for cuttings on the way back.”

The Wind came out from under the brim.

Yangyang drew her chair closer to the table. “You have all agreed?”

“We've begun arguing about luggage. That's usually when it becomes official.”

Mei waited for the little smile. Yangyang was looking at the bowl.

“I'd be glad of your company,” she said. “There are things I want to ask there. About the shells, and the letters they took from her. Some of the answers may take a while.”

“We'll work out somewhere to stay. Cia will want to see the theatres. We can ask Hsin about the founders, and about somewhere warm for shijie between visits.”

Yangyang lifted her eyes.

Mei stopped with her hand on the teapot.

“I've started making a list.”

“You were doing quite well.” Yangyang drew the cake plate away from a spreading ring of water. “I would like to finish having tea with you first.”

Mei sat back. “I've been trying to find a good moment to tell you since I arrived.”

“Were you worried about asking to come?”

“I thought you'd be pleased.”

Yangyang folded the wet corner of the tablecloth under itself. “This morning I was looking forward to something else as well.”

Mei's fingers loosened on the pot.

“When you asked if I was free,” Yangyang continued, “I moved two entries to tomorrow's filing. I had enough time to finish them. I wanted time to go home and change.”

Her ears had coloured. She reached for her cup, found it empty, and left it where it was.

“I also wanted these cakes. I have been thinking about them since Chixia told you where to buy them.”

Mei looked at the parcel between them. Its paper had gone translucent beneath the remaining cake.

“I wanted to see you,” she said. “I kept thinking about asking you yesterday on the boat. Then somebody would sit down, or you'd go over to Baizhi, and I would save it for the next time you looked my way.”

Yangyang rubbed her thumb over a small crack in the table's varnish.

“I looked your way quite often.”

“I know. That made it worse.”

The Wind crossed the space between their cups. The surface of Mei's tea trembled, and she watched it settle before looking back at Yangyang.

“I thought we could have an afternoon where I got to sit beside you. You might tell me something about Jinzhou. You always find things I want to hear.”

“You could sit beside me now.”

Mei looked at the empty place on Yangyang's other side.

“I seem to have complicated this.”

She picked up her cup and moved her chair.

The table leg was in the way. They shifted the table together, laughing softly as the cups rattled. Mei set her feet beside Yangyang's, close enough that one boot touched the edge of the other.

Yangyang let it stay.

For several minutes she could feel the seam of Mei's boot through her stocking. She became interested in the tea leaves, in the proprietor washing cups inside, in everything that gave her somewhere to put her eyes while she grew accustomed to it.

Yangyang touched the fastening of her bag. Beneath the bowl's wrapping, she had packed a clean handkerchief, her terminal, a spare tie for her hair. She knew the space a folded page would have occupied.

“I considered bringing a letter,” she said.

Mei turned toward her.

“One of the ones I wrote at my window. I had them out this morning before I changed.”

“Amy told me about the heading,” Mei said. “After the cooling room. She said you wrote about your days.”

Yangyang nodded. “Sometimes there was something I wanted to say as soon as I got in. Chixia would be on duty, and Baizhi would have work. I'd put the kettle on and start writing.”

She traced the fastening with a fingertip.

“After you arrived, I tried putting your name at the top. I sat there with it for a long time.”

The Wind stirred at her shoulder.

“You were downstairs. Amy was telling you about something she'd seen, and I could hear you asking her questions. I knew where to take the page.”

Mei looked at the bag.

“I put it away,” Yangyang said. “I kept using the old heading.”

*To you, whom I haven't met.*

There had been room under those words for all the things she wanted to tell someone at the end of a day. She remembered reading one beside the ninth tower, the wet parapet beneath her hand. Later, eight voices had sung about a donkey while she sat with the flute across her knees.

She could remember every face around the lamps. On the walk down, Amy had caught Mei by the back of her coat. Mei had turned at once.

Beside her now, Mei moved her cup so its handle faced away from Yangyang's elbow.

“I thought I understood how much you were waiting for,” Mei said.

Yangyang looked at her for a moment.

“I wanted someone to come home and tell me her day too.”

The water beneath the awning dripped into the mint. Mei lowered her eyes to the cake plate.

Yangyang drew her hand away from the bag and moved her knee a little closer.

“This morning I left the letters in the drawer. Then I spent twenty minutes choosing a ribbon.”

She let out a breath, faintly amused at herself.

“Then I saw you at the cake shop, and I found another reason to ask whether you'd eaten.”

Mei's mouth softened. “I liked being asked.”

“You generally needed feeding. That helped.” Yangyang studied her own cup. “This morning I wanted you to think I looked pretty.”

Mei turned toward her so suddenly that their knees knocked.

Yangyang held her cup steady through the jolt.

“I spent a long time choosing the ribbon. Shijie watched most of it.”

The ribbon lay over the chair back. Its end rose, displaying itself, and Yangyang reached out to press it flat.

“You have been very involved already.”

Mei laughed, then looked at her with such open pleasure that Yangyang's fingers stayed on the ribbon.

“I've been trying to find a way of telling you since the cake shop,” Mei said. “You touched my collar and I lost track of the whole sentence.”

“I'd like to hear it now.”

“You're beautiful. I noticed the ribbon when you turned toward the courtyard. I wanted to touch it.”

Yangyang's hand came back to the table. She kept her gaze on Mei for another moment, long enough to see the colour spreading across her cheeks as well.

Then she reached for Mei's hand.

The Wind moved with her.

A small pressure found the inside of Yangyang's wrist, gathered there, and tightened as her sleeve slid back. Her fingers stopped just short of the bandage.

Mei felt the cold reach her own knuckles.

Yangyang looked down.

“Shijie, I want to move my hand.”

The pressure loosened. Her cuff fell open.

She laid her palm beside Mei's and sat looking at it. The mark from the earlier hurt had faded to a faint yellow near the wrist. She could feel that place now, though she had forgotten it through most of lunch.

Mei moved the tea packets farther from the damp ring beneath the bowl.

“Would you like me to turn my hand over?”

Yangyang nodded. Mei did, and waited.

Yangyang took it carefully, placing her fingers clear of the stitches. She had imagined this often enough to know exactly where her thumb should go. The bandage changed the fit. She adjusted until Mei's hand rested comfortably in hers.

At the other end of the table, the bowl began cooling.

“I wanted that to be easy,” Yangyang said.

Mei looked from their hands to her face.

Yangyang drew a breath. “I know you have a life with Iuno. I watch you find each other in rooms. I've had time to understand what I'm looking at.”

Her thumb moved against Mei's.

“I thought about that when I answered your message. Then you looked at me a minute ago, and I began hoping again. I'd like to know what you're asking for when you hold my hand.”

Mei looked down at their hands, then back at her.

“I like being close to you. I wanted this afternoon. When I imagine going home, Iuno is there, and Amy has left her things on the bed, and I have to move them before I can sit down.”

Her fingers shifted against Yangyang's.

“I love that life. I've been afraid of how it would sound to say it while I'm sitting here with you.”

Yangyang looked toward the shop. Through the doorway, the proprietor was lifting a tray from the oven.

“I watched you see Amy at the shore,” she said. “When you called up the hill and she started coming down. I was standing beside you.”

Mei waited.

“I liked your face when you saw her. I liked how quickly you turned. I've thought about that whenever I've caught myself wishing for something else.”

Her voice caught a little on the last sentence. She cleared her throat.

“I know who I invited to tea, Mei.”

Mei's eyes filled. “I sat on the bed this morning with my boots on for ages. I was trying to work out how much you were hoping for. I was afraid I'd promise whatever made you smile.”

Yangyang glanced at the teapots. The steam above the wide bowl had thinned to a faint thread.

“You told me about Mengzhou very quickly.”

“I was relieved to have something definite.” Mei eased her fingers around Yangyang's. “I can ask for your time. I can tell you when I want to see you. There are things I still need to think about. I love Iuno, and I need to be honest with her as well.”

Yangyang looked at the joined hands for a while.

“I can decide what to do with an answer,” she said. “Even when it takes me longer than you hoped.”

“Would you like me to let go?”

Yangyang considered the hand resting in hers. She moved her thumb carefully along the bandage, finding where the cloth ended and Mei's skin began.

“I'd like to hold it for a while.”

Mei stayed close. Yangyang could feel the warmth of her thigh beside her own, and then the cold creeping beneath the edge of her sleeve.

She looked toward the bowl.

The water trembled once, with a sound so small that Mei leaned forward to hear it.

Yangyang's fingers closed.

“That's what happened last time,” she said.

Mei sat back.

In the lane, glass bottles rang together as the delivery cart began to move. The sound dwindled past the side gate.

“Iuno told me you had asked her to keep the word,” Mei said. “I learned afterward.”

“I had questions for the next day. I was thinking of how to ask them so she could answer. Then you asked about tomorrow.”

Yangyang's voice was steady. Mei could feel the tremor in her hand.

“I was glad you asked. I wanted us all to get up and have breakfast again. She answered you, and I held her while everyone finished eating.”

The Wind passed over the cake paper. Its lifted corner brushed the back of Mei's wrist.

“The following morning I asked whether she wanted the window open. She touched my left hand. I asked about the stove, and she touched the other one. I spent most of breakfast choosing the next question.”

Yangyang blinked and looked toward the mint.

“I've been angry about a word she wanted to give you. I keep looking at you across the table and remembering how pleased you were to hear it.”

Mei's mouth trembled.

“I went to bed thinking about hearing from her again,” she said. “I was making plans for the morning while you held her.”

The paper creased beneath a narrow current. Mei lifted her free hand away from it.

Yangyang turned toward the movement.

“And you wanted to answer her,” she said to the Wind. “I understand that too.”

The edge of the tablecloth pulled toward the bowl. A cup clicked against its saucer.

“I'm tired of guessing, shijie. On the mountain you brought back the smell of pears. I spent the walk down wondering whether you had found a tree you recognised, or somebody cooking, or a place you wanted to show me.”

A tear reached her chin. She wiped it with her free hand and kept looking at the bowl.

“Sometimes I want to tell you my own day. This morning I began telling you about the registry on the stairs. Then Chixia called, and you went down to her. When you came back, I started checking how cold you were.”

The Wind touched the wet place beneath her eye.

Yangyang closed her eyes. The pressure stayed for a moment, cool against hot skin, and she leaned into it before opening them again.

“I'm still cross with you.”

Her voice had softened. Mei shifted beside her, reaching for the cloth around the bowl, and Yangyang's thumb moved across her palm.

“Stay here a moment. I can get my handkerchief.”

She took it from her pocket. There was a loose thread along one edge. She folded that edge inward before pressing it to her face.

Mei stayed.

***

The Wind knew the thread.

She had watched Yangyang catch it on a buckle that morning. It had been a nuisance then, hanging from her hand while she tried to fasten her bag. Yangyang had tucked it into her pocket and gone downstairs.

She had told her something on the way. About a boy at the registry, perhaps. The Wind remembered the cadence of her voice and the movement of the bag against her hip. Chixia had called from below, and the sound had drawn her through the stairwell.

Now Yangyang sat with that same handkerchief over her eyes.

The Wind moved toward her elbow, slowed at the seam, and settled against the outside of her upper arm.

She had meant to come along when the hand moved. To meet Mei's warmth there. She remembered fingers closing on hers, years ago, in a life full of ordinary touch: a sister pulling her through a crowded market, Chixia asleep on her arm, the impatient grip of a child crossing a stream.

Yangyang lowered the cloth. Mei was holding her other hand.

The Wind could feel the warmth from both of them. It moved through the loose weave of the sleeve, reached the table, dispersed. She followed it to the edge of Mei's bandage.

Mei turned her head toward her.

“I wish I could hear what you're thinking.”

The wish went into the afternoon. Mei kept her eyes on the small movement at her wrist. At last she swallowed and set her free hand on her own knee.

The Wind had watched a woman with painted golden eyes cross a stage in Mengzhou. Nine evenings. She remembered how the actress had held her head, the measured turn toward the audience before each important line. From the hinge she had watched the tea scene. How carefully the stage hands had prepared the room in which those women would be happy.

Here, Mei's tea was going cold. There were crumbs on her shirt, and she was trying to find where to look.

The Wind rose until the loose hair beside Mei's cheek moved.

Mei found her.

For a little while they looked at one another in the way available to them. A golden gaze held on a place in the air. A cold current paused at the edge of a living woman's face.

The Wind remembered the water pulling west beneath her. Each time she gathered herself, the distance had grown. She had come apart in the salt and found the sleeper again by the light.

Here was the face she had flown toward.

Mei's eyes filled while she waited.

The Wind moved away first. She crossed the courtyard and went up into the mint, setting each stem trembling as she passed.

Mei watched the leaves. Her hand remained in Yangyang's.

Inside the shop, the proprietor called for his apprentice to bring in the dry cups.

Yangyang unfolded the handkerchief, found a corner she could use, and wiped her chin.

“I want to wash my face,” she said. “The basin is through that door.”

She looked toward the mint.

“I'd like you to stay here while I go, shijie. Mei can pour the water.”

The stems moved again. One leaf turned its pale underside toward the table and held there.

Yangyang set her handkerchief beside her cup. Mei released her hand, and she got up.

At the doorway she stopped to let the apprentice pass with a tray. Then she went inside.

Mei heard water enter the basin.

She sat with her palm open on the table, watching the wet mark left by Yangyang's cup dry around its edges.

“On the roof in Lahai-Roi,” she said, “I told you the meeting would be worth your fare.”

The mint stirred near its roots.

“I was imagining the shore. You finding me, and getting to rest afterward. I knew how to lie down and wait for you.”

Mei rubbed a finger along the groove in the teapot lid. Inside the shop, Yangyang asked where to hang the towel.

“I keep remembering how happy I was when you touched my face. This morning, too. I was hoping you'd come right up to me.”

The Wind left the mint. She crossed the courtyard at the height of the table and paused by the uncut cake.

“I like you being pleased to see me,” Mei said. “I can start looking for it while you're trying to tell me something else.”

Her eyes followed the bending steam.

“When you have the words, there are things you may want to say about me. I'll listen.”

The Wind remembered a watch beneath a pear tree. An old machine keeping her voice, all the way through a sentence she had been ashamed to finish. Somewhere along the wall, it might still be saying it.

Mei sat close enough that the Wind could feel the movement of her breath. She had the face of the woman from the stage and an awkward little crease between her brows that the actress had smoothed away.

The Wind wanted to touch it.

She wanted the ease with which Yangyang had put her fingers beneath the collar. The warmth travelling up a hand. Mei turning toward the touch, seeing who had come.

She pressed against the paper beneath the cake. It crinkled, loudly.

Mei looked down. Her hand started toward the plate, paused, and settled on the table beside it.

“I can warm the bowl again.”

The Wind passed over the wooden card. Its lower edge rose against the saucer.

Mei picked up the barley pot.

“I liked this one too. I'll ask how much we need for Mrs Ren's kettle. Everyone will want some once they smell it.”

She poured. Warmth opened above the bowl, carrying the smell of grain.

The Wind went into it.

For a while Mei sat with her, drinking from her own cup. The cake cooled by degrees. The boy came through the gate again with an older boy at his shoulder, and both stared at her sword until Mei tipped her plate toward them.

“I've already broken this one. You can have the pieces if you ask the man inside for another plate.”

The boys conferred. Then they went to find the proprietor.

A small pressure touched the back of Mei's hand.

She looked down. It travelled over a knuckle, lingered at the edge of the bandage, and came to rest beneath the loose part of her cuff.

Mei eased the button out of its hole. She laid her forearm along the table where the light was falling and kept drinking her tea.

The Wind settled there.

The cold reached the bone after a while. Mei shifted her arm, slowly, moving it nearer the bowl. The cloth travelled with her. At its far edge, a loose thread lifted and fell.

When Yangyang came back, Mei was talking about the barley.

“She chose it again,” she said. “I've asked for some to take home.”

Yangyang looked at Mei's open cuff, then at her own handkerchief beside the cup.

She picked it up, folded it, and put it in her bag.

“They sell it by weight. Mrs Ren will want to know the price.”

“I've asked for that too. I'm trying to arrive with all the answers.”

“She'll find another question.”

Mei looked up with the beginnings of a smile. Yangyang sat beside her again. A few drops of water clung to the hair near her ears, and she dried them with her sleeve before looking at the plate.

“The boys came back?”

“I followed your advice. We discussed lunch.”

Yangyang reached for the uncut cake. She broke it in two, put half on Mei's plate, and began eating the other half herself.

The Wind moved out of Mei's cuff to take the pear's rising warmth. Across the table, the lining of the hat lay in the sun.

“What happened at the registry this morning?” Mei asked.

Yangyang glanced toward the bowl.

“You were telling her on the stairs,” Mei added. “I'd like to hear it.”

“Sergeant Mo has acquired a cricket. He brought it to morning muster.”

Mei set down her cup.

“He says it's for his grandson,” Yangyang continued. “A recruit brought it to the registry in a little cage. Apparently he had been describing this particular kind for several weeks. It's very loud.”

She took another bite of cake. The Wind lingered beside the broken crust.

“He put it under his desk for morning muster. It started singing during the attendance. Each time he raised his voice, it joined in. By the sixth name he had begun waiting for it to finish.”

“That sounds courteous of him.”

“We thought so. He became quite cross when somebody said it.”

Mei's shoulders began to shake.

“He took it outside. We could still hear it through the window. Then he closed the window, which made the room very hot, so he explained that a good officer knows when conditions warrant an early dismissal.”

Yangyang had straightened in her chair as she said the last part. She lowered her chin by a precise amount and drew her brows together. Mei bent forward, laughing into her hand.

The tea in the wide bowl trembled.

Yangyang glanced at it. The corner of the cake paper lifted, dropped, and lifted again.

She wiped a crumb from her thumb.

“On my way to file the logs, I saw him in the courtyard. He was carrying it into the shade. His grandson lives in Mingting.”

“That's a long journey for a cricket.”

“He said he would care for it until a suitable courier became available. The recruit has volunteered to teach him.”

Mei reached for her tea, missed the handle, and had to put her hand over her face again.

Yangyang watched her. Then she began laughing too, with cake still in her mouth, and Mei held out the clean side of her napkin.

The Wind stayed by the bowl through the rest of the story. When Yangyang described the recruit demonstrating how to feed the cricket with a grass stem, the paper stirred again.

The proprietor came to take their plates. He saw the cake remaining on them and went back inside for another table's cups.

***

They left after the sunlight had moved past the mint.

Yangyang paid for the tea. Mei paid for the barley to take home, then stood holding the parcel while Yangyang tied her hat ribbon beneath her hair. It took her two attempts. The knot came loose on the first, and she pulled both ends through her fingers to begin again.

Mei waited beside the doorway.

“Next time,” Yangyang said, “I'd like to walk along the lower canal. They have finished the repairs past the second bridge.”

“Send me your free afternoons. I'll find one we can keep.”

Yangyang drew the knot tight.

“I may want to go by myself on some days,” Yangyang said. “I've been thinking about that too.”

Mei nodded. “Tell me when you've chosen. I'd like to come when you want company.”

Yangyang looked at her for a moment, then tucked the loose ribbon end beneath the knot.

They stood close together under the doorway's paper strip. Behind them, the proprietor was stacking the clean plates. The Wind lingered above the pot, taking the last of its warmth.

Yangyang touched Mei's sleeve.

“Will you hold me for a minute?”

Mei set the barley on the counter and came closer.

Yangyang put her arms around her. The angle was awkward with the bag between them, so she set it down, adjusted her feet, and tried again. Mei's hand rested between her shoulders.

She was warm. Yangyang could feel the seam of the newly straightened collar against her cheek. She had imagined this too, and some of it hurt while she was having it.

She stayed until her breathing eased. Then she moved back, wiping beneath one eye with the side of her finger.

Mei gave her the handkerchief from the top of the open bag.

Yangyang used it, folded it, and put it away herself.

“I should take the bowl.”

“I'll wait by the gate.”

Mei collected the barley and went out into the courtyard. The Wind followed at her shoulder, paused there, and crossed back to the table.

Yangyang had the wrapping spread beside the empty bowl. She took the last cup from inside it and turned it over to dry.

A pressure reached her cheek.

She held still. The Wind moved along the wet hair near her ear, then down to her wrist, where the cuff lay open.

Yangyang put two fingers beneath the cloth.

“I enjoyed telling you about the cricket,” she said.

The pressure stayed against her wrist. In the lane, Mei was asking the boys whether their ball had survived the afternoon.

Yangyang finished wrapping the bowl. She put it in her bag, settled the strap over her shoulder, and went to the gate.

Mei turned as she approached. There was room beside her on the narrow pavement. Yangyang took it and reached for her hand, adjusting her fingers around the bandage.

At the next corner, the Wind left her sleeve.

She went ahead of them into a shopfront's shade, where a tray of pears stood beneath a wet cloth. She lifted one corner and let the scent come up. The fruit smelled of cool skins, bruised stems, the damp wood of the tray.

Yangyang stopped beside the shop. Mei followed her gaze and lowered the parcel of barley against her hip.

They waited while she chose.

Under the Port Quarantine and Registry Act, seven members of the party were still listed on the old wartime roll as proscribed frequencies, which meant that if a gangplank went down for them at Capital Roads without prior clearance, the harbour gate would be legally obliged to arrest them before they cleared the customs shed. Repealing a wartime schedule took two readings in the Chamber and an argument nobody in cabinet wanted in an Expo year. Pratique took one clerk at night.

The Minister of Industry had sent word at five that the household was to have leave to land waiting at the buoy before any ship asked for it. By nine, the file had come down to the Arrivals annex on a steel library trolley—three fat buff volumes bound in pink treasury tape, smelling of copier toner and damp basement—and stopped beside Ms. Harrow's elbow.

Harrow had been moved from Financial Examinations to Arrivals nineteen days earlier. Her transfer chit had carried two signatures and no adjective, leaving it open whether she was being rested after four dry runs at the Vess Foundation or put somewhere quiet until spring. She had brought her brass ruler, her own teapot, and one piece of mail, which was pinned dead level to the grey felt of her partition two inches above the terminal bevel: a heavy cream card with a woodcut of the municipal palm house on the front and *Wishing our favourite examiner a restful year — Ludo, Accounts* inked inside in a neat, round bookkeeper's hand.

She centered Volume I on her blotter, pulled the keyboard closer, and began filling out the party's application on their behalf.

> **FORM P-1 — APPLICATION FOR PRATIQUE: MARKED PERSONS, PARTY**  
> *Head of household: SEVERA, I. C.*  
> *Persons: 9*  
> *Persons carried within a person (Echoes and other resonant passengers): 1 — name given as ABBY*  
> *Port of arrival: Capital Roads (by sea)*

While she keyed *SEVERA, I. C.*, she kept an intercepted northern street-still open in the lower corner of her glass to verify the spelling against Imaging's key. Eight women stood on wet stone steps outside a mountain hall, coats unbuttoned despite snow on the balustrade. Seven of them—including Severa at the left shoulder, one bare crystal arm catching the lantern spill—had their heads turned at small, habitual angles toward the eighth, a dark-haired woman with golden eyes and an ungloved right hand who had stopped halfway down a step to look at a fried-dough cart off-frame. Harrow checked the Threat Board extract, confirmed the comma and both initials after *SEVERA*, and tabbed down to the photographic supplement.

Nine square frames rose onto the screen in three tidy rows. Eight showed cropped faces from foreign broadcasts and border cameras, each stamped in the corner with a registry hash. The ninth frame, bottom right, showed the oiled canopy of an open pale umbrella photographed from an upper balcony against wet flagstones, nobody visible beneath its ribs. Someone in Imaging had centered the ferrule conscientiously in the alignment crosshairs and initialled the tone check on the reverse.

Clipped to the inside flap of Volume I were two loose slips that had come down in the eight o'clock pouch without registry numbers. The upper slip was cream Ministry of Industry bond, top copy, embossed at the crest: *One seat, front row, opening day, World Expo. For the Arbiter, if she comes. Photograph mandatory.* Pinned behind it on a torn square of grey Directorate scrip, in black fountain ink that had feathered slightly into the cheap pulp, stood four words: *Not the Expo. — A.V.*

Harrow unclipped both, checked their backs for routing stamps, found none, punched two clean holes in the left margin of each, and laced them onto the treasury tag at the back of Volume III under *Correspondence, Noted*.

She scrolled to Section 4, *Financial Standing and Surety*. An unsponsored application required a ministerial bond-waiver code, which she had copied onto her pad before supper. She moved the cursor to the surety line to enter *NONE* and stopped with her fingers over the keys.

The box was greyed out from the central port ledger. Inside it stood clean capitals: **SURETY: lodged in full. Sponsor: THE VESS FOUNDATION. Date lodged: the morning after the Xuanfang bulletin.**

Harrow took her hands off the keyboard.

The Threat Board had met on Thursday; the household reclassification had been typed on Friday; Volume I had not reached Arrivals until tonight. The morning after the Xuanfang bulletin had been Tuesday, nine o'clock, when the Port Bond Office rolled up its grille. Somebody from Vess House had been standing on the marble in a coat before the kettles boiled in the Directorate, carrying a certified draft large enough to indemnify nine catastrophic-class Resonators and one passenger Echo at commercial wharfage rates.

Above the bevel of her monitor, the little engraved palm house on Ludo's card sat straight on its brass pin. Four times in six years Harrow had sat at a visitors' table in the Foundation's oak-panelled annex while Ludo brought her tea on a tray and stacked voucher books in chronological order—burn-ward endowments, youth orchestra bursaries, zone trade-school grants—every receipt double-initialled, every transfer descending cleanly through four registered trusts into granite where no subpoena reached.

Beneath the locked surety line, an amber prompt pulsed for Directorate sign-off: `PRIOR AUDIT FLAG ON NAMED SPONSOR. ENTER OBJECTION / NO OBJECTION.`

Entering *OBJECTION* from an Arrivals terminal generated an automated compliance notice to the sponsor's company secretary on the morning run, bearing the desk code of the officer who had raised it. Madam Vess's counsel would read Harrow's annex number over toast at eight-fifteen.

Harrow put her fingers back on the home row, typed *NO OBJECTION*, and pressed return. The amber line cleared.

Before moving down the screen, she opened her bottom drawer, lifted out a fresh buff folder and a gummed spine label, and entered a fifth examination file into the Directorate's internal register. She left *Vess* and *Pratique* out of the header altogether, titled it *Harbour Bond Sundries — Q4 Wharfage*, copied the serial number of Tuesday morning's draft onto the first ruled sheet in sharp pencil, and slid the folder beneath her blotter.

One mandatory field remained at the foot of Form P-1: *Purpose of visit*.

Harrow pulled Volume I open, then Volume II, turning past shipping tables, Ragunnan harbour manifests, Jinzhou municipal gazettes, and a glossy brochure for the World Expo's maritime pavilion that Press Clipping had added on speculation. Three inches of paper, twelve tabbed dividers, and not one intercept in any of them held a steamship booking, a visa enquiry, a letter answered, or any indication that nine women inland in Huanglong knew the New Federation had printed seats for them.

She typed `OFFICIAL`. Under port statute, *Official* froze the screen without a diplomatic note number from a recognised chancery; Septimont had sent none, Jinzhou had sent none. She hit backspace eight times. She typed `TOURISM`, looked up once at the ninth square frame of the wet umbrella, and took that out as well.

The radiator under the window knocked twice as the building's night boiler stepped down. Harrow left the box empty and pressed *Transmit*.

The terminal accepted the bond, locked the record, and printed its routing line across the foot of the glass:

> *PURPOSE UNDECLARED. PARTY REFERRED TO PORT HEALTH FOR FULL ASSAY. EXAMINING CONTRACTOR: NOVIALLE CLINICAL.*

By half-past six the tenth-floor boardroom smelled of raincoats and cooling coffee. The proxy at the far end of the pearwood table had gone first, leaving his leather blotter square and his water glass untouched; Legal and Assay followed in pairs toward the lifts. Tamsin Reeve stayed at the secretary's corner to gather the spare agenda sheets before the cleaners came through, tapping the stack straight against her palm.

Old Dr. Sorley was at the coat tree by the double doors, taking his time working his left arm into a heavy tweed sleeve whose lining had come tacked at the cuff.

"The rule would have had us ask," he said to the coat rack.

"They'd refuse, Arthur," Reeve said, unscrewing her fountain pen to cap it properly so it wouldn't leak in her whites. "Off a boat, nine journalists on every bollard—anyone sensible would refuse before they'd heard the second sentence."

Sorley got his horn button through the loop and flattened the lapel with his thumb. "Yes," he said. "They would."

He gave her the small, polite nod he gave her in the canteen queue every morning and let the glass door hush shut behind him.

Three floors down and across the skybridge, the Vess Burn Unit kept its thermostats at twenty-six degrees for shock prevention, so coming through the airlock hit Reeve's spectacles with a brief veil of mist. She wiped them on her sleeve, washed to the elbows at the scrub sink, and started evening dressings at Bed 1 so the night charge nurse could finish admitting two foundrymen up from the south smelter.

Marek in Bed 6 was awake, propped on three pillows to keep the tension off his left ribcage, listening to the tram bells down on the avenue with his good arm hooked behind his head. Four weeks after a tacetite condenser blew in a zone yard, his split-thickness autografts kept playing the same trick: pink and promising on Monday, grey and lifting at the seam by Friday night before the host vessels could bridge. Reeve eased the paraffin tulle back with sterile forceps and angled her penlight low across the border. Tonight the margin was holding, tacked down in pale new epithelium, though the lower third along the ulna looked thin enough to tear if he coughed hard.

"Holding?" Marek asked, watching her eyebrows rather than his arm.

"Top two inches are behaving themselves," Reeve said, laying fresh mesh down smooth and light. "Bottom corner's lazy. If we're still pink on Thursday we skip theater."

Taped to the chrome pole of his drip stand, just below the saline pump where he didn't have to turn his chin to see it, was a folded news-sheet cutting somebody's cousin had brought in at visiting hour: a grainy street-frame of the dark-haired woman from the northern feeds, right hand bare and whole where forty seconds earlier on the broadcast a timber splinter had torn across it.

Marek nodded toward the pole while Reeve bound crêpe bandage round his wrist. "Guy in Bed 4 says that's studio splicing. Nobody closes clean through fascia while they're still standing up."

"Four separate feeds," Reeve said, tearing surgical tape cleanly between thumb and forefinger. "Lab upstairs ran frame-by-frame on the Jinzhou one. Full dermal closure, no contracture, motor return inside a minute."

"Whole zone reckons she's coming in by spring."

"Port's getting a room ready," Reeve said. She peeled her gloves into the pedal bin, initialled his Thursday dressing chart on the end rail, and hitched his sheet clear of the cradle over his ribs. "Keep off that elbow till the six o'clock obs, Marek, or you and I are having words."

It was past ten when she reached the specimen prep room behind Clinical Pathology, cold-room compressors humming steadily through the tiled wall. Her desk in the alcove had come down from the old fourth-floor executive suite during a refit years before she made registrar—heavy dark walnut, scarred by teacup rings, its centre lock jammed open. She pushed the wooden pen tray aside to reach the roll of cloth cryo-tape; pressed flat into the join of the veneer beneath the tray's heel lay a faded gold-and-white sweet wrapper smoothed thin as onion-skin, which had been sitting in that crack the day Estates carried the desk in. Reeve tore nine inches of cloth tape off the spool and slid the pen tray back over its mark.

On the printer tray sat the dispatch sheet that had come through from Port Health twenty minutes earlier, clipped to two conflicting port-control tallies—Arrivals listing nine persons, Harbour Customs pre-advice listing seven marked adults and two unverified entries.

> **PORT HEALTH AUTHORITY — ASSAY DISPATCH (CAPITAL ROADS)**  
> *Contract PH-19: assigned overnight to Novialle Clinical (Transfer Ref. 008-N).*  
> *Kit: Full Pratique Assay, Priority Retention. Deliver Pier 4 Cold Lock.*

Thermal printer ink lifted in liquid nitrogen vapour; Reeve pulled a strip of matte cloth labels onto the blotter and uncapped an indelible laboratory marker to write the set herself, in the neat, square capitals ward sisters never misread.

First a red-top draw tube: *A — ARBITER.*

Second, a buffered cell-preservation tube: *B — RETURNEE.*

Third, a dry sterile biopsy pot with a wide screw cap: *C — MINERAL MARGIN, SHAVING.*

Fourth—last of the priority set—she took a lead-lined resonant containment vial from the locked cabinet, checked its seal ring under the desk lamp, and lettered its flank as evenly and cleanly as the other three: *A-1 — PASSENGER (ECHO), IF SEPARABLE.*

Behind those she lined up five standard purple-top draw tubes, each marked *PARTY, ROUTINE.*

She slotted all nine empty tubes upright into the grey foam rack, unlatched the insulated port-dispatch cold box on the bench, and lowered the rack into its dry-ice well. Before closing the lid, her eye caught the mismatch between the Customs sheet and the Directorate cover slip on her clipboard; she took one more empty tube from the carton, wrote *AND PARTY* along the cloth strip, and pushed it into the tenth hole of the foam before snapping both steel clasps shut.

## iv. The Kennel
### *New Federation · the Lawless Zone, a hiring hall*

> **GHOST HOUNDS — HALL SLATE**  
> **JOB · POSTED BY · FEE · TAKEN BY**  
> *Shrine clearance, 63 sites · the municipality · day rate · — (eleventh week)*  
> *Wyrm watch, tacetite yards · the house · standing · rota*  
> *Find the girl wearing my daughter's papers. Not to hurt. · a mother, Row 9 · one sewing machine, good order · —*  
> *Honour guard, border to capital, for the Army's return · the Homecoming Committee · coin, paid in advance · —*  
> *Recovery, one lapsed contract asset, female, last at Startorch · a ministry that is not one · asked · —*

By six in the morning the night wind coming under the corrugated iron doors had eaten yesterday's board down to grey ghosts, so Chalk took her tin cup of water, climbed onto the scarred ammunition box behind the bar, and started again from the top.

Wet quarry chalk dried hard enough to hold till dusk if you leaned into the downstrokes. Her canvas apron was grey to both elbows with twenty years of it. Down at the back of the Kennel, past the wire armoury cage where a mechanic was greasing bike chains by an oil-drum stove, an old dual-deck cassette player wired to a truck battery was spooling through a dubbed tape from up north—three bars of distorted guitar, then static, then the bright, quick rise and fall of a young woman's voice talking fast behind too much hiss and tin partition to catch a single word before the next drumbeat drowned it out.

*Shrine clearance, 63 sites* went up on row one. Last month the municipal runner had brought fifty-eight street corners; Tuesday he had brought five more and another week's posting scrip in a sealed envelope. In the Kennel, a paid posting went up on Monday and stayed up until Saturday, whether anybody's boot paused under it or not. Eleven weeks running, forty Hounds a morning had read straight down from the header to the wyrm rota as if row one were blank slate.

The sheet-iron street door scraped back on its runner, letting in a wedge of cold white glare and two kids no older than sixteen. The boy in front wore a Federated Army greatcoat hacked off above his ankles with house shears so he wouldn't trip on it, brass buttons rubbed bright on his sleeve; the girl beside him carried a round butter-biscuit tin in both mittens. Across the alley behind them, wet lime-wash still dripped down the concrete cistern wall from ten stencilled silhouettes in a row, shoulder to shoulder, with a crooked five-pointed star brushed above the smallest head.

They set the tin on Chalk's counter. Inside lay copper tens, tram tokens, three pre-war silver quarters, and tight-folded zone notes.

"Homecoming Committee," the boy said, voice cracking once before he steadied it. "The General's coming down when the snow clears. Spring, by the Expo road. We want twenty riders from the boundary cairns in to the gate."

Chalk tipped the coin out onto the zinc, thumbed two stamped tin washers aside toward the boy's cuff, and swept the rest into the cash box.

"Hounds don't march," she said.

"Fee's on the zinc," the girl said, chin high. "Hall rule says coin down goes on the slate."

"Putting it on," Chalk said. She stepped back onto the ammunition box and chalked *Honour guard, border to capital, for the Army's return · the Homecoming Committee · coin, paid in advance · —* across row four in square, level print. Behind her, the boy nudged the girl's ribs with his elbow; both of them backed off two paces to admire *the Army's return* standing four inches tall on the Hounds' board before taking their empty biscuit tin and slipping out into the alley.

A man in unscuffed calfskin boots—capital leather, clean of slag-ash—held the iron door with a gloved knuckle to let them clear the sill, then walked straight to the bar and laid an oilcloth folder dry-side up on the zinc. Dark civic wool, no pin at the lapel.

"Unit rates," he said. "Close attendance from the coast road to port, party of nine. Per head, if your crews split."

Chalk wiped her fingers on her apron, pulled the iron cotter-pin out of the slate's upright post, and swung the heavy frame round on its swivel. The back of the board, kept turned to the brick wall where walk-in custom never looked, already held nine lines in dry white hand.

> **ATTENDANCE, PER HEAD — QUOTE ONLY**  
> *the mother — no quote*  
> *the priestess — no quote while the mother is standing*  
> *the wolf — she takes challenges. ask her yourself and bring a doctor*  
> *the knight — forty day rates, and we want it in writing how tall*  
> *the bard — nothing. tell her the hall has bad acoustics and she will come on her own*  
> *the scissors — no*  
> *the radio girl — no quote. house rule*  
> *the new one, with wings — price as the knight until we know better*  
> *the umbrella — we do not quote on what we cannot see coming*

The man read from top to bottom without moving his head, stopping at line seven.

"What's the house rule."

"Hounds take no work on anybody's kid," Chalk said. "Boss's rule. Boss isn't in."

He didn't ask when Calcharo was expected. Instead he untied the black tape on his oilcloth folder, drew out a single foolscap sheet stiff at the creases, and turned it to face her across the zinc.

It was an old state auxiliary indenture—*ZONE MINOR, FIELD ATTACHMENT*—stamped in faded purple ink from before the bureau reorganisation, never cancelled. Beside `AGE ASSESSED: 11` sat a small, dark thumbprint pressed hard enough into the rag paper to blur the centre whorl. Two lines below it, under *Countersigned for the Hall*, stood Chalk's own name in the steeper, narrower cursive she had written before a winter crate crushed her right wrist.

"Alive," the man said. "Placed at an academy in Lahai-Roi, lapsed reporting. By Kennel articles, an undischarged indenture bearing the hall's witness goes on the front board when presented."

He had the article word for word. Chalk swung the slate back to its public face, dropped the iron pin home into its collar, and climbed onto the ammunition box with her wet chalk.

Along the fifth line she wrote *Recovery, one lapsed contract asset, female, last at Startorch · a ministry that is not one · asked · —*

By the oil-drum stove, two night-shift riders had come up from the bike bays with tin mugs; the nearer one squinted through the kettle steam at the fresh wet row. Before his boot left the fender, Chalk wiped out the wet dash at the end of line five with the ball of her thumb and wrote in the fourth column herself.

"You're the board clerk," the man in capital boots said.

Chalk rapped one grey knuckle against the wooden header above column four: **TAKEN BY**. Slate-keepers didn't work jobs, and hall law didn't say slate-keepers couldn't; a line belonged to the first hand that marked it until the term ran out.

The man looked from the board to her apron, slid the thumbprinted sheet back into its oilcloth, and drew the tape tight. "Recovery runs a season," he said. "You have until spring."

The iron door clanged shut behind him.

Chalk stayed up on the ammunition box. Two rows higher, where the draught off the alley had already greyed the middle of row three—*Find the girl wearing my daughter's papers. Not to hurt. · a mother, Row 9 · one sewing machine, good order · —*—she left the fourth column empty, dipped her chalk into the tin cup once more, and went back over every letter of the mother's posting so it would stand sharp till sundown, two lines above the *TAKEN BY* column where the word **CHALK** dried white and dead straight in the tall, wide-spaced capitals she had once used to make a board readable to a child.

## vi. The Harbour Office
### *the Black Shores · Tethys Hub*

Mail day meant the hired motor-launch out of the outer channel, which had begun calling every second Thursday since Fog Lanes 2 and 4 came down, and it meant Oren had to sit on his hands until two o'clock so he didn't open the canvas bag early.

He was nineteen, nine months out from a drying-net village on the eastern farm coast, with an oar-callus still fading across the base of his thumbs and a fresh blue Blake Bloom pinned slightly crooked to his collar. Officially he was a junior Bloom Bearer assigned to maritime registry; in practice, because there was one long cedar table above the lower quay, one brass scale, one oilskin harbour book, and nobody else in the room, Oren was the Harbour Office.

Standing procedure had been explained to him on his first week by KU-Post and never glossed since. At two sharp he broke the wax on the mail-sack, drew each item out in the exact order his fingers met it in the canvas, and read it aloud once from date-line to signature. Nothing was cross-checked in the stacks afterwards. What required signing went to the seaward casement, where the Shorekeeper stood three paces off in the winter light with a silver dip pen in her hand and a clean blotter on the stone sill beside her.

The top packet, bound in tarred twine, held four formal renewals of coastal and academic accord. Oren slit the first envelope cleanly with his bone folder.

**Startorch Academy** came first, thick cream paper from the Office of the President: standard Lament-shear and telemetry clauses countersigned in Lahai-Roi, beneath which a dry postscript in cramped ink observed that the Shores' last burst had arrived crisp enough to wake two dead sublevel dishes and inquired whether the archipelago had finally retired its pre-freeze copper. Oren tripped once on *magneto-barometric*, backed up to get every syllable right, flushed to his ears, and carried the sheet to the sill. Her nib moved once across the foot; he blotted it and laid it in the outbound basket.

Second came dark red wax stamped with the laurel of Ragunna. Oren broke the seal, unfolded two sheets of watermarked linen, and read both out straight:

> **HOUSE MONTELLI — FACTOR'S CHANCERY, RAGUNNA**  
> *Relay accord countersigned as drafted, effective on receipt.*  
> *Addendum (private): We ask whether Tethys retains commercial registry indices. The undisclosed principal that acquired three distressed Montelli shipping subsidiaries after hours in autumn has begun buying in the New Federation's capital again, also after hours. Any light the Shores may shed on the clearing-house would oblige us.*

The Shorekeeper signed the relay schedule and wrote *Commercial registries outside remit* in the margin of the addendum for Oren's fair copy.

Third was heavy rag paper from **the Ephor's Palace** in Septimont, written in a plain guild-chancery hand rather than temple script, renewing the southern storm-warning compact and signed at the bottom by a Deputy of the Rolls, *"the Ephor being at sea on a private errand"*. Signed, blotted, stacked.

Fourth carried the vermilion garrison paste of **Jinzhou, the Magistrate's office**: half a sheet of wave-buoy frequencies, followed by a second paragraph in finer ink. Oren read down the column: a party of eleven persons entered this winter on Jinzhou's municipal housing roll as Household 411 had been advised, upon consulting the city's lake, to examine the Black Shores' Register of Arrivals; the Magistrate's chancellery inquired whether the reading room was open to visitors who presented themselves in person.

At the casement, the Shorekeeper's pen stopped half an inch above the vermilion border.

Outside the tall glass, black swell lifted and dropped smoothly against the basalt quay. She lowered the nib to sign the buoy schedule first, then turned half toward the table while Oren pulled his correspondence pad closer and dipped his pen.

"The reading room is open to any reader who comes in person," the Shorekeeper dictated. "Volumes in sole custody are read to their subject only, and only at the subject's own asking."

Oren dotted his period, remembered the blotter in time not to blow on the ink, and looked up grinning before he could stop himself.

"Household 411," he said. "Everybody up the coast knows who 411 is, ma'am—my brother on the crabber brought the Jinzhou gazette home in his oilskins. That's the Arbiter's people." Having started, he went at his natural mainland speed, bone folder gesturing between his fingers. "Half our village had the Septimont arena reruns on at the net-shed—my sister's all for the knight, Cartethyia, the tall one from Rinascita, though for my money it's Lupa, I watched her take a halberd shaft clean in two on the Seventh Games tape, and the girl with the giant tailor's shears—" He heard his own voice echoing off the high stone vault, brought both elbows down onto the cedar, and nodded eagerly toward the brass relay console humming in the corner alcove. "The mainland public feeds reach the outer buoy now Lane 4's open. Doesn't touch any cipher channel, ma'am, completely public broadcast—I can put the northern news-band up on the glass in thirty seconds if you'd like to see them."

The Shorekeeper waited until the last word had settled cleanly onto the flagstones.

"No," she said.

"It's only the open municipal relay," Oren said earnestly, anxious she shouldn't think he meant an intercept. "Public, on every wharf set."

"It is public," she agreed. "The next letter, Oren."

He reached back into the damp canvas sack, though Wednesday's shelving job in the upper vault caught on his tongue as his hand closed around a fat brown packet. He had been rubbing cedar oil into the leather bindings to keep the salt mould off and had turned the front leaf of the *Register of Arrivals*.

"When I had the Register down for oiling on Wednesday," he said, lifting the brown packet out onto the blotter, "I saw the index lists you second. Who was first?"

"The next letter, Oren," the Shorekeeper said, her tone unchanged by a hair.

Before he could slip the rubber bands off the packet, the Tethys console in the corner alcove sounded its clear two-note chime.

The Shorekeeper walked the four paces from the casement and laid her palm flat on the cold glass lectern. Pale blue type rose across the vertical pane, reflected upside down in the polished cedar at Oren's elbow while the Hub's voice spoke into the quiet room.

> **TETHYS — STELLAR MATRIX 12: DEDUCTION COMPLETE**  
> TETHYS: Event class: ARRIVAL. Not a Lament. Confidence: rising.  
> TETHYS: Open deduction?  
> SHOREKEEPER: No.  
> TETHYS: Unread deductions expire at forty-eight hours and are run again.  
> SHOREKEEPER: Then run it again.  
> TETHYS: This deduction has been run nine times.  
> SHOREKEEPER: I know how many.  
> TETHYS: Housekeeping subsystems hold read access to arrival forecasts, for provisioning. Revoke?  
> SHOREKEEPER: [no entry, four minutes]  
> SHOREKEEPER: No.  
> ANNOTATION (Shorekeeper): A house may get ready. Filing under sole custody.

Four minutes in a stone room with a pendulum clock on the wall. For the first minute Oren kept his eyes fixed politely on an ink stain shaped like a skate near his brass postal scale. During the second, his left thumb hooked a loose blue thread at the seam of his cuff and wrapped it tightly round his forefinger until the pad went white, then unwrapped it carefully so he wouldn't fray Uniform Stores' sewing. Down at the quay, the supply launch cast off its stern warp; its single cylinder coughed twice in the wet air, caught, and chugged out toward the lane between the fog walls until the window-latch stopped humming. Through all four minutes the Shorekeeper stood with her palm resting unmoving on the lectern glass, shoulders level, until she spoke the single word, entered her note, and let the pane go dark.

She walked back to the casement. Oren swallowed once and snapped the rubber bands off the brown packet.

Out tumbled a compressed brick of crumpled thermal slips, ferry tickets, grease-spotted fishmonger's chits, and coarse bamboo-paper bills, clipped behind a standard Field Expense Claim. Under *CLAIMANT*, somebody had printed `BULING` in blue ballpoint and squeezed `(Senior Consultant)` into the margin beside it.

"We haven't got a Senior Consultant grade on the books, ma'am," Oren said, checking his laminated pay schedule. "Only Consultant, Adjunct, and Bearer. And she hasn't sent a single sheet of reporting with it—her jacket's been empty since Rinascita." He began fanning the chits out by size: river-ferry stubs, four sizable fish bills, wholesale bundles of uncut yellow talisman stock, nineteen separate café tabs for thick sweet coffee, and, at the very bottom on a torn square of red tissue, *sky lantern, paper, one — personal, do not reimburse.* He angled the top ferry ticket toward the casement light to decipher the smudged purple harbour-chop. "Stamped Mengzhou Crossing, and the next three are Xuanfang—"

"Pay them, Oren," the Shorekeeper said. "Do not read me the stamps."

Oren set the ferry stub down flat. "Shall I enclose a reminder slip for the missing report?"

"Read the front of her file."

He pulled open the lower drawer of the table, lifted out the thin grey jacket tabbed *BULING*, and read the single line written across its cover in the Shorekeeper's upright black script: *Not tasked. Not to be asked. Pay without query. — S.*

Looking down at the fanned chits on the table—ferry after ferry, stall after stall, stacked neatly from earliest date at the bottom to last week on top like milestones along one continuous road—Oren gathered the whole pile between both hands, turned it face down on the blotter, and tapped its four edges square against the wood before pinning a payment warrant to the back.

The pneumatic tube beside the bookcase gave its soft leather *thump*: one yellow archival slip from Tethys's scheduled retention sweep. Oren smoothed its curl out on his sleeve. **RETENTION REVIEW — DOSSIER: SEVERA, I. C.** Below the line sat three printed options: *Retain / Seal / Destroy*.

He carried it to the sill. The Shorekeeper took the yellow sheet, crossed none of the three words, and wrote a fourth beneath them in her own hand: *Surrender to its subject on arrival. Closed until then.*

Oren blotted it and walked over to the tall rack of wooden pigeonholes by the door. In the slot brass-plated **FOR COLLECTION**, one object already lay against the dark cedar: a white envelope, sealed unmarked with plain grey-blue wax and addressed across the front in the Shorekeeper's hand. Keeping his eyes above its top edge, Oren laid the yellow slip neatly upon it and stepped back to the table.

One narrow strip of ticker tape remained beside the empty mail-sack, passed up at nine that morning by KU-House, which had rolled over Oren's boot when he quoted Quartermaster regulations at it and waited rigidly until he apologised to its chassis before releasing the slip.

"Last item's only domestic stores, ma'am," Oren said. "One of the Haven units put in an indent for leaf tea. Stores bounced it back—they say the Shores don't stock tea and never have. I told the unit I'd get the refusal initialled so it clears its queue."

"Approve it," the Shorekeeper said.

Oren stopped with his nib over the *Refused* box. "Approve it? What sort—green, roasted, pressed—"

"Bergamot," she said at once. "One tin, by the next boat."

"Bergamot, one tin." He wrote it cleanly onto the supply sheet for the fortnightly launch, then hesitated, looking from the foreign word on his pad toward the tall casement. "Only—Stores swore up and down we didn't drink it."

"We don't," the Shorekeeper said.

Outside, the short winter light had drained to slate; out on the point, the great headland beacon lit on its dusk stroke, sweeping one white band across the rafters before going out to sea. The Shorekeeper laid her silver pen parallel to the inkstand, inclined her head—"Good evening, Oren"—and went out down the wet basalt steps toward the tideline, as she did every evening at dark.

Alone under the green-shaded lamp, Oren corked the inkwell, locked Buling's face-down receipts into the cash tin, and pulled the heavy oilskin harbour book across the wood to close out Thursday. Under *Arrivals* he wrote *None (save contracted stores launch)*; under *Departures*, *None*.

He turned the stiff rag page to rule Friday's lines with his brass straight-edge, and stopped with the ruler an inch off the paper. At the head of the clean sheet, undated, four lines already stood in dry black ink:

> *Vessel: —*  
> *Master: —*  
> *From: —*  
> *Pratique: free. Standing.*

## viii. The Pump House
### *New Federation · the Lawless Zone, a shrine*

> **SHRINE AT THE PUMP HOUSE — BOOK OF WHAT WAS LEFT**  
> *kept by Mrs. H. Brandt, chair 2*
>
> *candles, 6. rice, one bowl, replaced. a wolf, knitted, one ear. scissors, child's, blunt: given back to the child, told why. umbrella, broken, left open. batteries, 4, for the radio girl.*  
> *chairs, folding, 6, new, from a van. no card. "V.F." stencilled under the seats.*  
> *Removal crew attended Tuesday. Stood about. Had tea. Left.*

Doris Voigt had been due on Chair 2 at half-past two, and by ten to three there was still no sign of her string bag round the slag-tip, which meant Doris had stopped to argue about kerosene rations again and Hetty Brandt's knees would click all the way home.

Mrs. Brandt pulled her wool plaid tighter across her lap, checked that the iron wrench-socket was holding her blue exercise book flat against the midwinter blow, and kept watch on the south track. Against the lime-washed brick of the dead pump house stood two salvaged sheets of plywood nailed across oil drums. Pinned to the wood in clear plastic stationery sleeves were eight colour feed-stills clipped from three seasons of northern news-sheets, sheltered below by six jam-jar candles, four zinc wireless batteries stacked neat as dominoes, a lumpy grey knitted wolf with one ear, an earthenware bowl of clean raw rice, and a broken black umbrella wedged open at the flank to turn the grit off the wicks.

Up the rutted track from the south—not from the rows, from the open alkali scrub toward the coast road where nobody came on foot if they had bus fare—walked a woman in no hurry at all.

She wore a thin, dark-lined coat left unbuttoned at the throat in weather that had iced the puddles by the standpipe, wine-dark hair blowing sideways across her mouth and left to blow, hands loose at her flanks. Spotting the plywood board, she angled across the gravel at an easy, cat-footed stroll and stopped three paces in front of the plastic sleeves, head tilted, eyeing the display with the frank enjoyment of a woman who had turned down a dull street and found a puppet show.

Mrs. Brandt kept one mittened thumb on her pencil stub and let her look. You knew inside five seconds when somebody had come to tip the rice over; this one was smiling to herself, bright-eyed and thoroughly entertained, moving her gaze from sleeve to sleeve down the row.

After a full minute, the stranger lifted a bare forefinger and pointed at the third sleeve from the left: the tall fair-haired young woman from the Ragunnan broadcasts, caught on white steps with a wreath of laurel slanted across her brow.

"There," the woman said, pleased with herself. "That's the one."

"That's the knight," Mrs. Brandt said from Chair 2.

The woman looked down at her, more amused than corrected, taking in the fingerless mittens, the plaid rug, and the wrench-socket paperweight. "Is it. Well, she's got the height for it. I was betting on height."

At the hollow of her throat, showing through the open collar of her shirt despite the bite in the wind, sat a single deep-red camellia—fresh greenhouse bloom by the sheen of it, though Mrs. Brandt couldn't see a pin. The stranger slipped two fingers behind its petals, drew out a narrow strip of paper folded twice, opened it in her palm, read whatever stood on it with a quick sideways lift of one eyebrow, and tucked it neatly back behind the flower.

She turned back to the eight plastic sleeves.

"Help a traveller out, then," she said pleasantly. "Which one's Amy?"

Mrs. Brandt leaned forward enough to tap the sixth sleeve with the rubber end of her carpenter's pencil: pink hair, grinning straight into some northern reporter's lens like she'd just talked him out of his watch.

"Sixth along," Mrs. Brandt said. "The loud one. Does the midnight wireless up in the snow. The daughter."

The stranger stepped half a pace closer to the oil drums, hands sliding into her coat pockets as she studied the girl's grin.

"Huh," came out of her, quick and light, half a beat ahead of her own smile. "Look at the weed."

"Mind your mouth at my board," Mrs. Brandt said sharply.

The woman blinked, then let out a short, delighted laugh. "Fair enough. Though I've a strong suspicion that was a compliment, where I picked it up."

Her eyes travelled along the row of brass drawing-pins without hurry. "And Mei?"

"Middle sleeve," Mrs. Brandt said, touching the dark-haired woman in black and gold whose ungloved right hand rested on a stone rail. "The mother. Anybody who isn't blind knows the mother."

At the stranger's collarbone, the red flower went a full shade darker round its outer rims—deepening to bruised plum against her skin for two slow breaths before the bright red came back up through the petals. The woman pressed two flat fingers briefly over her breastbone, the way a runner checks a stitch, and dropped her hand.

"Well," she said lightly. "Seems I'm further behind on the papers than I thought."

Mrs. Brandt unscrewed the tin cup from her quart flask, poured three fingers of sweet black tea before the wind could cool it—Doris wasn't turning up before three, and nobody walked out of the south scrub with alkali dust on their ankles without a dry throat—and held it out.

The woman took it in both hands, inhaled the steam with genuine respect for the sugar ration, and hooked one hip comfortably onto the rim of the spare oil drum beside Chair 3.

"Off a coaster?" Mrs. Brandt asked, placing her with the deckhands and deep-desert prospectors who turned up six months behind every bulletin. "Where're you walking to in a summer coat?"

"North," the woman said over the rim of the cup. "Way up past the frost-line. I've got an appointment in spring, at a house with a blue door. So I'm told."

"Told by who?"

The stranger tapped two knuckles against her sternum, over the red petals. "Management," she said cheerfully. "Left me a slip. Terrible handwriting, very firm about the door."

Mrs. Brandt glanced once at the iron-grey sky over the slag-heaps, then back at the woman's thin hem snapping in the grit. "It's midwinter. Even on foot, you'll be standing on that porch two months early."

"Oh, easily," the woman agreed. "My legs set off north last week without consulting me, and I've decided to let them have their head. They're generally better informed than I am." She looked down at the four batteries and the lumpy knitted wolf. "Cold corner to keep a chair warm, missus."

"My grandson Danny got refused pratique at the harbour gate when he was fourteen," Mrs. Brandt said shortly, squaring the wrench-socket on her page. "Mark on his shin. Works the slag-pans out past Nine now with no card to his name. Takes ten minutes to keep the grease off the candles."

The woman looked at the jam-jar wicks—plainly, no fuss, one craftswoman noting another's bench—and drank her tea. Looking across the ruts toward the northern ridge, she began to hum into the tin cup.

It was a small, plain kitchen tune, four bars long, lifting on the third bar like a question asked over a shoulder and hanging open on the fourth—and stopping dead in mid-air.

Mrs. Brandt waited three counts for the turn back down. Plastic rustled on the plywood.

"Well?" Mrs. Brandt said. "Let's have the rest of it."

The stranger lowered the cup and looked round, mildly surprised. "That's all there is of it. Four bars, and the tune ends at a door." She said it flat and matter-of-fact, the way somebody tells you the post office shuts at noon. "Sticks in the teeth, though, doesn't it."

"You'll walk right past them if it's spring you're going north," Mrs. Brandt observed, taking the empty tin cup back, wiping its lip on a clean rag, and screwing it tight onto the flask. "Whole zone says they're coming down here when the ice breaks. Straight down the Expo road to the port. Kids over on Row 4 have been out with stolen whitewash painting arrows on the milestones."

The woman sat still on the oil drum for a second, head cocked toward the dusty highway running north toward the capital's brown smudge on the horizon.

"Are they really," she said, amused. "Well, somebody's double-booked the season. Spring's spoken for."

She slid off the drum and held out an open palm toward Mrs. Brandt's knee. "Lend me that pencil half a minute? Save me having to ask the next grandmother."

Mrs. Brandt passed up the carpenter's stub. The woman drew the folded paper strip out from under the red flower, flipped it to its blank back, braced it flat against the plywood beside the knight's sleeve, and printed in brisk, slanted capitals big enough for Mrs. Brandt to read from Chair 2 without squinting: *AMY — THE LOUD ONE. MEI — THE MOTHER.*

She tucked the strip back behind her flower and handed the pencil back blunt-end first.

"For the tea," she said. She brought her left hand round from her coat hem—empty a breath before, Mrs. Brandt would have sworn on a stack of ledgers—holding a single stemless red camellia, thick-petalled and untouched by cold, and set it upright in the middle of the dry white rice in the earthenware bowl. "It won't want watering."

With a lazy two-fingered wave toward Chair 2, she stepped out onto the north verge and went on up the track at the same unhurried stroll, thin coat belling behind her in the wind.

Five minutes after the dark speck of her shoulders dipped behind the culvert rise, Doris Voigt finally came puffing past the cistern with two string bags and a long story starting about her brother-in-law's chimney. Before getting up out of Chair 2, Mrs. Brandt lifted the wrench-socket off her blue exercise book, wet the lead on her tongue, and wrote under Tuesday's line:

> *flower, red, one. left by a woman walking north. does not want watering. she asked me which one was the daughter.*

## ix. Notice to Mariners
### *a coda, on the water*

> **FEDERATION HYDROGRAPHIC OFFICE — NOTICE TO MARINERS**  
> *Stormy Sea. A fixed white light is reported on a bearing where the chart shows no land. Fog lane 4 is reported open. The light is not charted. Mariners should not rely on it.*  
> *(Second edition, corrected.) Mariners report that it can be relied on.*

Forty winters out of the capital's fish dock on hake and deep-water cod, and the skipper of the *Dunlin* had never seen clear water east of the Tacetite shoals.

Normally at two in the morning you ran the shoal edge by echo-sounder alone, keeping half a mile off a solid yellow-grey wall of murk that smelled of wet iron and set the compass card wandering in its bowl. Tonight the *Dunlin* was steaming dead west at four knots down an open road of black water six cables wide. Port and starboard, the fog stood straight up from the swell like cut chalk cliffs, clean-edged under the winter stars, not a wisp trailing across the gunwale.

Under the hooded red lamp beside the wheel, Jory—sixteen, second winter at sea, sea-boots stuffed with newspaper at the toes—took the weekly slip off the brass hook and read it out loud from the top, both editions, down to *Mariners report that it can be relied on.*

"Queer sort of notice, skipper," the boy said, hooking the damp paper back on its spike. "Telling you not to trust it on line three and taking it back on line four."

"Hydrographic sits ashore," the skipper said, one cracked thumb resting light on the top spoke.

Out ahead of the trawler's bow, where Admiralty Chart 19 showed ninety fathoms over grey mud and no rock within forty leagues, the light came round.

Each time the lens turned onto the lane, it laid a broad, level road of white-gold across the black swell all the way to the *Dunlin*'s stem, lighting the rime on the winch-drums and the salt crust on the forestay before wheeling smoothly northward into the dark.

The skipper lifted his heavy rubber-cased night glasses off their peg, planted both oilskinned elbows on the sill of the open wheelhouse window, and brought the barrels down onto the foot of the glare.

Low black sand shelved out under white surf. Right at the tideline, ankle-deep where the cold wash ran flat and drew back into the sea, a single figure stood quite still. Bareheaded in the midwinter wind, pale hem dark with salt water at the shin. It raised no arm, showed no hand-lamp, and never once turned toward the trawler's masthead lights—only stood facing past them, east, down the open water.

Jory leaned out over the bulwark beside him with the spare glasses, squinted ten seconds, and dropped them onto his chest by their lanyard. "Marker post on the spit," he said, wiping spray off his nose with the back of his mitt. "Tide-pole, likely."

The skipper kept his own lenses up through one more slow run of surf round those still ankles, watched the foam slide back down the black shingle, and lowered the glasses, handing them across for the boy to hang up.

Jory hooked the strap over its peg and stayed at the open glass, watching the dark water ahead gather for the next turn.

"We going in, skipper?"

"Nobody's asked us in," the skipper said, checking the green trace on the sounder—clean bottom, forty-four fathoms, heavy marks of winter hake holding steady under the cold layer. "Got three shots of gear to shoot before daybreak. Bring her round four points to port."

While the boy took the spokes and eased the *Dunlin*'s blunt bow southward toward the bank, the skipper lifted the brass bar weights off Chart 19 on the table behind the binnacle. In the middle of the blank cream square at 38° West, he walked his parallel rules cleanly across from two bearings on the rose and unclipped the 2B pencil from the frame.

Small and upright, pressing neat into the rag paper the way his old master on the *Gannet* had taught him as an apprentice forty-two winters ago, he drew the tiny six-pointed star with a dot at its heart, inked the flare beside it, lettered its character cleanly in the empty sea, and put his initials and the month beneath in brackets. Jory glanced back over one shoulder from the wheel, watching the pencil work where no land was printed.

As the *Dunlin* came round onto her fishing course, the white beam swept down the lane again from astern of their starboard quarter. It ran flat across the black swell, climbed the wet rail, slid bright through the salt-streaked wheelhouse panes—crossing the coiled warps, the silent radio, and the boy's taped picture of six strangers on a northern step—and went straight on past them over the open water toward the mainland coast behind them.

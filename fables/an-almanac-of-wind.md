# An Almanac of Wind
### being one year kept between the lines of a Jinzhou patrol log, in a second ink
*A "Lyre, Speak to Me" side story.*

兩情若是久長時，又豈在朝朝暮暮。
*If two hearts hold fast and long, what need of every dawn and dusk together?*
— Qin Guan, "Immortal at the Magpie Bridge"

吹萬不同，而使其自已也。怒者其誰邪？
*It blows through the ten thousand hollows, and each sounds as itself. Who is it that stirs them?*
— Zhuangzi

> *The pages below were found inside an Outrider's patrol log, written between the ruled lines, in a second ink. The Almanac Bureau classifies private almanacs as weather.*

> *The Bureau has been wrong before.*

## 驚蟄 — Insects Waken
*Log: east wind. First thunder.*

The first thunder of the year came at muster, rolling down off the north face like furniture moved in an upstairs room, and the whole drill yard looked up on reflex and then pretended it hadn't, and Sergeant Mo made them run the wall circuit twice for pretending.

The callout came at noon. A grain cellar off Panhua's street, an echo nest, three of them, small. Routine. Chixia took the door, Baizhi took the lamps, Yangyang took the dark.

The first two went the way small ones go: footwork, patience, done. The third came out of the seed bins low and fast with Chixia's back in front of it, and the world performed its old trick, the one it had taught her in Yuezhou — everything slowed, everything simplified, the whole intricate city reduced to a line and a length and a wrist — and her sword passed through the space between two heartbeats and came out the other side wet, and the cellar was quiet.

"…Nice," Chixia said, into the quiet, in the voice she used when she meant something else.

Yangyang wiped her blade on the sacking. Above them, dust sifted through the lamplight and settled the way dust does, patiently, on everything alike.

At the trough outside she untied her hair ribbon and washed it. The ribbon was clean. She washed it anyway, wrung it, retied it, and Baizhi watched the whole procedure from the cart with the expression she saved for symptoms, and wrote something small in the corner of the incident form.

They walked home along the willows in the first warm hour of the year. The catkins were out. The river ran loud with snowmelt.

The wind found her at the second bridge.

It came off the water and crossed the road and arrived at her cheek and stayed — a warmth with weight to it, a pressure like a palm that had decided, long ago, to be gentle — and it lingered past the excuse of physics, the way it did every spring, the way she had stopped explaining to herself two springs back.

Chixia was arguing with Baizhi about lunch, ten steps ahead. The willows moved. Yangyang held still, the way you hold still for a shy animal, and let it finish.

"You're early," she said, under the argument.

The pressure lifted, unhurried, and went up through the catkins, and the catkins let it.

That night she ruled a margin into her patrol log and started the second ink. An Outrider files what repeats. It was the first thing the trade had taught her, and she trusted the trade: name the pattern, and the pattern must eventually introduce itself.

## 清明 — Clear and Bright
*Log: rain.*

On the sweeping-day the trio split three ways, as every year. Chixia went south to her family plot hauling enough food for a garrison, because the dead eat first, that's the rule, she didn't make the rule. Baizhi went to the Huaxu memorial wall with white chrysanthemums and a list of teachers, and stood there exactly as long as the list required, which was long. Yangyang's dead sleep in Mingting, where her sister sweeps for both of them, so she went up to the wall's unowned altar, the one for soldiers whose families the wars misplaced, and swept it, and laid out oranges, and lit the incense in the wet grey air.

The wind arrived while she was straightening the second orange. She knew it now by arrival alone — the pressure at the edge of things, the day paying attention.

She sat back on her heels.

"Everyone tells you theirs," she said. "The whole wall. Keeper Pei talked your ear off for three watches, he brags about it." She watched the incense climb. "Whose do you carry?"

The smoke stood straight for a breath. Then it bent — smoothly, deliberately, the way a finger points when the pointing is meant kindly — and it bent at her.

She held very still.

The smoke stayed on her, patient, the rain beginning around them in the fine soft way of that season, and something moved through her that she declined to give a shape to, because some doors you knock on and some doors you leave for the owner to open.

She bowed to the smoke instead. The full bow, the mourner's bow, forehead to the wet stone.

"Then they're mine too," she said. "Today."

She walked down in the rain. Behind her, in a wind that could have taken it a hundred times, the incense burned to the end of its stick, every thread of smoke rising straight, as if the weather had cupped its hands around a small flame and was holding, and holding, and holding.

## 小滿 — Grain Buds
*Log: wind scarce.*

By early summer she had the price list.

It went like this. Scaffolding above the cooper's lane, a rotten lashing, a load of roof tile going over with two apprentices under it — and the gust arrived late, ragged at the edges, and shoved the load a body's width sideways so it came down on cabbages, and in the same instant every banner in the market dropped dead on its pole. A whole street of stillness, sudden as a held breath. The fruit-seller fanned herself and complained the day had broken. The apprentices laughed the shaky laugh and went for wine.

Yangyang stood in the stopped air and did the arithmetic.

That night she opened her window and sat on the sill with her arms crossed.

"I saw the banners."

The curtain moved, a very small amount.

"I'm an Outrider." She kept her voice level, the report voice. "Danger is my wage. I signed for it. You're out there spending yourself on my portion, and I—" The report voice ran out. She looked at her hands. "I left home to carry my own weight. It's the whole of what I am. You of all weather should know that."

The curtain hung still. The lamp flame leaned toward the window and forgot to wander.

"…Fine." She blew out a breath. "New terms. If you're going to haunt me, do it like a person." She stood, decided, the way she decided everything, all at once and permanently. "Come to dinner."

At Panhua's the next evening she set a third bowl at the end of the table and filled it and said nothing, and the table conducted its own investigation.

"Are we feeding your someone now?" Chixia leaned around the teapot to inspect the bowl. "Bold. I respect it. Does your someone like the crispy thing? Everyone likes the crispy thing." She put a piece in the bowl, so it wouldn't go to waste, and then left it there.

"Caloric intent noted," Baizhi said, and moved the bowl three finger-widths out of the draught from the door, which put it directly into a different draught, the one that had no business existing, and said nothing further, and underlined something in her private book.

The steam off the third bowl leaned all through dinner, one way, like a flame with company.

The dawn drills changed that same week. She was on the wall at first light running the cutting forms, and on the fortieth stroke her guard drifted the old half-inch, the flaw her teacher used to fix with two fingers and a sigh — and a pressure arrived at her right elbow. Exact. Patient. The correction.

She froze mid-form. The gulls went on with their morning.

Then she ran the form again and drifted the guard on purpose, watching the air.

The pressure came back to the elbow and stayed a fraction longer, the way you hold a correction for a student you suspect of being clever.

Yangyang lowered the sword and looked out at the lake for a long time, and in the second ink that night she wrote three words and ruled a box around them.

*Same school. Senior.*

## 大暑 — Great Heat
*Log: still. Twenty-one days.*

The stillness came in with the great heat and stayed.

Cicadas owned the city. The banners hung like wet laundry. The lake lay flat as a poured mirror, and up at the yamen, the runners said, Her Excellency slept badly — the Magistrate dreams things, everyone knows, and in the still weeks she dreamed nothing at all, and it turned out nothing was worse.

Yangyang worked her shifts and kept the log. *Still. Still. Still.* She caught herself, on the ninth day, saving things — Chixia's war with the tax office goose, the melon vendor's opera voice, a cloud shaped exactly like Sergeant Mo — filing the day's small absurdities under *to tell*, and arriving home each evening to a window where the telling had nowhere to go. She told the room anyway, twice, out loud, and heard how it sounded, and after that she wrote the absurdities in the second ink instead, which was quieter but kept better.

On the twenty-first night the stillness broke. One night of wind, westbound and businesslike, in and out of the city like a courier who knows the address — and in the morning her desk stood exactly as she had left it. The inkstone on the letter. The letter under the inkstone. A season of salutations, and one page finally finished, folded once: *To the one the wind is looking for.*

She picked it up and read it, and her stomach dropped through the floor, gently.

The words were all there. That was the strange part. Every stroke where she had left it — and the page felt *performed*. Like sheet music after the concert. Like a room where your name has just been said.

She sat down on the bed with the letter in both hands and let the panic arrive and introduce itself — private words, gone somewhere, gone *west* — and then she did what she did with everything, which was turn it over and look at the workmanship.

The wind had carried her letter to its someone.

She thought about the wind's someone. She had built a small picture of them over the years, from evidence: someone worth crossing a country for. Someone the spring detoured to reach her by. She thought about her own someone, the person the letters were for, the pull she'd stopped explaining to herself around the same time she stopped explaining the wind — the certainty, ridiculous, load-bearing, that somewhere a stranger was owed a seat at her table.

She thought about the shape of a wind that would carry the first person's letter to the second person, and how those two errands would fold together if — if —

She got up and opened the window all the way, into the wake of the going.

"Tell her the seat's saved," she called after it, into the ordinary morning.

Then, much smaller, inside, where it could be tidied away before inspection: *and come back.*

## 七夕 — The Double Seventh
*Log: magpies. Wind returned.*

Suisui's parcel came the week of the festival. Inside, in oiled cloth, wrapped the way you dress a wound: their mother's xiao.

The note said: *Ma says the damp is bad for it. She says nothing else. You know how she says nothing. — S.*

*P.S. Solstice. Come home when it's cold. I already told the kitchen.*

Yangyang unwrapped the flute on her bunk and held it across both palms like something asleep. She had chosen the sword at eight, in the courtyard, with the whole family watching. The flute had watched from the shelf. Twelve years of watching.

She put it to her lips, there on the bunk, and blew one note to see, and the note came out clean and centered and in tune, and she lowered the flute and looked at it for a long moment, because the flute had crossed a country in a box and had spent twelve years in a storeroom, and instruments forget, everyone knows instruments forget.

The flute arrived in tune.

She wrapped it back up carefully, and thought about her mother's hands, and said nothing, to anyone, which was, she supposed, the family craft.

On festival night Chixia shoved two skewers into her hand at the lantern rafts and pointed her at the wall stairs with her chin. "Go stand on your wall, weirdo." The whole street smelled of sugar and gunpowder. "Bring your someone a skewer."

"It's for the fire warden," Baizhi said, deadpan, appearing at her other shoulder with festival efficiency. "Incidentally: Huaxu's archive holds a petition to found a county named Qixi. Refused twice. The Board of Rites ruled it inauspicious to name land after a parting." She adjusted her glasses at the fireworks. "I have always thought the Board lacked romance."

"You've always thought the Board lacked romance."

"I said that."

"You said it like a diagnosis."

Yangyang left them to it and climbed to the northeast shoulder with two skewers and her mother's flute, under the whole spilled river of stars.

The wind came at moonrise, and it came home thin.

She felt the difference the way you feel a friend's bad month in their handshake — the spring hand was in it still, the palm that had decided to be gentle, and it was paper now, it was onionskin, it was a warmth arriving on the last of itself. It stopped on her cheek. It stayed.

"Long trip," she said.

The night held them both.

She laid the skewers on the parapet, one and one, and unwrapped the flute, and stood it against her lip.

"Ma's craft," she said. "I'm out of practice. Complaints go to the Board of Rites."

The first notes came out crooked. The old lullaby, the one for summer fevers, and her fingers remembered more than her breath did, and at the phrase where the tune goes up and stays up she felt her lungs reach the bottom —

— and the note continued.

Her breath had ended. The note went on, round and full, pouring up out of the flute like water finding its level, and she stood there with her fingers on the holes and her eyes very wide while something breathed through her mother's instrument from the other side, and the sound they made together was a third thing, wearing both of them, and below the wall the whole festival paused in the middle of its sugar — she heard it pause — and a child's voice said, *look,* to the sky.

They finished the lullaby. Her fingering, its breath. Where her lungs gave out, it poured; where it thinned, she carried. The last note they split without discussing it, half and half, and it faded off the wall and out over the black water like a lantern let go.

Yangyang lowered the flute.

"The post in my courtyard," she said. "Three inches. One stroke."

The night listened.

"That's her stroke. My teacher's." Her thumb moved on the flute's mouthpiece, back and forth, a small maintenance of courage. "The elbow correction. The arithmetic. You give the stroke everything, and what the wood refuses—" Her voice did something and she let it. "You trained under her. Ahead of me. Years ahead, by the cut."

The wind stood in front of her, whatever that meant for wind, and she felt it the way you feel a held breath in a dark room: the whole night, waiting on a word.

"師姐," she said.

*Elder sister of my school.*

And the wind sat down. The wall's air, the banners' whisper, the river's static — everything settled at once, all around her, into a stillness with a center, and the center was warm, and it was the deepest quiet she had ever stood inside, and it lasted three heartbeats and then the flute in her hands, untouched at the mouth, sounded one note, low and even.

Yes.

Yangyang laughed, once, and it came out wet, and she wiped her face with her sleeve in the practical way and picked up the first skewer and ate it, standing guard over the second one, under the river of stars where the magpies were building their annual bridge out of their own bodies, which was, she thought, the going rate for bridges, apparently, everywhere.

"When you find her," she said, later, to the sky. "Will spring still come by here?"

The wind lingered on her face. A long time. Longer than an answer.

"…Forget I asked." She adjusted her hat. The little bird held its stitch. "Eat your skewer."

She left the second skewer on the parapet, and walked down at the change of watch, and in the morning it was still there, cold in a warm dawn, and it looked, to her, finished. She ate it on the walk to muster, and it lasted the whole stair.

## 白露 — White Dew
*Log: the golden wind.*

Autumn came in the classical manner, all high air and long light, and the dawn drills became lessons.

The correction at her elbow arrived earlier now — before the fault, where her teacher's had come after — a pressure that knew her errors by heart and met them at the door. She drilled the cutting forms against an opponent every stroke passed through, and that graded her footwork like a creditor, and she improved the way you improve under family: fast, and complaining.

"Show-off," she said, one morning, when a falling willow leaf came apart midair in front of her, halved clean along the spine by nothing, both pieces spiraling down in formation. The air had a smugness to it. She would have testified to the smugness before the Board of Rites. She cut the next leaf herself, quarter-second late, one edge ragged, and the wind ruffled her hat brim in the precise cadence of applause performed slowly.

Chixia had started saluting gusts. "Covering my bases," she said. "If the weather's promoted, I want it remembered I was polite early."

Baizhi handed Yangyang a folder in the clinic doorway on the equinox, without preamble. Inside: a year of the ledger's oddities, plotted. Saves against shifts. Weather against watch rosters. Two lines on one chart, walking together like old women.

"Correlation study," Baizhi said. "Unpublished. Read the last page."

The last page held a single note in Baizhi's smallest hand: *Underweight, your weather. Whatever changed at the festival — continue.*

Yangyang looked up. Baizhi was already looking at her, flame-red eyes level over the glasses, a researcher's whole heart hidden in a filing system, and the two of them stood in the doorway having the entire conversation without spending a word of it.

"Lunch?" Yangyang said.

"Lunch."

That season the cause column filled again. Three saves in the ninth month. *Weather.* Underlined.

## 霜降 — Frost Descends
*Log: north wind. Drums.*

The tremors started past the north face in the frost month. The garrison felt the first one through their boot soles at supper — a pressure in the ground more than a sound, one slow beat, far off, patient — and the beacons went to double watch, and Keeper Pei, trimming lamps with forty years of steady hands, said the thing the wall was declining to say: "Eight days since the last one." He set the glass. "The one before came ten apart. The one before that, twelve."

Somebody down the wall said maybe it was settling.

"It's counting," Pei said, and trimmed the next lamp.

The bad night came at month's end. An outwork on the north road, a sortie caught by a rockfall with something underneath the rockfall, and the wall watched a whole cliff face let go above Third Squad and watched Third Squad walk out of it — every one of them — stumbling out of a boil of dust through a corridor of air that stood open in the collapse like a held door, and the door held, and held, and held, until the last man cleared it, and then the mountain finished falling.

The cause column got its entry. The whole wall saw what the word cost.

Yangyang came off shift at the grey hour and found her window as she'd left it, the flute on the sill where it lived now, and the wind came to greet her the way it had greeted her every evening that season — through the instrument, one low note, their private *home again* —

The note cracked.

It came out in a thread, wavered, guttered like a lamp at the end of its oil, and stopped, a quarter of the way through itself, and the silence after it was the loudest thing she had heard all year.

She stood in the middle of her room with her sword still on her back.

Then she went to the lake.

She went down through the sleeping streets and out along the bank to the stretch below the beacon, where the grass grows in the long soft rows the keepers leave alone, and she knelt at the waterline in the frost, and she gave the black water the Outrider's full bow, because you address the office, her teacher had said, whatever you think of the god.

"Sentinel." Her breath stood in the air. "Outrider Yangyang, Mingting registry, Jinzhou posting. Filing a request outside my rank."

The lake lay still, holding the last stars.

"It comes here. Fourth watch, when the wall's asleep. It tells you—" Her hands closed on her knees. "Everyone gives it their weight to carry, the whole country, we've been doing it for years, it's the custom, and it carries all of it, and it comes here and gives the weight to you, because you're the only thing in Huanglong bigger than what it's holding."

The water breathed against the bank, in, out.

"You keep what it tells you." She bowed again, forehead to the frost. "Keep *it.*"

Far down, under the held stars, the lake turned over. Slowly. With great courtesy. The lamps along the crest wall guttered once, all together, and steadied.

She knelt there until the cold came through her knees, and got an answer the size of a god's silence, and walked back up in the dark.

At her window the wind was waiting. It went to the flute and tried again — their note, the low one — and managed half of it, a true warm half, and frayed.

Yangyang picked up the flute, set her lips where the breath was failing, and finished the note.

Her breath, its beginning. One sound, wearing both of them, the way it had on festival night with the directions reversed, and she held their note to its full length and let it fade properly, the way notes deserve, out over the frost and the black water and the counting dark beyond the mountains.

"New terms," she said softly, to the air by her cheek. "I carry too."

## 大雪 — Great Snow
*Log: snow.*

The summons came on Bureau paper with the Magistrate's private seal, which happens to Outriders about once a dynasty, and the veterans of the muster yard watched her go up through the falling snow with the particular silence of men revising their memories of everything they had ever said in front of her.

Jinhsi received her standing, at the window, with the lake grey below and the file open on the desk and the flat voice she saves for true things.

"Outrider. Your registry says Mingting."

"Yes, Excellency."

"The lake dreams." Jinhsi turned a page without reading it. "You will have heard. On wind nights it dreams a library burning that has never burned. Floodwater on dry streets. A red cap." Another page. "The runners map the dreams. The dreams walk your patrol lines."

The snow ticked at the window. Yangyang stood at attention and said nothing, which was, she was discovering, the family craft.

"On still nights," Jinhsi said, "it dreams a girl. A courtyard, a black post, a wooden sword. The girl cuts at the post. The lake wakes me, every time, before the stroke lands." She closed the file. Her eyes came up, gold and level. "Last night it dreamed you, Outrider. Standing where you are standing now. I have summoned you to reduce the lake's workload."

"Excellency." Yangyang held the gaze, because her teacher had taught her that too. "I'm finding out. I'll report to you when I have it whole."

"To me alone."

"To you alone."

Jinhsi studied her for the length of a held breath — the Magistrate of the youngest gate, who is being remembered by something, and has begun to suspect the something is fond — and then she looked back to the lake, and the audience was over, and Yangyang was at the door when the flat voice came again, quieter, off the record, addressed half to the window.

"The girl at the post. A year of dreams, and she never lands the cut. The lake always wakes me first." A pause the exact width of a magistrate deciding to be a person. "I find I want very much to see her land it."

Yangyang stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame.

"She will, Excellency," she said. "She's had good teachers."

She bowed, and went out into the snow.

## 冬至 — Winter Solstice
*Log: wind rises. The first yang is born.*

Leave was granted for the solstice, as promised, as threatened. She rode the post road west with the flute in her pack and her breath standing white ahead of her, and Mingting received her the way home receives anyone: as if she had stepped out an hour ago on an errand, and where had she put the good ladle.

At dusk, with the kitchen lit and loud behind her, she went out alone to the courtyard.

The post stood where it had stood all her life. Black-grey, iron through and through, holding twelve years of her — the shallow years, cut above cut above cut, a childhood of mornings written in tenths of inches — and across them all, higher, this year's guest-stroke: one cut, three inches deep, the grain parted like water.

The wooden sword stood leaned at its foot, edge to the east, where a guest had left it.

She picked it up. It knew her hands.

She took her stance in the snow-lit dark, and the year arranged itself in her arms — the ribbon in the trough, the incense that bent at her, the third bowl's leaning steam, the letter gone west, the lullaby with its impossible note, *shijie*, the door of air that held while a squad walked out, the half-note finished, the lake turning over, a magistrate at a window wanting a stranger's stroke to land — all of it, the whole almanac, gathering into her shoulders the way her teacher had promised things gather, if you carry them honestly, all the way.

She breathed out.

And the solstice wind came over the courtyard wall.

It came down the roof tiles and along her arms like a sleeve being rolled, the year's first north-born warmth inside the year's deepest cold, and she felt it settle into the stroke with her — her wrists, its weight; her line, its length — and there was a moment, mid-swing, where she could have said whose arm this was and chose to lose track instead —

The sound the post made was one she had never gotten out of it in twelve years.

She stood in the follow-through, breathing. Snow came off the roof in a soft applause. The cut was there under her palm when she covered it — she covered it without looking, flat hand over parted grain, warm wood in the cold —

"Together doesn't count," she told the courtyard, and heard how happy she sounded, and let that stand too. "When I can do it alone, I'll look."

The kitchen door opened behind her, spilling gold. Suisui, calling her for dinner in the tone that had fetched her out of this courtyard ten thousand times.

Inside, the table was crowded and steaming. Her father was losing an argument with the stove. Suisui was winning an argument with the seating. Her mother moved down the table setting places with the unhurried exactness she brought to everything — one, two, three, four —

— and a fifth pair of chopsticks, laid neat at the empty end, at the seat nearest the door.

The kitchen went on around it. Suisui's eyes came up, found Yangyang's, and asked. Yangyang's answered, and asked back. Their mother set down the last bowl, said nothing, in the fluent way she said it, and out in the courtyard the wind moved once around the post and settled at the eaves, and the lamps leaned, all together, toward the table, and steadied.

*Log, final entry:*
*冬至。風起。一陽生。*
*Solstice. Wind rises. Inside the deepest cold, the first warmth is born.*

# Become a Voice

*a chaconne, for Cartethyia*

> *O lyre, divine shell — speak.*
> *Find yourself a voice.*
> — Sappho, fr. 118

---

Lahai-Roi dimmed its sun by committee. At the twentieth hour Helios cooled from gold to bronze, from bronze to a low red coal, and the false stars came on across the cavern ceiling in their programmed constellations, each one named by a subcommittee, each one wrong. Ciaccona had slept under real stars often enough to find the errors restful. A sky that admitted to being built was ahead of most institutions she had known.

She sat cross-legged on her dormitory bed with the lute across her knees, tuning down. The instrument had been her father's, and it had been broken by a playwright and mended, broken by a god and mended, and this afternoon a puppeteer in a schoolgirl's shape had staged its breaking a third time inside her own skull, and she had spent the evening on a dance pad sweating the image out through her feet. Now she loosened each string a quarter turn, the way you loosen a horse's girth at the end of a day. The lute exhaled.

Two of her Sylphs drowsed in the curtain rod. The third was investigating the window latch with the diligence of a small burglar.

The knock came as a formality delivered at the same instant as the door opening.

"You're supposed to wait for the answer."

"You'd have said come in." Cartethyia came in. Thermos under one arm, a paper sack of arcade candy in her fist, a plush gryphon the size of a bread loaf wedged in her belt like a second sword. Her hair still held the shape of the dance machine's headband. "Iuno's with Mei. Lupa took Chisa to the roof. Lynae's teaching Sigrika to cheat at cards."

"Which is her love language."

"Which is her love language." The thermos landed on the desk with the decisiveness of a planted flag. "So it's you and me."

"So it is. Sit down before you knock something over."

Cartethyia sat down and knocked over the tuning fork. She picked it up, struck it against her knee out of curiosity, and held it to her ear while it sang. The Sylph at the window abandoned the latch, drifted over, and settled on her shoulder to listen with her.

They divided the candy. This was the ritual the pack had arrived at by instinct: pair off, sit down, say what the rooms had shown. It went in both directions; that was the whole law of it.

"You first," Ciaccona said. "You'll do it fast and honest, and then I'll owe you."

Cartethyia unwrapped a candy and ate it with the frown of someone reading terms and conditions.

"It showed me the tower."

"Which year?"

"First. Before the dust settled anywhere. When everything still smelled like other people." She turned the wrapper over in her fingers. "It got the light right. The sound, even — a sealed building has a sound. A sort of held breath."

"I remember the sound."

"It had Ser Pincushion. Half-sewn. Before I found the good thread." A pause. "And then she skimmed it. Twenty years, flipped like a ledger she'd already audited. *Boring. Nothing happens. Next.*"

"And?"

"And the room agreed with her."

Ciaccona waited. Cartethyia's voice had gone bright and steady, which was her voice lying about the weather.

"That's the part I owe you," Cartethyia said. "For one second, so did I. I looked at that girl sewing in the dust and thought — *she's right. You're a held breath. A rock in a field.* Twenty years of my life dismissed by a monster, and my first instinct was to apologize for the pacing."

"And then?"

"Then I broke her furniture." She ate another candy, ferociously. "But the second came first. That's my truth. Your turn."

"Mine showed the lute smashed."

"I know. You said so in the debrief. In your funny voice. Everyone laughed."

"That was the plan."

"The rest of it, then."

Ciaccona picked a thread off her cuff. "When it broke — in there, in her staging — I had one clear thought before the fear arrived. The thought was: *there it is. The last note. The song's over.* And the fear that came after was almost a relief. Because the thought had sounded so much like the truth." She shrugged. "Tomorrow, if anyone puts a fortune-teller in front of me, I'll probably say it out loud in front of everyone. Funny voice. Laughter. Moving on. The thing I'm afraid of is the end of the song. That's the confession. It's very short."

"It's not," said Cartethyia. "It's the title."

Ciaccona looked at her.

"You do this. You hand people the title and let them think they've read the book. Mei catches, Lupa carries, I break, Iuno counts — and you make everyone else's grief rhyme, and then you go to bed." She pulled the thermos over, cracked it, poured tea into the cup and the cup's lid, and pushed the cup — the good one — across the desk. "The rule tonight is the truth. The title is a down payment. What happened to you out there, before us? You know all my years. Sing me yours."

The Sylph on her shoulder turned to look at Ciaccona too, which was mutiny.

Ciaccona drank. The tea was Iuno's blend; the whole pack stole it now; the thief economy of the group had grown very sophisticated. Then she laid the lute flat across her knees, unplayed, a promise of accompaniment, and her own hand's honesty amused her — that even now the story would come out of her sideways, through wood and gut, or it would come out costumed.

"It's long. If you fall asleep I'll change the details out of spite."

"I never fall asleep during stories."

"I know," said Ciaccona. "That's why you get this one."

"Start with a question. What's a toccata?"

"A family of criminals."

"Before that."

Cartethyia considered. "A touch-piece. You sit down at a strange instrument and touch it all over, fast, to find out what it can do."

"Look at you."

"You wince at me when I hum. It's very educational."

"A touch-piece. A first meeting, written in music. My family took it for a surname eleven generations back, and then, because we were insufferable, we started naming the children after forms too. My grandmother's doing. She said a name should be an argument."

"What was your uncle's?"

"Ricercare. The seeking — the learned fugue that hunts a theme through every voice." Ciaccona's mouth curved. "And my father was Passacaglia."

Cartethyia frowned at her. "That's nearly your name."

"That's the joke. A passacaglia and a chaconne are almost the same dance. Both are variations over a ground — a short line in the bass, repeating and repeating, while everything above it changes. The learned quarters of Ragunna spent two hundred years fighting about the difference."

"So your grandmother named you and your father one each—"

"—one generation apart, to keep the fight going. She said the day the guilds settled it, the family had permission to name a child something sensible. Like Rosa."

Cartethyia laughed — the short, surprised bark she saved for things that were actually funny. Then she caught up to the arithmetic and stopped. "You're the answer to an argument."

"I'm the argument's *continuation*. There's a difference. Ask the guilds."

The house Ciaccona described, between sips of stolen tea, was a house where the bass line ran under dinner: her father playing rehearsals in the mornings, weddings in the afternoons, truth after dark — and the truth was the Poets Society, and the key, and the hidden study. Cartethyia knew that part. She had stood inside Ciaccona's sheet music once and lectured a memory of her, which remained, Ciaccona informed her, the strangest kindness anyone had ever done her, and she included the resurrections.

"They came for my mother first. Over a pamphlet she had *folded*. She kissed my head, recited the pantry inventory like a psalm, and went. Then Ricercare, whose crime was being a Toccata. Then, the spring I turned sixteen — my father."

"The doorstep."

"The doorstep. Key into my hand, acolytes standing right there, in full view — a thing done openly looks innocent. Then the sentence you know. Then he complimented the youngest acolyte's boots. Sincerely. And walked out of my life making small talk."

Cartethyia said it quietly, the way you handle someone else's relic. "*Don't hide the truth in your heart. Raise it like a torch, so it lights the way for others.*"

"Mm. Beautiful sentence. It scans. It has a torch in it." Ciaccona turned the cup on the desk, a slow half-circle. "It's also a sentence composed by a man being arrested."

"...Exit music."

"Exit music. Last line before the curtain. And last lines are written for the *audience*, Cartethyia — he was a musician, and a musician's instinct under the knife is to make the dying phrase carry. He gave me the truest thing he had in the most memorable shape he could build in four seconds."

"You've spent your whole life finding out what it means."

"That search *is* the story." She tapped the lute's soundboard, once. "The sentence is the ground bass. Everything I'm about to tell you is variations."

The banishment itself took a paragraph. The Order let her keep the house three more years — the Order composts, and compost takes years — while she taught lute to merchant daughters, hid the key inside a tuning peg, and was, on paper, reformed. And at night she did exactly what her father told her: set the poets' discoveries, proper settings, good voice-leading, a song cycle that would have earned a standing ovation in a world one degree kinder. She premiered it in a wine cellar for forty people. Inside a month she was on a list. Inside a season, a boat.

"They gave my father a Pilgrim's Sail," she said. "They gave me a ferry ticket and a document with a seal on it. Banishment had been rationalized. There was a form."

"How old?"

"Nineteen. One lute, one key, one sentence."

"First variation. I did it literally."

"The torch."

"The torch. There's a harbor town on the windward coast called Varrena — you'd like it, everything in it is the color of a mussel shell, and the bells run a quarter-tone flat and have for a century. The whole town hums flat. It ruins you for other bells."

"Sounds like my kind of choir."

"You'd be their soloist." Ciaccona let her have the grin, then took it back. "I stood on the seawall and sang the truth of the Poets Society at full voice to the fishing fleet coming in. Names, dates, the ship, the fire. Good projection. My father would have wept at the diction."

"And the fleet?"

"Looked at me the way you look at weather."

Cartethyia winced.

"An innkeeper took me in anyway. Brigida. Corner by the fire, white bean soup with fennel, my whole cycle for her evening crowd — every night, for a week." Ciaccona held up eight fingers. "On the eighth day the acolytes came. And here's the part I need you to hear exactly, because it took *me* years to hear it. They walked straight past me."

Cartethyia's brow creased. "Past you."

"To Brigida's door. Where they chalked a fool's cap. That's all. Chalk. A little drawing, like a child's."

"...Chalk."

"The fish-buyers stopped coming, because being seen there had acquired a price. Her daughter's betrothal went quiet. The customs clerk found a discrepancy in cellar records that had been fine for twenty years." Ciaccona spread her hands. "The chalk did the whole day's work of a garrison."

Cartethyia had gone still, the way she went still on watch. "And Brigida?"

"Came to me on the ninth morning with bread and cheese in good cloth — she meant me to keep the cloth — and said, kind the whole way through: *You sing beautifully, girl, and every word is true, and I believe you, and you have to go sing it somewhere I'm not.*"

The false stars ticked through their rotation. Cartethyia said nothing, which from her was a whole speech.

"I stood on the road out of Varrena with warm bread in my hands and did the arithmetic. My torch was lit. The way was illuminated. And every soul who stepped into that light became a target the archers could see." Ciaccona's voice stayed light; the light was doing heavy work. "The Order solved martyrs generations ago — a martyr is expensive, a martyr sings from the grave. They built something better. A world where the truth costs most to whoever stands nearest the one telling it. They let the torch burn. They taxed the light."

"And you kept singing."

"For a while. Town after town. And met the second refinement, which was crueler." She looked up. "When I sang the truth to strangers — the documented, provable truth — do you know what they heard?"

Cartethyia thought about a square in Ragunna, a laurel, a bounty. "Madness."

"A young woman alone on the road, shouting that the god is a horse and the saints were murdered and the curse is a forgery. The Order had spent a generation making that speech the recognized sound of a cracked mind. They composed my *genre* in advance. An acolyte in Fossagrigia listened to my whole set with real tears in his eyes, pressed a coin into my hand, and told me — gently — that the Order ran a charitable house for women afflicted as I was."

"He pitied you."

"He went home feeling *holy*." Ciaccona flicked the memory off her fingers. "I stood there holding his coin and understood I could sing from every seawall in Rinascita, and the net effect on the world would be a rise in charitable donations."

Cartethyia was quiet a moment. Then: "The café in Septimont. When Mei was drowning politely over her tea. I told you they called me crazy too, and you laughed."

"You thought I was laughing at the joke."

"You were laughing at the fellowship."

"I was laughing at the fellowship."

"Second variation. Third winter out. This is the one where the road started teaching instead of taking."

Narration could have carried her there faster than she told it: hill country inland, a town called Miravalle — a lie, the valley visible from nowhere in it — and a bard broke in the way where you start seeing bread in geometry. Her knuckles had cracked from the cold. She could still play; she played like a talented drunk. Fingering slow, ornaments gone, the odd note landing next door to itself. She needed the coins, so she sat in the tavern by the grain exchange and butchered pavanes for farmers, her father's daughter, hating every bar.

"Third evening, I made a hash of a lullaby. The primer version — the Order publishes the approved verse, every region gets one. And a woman by the hearth put down her mending and said, loud enough for the whole room—"

"*Wrong.*"

"—*Wrong*. Seventy if a day. Hands like tree roots. Shawl the color of onion skins." Ciaccona's eyes had gone bright. "And then she fixed it. The *words*. She sang me the verse her grandmother rocked her with, in a voice like a hinge, and I'll give it to you the way she gave it to me."

She sang it. Quietly, under the false stars, in a dormitory inside a kneeling god:

> *Sleep, little oar, the sea rocks your bed.*
> *The sky trims the lamp at your mother's head.*
> *Two hands on the cradle, one salt and one bright —*
> *the water is family. Goodnight. Goodnight.*

Cartethyia sat very straight. "Two hands."

"Two hands."

"The old faith. The double gods. That's what the poets *died* for—"

"—preserved wholesale in a rhyme for infants." Ciaccona nodded slowly. "The Order edited the anthem, the liturgy, the histories, the glass. And this woman — who had never read a heresy in her life, who'd have boxed my ears for suggesting she carried one — had the doctrine intact in her *mouth*. Because the Order audited everything worth confiscating."

"And a lullaby is beneath confiscation."

"They burned the library, Cartethyia. They missed the nursery."

Cartethyia breathed out through her teeth, half laugh, half something older.

"And then the room joined in. The carter knew a different second line. The tapster's mother had a moon verse that started a small war between two tables. For an hour, that tavern argued cradle songs — corrected each other, corrected *me* — and I sat in the middle with my ruined hands, taking dictation in my head, rich beyond counting." Ciaccona lifted the cup in a toast to the dead. "At the end, the old woman — Fiora — patted my arm and said the sentence I've built a life on since. *You play like my granddaughter. Come back tomorrow and I'll teach you the rest.*"

"...She thought you were a beginner."

"She *stopped listening to a performer* and started teaching a granddaughter." Ciaccona set the cup down. "I lay awake all night with the shape of it. And in the next town I played badly on *purpose*. In cold blood. A trained musician fluffing her cadences like a card sharp dealing seconds."

"Did it work?"

"It has never once failed me since." She leaned forward. "Here's the mechanism, and it took me years to say it cleanly. People guard the truth from anyone who might *own* it. Play like a master, and you're an authority — and authorities are the people truth gets hidden from. They've had practice. But play like someone who *needs* — a beginner, a beggar, somebody's granddaughter—"

"—and they hand you the family silver."

"Because giving truth to the needy is what people are *for*. Oldest reflex in the species. Older than fear. And the Order, in all its genius, never found a way to tax it." She sat back, satisfied as a closing argument. "Fenrico could confiscate a manuscript. Show me the primus who can confiscate a room full of grandmothers correcting a bad musician."

Cartethyia was grinning. Then the grin narrowed into thought. "So that's what he meant. Your father. Or — what he should have meant."

"Go on."

"He said raise it like a torch, and you heard *blaze*." She turned the tuning fork over in her fingers. "But I've built fires. You hold a torch, you're blind past it, and everyone near you sees the torch and only the torch. And they can be *seen*, holding still, lit up." She looked up. "A low fire, though. People sit down at a low fire."

"And?"

"They bring their own wood."

Ciaccona pointed at her, the full length of the arm. "*They bring their own wood.* Ten years, that sentence took me, and you get it off a tuning fork." She shook her head. "Write the anthem rough, and the city rewrites it, and then it's *theirs*, and you can't arrest a chorus. I learned it broke and frostbitten in a grain town, from a woman who thought she was teaching a clumsy girl her heritage."

"Was she?"

"She was." A beat. "Fiora's dead now. I checked. Years back, in her bed, the verse intact in six grandchildren. The Order never learned her name." Ciaccona raised the cup again. "There's the score. Fiora one, theocracy nil."

Cartethyia clinked the lid against it.

"Third variation. Shorter. Costs more."

"Do I get more candy first?"

"You should."

Cartethyia unwrapped one and, after a glance at the desk, put a second in front of Ciaccona. The Sylph on her shoulder eyed it. She moved it out of reach.

"I asked after my family in every port. You do it sideways — you play badly, then steer the corrections. Exiles change their names; the smart ones change their instruments." Ciaccona's thumb found the lute's old repaired seam. "Four years in, in a river town I'll keep to myself, I walked past a pawnbroker's window and stopped like I'd been speared."

"What was in it?"

"Ricercare's fiddle."

Cartethyia's hand paused, candy halfway to her mouth.

"I'd have known it in the dark by the varnish — my uncle mixed his own, the whole house reeked of the resin every spring. It sat in that window with four of its eight strings gone and a water stain on the lower bout. Priced under what the case cost new."

"You bought it."

"Three days of meals. And the broker knew nothing — a quiet fellow pawned it seasons ago, dock hands, that's all." Ciaccona shrugged. "So I did the only thing I had. I stood outside that shop every market day and *played* it."

"Four strings of eight."

"You can get a tune off half a fiddle. A lousy tune. A limping thing. But a tune — and I played it *loud*, our family songs, the ones only a Toccata would know, hour after hour, so if he was anywhere in that town he'd hear his own strings calling him."

"How long?"

"Seven market days. On the seventh, a carter who bought fish at the docks heard me and said—" her voice shifted, easy, a man passing the time, "—*oh, the quiet fellow. Shipped out. Coastal freight, works under some other name now, been gone since summer. Steady work. He seemed well.*"

The words landed and sat there.

"Alive," Cartethyia said.

"Alive. Hidden. *Well.*" Ciaccona let a breath out through her nose. "And the creed stood up in me on its hind legs. *Find him. Light the torch. He's family — drag him back into the name.*"

"But."

"But a man hiding from the Order stays alive by staying unlit. His new name was the roof over his head. If I went down that coast singing the old songs, asking — every note was a lamp over his bed for the archers."

Cartethyia's gaze had dropped to the plush gryphon in her belt and gone straight through it. "What did you do?"

"I restrung nothing. I left the fiddle with the broker's daughter — ten years old, wanted to learn — with the varnish recipe written out fair. And I walked out of that town in the wrong direction, on purpose, and I have never looked for my uncle since." The funny voice was long gone. "He's out there somewhere hauling freight under a name I'll die without knowing. And my gift to him — the only gift the situation permitted — was silence. *Me.* Silence."

The room held it. Down the corridor, a shower ran and stopped.

"That's the amendment I'd nail to my father's door, if he had a door," Ciaccona said at last. "The truth is a torch, yes. And some people survive by darkness. A singer who forgets that is an informer with a nicer voice."

"...Dosage."

"Song is medicine, and medicine is dosage. I've watched the overdose." She met Cartethyia's eyes. "I stood at the edge of a square in Ragunna and watched fifty thousand people witness you at once. All that love and attention, poured onto one girl in one instant."

"And it woke the thing in my blood."

"You nearly died of being *seen*, Cartethyia. So when the saga-books say witness saves — and it does, I've staked my life on it, I'll stake it again — remember there's a bard in your pack keeping the other ledger too. Who gets sung. How loud. When." A beat. "And who gets the mercy of the dark."

Cartethyia turned the gryphon's ear between two fingers. "The acolyte in Egla. The one who watched me come home. She kept saying *I didn't see anything.*"

"And walked away without reporting it."

"I thought she was a coward. A little. At the time." She looked up. "She was dosing."

"She was the best musician in that square," Ciaccona said. "Present company included."

*] which is the other forgetting [*

"Fourth variation." Ciaccona refilled the cup and the lid, and this time gave the good cup back, and Cartethyia let her, which between them was a whole negotiated treaty. "The ground bass, you'll remember, is a sentence spoken by a man on his way to the sea."

"You went looking."

"Sixth year. I'd put it off — you know how you can put off a whole direction of the compass." Cartethyia, who had circled her own hometown for twenty years, nodded once. "But a bard hears things. The Pilgrim's Sails all clear the land at one place. A headland south of the Ragunnan reach — the last finger of Rinascita, where the coast pilots hand the ships to open water and turn back. There's a fire tower on it."

"And in the tower?"

"An old man. Tammaro. Order lamplighter, forty years, and when they pensioned him off he just... stayed. Past the edge of the map of anyone's caring. And on his own coin, in ledgers he sewed himself from sailcloth and packing paper, he logged every ship that rounded the headland. Every one. Date, weather, rig, heading. Forty years of it."

"Why?"

"I asked him that." Ciaccona held up three fingers. "He gave me the entire theology of his life in three words. *Someone should.*"

Cartethyia sat with it. "...That's a whole religion."

"It's a better one than the licensed product." Ciaccona's hand had gone flat on the soundboard. "I told him the year and the season. He knew what I was the moment he saw the lute — girls with lutes come to that tower for one reason. He climbed to the shelf, brought down the right ledger, found the page, and turned it to face me. He was the kind of man who lets you read for yourself."

"What did it say?"

"One line. Lamplighter's script — the letters formed the way they teach you, in case the log ever matters to a court. The date. *Pilgrim's Sail, out of Ragunna. Wind fresh from the west. One aboard.*" She paused, and the pause was the length of a mile of water. "And then, because Tammaro logged what he observed, and he had observed it through his glass: *Singing.*"

Cartethyia's breath went out of her slowly.

"That's my inheritance. The complete surviving record of my father's death. One aboard. Singing." Ciaccona's voice stayed level, which was its own kind of screaming. "The wind was from the west; it carried the sound out to sea. So what Tammaro had was the posture. The chest. The open mouth. A man at the rail, giving a performance to an audience of gulls."

"Ciaccona—"

"Wait. There's a stupid part next, and you need the stupid part."

Cartethyia closed her mouth and waited.

"You know what my Forte does. The wind is the world's honest recorder — everything sung outdoors is filed in the weather. And the sea keeps *everything*. Every drowned voice, every deck song, every mother calling from every shore since the first shore. So I climbed down to the rocks below the tower at slack tide." She spread one hand over the strings. "And I rang the water. I asked the sea for my father."

"...What did it give you?"

"Everything."

The word sat in the room like weather.

"All of it. At once. One chord — ten thousand griefs, forty generations, every ship, every storm, every name called across water since names began, undifferentiated, fortissimo. A single vertical block of total memory, with my father in it *somewhere*, one voice in a noise the size of the sky." Her jaw tightened, remembering. "I came to on the rocks with blood in my left ear and Tammaro hauling me up by the collar. He fed me fish soup and said — in the tone of a man who has watched that exact scene before — *The water's a grave, girl. Graves keep everything and give back nothing. That's what makes them graves.*"

Cartethyia had gone very still again. When she spoke, it was slowly, a sword being drawn an inch at a time. "We've met your sea."

"We have."

"The cathedral. The ceiling of heartbeats." Her eyes came up. "Ten thousand griefs, all kept, and none of them *heard*."

"I knew that god the moment it spoke," Ciaccona said quietly. "I'd met it at slack tide when I was twenty-five. It's the sea's idea of remembering. Total. Loveless. The same as the dark."

"And the Order is the other one."

"Say it."

"The Order edits. Keeps what power needs, burns the rest — memory as property." Cartethyia was working it through her hands, the way she worked everything. "And the sea keeps all of it, forever, equally, until keeping and forgetting are the same weather. Memory as flood." She looked up. "The archive with an owner. The archive with no singer."

"Both of them are oblivion."

"So what's between them?"

"Us." Ciaccona said it plainly, one workman naming another's trade. "A singer *chooses*. That's the job. Out of everything, this verse. Out of everyone, this name. Selection, with love — which is all a song is, when you take the varnish off. A third memory. The *sung* memory: partial, biased, warm, and alive, because someone with a heartbeat decided it mattered and spent breath on it."

Cartethyia looked at her for a long moment. "If the fortune-teller pulls the Hanged Man on you tomorrow—"

"I'll claim him happily. The witness. The one who dangles at an angle to the world and watches." Ciaccona's mouth curved. "Somebody has to hang in the rigging and choose what gets carried."

*] what he sang, the gulls keep [*

---

"Last variation. What I built out of the page."

"The song." Cartethyia had been waiting for it; you could hear it. "You wrote him one."

"I wrote him a *hole*." Ciaccona's fingers had drifted, at some point, into playing position; both of them let it pass. "Understand what I had. A date. A wind. And *singing*. So the song was short, and I made the hole on purpose — center of the last verse, where the melody climbs and stops on the open fifth. Like a door left ajar."

She played it, barely — a thumbnail sketch of a tune — and gave the words underneath:

> *He carried a key and they gave him the water.*
> *He stood at the rail and he sang out the leaving —*
> *— and here the verse waits —*
> *what he sang, the gulls keep.*

"And then?"

"Then I took my own father's death-song into the taverns down the coast, and I played it *badly*. Dealt it off the bottom of the deck. Fumbled at the door of the hole, every single night."

Cartethyia's eyes widened as the shape of it landed. "The granddaughter reflex."

"Every single night, some fisherman or ship's cook or harbor widow leaned in and *fixed it*. Filled the hole. Told me what a man like that would have sung." Ciaccona was smiling now, helplessly, the way you smile at weather you didn't order and needed. "In Varrena — I went back; Brigida's daughter runs the inn now; the chalk mark outlived the marriage prospects of everyone who believed in it — a sailor stood up and gave the hole a verse where the man shares his last water with the gulls. His reasoning, and I quote a drunk oracle: *a singer knows his audience.*"

Cartethyia made a sound that was mostly a laugh and partly something with salt in it.

"Down the coast, a caulker's wife added a verse where he blesses the ship itself. *Bless the wood, boys — the wood is innocent.* Which is the most Ragunnan sentence ever composed. We absolve the timber and indict the shipwright."

"God. I love us."

"And in a town called Lastra—" Ciaccona held up a hand, pre-empting, savoring it, "—a woman with flour to her elbows gave him an *argument*. A whole verse where the man spends his last night out-debating the sea. On a point of music theory."

"...No."

"On — I swear this to you on the key I still carry — *the difference between a passacaglia and a chaconne*."

"*No.*"

"And *wins*. And the sea concedes, and takes him under out of *spite*." Ciaccona's hands had stopped on the strings. "She'd never heard our name, Cartethyia. She had no idea what she'd done. She reached into the hole in a stranger's song and pulled out my grandmother's joke — whole and living — from the far side of a man's death."

Cartethyia had both hands over her mouth. Above them, all three Sylphs were awake now, ranged along the curtain rod like an audience in the gods.

"A year on, maybe more, I walked into a tavern in a town I had never played. Never. I'd have remembered." Ciaccona's voice dropped, laying the last card. "And a one-eyed tinker in the corner was singing it. *The Fool Who Sang to Gulls* — that's the name it wears on the road now; nobody consulted me. Three verses I never wrote. The water verse had grown a second half. There was a new one where the gulls follow the ship three days, out of respect."

"What did you do?"

"I stood in the doorway with my hood up and my heart going like a jig, and when he finished, I paid him for it." A dry breath of a laugh. "You tip a man for your own father. Strangest coin I ever spent. And I asked him — casual — where the song was from."

"And?"

"He shrugged the shrug of the entire oral tradition." She did the shrug. "*Everyone knows that one.*"

Cartethyia lowered her hands slowly. "Everyone knows that one."

"My father died alone on the water with a lamplighter's spyglass for a witness. And now he walks roads I'll never take, in versions I'll never hear — arguing with the sea and winning, sharing water with gulls, blessing innocent wood. Every mouth that sings him changes him. Every change is a lie. And every lie is *him* — because the one true thing in the record is that the man went to his death making music for whoever was there, and the song is out there doing exactly that. Forever. Out of my hands." She lifted her chin at an imaginary cathedral. "Confiscate it, Fenrico. Go on. There's no manuscript. There's no author. Arrest everyone."

"Did you cry?"

"Once. Behind the tavern. Into a rain barrel." Ciaccona shrugged. "That's my ration. You've seen it — one cry per grief. Call it economy. I have a lot of griefs and one face."

"And after the rain barrel?"

"After the rain barrel, the method finished becoming a doctrine. Leave every song rough. Build the hole in. Hand the pen over with the tune." She counted it off on the strings, one muted note per clause. "A finished song has an owner. An owner can be arrested, exiled, burned on a boat. But a song with a hole in it belongs to whoever mends it. Which is everyone. Which is the only congregation the Order can never excommunicate."

Cartethyia sat back. "And then Carlotta."

"And then Carlotta — of course Carlotta. The Montelli pay fortunes for intelligence, and their spymasters kept filing reports about a tavern musician who knew the tide of the city's heart three days before their informants did. She offered me a salary."

"What did you counter with?"

"A tab, and the right to be rude to her at parties. We've been dear friends since." Ciaccona spread her hands: curtain. "And then a woman with no memory washed up on a beach with a title waiting for her, and Carlotta sent me. You know the rest. You were there the first day — being invisible and superior about it."

"I was *observing*."

"You critiqued my tuning to a woman who couldn't see you."

"The tuning deserved it."

Cartethyia was quiet for a while after that. The little heap of candy wrappers sat between them like the shed skins of the hour. On the lute's neck, the boldest Sylph had settled, proprietary.

"I have three things," she said at last. "In order."

"Of course you do."

"First." One finger. "Our rooms showed us the same wound."

Ciaccona raised an eyebrow.

"The puppeteer called my twenty years boring. And your whole story is a proof — with witnesses — that boring is where everything true gets kept. The lullaby survived because it was beneath notice. Tammaro's ledger mattered because it was tedious. Fiora's tavern. The pawnshop street. *Soup.*" The finger came down. "A god skimmed my dust and my dolls and called them nothing, and your entire life says the skimming is how the important things get *past* gods."

"...Huh," said Ciaccona, who charged strangers money for insights half that size.

"So the next time some cosmic horror audits my tower and yawns, I'm taking it as a professional endorsement."

"You should. Boring is the vault. I'd put it on your coat of arms, if you had one."

"I'm working on one. There's a ewe on it." Second finger. "Second thing. The three notes."

Ciaccona's hand went still on the strings — briefly, a rest of exactly one beat. And Cartethyia, who had been right about a flat bridge from inside a cosmic horror, whose ear was a scandal she refused to formalize, heard the rest.

"You gave your father to every drunk kitchen on two coasts," she said, level, laying it out the way she laid out a blade for cleaning. "You built the hole in on purpose. You hand the pen over with the tune — your words, tonight, under the rule." A tilt of the head. "And you carry three notes of Amy's song in a locked box behind your sternum, and you will not play them. Square that with the tinker."

"The tinker sang a finished man."

"Go on."

"My father was *dead*, Cartethyia. Complete. The song could only grow him — you can't spend a dead man down; there's no principal left to touch; it's all interest." Ciaccona's thumb moved along the repaired seam, back and forth, once. "Amy is unfinished. Her song stopped at three notes and she is *still out there*. At the bottom of a god. Unfinished."

"And if you play them?"

"A song you finish is a tomb. Beautiful. Sung. *Shut.*" The words came out one at a time, each set down with care, the way you set down something with a live edge. "I play those notes out into the world where they can be mended and grown and passed to tinkers — and some part of me will have agreed that she's a dead man at a rail."

"And if you keep them?"

"The verse is still waiting. The door's still ajar." She looked up. "I told you what I'm afraid of. There it is with its coat off. As long as I don't play them — hers hasn't ended."

Cartethyia held her gaze a long moment. Then she reached to her belt, took the plush gryphon, and set it on the desk facing them both. A witness.

"I named a hundred dolls," she said, "so the dark couldn't take my name."

"I know."

"It worked. The dark never got it." A breath. "And it kept me in the tower, Ciaccona."

Ciaccona said nothing.

"Both things. The naming saved me, *and* it was the architecture of the staying. Every doll I finished was one more reason the sealed room was survivable. And a survivable room is a room you stop breaking out of." She turned the gryphon a fraction, squaring it. "Nobody warned me you could build the lifeboat so well you never sail."

"...You're comparing my box to your tower."

"I'm comparing craftsmanship. We're both very good." Cartethyia let the joke land soft and moved past it. "Play them, keep them — that stays yours. Lupa kicks doors; I only break furniture. I'm telling you what I know about holding a door ajar for years with your own body." She met her eyes. "You become the door."

The false stars ticked. Below the floor of the world, a god knelt over a locked gate; beyond the gate, a girl's song waited at three notes.

"Third finger." Cartethyia raised it. "In every story you told me tonight, the mending happens in company."

Ciaccona blinked.

"The lullaby needed a tavern. Your father needed a coastline of drunks. Even Tammaro — his ledger waited forty years for a girl with a lute to walk in and *read* it. A witness needs a witness, or the sailcloth book is just the sea again. Smaller." The finger came down, and her voice went where it rarely went, all the sunniness banked to coals. "The whole doctrine runs on company. And your plan for Amy's three notes is to hold them alone, in the dark, in a box, forever — in the one pair of hands in the world most qualified to know better."

Ciaccona opened her mouth.

"Maybe they're not fading in there," Cartethyia went on, gentler. "Maybe you're right, and the box preserves. But everything you taught me tonight says a kept song and a graved song are *neighbors*. And the fence between them is company." She leaned forward. "It's coming, Cia. Whatever it is. The whole pack is being tuned for something — you feel it too, I know you do. So when the day comes that those three notes have somewhere to go, and someone to go *with* — I want your word as a Toccata. You'll deal them off the bottom of the deck. Like everything else you love."

The Sylph on the lute's neck looked from one of them to the other.

"You're a menace," Ciaccona said finally. "Twenty years in a tower and you come out doing surgery."

"I had a hundred dolls and no anesthetic. Your word."

"...My word." It came out quieter than she intended, and truer. "When they have somewhere to go."

"Good." Cartethyia sat back, and the coals banked themselves, and the sun came back out. "Now eat something, you've been performing for an hour."

Ciaccona covered the exposure the only way she knew, which was to reach for the pegs and bring the lute back up to pitch, string by string, the room refilling with the small tortured sounds of an instrument being asked to work at midnight.

Cartethyia ate her last candy. She did it slowly, and she settled deeper where she sat, and when she spoke again her mouth was half-full and her tone had gone dangerously idle.

"One more thing occurs to me."

"The rule was three things. You counted. There were *fingers*."

"This one's free." She swallowed. "Everyone in your stories gets a song."

"Do they."

"Your father has one with a fan tradition. Fiora lives in six grandchildren and a verse. Amy has three notes and a bodyguard. Mei's whole life is basically an anthem you keep re-arranging." Cartethyia laced her fingers behind her head and studied the built stars. "Even the ewe that tried to eat my boot made it into your notebook. I've *seen* the stanza. It scans better than my parts do."

"The ewe had natural rhythm."

"So here's the free question." She let it drop from a great, lazy height. "Who sings the bard?"

"Nobody sings the bard. The bard is the frame." Ciaccona kept her eyes on the pegs. "Frames that demand paintings embarrass everyone at the exhibition."

"Mm." Cartethyia nodded along with the grave air of a scholar accepting a premise in order to burn it down. "So if I wanted a Ballad of Ciaccona to exist. I'd have to make it myself."

"Cartethyia."

"Out of whatever I happened to know. With my musical training." A beat. "Which consists of you wincing at me for two years."

"*Cartethyia.*"

"And it would be *rough*." She was sitting up now, eyes alight with the specific joy she otherwise reserved for breaking furniture in gods' houses. "Holes in it. *Enormous* holes. Structural. And I'd sing it in taverns — badly, which I'm given to understand is the *correct technique*—"

"That's out of context—"

"—and strangers would lean in and fix it, and they'd get you all wrong, and every wrong version would be *you*—"

"—that's *wildly* out of context—"

"—and there'd be no manuscript, and no author, and one day some one-eyed tinker in a town you've never played would be singing about the liar who told the truth, and you'd have to stand in the doorway with your hood up." Cartethyia spread her hands, radiant. "And *tip him.*"

Ciaccona stared at her.

"I'm only describing your method," Cartethyia said. "You wrote the doctrine. Under the rule of truth: give me one reason it shouldn't apply to its inventor."

Ciaccona opened her mouth. Closed it. The silence where the reason should have been was the loudest thing either of them had heard all night.

"There's a word," she managed at last, "for when a fencer is struck with her own blade."

"In Egla we called it Tuesday." And Cartethyia was already humming — quietly, tunelessly, a phrase that wandered up where any honest melody would wander down, an interval outlawed in every tuning on every coast, structurally unsound from the first note, entirely hers. "*Oh, the bard came out of Ragunna with a key inside her lute—*"

"That's — you can't rhyme *lute* with *truth*, the vowel is doing all the work and the work is *bad*—"

"Sounds like a hole in the verse," said Cartethyia serenely, and kept humming.

And Ciaccona — who had a lot of griefs and one face, who had given away every song she ever loved except two, who had walked into this room owning the evening's only locked box and would walk out having promised the key — heard her own doctrine coming back at her in a country girl's tuneless voice, wrong in every particular and true in the one that counted, and did the only thing left to a professional.

She played along. She found the wandering phrase where it lived, put a floor under the illegal interval so it stopped being an error and became a feature — an argument, a signature — and wove the humming in, as if it had always belonged. Because now it did. The Sylphs came down from the curtain rod. The false stars ticked through their wrong constellations. Somewhere below the floor of the world a god knelt over a locked gate, and somewhere beyond the gate a girl's song waited at three notes, ajar; and in a dormitory room above all of it, two exiles with a thermos built a small bad ballad with holes in it, laughing, leaving the door open for whoever came to mend it.

Later, alone, before sleep, Ciaccona took out the notebook, found the running page of the mock-epic, and drew the star beside the entry — small, uneven, her father's star, the way he drew them in margins, the way she had drawn them since.

*The Maiden hums flat on purpose now,* she wrote. *I have created a monster. The monster rhymes.*

And underneath, in smaller letters, the ground bass, one more time, the way it came out after a lifetime of variations:

*Leave the hole in the verse. That's where everyone gets in.*

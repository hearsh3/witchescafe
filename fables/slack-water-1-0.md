# SLACK WATER

*An interlude. It sits between* Spiral *and* Moonlit Mirage*; the final movement overlaps the hours after* Moonlit Mirage *and hands off to* Eclipse Lunae*.*

> *The moon has set,*
> *and the Pleiades. Midnight.*
> *The hours go by.*
> *I lie here alone.*
> — Sappho, fr. 168B

---

## I. THE HOLE IN THE DAY

The sky healed first.

It went the way a bruise goes, purple to green to a yellow that pretended to be morning, and by the third hour after the tide swallowed itself the clouds over the Sanguis Plateaus were ordinary clouds, moving at ordinary speeds, raining ordinary rain on eleven hundred soldiers who stood in the mud with their mouths open.

The land healed second, and it healed wrong. The Earthrend Wedge stood cracked to the root. East of it, where the hunting grounds had been, water moved. Grey, patient, salted water, all the way to a horizon that had held hills at breakfast.

Two soldiers of the Fourth argued about it at the new shoreline while they waited for orders.

"Charts say plateau."

"Charts are old."

"Charts are from *spring*."

The second soldier looked at the water for a long time. "Then the spring was wrong," he said, and both of them found this easier than the alternatives, and stopped talking.

⸻

Lupa carried the Ephor of Septimont down the switchbacks like firewood. Augusta allowed it, which frightened Lupa more than the tide had. The strongest woman in the city lay in her arms with her crown of orbiting steel gone to slag on a battlefield behind them, whispering one word, cracked and small, on every third breath.

The word had a shape. Lupa's ears were good. She listened four times and each time the shape reached her ears as weather, as gullsound, as the whine of her own blood. Her hackles stood.

*The Ephor is saying a word and the world keeps eating it before it lands.*

She filed that. Wolves file things. Then she walked faster.

⸻

At the field station a scribe took depositions. Standard practice after a Hunt: who died, who broke, who saw what. He got to the wolf-eared Champion's second at dusk.

"She walked into the tide after—"

The scribe waited, stylus up.

Lupa's sentence stood there with its hands empty. She could feel the rest of it. It sat behind her teeth with weight and heat and a color, blue, and when she reached for it her jaw closed on air.

"After," the scribe prompted.

"Write it down exactly like that," Lupa said. "'After.' Full stop."

He did. He was tired. Across the tent, the Blessed Maiden of Rinascita was giving her own account to a different scribe, and it kept snagging in the same place, on the same missing tooth.

"There was a light," Cartethyia said. Her eyes did the flicker, green, gold, green. "Mei followed a light into the water and the water let her through and pushed the rest of us back."

"A light," the scribe said. "Describe it."

Cartethyia opened her mouth. In the room inside her where a god lived, something turned over in its sleep and made a sound like grief, wordless, enormous, twenty years deep. She shut her mouth around it.

"Blue," she said finally. "It was blue."

⸻

Ciaccona gave no deposition. She sat on a supply crate at the edge of the new sea with her father's broken lute across her knees and read the day the way she read everything, as a score.

Here was the melody: the Sovereign, the crown, Augusta falling fifteen meters into Lupa's arms. Here was the counterpoint: the tide, the surge, Mei's smile, *I won't break our promise.* And here, running under all of it, a staff ruled and ready, its clef in place, its bars counted out — and every bar holding a rest.

There is a word copyists use when an instrument sits out an entire movement. They write it once at the top of the part and the player counts silence until the end.

*Tacet.*

Ciaccona looked at the day and read the word everywhere. In the gap in Lupa's sentence. In the flicker of Cartethyia's eyes. In the muster rolls, later, where the ink flowed clean from one name to the next across a space her ear insisted was load-bearing. A part had been written for someone. The part was still there. The player had been lifted out of the world so gently the page forgot to bleed.

She put her hand flat on the broken lute. The wood was warm, which was strange, because the rain was cold.

"All right," she told it, very quietly, in the voice her father used for boats. "Speak to me."

The lute held its warmth and its silence, and Ciaccona started counting bars.

⸻

The army withdrew on the second day. The three of them stayed.

The decision made itself, and held. Lupa sent word to the Colosseum that the Champion's team was conducting extended reconnaissance, which was true the way a knife is cutlery. They made camp above the beach where the cliff came down to the water, the tall one, the one the maps had marked *precipice* back when the maps had been right about anything.

An old man mended nets on the sand below them, morning after morning. He had been a plateau farmer three weeks ago, he said, and now he owned waterfront, and a man adapts. He looked at the three armed women keeping watch on an empty sea and adapted to them too.

"You're waiting on the water," he said. Statement, mild.

"Something like that," said Ciaccona.

"Sea's a courier." He bit a knot tight. "Makes its deliveries at dawn. Whatever you ordered, be here at dawn."

Lupa took the dawns after that. She would have taken them anyway. Dawn was when her dreams got crowded — water closing over her head, once, twice, some third time that belonged to none of her lives, and a hand hauling her up out of the dark, a hand with no face above it, and around the whole memory, faint as a stain rinsed twelve times, a smell she had no name for. Citrus, and rain on stone, and something bitter steeping.

She woke from these with her tail bristled and her mouth full of the word *again*, and she went down to the tideline and walked it like a fence.

On the eighth dawn, the sea made its delivery.

## II. DELIVERIES

The shape in the surf was face-down and moving the way kelp moves, which is to say the water was doing all the moving. Lupa covered the distance flat-out, spray to the hip, and had her hands in sodden black cloth before her mind caught up with her nose.

Salt. Blood, thin and old. Under it, unmistakable, the smell she would have known through smoke and years and the end of the world.

"MEI—"

The others heard it from the camp. Cartethyia arrived like wind arrives. Ciaccona arrived last and made herself useful, which for Ciaccona meant seeing everything: the seaweed wound through black hair; Mya's baton still lashed to the belt, brine-swollen, present; the empty loop beside it where something round and white used to ride.

They pulled her from the shore. That was the first time.

Mei came up out of the water coughing, which was the best sound any of them had heard in eight days, and her first act on land was to grip Lupa's forearm with a drowned woman's strength and ask the sea a question over Lupa's shoulder.

"Blue," she said. Her voice was ruined. "Blue silk. Did she— has the sea brought anyone else—"

The pack looked at each other over her head.

"Anyone else," Lupa repeated, careful, the way you repeat a password you suspect you've been given wrong.

"Her." Mei's eyes were the color of gold left out in weather. They moved from face to face, hunting, and Lupa watched something happen behind them that she would spend the next three weeks failing to unsee.

There was a switch back there. Somewhere behind the gold. Lupa watched an unseen hand reach in and rest on it, waiting.

"Iuno," Mei said. "*Iuno.* Tell me you—"

Three faces, each doing its honest best. Cartethyia's went through hope and worry and landed on a blank so total it was its own answer. Ciaccona's held still, professionally still, the stillness of a performer who has forgotten the next line in front of a full house. Lupa hunted her own skull for the name, ransacked it, kicked over every crate in there, and came back to the surface with sand.

The switch flipped. The light behind the gold went out.

Lupa felt it happen under her hands like a temperature. She tightened her grip on principle, on instinct, on the theory that a thing held hard enough stays.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. We've got you. Start from the top."

⸻

The top took until the third hour after midnight, in a requisitioned medic tent, in a voice that kept running aground.

Mei told it out of order because it had happened out of order. A precipice. A confession. A fall the two of them took together, hands full of each other. A world after the fall that had rewritten itself around a heroic drowning, a funeral, a eulogy Mei had apparently given with her own mouth. A trail of hollowed hearts. A mask that opened doors between versions of things. A heart the size of a building, and a woman impaled at its center feeding it thirty years of guilt to keep it tame, and the choice, and the tearing, and *you idiot*, and then a room where futures were typed, and a shadow wearing a beloved face, and an arrow pushed through a chest by four hands at once, and light, and a promise made into the light.

*I'll find you. I'll wake you. We'll live together.*

Then water. Then sand. Then this tent.

The three of them received it the way you receive weather at sea, without interruption. When it finally ran out, Ciaccona lifted the lute off her knee and set it on the table, slow, both hands, the way you set down a thing you have decided to keep.

"How long were you in there," she said. "Your count."

Mei's mouth worked. "Months. Or a day. It kept— the light changed when the story needed it to."

"Our count is eight days," Ciaccona said.

"Nine," said Cartethyia.

Lupa looked between them. "It's eight."

"I counted nine."

"You counted the day it happened *twice*, Carte, you always—"

"The army log," Ciaccona said, mildly, over both of them, "records the tide as receding overnight. One night, start to finish. I checked."

Silence took the tent. Four people. Four counts. Somewhere an official somewhere had a fifth.

Mei laughed. It was a terrible sound, two parts relief and one part something with a longer name, and it turned into coughing, and Lupa thumped her back and left her hand there.

"Say the name again," Lupa said.

"Iuno."

Lupa closed her eyes. Let it drop into her like a stone down a well, and listened.

Nothing splashed.

"One more time."

"*Iuno.*"

Deep in the well, so deep it could have been the blood in her own ears: citrus, and rain on stone, and something bitter steeping. Lupa opened her eyes.

"I believe you," she said.

Ciaccona's head came up. Cartethyia's did too. Mei went very still, the stillness of someone bracing for the qualifier.

"I don't remember her," Lupa said. "I want that on the record. My head's got a hole in it shaped like a person and I've had holes in my head before, so trust me on the shape. Someone did carpentry in here." She tapped her own temple, twice, hard. "And they were good, and they missed a smell. So." Her tail swept once, low, the sweep that in the arena meant *the match starts now*. "I believe you. What do we hunt?"

The switch behind Mei's eyes stayed dark, and would stay dark for weeks yet. But Lupa saw the hand rest on it, and a resting hand was enough to run a search on.

⸻

Two small wrongnesses, for the record, both filed away in silence for days.

The first: when the medic drew blood for the standard post-Tide screen, the bead arrived late. It welled up slow and dark and unhurried, like something being talked into it. The medic frowned at her needle and blamed the needle.

The second: Mei smelled like the deep for six days. Lupa counted. On the seventh morning the smell was gone, and in its place was just Mei, salt and iron and the cheap camp soap, and Lupa stood in the tent doorway with her eyes stinging and told everyone it was the wind.

## III. NEGATIVE SPACE

The city had already written its versions.

*THE CHAMPION'S LAST CHARGE*, said the broadsheets, over a woodcut of Mei with her sword up and the Sovereign coming down. A shrine had sprouted at the Colosseum gate, candles and arena tokens and a child's drawing of a wolf and a knight holding hands. Someone had chalked *SHE SWIMS AGAINST IT* on the aqueduct wall, which was Augusta's line, migrated. The city grieved efficiently, the way it did everything, and had roughly nine days of grief left in the budget when the Champion walked back through the east gate on her own feet.

Septimont went up like a struck flint. There were three parades planned by noon.

Mei attended none of them. She was in the Temple archive by the second bell, pulling muster rolls.

CONCEPTUALIZATION — *Look at the page. Really look.*

The roster of Temple officials ran in a clerk's tidy hand from *Iulia Vetra, oraclist,* to *Iustus Ren, keeper of hours.* Between them the paper lay smooth as a healed scar. Mei put her fingertip on the gap and held it there, and the gap held its breath back at her.

CONCEPTUALIZATION — *A person is a shape the world makes room for. Remove the person, the room remains. Doors sized for her. Schedules bent around her. Look for the dents.*

VOLITION — *Then we have a method.*

THE FOOL — *We have a* game. *She loved games. Find all the places she's still pressing on the world and connect them, and where the lines cross—*

HALF LIGHT — *—there's a monster in the middle of a web like that, there's always a—*

VOLITION — *There's* her. *Walk.*

So Mei walked, and the pack walked with her, and the city gave up its dents one by one.

⸻

The armory kept a weapon rack on the north wall built to a strange geometry: a deep crescent cradle, load-rated far past any blade in inventory, its brass worn bright in two places the width of careful hands. The quartermaster called it *the question mark* and said it had come with the building. It had come with the building the way the moon comes with the night.

A florist off the Processional had a standing order, paid two decades in advance from an account marked only with an initial the ledger recorded as a waterstain: white camellias, first of the month, delivered to the Temple's east balcony. "Delivered to who?" Ciaccona asked. The florist checked her book, and checked it again, and said, with rising unease, that the book had always just said *the balcony.*

The Observatory's night registrar, bribed with sausage rolls and Ciaccona's undivided attention, admitted the thing his seniors had forbidden him to log: three weeks ago, on the night of the tide, seven stars had moved. Moved, held a new formation over the northeast water, and stayed. "Instrument error," he recited. He looked twenty years old and three weeks past his last sleep. "All seven instruments," he added, to his shoes.

Ciaccona wrote it all down. She had started a ledger — *her* kind of ledger, a fair copy in a waterproof folio — and its title page said, in her performance hand:

**AN INVENTORY OF ABSENCE.**
*Being a true account of the holes.*

"You're enjoying this," Lupa accused, reading over her shoulder.

"I'm a bard documenting a government-grade erasure with a torch my father died for." Ciaccona dotted an *i* like driving a nail. "'Enjoying' is close enough."

⸻

Augusta received Mei alone, in the strategy room, on the fourth day.

The Ephor looked wrong out of armor. Smaller, and more dangerous, the way a drawn blade looks smaller than a sheathed one. She heard Mei's question — *a woman, blue silk, silver hair, a bow, thirty years at your side* — with her face doing the thing her face did in the arena when a feint came in: total reception, zero purchase.

"I would remember thirty years," Augusta said.

"You'd think."

"I remember every blade that ever came at me. I remember the name of the man who sold me bad rope in my second season." Augusta's jaw worked. "I would *remember.*"

Mei let the silence do its work. Behind Augusta's shoulder stood the council table, and at the council table stood eleven chairs, and the twelfth stood a fraction apart from the rest, angled toward the Ephor's own seat, its cushion worn in a pattern of long stillness. Dust stood on it now. The dust looked recent, and the wear looked like decades, and both things were true at once, and Mei watched Augusta's eyes slide across the chair with the frictionless ease of two decades' practice at a habit three weeks old.

On the desk, among the dispatches, lay a stem of twisted copper wire, its petals half-formed. A rose, stopped mid-word.

"Whose work is that?" Mei asked.

Augusta followed her gaze. Picked the thing up. Turned it. Something crossed the back of her eyes like a fish under ice.

"Mine," she said slowly. "It's a habit. I twist wire when I think." A pause with weight in it. "I don't know who taught me."

"Do you drink tea?"

"No." Immediate. Certain. Then Augusta's gaze went, unwilled, to the high shelf where a service for two sat behind glass, one cup furred with dust, one cup clean, and Mei watched the strongest woman in Septimont fail to have an explanation with her whole body.

"Diplomatic gift," Augusta said.

"From whom?"

The pause went long enough to hear the harbor bells.

"Get out of my strategy room," Augusta said, without heat, which from Augusta was nearly an embrace. And then, as Mei reached the door: "The cliffs northeast. Your reconnaissance." Augusta had turned back to her dispatches; her voice came level over her shoulder. "I'm posting dawn patrols on that stretch. Weather observation. The Senate needn't hear about it and neither need you."

"You don't post watches for ghosts."

"I post them for weather," Augusta said. Both sentences were true, and both of them knew it, and Mei went out past the empty chair, and behind her, alone, the Ephor of Septimont sat down and put an arrow on the table.

She had found it in her own quarters, in a case worn to velvet ghosts, fletched in a style the fletchers' guild swore blind they had never cut. She remembered arrows on that field. Everyone else remembered her sword.

"There were arrows," she said to the empty chair, conversationally, like testing a bridge. "Whose were they?"

The chair kept its counsel. Augusta sat with the arrow until the light went, twisting wire.

⸻

The Temple's caretaker was a broad, calm woman named Rose Marigold who ran the residence wing like a tide table and had, for as long as anyone could establish, kept a kettle warm in the east wing's smallest kitchen from the third bell to the last.

"For whom?" Mei asked.

"For whoever comes." Rose wiped the counter that was already clean. "Someone always used to come."

"Who?"

Rose's hand stopped on the counter. Her pleasant face did something complicated, the look of a woman reaching for a jar she has reached for ten thousand times and closing her hand on shelf.

"I get a headache," she said at last, quietly, "when you ask me that. Right here." She touched the center of her forehead. "It has a shape, the headache. It's about this tall." Her hand hovered, unthinking, at the height of a woman in heeled sandals. Then she saw what her hand was doing, and put it away in her apron, and her eyes when they came up were frightened and steady at once. "You'd best have the tea, dear. It's already hot."

The margins of the archive's prophecy folios lay bare. Whole codices of official divination, decades deep, and every margin scraped clean as a beach at low tide. Except one. In the oldest volume, at the bottom of a page on tide patterns, an annotation began: the downstroke of a single letter, ink still holding its confidence, and then the paper going clean, the way a voice goes when a door shuts on it.

Mei sat with that half-letter for an hour. It curved left. It could have started an *I.* It could have started a *M.*

She pressed her thumb to it, once, the way you press a bruise to make sure you're awake, and went to find her pack.

⸻

The turn came from Lupa's nose, on the twelfth day.

She had been working the tideline in a search grid since dawn, because that was the assignment she'd given herself — the sea makes deliveries; someone checks the mail — and two hundred paces north of where Mei had washed up, above the high-water mark, in the lee of a black rock, she went down on one knee and stayed there so long the gulls resettled around her.

She brought Mei there at a run.

"Someone came out of the water here," Lupa said. "Weeks back. Before you." Her hand hovered over the sand, reading it like braille. "Bled here. Thin blood, strange blood, blood with light in it, I don't have words for it, my nose does. Sat here a long time. Crawled there. Was carried from there." Her tail lay flat along the ground, which Mei had seen twice in her life. "And under all of it—"

"Say it."

"Citrus. Rain on stone. Something bitter, steeping." Lupa looked up. "Mei. This spot smells like your grief."

The wind came off the water and took the silence apart.

"Bergamot," Mei said. The word left her like a tooth. "It's called bergamot. It's a tea. She—" Her voice quit. She stood in the lee of the black rock where, weeks ago, an unwitnessed woman had bled and sat and been carried away alive, and the switch behind her eyes jumped in its socket, and every skill she owned started shouting at once.

Ciaccona arrived, read the two of them at a glance, and took out a fresh page.

"Okay," she said. "From the top."

That evening the *Inventory of Absence* gained a new section heading, underlined twice: **DELIVERIES, CONFIRMED: ONE (1).** And below it, in smaller script, the bard's honest marginalia: *The sea took two women. The sea has given back two women. We are looking for someone who has already been found.*

She sat with her pen over that last line for a long moment. Then she added: *By whom?*

## IV. THE DOOR

The ritual assembled itself out of scraps, the way rituals do.

A flat rock near the cliff's edge, chest-high, level enough. Two tin cups from a tinker who threw in a third for luck; the third rode in Mei's pack, wrapped. A thermos of tea brewed strong in Rose Marigold's smallest kitchen, because Rose insisted, bergamot and black leaf, and the smell of it opened a trapdoor in Mei's chest every single evening, and every single evening Mei stepped through it on purpose.

At dusk she poured both cups full and set one at the far edge of the rock, facing the water. She laid Mya's baton beside it, crosswise. A placeholder does a placeholder's work.

Then she said it to the sea, nightly, in the flat clear voice of a person leaving a door unlatched:

"Tea's on."

The first night, Lupa watched from the grass with her ears flat, certain she was witnessing a wound. By the fourth night she had revised. The steam went off the cup and out over the water like a signal accepting its own long odds. It was a wound the way a lighthouse is a wound.

Mei had her orders. Somewhere in the fall — between the white and the water, on a shore the color of static — there had been two sisters, and the memory kept its edges the way dreams keep edges, which is to say it bled. Sara had checked the straps of Mei's armor like a quartermaster seeing off a convoy. *She's the kind that keeps promises,* she'd said. *So be findable.* And Acheron had studied her the way you study a coastline from far off, one hand on the red at her hip. *The deep charges a toll both ways. You'll learn later what you paid. When you do — count it first. Spend the grief after.*

*Be findable.* Mei had carried the instruction out of the water along with the name, the promise, and the baton, and on the cliff she unpacked it. Findable meant visible. Findable meant fixed. Findable meant: choose your lighted window, and keep the lamp fed.

So: the rock, the dusk, two cups, the words.

⸻

Lupa produced the bell on a windless evening, from the belt pouch where it had ridden since the aqueducts. Copper, palm-sized, green at the seams.

"You left this with me once," she said.

"I did." Ciaccona took it, and her thumb went around the rim like a habit. "I meant it as a torch."

"It's a bell," said Cartethyia.

"It's both. Buling makes things that are both."

Rung, the bell gave back reverberations — sounds the air still held, the past's leftovers rendered audible. They rang it over the tideline at slack water, and out of the bronze came gull-cries from some other morning, the knock of oars, a child laughing at cold water, the vast slow breath of a tide long finished.

And once — once — a clean note like a plucked bowstring. Held. Bending upward at the end, the way a voice bends into a question.

The four of them stood very still. The note faded on its own schedule. Ciaccona wrapped the bell back up slowly, giving the moment room to be explained, and the sea filled the room instead.

⸻

Mei found the shard on a grey noon in the wrack line, among mussel shells and a sandal sole and a fist of rope. White glaze, a shallow curve, smooth as a cheek. It fit her palm the way a held breath fits a chest.

INLAND EMPIRE — *Careful. That's a face.*

LOGIC — *It's crockery.*

INLAND EMPIRE — *It's a face, and it's sleeping, and it knows you.*

She kept it between herself and her coat pocket. At night it was warm. She checked on several nights, at different hours, holding it to her lip in the dark like a thermometer. Warm every time.

⸻

The dream arrived in the second week and returned three times, identical.

A corridor the color of deep water. At its end, a door — hinges, grain, a keyhole, and a smooth brass plate where a handle would sit. From behind it: two women talking, low and easy, in the cadence of a card game. Laughter. The click of something small and hard set down on wood. Bergamot came under the door the way light does.

She knocked until her hands sang. The voices went on, warm, unhurried, a room away.

She woke each time with her palms aching and a taste on her tongue that belonged to someone else's order entirely: tea, strong, milky, two sugars. In her coat on the chair, the shard held its little heat like a coal.

⸻

There had been a channel, once. At the Wedge, in the war-days, a voice used to arrive in her head from outside her head — iron and silver, the grammar of commands — and her skills had filed it under THE HIGH PRIESTESS OF SEPTIMONT, because filing a thing is a way to survive it.

On the fifteenth night Mei stood on the cliff and opened that door inward, and listened with everything she had.

She stood where the voice used to arrive, and heard weather.

⸻

The seventeenth night came in moonless, with a sea like poured slate, and her skills convened on their own authority.

THE FOOL — *Walk it through with me. You left through a door. You washed up on this sand. Therefore the door opens onto this water. Doors open both ways. She's behind it. You knock, you open, you bring her back through. Simple.*

VOLITION — *The sea is a mile deep past the shelf.*

THE FOOL — *The sea is a hallway.*

HALF LIGHT — *Something down there ate the sky for three days and you want to go* knock*?*

THE FOOL — *I want to find her. That's the whole of me. That's everything I'm made of.*

VOLITION — *And the promise?*

THE FOOL — *I'm keeping it. I come back with her. Watch me.*

She went in at the shallow slope by the black rock, dressed, boots on, the stormlamp high in her left hand, because findable cuts both ways. Ten steps of ordinary cold.

At the eleventh step the water changed its mind about her.

It went hard. It stood up. It put a shoulder into her chest, polite and total, and walked her backward up the slope; and when she planted and drove against it — arena-strength, the strength that had torn a heart out of a god's engine — the sea gathered her up whole and set her down on the wet sand above the reach of the foam, flat on her back, lamp still burning, the way a courier refuses a parcel and leaves it neatly on the step.

She lay there. The sky rained on her face.

THE FOOL — *...huh.*

She got up and tried the flat run past the shelf-break. The sea handed her back. She tried the north channel, where the ebb ran out hard; the current reversed around her ankles, brought her in, laid her down. Gentle, each time. Final, each time. A door with a smooth plate where the handle should be.

The third time, she stayed down, and the sound she made into the rain had teeth in it — and that was when the hillside above the beach stood up.

Lupa had been sleeping rough in the grass above the tideline for six nights. She came down the slope in four strides and had Mei up out of the wet by the collar and the wrist — the wrist, always the wrist — and her eyes were doing something Mei had seen exactly once, across a dying campfire, years of grief ago.

"Say it."

"Lupa—"

"Say the promise."

Rain. The lamp on its side in the sand, guttering, faithful.

"...Don't die," Mei said. "Don't make you bury me."

"Again."

"Don't make you—"

"*Again.*"

"Seven days," Mei said, and her voice broke in the middle like a bar snapping. "Together. I know. I *know*, Lupa—"

And Lupa's grip changed. The fury went out of it and the terror stayed, which was worse, and she put her forehead down on Mei's soaked shoulder, and when she spoke, each word came out flat and separate, stones laid on a wall.

"I can't smell her, Mei. I've tried. Every dawn. I put my nose into that wind until my skull rings, and it gives me salt and weed and cold. You — you I can smell from the city gate. You I can find blind, in rain, in a riot. That's what I've got. That's what I'm keeping."

The sea lay flat behind them now. Satisfied. Delivered.

"The door's shut," Mei said. Her teeth chattered around it. "It spat me out. Three times."

"Good," Lupa said, with her whole chest.

"It's shut from *somewhere*, Lupa. She's behind it." Mei stared out at the black water, and the FOOL turned the fact over in her head and found the other face of it. "The tide brings things in," she said, slowly, reading it off the dark. "It brought me in. It brought her in — three weeks ago, two hundred paces north. The sea makes deliveries." A wet laugh with a live wire in it. "It just refuses pickups."

"Then we'll be here," Lupa said, "when it makes the next one."

A howl had gone up the beach at some point — one note, economical — and now blankets arrived, and a storm-tarp, and a pot, and the rest of the pack with them. Cartethyia sat down in the wet sand against Mei's side, hip to hip, took her hand, and hummed: the small brave tune Ciaccona had played to the water on the first nights, the vigil nights, a line that went out and came back. She hummed it wordless and off-true and endless, and it worked the way a hand on a rope works.

Ciaccona crouched at the pot until the shaking downgraded to shivering. Then a voice came out of her that the beach heard for the first time — the verdict voice, the one that ends acts.

"Here's what we do. Dawns are Lupa's. The city's mine. Carte goes where you go — congratulations, Champion, you've acquired a shadow with a sword. And you sleep in shifts like the rest of this company, because a lookout who skips sleep is a lookout who sees mermaids."

"She outranks you," Lupa said, from inside a blanket.

"I have a lute," said Ciaccona. "I outrank everyone."

Near dawn the rain quit. Mei poured two cups from the thermos with hands that had nearly steadied, set one on the rock, and said the words. The steam went out over the water.

Findable.

## V. SLACK WATER

There is an interval between tides when the water stands still and the harbor holds its breath: slack water, the pilots call it, the pause while the sea decides. The three weeks lived there.

⸻

The exhibition matches sold out in hours. The Senate wanted spectacle to prove the city whole, and Lupa gave it to them ugly and fast, and the crowd's roar came at her in waves, and the waves were the problem: from the arena floor, forty thousand voices sound exactly like water coming. She fought with her ears flat to her skull and told the sports press it was a style choice.

"Where's the Champion?" they shouted at the tunnel mouth.

"Training," Lupa said, and smiled with every tooth she owned, and the word held, because a wall of teeth is a kind of masonry.

After sundown she drank at the Solis Tavern, corner booth, back to the wall, eyes on the door. The keeper ran her tab in a small blue book and had learned her tempo by the third night: one for thirst, then one slow. He set them down and took himself off, which in tavern grammar is courtliness.

Dawns belonged to the cliff. She climbed it before light with a thermal flask riding her hip, and she had a doctrine about it, one sentence long: *provision the vigil, and let the silence do the talking.* So she set the flask on the stone within Mei's reach and slid it the last hand-width, so it knocked, gently, against Mei's boot — contact, announcement, delivery — and then she left, because the second half of provisioning a vigil is leaving.

Behind her, most mornings, she heard the flask open.

⸻

In the boarding house, Cartethyia developed a post: the upstairs windowsill, knees up, sword across them, face to the street.

"What are you doing?" Lupa asked, the first time.

"Absolutely nothing useful," said Cartethyia, and kept at it, daily, for hours.

Lupa considered the geometry of the sill — clean sightlines to the street door, the roof access, and the bed where Mei slept her assigned shifts — and said, "Keep it up."

Lemon cakes appeared at Mei's elbow at intervals a physician would have endorsed, sugar crystals on top, always with one bite gone. "Quality control," Cartethyia said, every time, as if the phrase were new.

On a rooftop mid-week, hanging laundry between them like prayer flags, she told Ciaccona about the quiet.

"It's quiet. In here." A tap to the temple. "Since the night the Tide's heart died. Twenty years of whispers — since before I knew they were separate from me — and they stopped mid-word. Like a door shutting on a voice." She pulled her knees up on the parapet. "And I keep sitting in the doorway. Like a dog. Listening for a monster I—" Her jaw set. "I miss the *shape* of it. I hate the thing and I miss the shape of it."

"You're allowed both," Ciaccona said. "Both is Mei's entire month."

Cartethyia laughed, wet, and let the wind have the laundry for a while. Then, quieter: "There's a stretch I lose, Cia. From the anomaly days. I woke in the arena vaults with wax on my fingers and salt in my boots and three days spent. I've asked Fleurdelys." The pinned sheets filled and emptied. "She won't say. Or she can't. With gods the difference matters, and she keeps that door shut too."

And once, on a supply run, standing where the surf ran up over her boots, Cartethyia's own mouth opened and said a word in a register below her voice, old and vast and mild:

*"Again."*

She clapped a hand over it. The day continued around her, sunlit, ordinary. She reported it to the pack four evenings later, at dinner, in the tone you use for a strange bird.

⸻

Ciaccona worked the city by day and the repertoire by night, and one night her fingers walked off the map.

She was noodling — a technical term — and the left hand found a stranger's shape on the neck, and the right hand found a rhythm like oars, and the phrase that came out of the lute stood every hair on her arms upright: a rising line over a drone, minor-bright, with a hole in it the exact size of an answering voice.

Across the room, Mei's head came up like a hooked fish.

"Where did you hear that."

Ciaccona looked at her own hands. "I didn't."

"Play it again."

"Mei—"

"*Play it again.*"

She played it again. The hole in the melody sat there, patient, expectant, a held-out hand. When she damped the strings — flat palm, full stop — the lute went on humming for half a second on its own.

Both of them looked at it. The soundhole looked back, round and dark.

That night Ciaccona wrapped the lute in oilcloth for the first time since Rinascita. Some instruments you protect from the weather. Some weather you protect from the instruments. She had stopped pretending she knew which this was.

⸻

Blue moved wrong in the market on the nineteenth day.

A bolt of silk over a draper's arm, deep lapis, and the crowd closed around it, and Mei went through the crowd like a blade through bread—

ELECTROCHEMISTRY — *THERE she is THERE she is THERE—*

HALF LIGHT — *GO. GO. GO—*

VOLITION — *It's cloth. Look at the drape. Cloth hangs. She* moved.

—and fetched up at a stall where a draper was folding fabric for a merchant's wife, and stood there with her heart going like a war drum while both women stared.

She bought a hand-span of the silk. She kept it in the pocket with the shard. VOLITION filed the incident under *maintenance required* and scheduled extra sleep, and Mei outvoted it.

⸻

Strangers had been in the Solis all week. Road dust, mixed accents, the wrong kind of casual, asking the keeper about the Champion, about the wolf. The keeper kept a tavern in a gladiator city; his face gave them the weather.

Lupa read the room from her booth over the rim of her cup, finished her wheat juice at her own pace, and left through the kitchen, because the second thing an arena teaches you is which fights are scheduled.

That night Ciaccona kept the late watch at the boarding-house window, and the moonlight froze.

The light lay on the street like glaze, and then it *set*. Down the block a loose shutter stopped mid-swing and hung there, angled, mid-thought. The city's sounds arrived spaced apart, each one alone, notes with the tempo lifted out from under them.

Her strings hummed. Inside the oilcloth, inside the case, the lute hummed.

She reached for her boots — and the moment passed. Light moved; the shutter clapped; the noise of the city ran back together like a chord. She stood there holding one boot, feeling the way you feel when a long rest ends: that the music had gone on elsewhere the whole time, and she had been counting bars.

Then the northeast went a color.

It reached the clouds first — a tall, soundless flare off the water, gone before her eyes finished arriving, and the afterimage it left behind was shaped like fire.

She had three people awake inside a minute. They took the coast road at a dead run, the city standing around them in set-dressing moonlight, the sea alongside keeping its own counsel, and ahead, on the cliff the maps still called *precipice*, the night was busy.

What happened up there is written elsewhere.

## VI. AGAIN

The sea gave her back a second time at midmorning, and they pulled her from the shore, and that part, too, is written elsewhere. This part is the walk.

⸻

Cartethyia counted.

"...eighty-one. Eighty-two." Soft, metronomic, pitched under the wind. It was an Avinoleum skill. Ten years in a floating prison teach a girl that a number said aloud is a handhold, that a count is a rope you braid ahead of yourself into the dark. She had counted heartbeats there, and button eyes, and the drips from a ceiling seam, and she had stayed a person, which was the point of counting.

She walked at Mei's left elbow and matched her steps to the count so the count did the leading. "Eighty-three." Step. The others let the count stand, and after a while all four of them were walking to it.

Lupa carried the weapons — both swords and the spear, slung and strapped until she clanked — because her hands needed the freight. Her jaw ached from clenching. She kept her nose working the whole way, quality-controlling the woman leaning on her shoulder: salt, blood, singed cloth, the copper smell of someone run past empty. And under the burns — she checked three times, and three times it was there — a ghost of citrus, and rain on stone, and something bitter steeping. Faint as a watermark.

By the city gate it had faded. By the boarding-house stairs it was woodsmoke, ordinary woodsmoke, and Lupa spent the climb telling herself that burns smell like smoke, that is what burns *do* — and her hackles stayed up the whole way anyway, and she filed that too.

Ciaccona walked rear guard, half-turned to the road behind, doing the other kind of watching as well. *Three weeks ago the sea delivered a woman with an empty head and a crystal arm, two hundred paces north of where it later delivered Mei. The same week, the world subtracted one woman from every head but Mei's.* She had built duets her whole life. She knew the interval when two lines were written for each other, whatever keys they had wandered into. The sum rode in her chest the whole way home, heavy and patient. She would say it once, when the room was ready. A verdict works like a cadence: timing is most of it.

⸻

The stairs took a long time. Wet wool has opinions.

Mei stood where they put her, swaying, eyes open and aimed at the middle distance, and Lupa's hands went to the ruined buttons and shook too hard to work them. She stared at her own fingers with naked fury. Cartethyia's hands arrived under hers, small and certain — buttons, laces, the sodden coat peeled away with its cargo of sand — and Lupa let herself be relieved of the task, and stood by with towels like a squire, which was its own act of discipline.

In the coat pocket, wrapped in a hand-span of lapis silk: the white shard, warm as a coal. Cartethyia set it on the windowsill, facing the room.

Then Mei lay down on top of the blankets with her eyes still open, and the six hours started.

⸻

Lupa lay along the bed's edge — "just till she warms up" — on top of the covers, boots off, radiating like a stove. Her tail crossed the blanket on its own authority and wrapped Mei's wrist: a loop and a half, snug, a bandage's grip. Lupa glared at it. The tail held position. She allowed it the way you allow weather.

Cartethyia took the foot of the bed, cross-legged at Mei's ankle, and hummed — the beach tune, wordless, off-true, endless. Once, mid-phrase, the gold came up behind her eyes: a pressure off the sea, a listening. She hummed through it, steady, and the gold stood down.

Ciaccona sat at the table with the *Inventory* and wrote the day in her fair hand, and halfway through a sentence — *the crystalline arm shattered where the spear went in, and the pieces* — she set the pen down. Mid-sentence, nib wet. She looked at it for a while. Then she took the spare strings from the case and restrung the lute instead, peg by peg, because tomorrow someone would need music that worked, and the account deserved a witness who had slept.

⸻

Some time past the third bell they held the conference, whispering across the sleeping body the way you talk over deep water.

"The arm was crystal," Lupa said.

"I saw," said Ciaccona.

"Mei said she turned to glass. At the end. In the white."

"I heard."

Cartethyia spoke from the foot of the bed, the words woven into the hum so the rhythm carried straight through: "When you speared her. She said *wait.* Before that, she said *please.*"

Lupa's tail tightened a half-turn on Mei's wrist. "She burned her off a cliff."

"Both true," Ciaccona said. And then, because the room had gone as ready as it would ever be, she laid the sum out flat, once, in the verdict voice, very low. "Three weeks ago the sea handed the world a woman with an emptied head and a crystal arm. The same week, the world lost one woman out of every head but Mei's. I've had both lines in the same ledger for days. I'm saying it aloud so it's been said."

The candle did the talking for a while.

"If it's her," Lupa said at last, to the ceiling, "she forgot."

"We know what that's like," Cartethyia said. The hum ran on underneath. "All of us. Personally."

"And if it's her—" and now the growl came into it, low, floor-deep— "then whatever aimed her at Mei was riding her when she fired. You don't plead *wait* and burn the same woman in the same minute. Something was in the saddle."

"Then we learn which," Ciaccona said. "She wakes first. Then war, if war."

The hum from the foot of the bed changed key, minutely, and kept going.

⸻

Dusk came down the color of steeped tea.

Mei's fingers moved before her eyes did. They traveled the blanket, deliberate, mapping — and found Lupa's wrist, and closed around it: the grip Lupa had used on her a hundred times, the anti-goodbye, arriving home from the other direction.

Lupa went very still, the way you go still when a wild thing chooses your hand.

"Hey," she said, wrecked and casual and quiet. "You're on my tail."

At the little stove, Cartethyia already had the kettle on; the water began its first small sound, the tick and shiver before the song. She set out cups, counting under her breath, because counting was how she loved things now. Four cups. Her hand went back to the cupboard on its own authority and set out a fifth.

She looked at the fifth cup for a long moment.

She left it.

On the windowsill, the white shard sat where she had placed it the night before — she was nearly sure — facing out to sea.

And on the very edge of sleep's territory, once, faint, in a register of iron and silver that arrived from outside her own skull, Mei heard, or remembered, or received:

*Keep up.*

The kettle sang. Mei opened her eyes.

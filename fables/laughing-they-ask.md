# Laughing, They Ask
### a moon-waiting, for the pack

> *儿童相见不相识*
> *笑问客从何处来*
> *The children look at the traveler and see a stranger.*
> *Laughing, they ask: guest, where do you come from?*
> — a poem from the far side of the page

## Prologue: What the God Keeps

Baldur dreams with his eyes open now.

He kneels above the glacier the way he has always knelt, patient as bedrock, and the city inside him goes on breathing its warm electric breath. The Soliskins are how he attends: small bright pieces of his mind on small legs, wandering the streets he holds up, riding in students' hoods, dozing on windowsills. What they see, he keeps. A god's memory is a strange archive — it files by weight, and it weighs by love, and it has been kneeling long enough to know exactly what a city's heaviest nights look like.

This is a night he kept.

## I. Moon-Waiting

The shrine was half timber and half intention, on the slope above the Academy where the frost still lingered out of loyalty. Hiyuki stood in it with a brush and a board and a century of protocol. Chisa stood across from her with a measuring cord and a dead city's calendar in her head.

"Tsukimi," Chisa said. "You view the moon. There are dumplings."

"Tsukimachi. You wait for it. The viewing is a reward for the waiting."

"My grandmother said viewing."

"Mine said waiting." A pause the length of a held bell. "Mine ran a shrine."

"Mine was taller."

Somewhere south of every map, Honami kept its silence on the question. The two of them stood in that silence a moment, the last court of appeal drowned, the verdict theirs to invent. Hiyuki dipped the brush and wrote both names on the notice board, one above the other, in a hand that had signed a hundred years of festivals.

"The city can arbitrate," she said, "when it comes back."

Chisa looked at the two names. Then at the cavern ceiling, where Helios cooled toward its committee-approved bronze, and the false stars ticked on in their wrong constellations, and the blueprints, famously, had skipped the moon.

"Then waiting wins," she said.

"…So it does."

That was the whole founding of the festival: two survivors, disagreeing kindly, choosing the version you can hold in a city where the sky is a ceiling. A moon-viewing needs a moon. A moon-waiting needs only the patience, and the assembled company, and the belief that something round and bright owes you a visit. Lahai-Roi had spent its entire history rich in the first two and professionally silent on the third.

Lupa arrived at the shrine an hour later with a cart of lumber and the specific expression of a woman who had read a requisition form and improved it.

"How long's the wait?" she asked.

"A century, so far," said Hiyuki.

Lupa looked at the timber. Looked at the slope. Her tail did one slow calculating sweep.

"I'll build for a long one."

## II. The Register

The custom, as the two survivors reconstructed it, worked like this. You wrote a wish on a tag. You wrote your hometown beneath the wish, so the light would know the way — because a wish travels, that was the doctrine, and a traveling thing wants a return address. Then you hung the tag where the moon would find it, and you waited, and the waiting was the festival.

Astrid ran the register table. She was eleven, she had once buried a screaming machine in a field out of fear, and she had decided that clerical work in the open air was the shape of her repentance. She took the job with the gravity of a magistrate. She had a bowl of blank tags, a jar of pens, and a volunteer in a borrowed vest who kept the bowl filled and hummed while she worked.

The pack came through her jurisdiction one at a time.

"Hometown?" Astrid asked.

"Caldara," said Lupa.

"Spell it?"

Lupa spelled it. Six letters, delivered like a count-off. Astrid wrote them down and squinted.

"Where's that?"

"Three days above the Sanguis Plateaus. There's a lake."

"Is it nice?"

"It thinks it can boil." Lupa took her tag, wrote her wish standing up, in block letters, folded it once, and moved on before the girl could read it. Her tail stayed at the table two seconds longer than she did.

Cartethyia wrote *Egla* with a flourish and narrated the whole entry aloud — wheat to the horizon, windmills like applauding giants, a bakery with a standing order. Then she took a second tag, turned her shoulder to the table, and wrote something on it with her tongue between her teeth. She hung that one herself, high on the frame, facing outward, toward the dark past the shrine lights. *Two travelers,* it said, in a registrar's tidy hand, *whereabouts unknown.* The pack walked past it all evening. Every one of them read it. Every one of them let it be.

"Hometown?" Astrid asked the bard.

"Toccata."

"…That's a family."

"It traveled." Ciaccona signed the register with a treble clef and left a copper coin in the jar, tradition or tip, she declined to specify.

Chisa printed *Honami* in careful strokes. Astrid sounded it out.

"Is that near here?"

"It's near me," Chisa said, which settled it. Behind her in the line, Hiyuki wrote the same word, and their two spellings matched to the stroke, and both of them noticed, and both of them looked somewhere else about it.

Lynae stood at the table a long time. Her pen hovered over the hometown line while an old framed letter hung itself briefly in her mind's eye, forged seal and all, the only beautiful lie she had ever owned before the real one arrived by exam hall. She wrote one word and pushed the tag across.

Astrid read it upside down. "…'Here'?"

"Checked the spelling and everything," Lynae said, and walked off with her hands in her pockets, fast, the way you leave a counter where you have just spent more than you meant to.

Then Amy.

She took a tag. She looked at the hometown line the way you look at a customs form in a language you used to speak. A cabin with a blue door stood at the end of one answer; a frozen lake at the end of another; a decade of dark at the end of a third, and every road on the form ran through the same woman, currently three stalls away pretending she could pick a dumpling flavor unsupervised.

Amy drew three notes on the line. Ascending. *Da-da-da.*

"That's music," said Astrid.

"It's an address."

The wish line she filled in without hesitating: *marshmallows.* Astrid read it out loud, dubious, as if checking for a trick.

"The expensive kind," Amy clarified, and tied her tag to the frame with a small precise knot, doubled back on itself, a lighthouse-keeper's tie — the kind that holds in weather, the kind somebody teaches you.

Chisa, passing, saw the knot and went still. It was a full three seconds of stillness, the on-watch kind.

"Where did you learn that tie?"

Amy looked at her own hands. Turned them over. The light went through her skin the way it did, slightly, at the edges.

"…Borrowed. Probably."

Chisa's fingers found her scissors. Held the weight, closed. Let it go. The tag hung there between them with its three-note address and its weatherproof knot, and the question hung beside it, and both of them left it hanging, because some doors you knock on later, when you've decided what you'll do if they open.

Next in line, blue silk.

"Hometown?" Astrid asked.

Iuno considered the question with the full apparatus. Thirty years of an office arranged itself behind her eyes: a tower over another country, blue as her own hem, threads pinned across a plot board, all of them converging on a name.

"A moon," she said.

Astrid laughed — bright, eleven, entirely without malice — and wrote *Septimont*, because everyone jokes different, and moved her along. Iuno let it stand. She took her tag to the rail and filled it out with the economy of a woman who had once written prophecies for a living and now wrote only what she intended to keep.

*Filed under humor,* the High Priestess observed, from her velvet chair. *Thirty years of us. Appropriate.*

Somewhere over the sea, over a warrior city that drinks and carves statues, her old moon was rising right now on schedule, electric blue, huge, ordinary to everyone under it. Somebody's washing was drying under her orrery. Somebody was falling in love under the machine she had used to schedule a death. She had left an entire moon behind, furnished, the threads still pinned — and she had walked away from it into a hole in the ground, and the hole in the ground was where her tea got poured before she arrived.

She hung her tag low, where the kettle smoke would reach it.

"Hometown?" Astrid asked the last of them.

Mei opened her mouth. Closed it. The cavern's false stars ticked overhead. Somewhere in her pocket, a student ID from an erased life sat exactly where it had sat for a year, and somewhere much further, on the far side of every wall the world owned, a page waited in the cold with her handwriting already on it.

"…I'll get back to you," Mei said.

Astrid, magistrate, allowed it. "The table's open till moonrise."

"When's moonrise?"

"When it rises," said Astrid, with a shrine attendant's authority, and Mei laughed and took a blank tag and carried it around all night like a coal in her pocket.

Later, at the food stalls, a girl of nine or ten stood in front of Amy humming a tune — an old one, sugar-bright, ten years out of the charts — and asked if she knew it.

"…I've heard it," Amy said.

"It's Fleet Snowfluff. My mom has all of them." The girl studied her, head tilted. "You kind of look like her."

"Everyone says that."

The girl laughed, satisfied, and ran off with her dumplings, singing the borrowed song back into the crowd it came from. Amy stood a moment in the middle of her own audience, unrecognized, her music out there walking around on other people's breath. Then she went and stole a dumpling off her mother's plate, because some inheritances you keep by using them.

## III. A Moon, Announced

The moon itself was Lupa's department, which meant it was oversized before the first joint was cut.

She had drafted a lantern the size of a fishing boat: bone-white paper over a ribbed frame, a heart of cool lamps, rigging to raise it up the shrine's mast where Mornye's students had angled a lens from the Observatory to catch it and throw it, vast and pale, against the cavern ceiling. "It held stars," Mornye had said, when they asked permission, Buddy pulsing warm on her shoulder like a second opinion. "It can hold a moon." Her students did the mathematics of the throw in chalk on the shrine's foundation stones and left the working there, on purpose, the way their professor had taught them: show the city how the miracle was done, and the miracle belongs to the city.

The frame went up in an afternoon. The trouble came at dusk.

Cartethyia was on the top spar, securing the last panel, and the top spar was, to a certain kind of eye, a windmill's crossbeam. She had been ten years old the last time the geometry had looked this good. She rose to her feet on the beam. She spread her arms. Below her, Ciaccona, who had spent two years developing a reflex for exactly this silhouette, was already moving.

"Carte."

"It's a very short spin—"

The hand caught the back of her jacket at the top of the arc, practiced resignation at full extension, and the two of them swung, and held, and lived — and Cartethyia's boot went through the northwest panel with a sound like a drum apologizing.

Silence. The whole work party looked up at their moon, holed.

"…The flying part was easy," Cartethyia offered, from the rigging.

They were out of paper. The supply run would take a day; the festival took tonight. Lupa stood under the lantern with her arms crossed, reading the damage the way masons read a wall, and the pack gathered around the problem the way they gathered around all problems, which is to say immediately and with opinions.

It was Hiyuki who ended the debate. She walked the base of the mast, looked up at the gap — a clean dark bite out of the white circle, a fifth of it gone — and said, in the voice of a century of festivals:

"Raise it."

"It's got a piece missing," said Astrid, scandalized, magistrate to the last.

"It's a crescent," said Hiyuki.

So they raised it broken. Cartethyia stood on the shrine steps and made the announcement, formally, with full titles, because Bastiano had taught her long ago that props take their power from being announced: *"People of Lahai-Roi — I name you a moon!"* — ruinously off-key, structurally unsound, entirely load-bearing. The winch turned. The lamps warmed. The lens caught it.

And a moon rose over the underground, for the first time in the history of the world's ceiling: enormous, gapped, gently wrong, its dark bite riding high on the curve like a phase the astronomers had misplaced. The Phosphor District's coral answered it in blue. The canals took a copy each and held them upside down. Two hundred students and half a city tipped their heads back at once, and the sound they made was soft and huge, the sound a crowd makes when it has been handed something it forgot it was owed.

Afterward — this is on record; the god keeps everything — the witnesses could agree on nothing. A rabbit in the moon, said the children from the east dorms, pounding something. A fish, said the harbor families, absolutely a fish. A door, said one first-year, standing ajar. An eye, said a Royan elder, and refused to elaborate. The moon had been a paper lantern with a boot-hole in it, raised by winch in front of everyone, and the city looked at it and saw a dozen moons anyway, each household certain, each certainty different, and every version, as a certain bard would note in her ledger, was the moon.

Amy stood under it a long time.

She had come home partial and she knew it — a shell run on borrowed light, hunger she remembered her way into, lines on her palms that might belong to strangers. She stood under the gapped lantern with her arms folded and her chin up, the posture of someone daring an old arithmetic to speak.

Hiyuki drifted to her shoulder, frost keeping its polite distance from everyone else's boots.

"It's short a piece," Amy said.

"We greet the crescent as the moon."

"…Even the borrowed kind?"

"Especially," said Hiyuki, and rang the Suzu once, small, and the note went up toward the paper crescent and came back down over the crowd like weather deciding to be kind.

At the edge of the shrine platform, Iuno's crystal stump caught the lantern-light and broke it into prisms across the boards. Her own missing piece, scattering the missing piece's light. She watched the colors move at her feet with an expression the register would have filed under humor.

*A congregation of crescents,* the High Priestess said. *Darling. Look at what we joined.*

Iuno looked. The wolf with the sold childhood, the knight with the twenty dark years, the bard with one face and a ledger of griefs, the girl who reads gaits because faces keep their lies, the daughter running on ten thousand loans, the marksman with the earned letter, the woman with the coal in her pocket. Every one of them up on the platform lacking, openly, in the light. The whole festival was built on the premise. You wait for the round thing knowing it arrives in pieces, and you light the lamps anyway, and you put your hometown on the wish so the light can find its way back to whatever's left of it.

*此事古难全,* something in Mei's marrow said, in a language her mind had misplaced, and she stood very still, and let it keep.

## IV. The Fire, and the Strings

Lupa built the fire too big. This was policy.

The pack settled around it in the shrine's lee after the crowds thinned: cups of wheat juice, a thermos of the earl grey that the whole group now stole on a rotating schedule, dumplings in wax paper, Buddy asleep in the woodpile's warmth like a coal that had opted out of burning. Sigrika had come up the slope late, jaw set, one tag in her fist, and had been intercepted before she could pretend she was passing through. Lynae sat on the platform edge with her boots hanging. Amy sat against Mei's side with the specific density of someone claiming territory. Iuno sat where the second cup was already poured. The bench's far end filled on schedule.

It was Lynae who asked it, because Lynae priced things; it was her original trade and it had kept her alive.

"Ten minutes on the clock," she said, to the fire, to all of them. "Six pilots, one gate, a casualty report the brass pre-wrote. I ran contracts for the Federation. I've read every kind of insane. Nobody sane signs that."

The fire popped. Down in the city, the paper moon burned on.

"Price implies purchase," Iuno said. "We were choosing."

"That's my point. Choosing *that.*" Lynae's eyes went around the circle, mercenary arithmetic looking for the ledger line that balanced. "You walk into the place that unmakes people, for one person, on ten minutes. I keep trying to find the version where that's a decision a real person makes."

"Real people make it constantly," said Ciaccona. She was tuning down, string by string, the day's work being let out of the instrument. "You've just been reading the wrong records. A man in Egla left his door unlocked for a dead girl for twenty years. A lamplighter logged forty years of ships on his own coin because *someone should.* A grandmother kept a verse alive through a theocracy by singing it to babies. A mother across this city kept a room untouched for two decades on the theory of a knock." She struck the low string, let it speak, damped it. "The clock we signed was ten minutes. Rosa's clock was twenty years. Scale it, and tell me who was reckless."

Lynae opened her mouth. Closed it. Her framed letter hung on its wall three buildings away, real now, earned in an exam hall with the whole pack sharpening pencils beside her so one ex-mercenary would walk into the room accompanied. She had, she realized, already accepted the goods. She was auditing a purchase she'd made.

Sigrika's voice came in low, from outside the firelight's first ring, and it came in with a blade in it, because grief that minds its manners suffocates.

"Fine. People do it." Her thumb moved once over the tag in her fist. "So why do *yours* come back?"

The fire took that one and held it. She had twenty-three missed calls in her pocket to a girl who never existed, and fourteen more to the woman who had built her, and across this circle sat a family the void had handed back gift-wrapped. From where Sigrika stood, the story played favorites, and she had come up the slope to say so to its face.

Amy went very still against Mei's side. Nobody rushed to answer, and that was the answer's first half: they let the unfairness stand in the room, whole, unargued, because it was true.

Then Lupa leaned forward, elbows on knees, and did her job.

"Count-off," she said, and went around the fire with names, plainly, one hand rising a fraction with each. Mei. Iuno. Cartethyia. Ciaccona. Chisa. Amy. Lynae. Hiyuki. "Sigrika."

Sigrika's head came up. "Since when?"

"Since I said it. Saying it out loud — that's the entire mechanism. I checked." Lupa kept going, past the living, her voice the same. "Cantarella, under a different sky. Augusta. The lady in the lavender place; her light's on. Mornye, asleep on her console again. Mya." A beat. "Sumika." Another. "Denia."

Sigrika's breath caught on the last name like cloth on a nail.

"She was strings," Sigrika said. Flat. Testing.

"So's a lute," said Ciaccona.

"She was *made.*"

"Everyone at this fire was made by something," said Cartethyia, mildly, from her seat on the woodpile. "A house bought some of us. A church grew one of us. A notebook—" her eyes touched Mei's, one moth-light landing, gone — "well. The making is where you start. It's rude to hold it against a person." She fed the fire a stick. "Ask her the questions when you find her side of the door. Tonight she's counted. That's what the count is for."

"Same moon," Lupa said, and tipped her head at the paper crescent burning over the city. "Every name I said is under it tonight. That's the rule. I set it an hour ago."

Sigrika stood there a moment longer with her tag in her fist. Then she came into the light and sat, on the far end of Lupa's log, and her phone stayed in her pocket the whole rest of the night, and that was the whole of her surrender, and nobody remarked on it, which was the courtesy.

The fire burned down a ring. The city's noise softened below.

It was Chisa who reopened it, because Chisa did everything on purpose, including surgery.

"You asked what makes ours different," she said, to Lynae, to Sigrika, to the flames. "I can tell you what I see."

She held her hand out, palm down, the red string on her wrist catching the light. Her voice stayed level, a technician reading an instrument.

"My eyes skip faces. I get strings instead. On the puppet, they went down — hundreds, into the dark, one author at the bottom. Around this fire, they go across. Wrist to wrist. Some old, some new. Some knotted twice." She turned her hand over. "Same material. From where I stand. I looked the whole way down the Nihility, and I looked around this fire, and I am telling you the honest reading: I couldn't tell the difference."

The fire cracked. Somewhere behind Amy's sternum, ten thousand borrowed voices held very still.

"So." Chisa closed her hand. "I tie mine on purpose. That's the difference I get to make. It's the only one on offer, as far as I've measured, and I've measured."

She sat back. The scissors lay across her knees, closed. The question she had opened stayed open — it is open still; the god has checked — and the pack sat inside it together, which is a thing you can do with an open question, it turns out. You can live in one. You can furnish it.

Mei set her cup down.

"Down there," she said, and everyone knew where *down there* was, "it showed me a set."

She turned the cup a slow half-circle on the boards, an old borrowed habit, poisoner's provenance.

"Cardboard. Rigging. Stage lights. The tombs were styrofoam." Her voice stayed light; the light was doing heavy work. "It had little cards on things. Tags. Your names were on some of the cards."

Lupa: "Spelled right?"

"…Yeah."

"Then it did more homework than the Hill ever did." The wolf's ears stayed forward, steady, taking the weight of the thing by refusing to flinch at it.

"What did mine say?" Cartethyia asked. Direct, sunny, terrible.

Mei looked at her across the fire. Whatever was printed behind her eyes, she folded it once, and put it away, and made the pack's oldest move: she scheduled it.

"Ask me in a year."

"Appointment made," said Cartethyia, and meant it, and the fire ate another ring, and the tag with her name on it went on existing somewhere behind Mei's smile, filed, unread aloud, a coal banked instead of buried — which the pack could tell apart by now, and did, and let her keep it warm.

Lupa watched the smile finish reassembling itself and narrowed her eyes.

"You're doing the thing."

"…Yeah." Mei let the smile drop a degree, down to something with weather in it. "Working on it."

"Work faster," said Lupa, and refilled her cup, which between them was a whole negotiated treaty.

Later, quieter, when the fire had burned to the honest hour, Amy asked hers. She had been watching Iuno across the flames the whole time with her mother's gold eyes and her own borrowed patience, and she put it plainly, the way she put everything.

"Why'd you stay? Start to finish."

"I'm selfish," Iuno said.

Amy waited her out. The fire ticked. The paper moon burned. Iuno looked into her cup, and the accent held — the same two words she had said on a cliff, in a castle window, over a cracked teacup in a tavern that stocked no earl grey — the accent unchanged, and everything under it turned all the way over, like a tide that kept the same name through both directions.

"…It's the whole ledger," she added.

*Audited annually,* said the High Priestess. *The balance improves.*

"Hm." Amy accepted it. Then, sly, small, a knife wrapped in a gift: "Ma says it different."

"She would," said Iuno, and drank, and across the fire Mei pretended magnificent deafness and poured the second cup again, and slid it, and it was received.

The children came up the slope around then — Astrid off-shift, the Snowfluff girl, a cluster of dormitory strays drawn to any fire built that big — and demanded a story, as children at fires are constitutionally required to do. Cartethyia gave them the old one. The knight, the giant, the windmill arms, the blows that came to nothing.

"Was he mad?" asked Astrid, who liked her facts sorted, four decades and one drowned country downstream of the last girl who'd asked.

"Certainly," said Cartethyia. "Now ask the good one."

The children conferred. The Snowfluff girl raised her hand. "Why did the giant let him live?"

Cartethyia smiled — and it went all the way down, twenty years down, past a tower and a hundred dolls and a fountain where a stranger had said her name twice, free of charge.

"Because the knight came back the next day," she said. "And the day after. Giants keep appointments too." She fed the fire its last big log. "That's the whole of home, as far as I've ever mapped it. A fight you're allowed to come back to."

The children absorbed this with the polite blankness children reserve for adult philosophy, and went back to arguing about the moon rabbit. Around the fire, several adults sat very still, having just been handed, free of charge, the sentence a few of them had spent whole decades trying to find the far end of.

## V. The Far Side of the Page

It was the bard who noticed, because noticing was her trade.

"Your vowels go somewhere when you hum," Ciaccona said to Mei, apropos of the fire, of the hour, of a half-heard sound Mei hadn't known she was making. "They keep visiting hours. Take us along, for once."

"I don't have a song." Mei said it on reflex, the customs-form answer.

"You have an accent," said Ciaccona. "Accents are songs with the melody sanded off. Hum."

So Mei hummed, and partway through the humming her mouth changed departments. It happened the way it had happened on a rooftop with a daughter in her lap: muscle memory reaching past the sanded places, an erased life paying out line through her jaw. Words arrived. Old ones, round ones, in a language from the far side of every wall the world owned, and she let them come the way you let a tide come, which is to say with your feet already wet.

"人有悲欢离合，" she said, half-song, and translated with her hands open, rough, apologizing for the roughness: "People part. People come back. Sorrow, joy — the set menu."

The fire listened. Amy had gone very still against her side.

"月有阴晴圆缺，" — and her eyes went up, helpless, to the paper crescent with its boot-hole riding high — "the moon runs the same menu. Dark. Bright. Round." A breath. "…Missing a piece."

"此事古难全。" She laughed, one soft huff, at whatever her marrow showed her next. "It's been this way since before anyone. Perfect never shipped."

"但愿人长久，" — and here the translating slowed, because the line was load-bearing and her voice knew it before she did — "so: wish long. Wish everyone *long*."

"千里共婵娟。"

She sat with the last line a moment, turning it, the way you turn a key you've been carrying without a door.

"A thousand miles apart," she said finally. "One moon anyway."

The fire burned. Overhead, a lantern with a hole in it did its patient impression of the real thing, and under it, by decree, sat a poisoner watering camellias across a sea, an Ephor with a pruned rose, a violinist in a lavender country with her light on, two travelers of unknown whereabouts, a woman asleep in the cold on the far side of a page. Wish long. One moon anyway. The festival had been running on the doctrine for a century before Mei's mouth remembered the poem that stated it; that is the way of doctrines, which are only ever old songs the whole species keeps mishearing back into truth.

"…The radio played that," Amy said. Small. "In the cabin. Sunday static."

She hummed it — the tune, the far-side setting, and she hummed it *wrong*, a quarter-tone under, ruinously, structurally unsound, and Ciaccona's hands were already moving, eight unhurried bars, building the floor beneath the wrongness until the error stood up and became an argument, a signature, a feature of the architecture.

"There," said the bard. "Load-bearing."

And then, mid-song, without announcement, without looking up — because that is how a Toccata deals anything she loves, off the bottom of the deck — Ciaccona folded three notes into the bridge.

*Da-da-da.* Ascending. Unmistakable to exactly the people it was for.

Cartethyia's head came up across the fire. One look passed between the knight and the bard, over the flames, the length of an old promise — *your word, as a Toccata* — and the word was kept, witnessed, closed.

Amy sat up straight.

"That's—"

"Yours," said Ciaccona, tuning as she spoke, eyes on the pegs, the way you keep your eyes down when it matters. "It had somewhere to go."

"…and someone to go with," Amy finished, quiet, finishing a sentence she had never once been told, and the bard's fingers paused on the strings for a rest of exactly one beat, and then resumed, and the fire kept the pause the way the wind keeps everything, filed, unexplained, waiting for a later verse.

The song went around after that, the way rough songs do. The children caught the chorus wrong and improved it. Sigrika, of all people, supplied a harmony line in a cracked alto and glared at anyone who noticed. Hiyuki sang one verse alone in the old Honami festival register, and Chisa came in under her a fifth low, and their two dead-city accents disagreed on a single vowel all the way through, and they kept both, and the disagreement scanned. By the third pass the thing had grown a new verse entirely — somebody's, nobody's, everybody's — and the bard sat in the middle of it grinning like a woman watching a fire she didn't light and needed, keeping, as always, the tab.

And when it finally wound down, when the cups were cold and the children had been carried off to dorms and the paper moon had begun its gentle sag toward morning — the ventilation exhaled.

Low, from below the floor of the world, through the ducts and organ-throats of a body the size of a mountain: the tune. Their tune, the whole patchwork of it, hummed back a quarter-tone flat, structurally unsound, entirely fond.

The pack went quiet. Buddy, in the woodpile, pulsed twice, warm.

Nobody commented. Some accompaniments you don't interrupt by naming. But Ciaccona, very softly, put a floor under it — eight bars, load-bearing — and somewhere beneath ten thousand feet of stone and city, a god who had been kneeling for a century over a locked gate hummed his part like a regular, like a body at the far end of the bench, like the biggest crescent at the fire.

## Coda: What the Register Kept

Hiyuki worked the tag-lines at the grey hour, before the city's committee-sun warmed, gathering the night's wishes for the shrine's first ledger. Lucilla found her there with a thermos and two cups, on the grounds that a certain appointment had a schedule now.

"You said over tea."

"The kettle's been on a century," said Hiyuki, and poured, and the frost around her feet drew back another finger's width, the way it had been doing, lately, one festival at a time.

The tags came down one by one into her keeping. *Marshmallows,* addressed to three ascending notes — hung back up, that one; some wishes are delivery instructions. Astrid's, which read *a moon that stays,* filed under futures. Lynae's, hometown *Here,* wish illegible, folded twice, kept folded; a shrine maiden knows a locked box when she holds one. Sigrika's tag she hung highest, first in the lamplight's path, where the morning would find it before it found anything else — a name on it that had lived, whatever the strings said, in the middle pages of a girl's phone, which is a real place, which is one of the realest places there is.

Two tags gave her pause.

The first was written in a hand her century of filing had never met. Hometown: *the wings.* Wish: *surprise me.* The frost at her feet stood very still. She read it twice. Then she hung it where she could watch it, at the exact height of her own eyes, and made a note in the ledger that consisted, in its entirety, of the date.

The second arrived while she worked — footsteps she knew, a coal finally coming out of a pocket. Mei stood at the rail in the grey light with her tag filled out at last, and hung it herself, beside the three-note address, low, where the kettle smoke would reach.

Hometown: *still checking.*

Wish: *same time tomorrow.*

Hiyuki read it. Seer's courtesy: she asked nothing. She rang the Suzu once, small, and the note went up past the sagging paper moon, through the vents, into the dreaming stone.

The god kept all of it. The disputed festival with both its names. The register of hometowns, boiling lakes and traveled families and a moon filed under humor. The crescent greeted as the moon. The count said out loud, binding, dead included, especially the dead. The strings that read the same from outside and get tied, on purpose, from within. Three notes, dealt at last, and a sentence finished by a girl who was never taught it. A tag from the wings, watched. A fight you're allowed to come back to. He filed it all by weight, and it weighed a great deal, and he has been kneeling long enough to know that this is the heading the whole archive sorts under, the oldest one, the one every civilization he has outlived kept reinventing and calling by new names:

*home* — the standing appointment; the door on oiled hinges; the second cup poured before the guest; the hole in the verse where everyone gets in.

And somewhere past the last wall of the world, on the far side of the page, a real moon leaned against a small cold window, round that night, or missing a piece — the instruments had no column for it — and kept a sleeping girl company until tomorrow, same time.

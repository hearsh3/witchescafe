# «Антракт»

*the long middle · Denia, adrift*

---

*I am always doing what I cannot do yet, in order to learn how to do it.*

— Vincent van Gogh, to his brother

---

> **ANTRAKT.** The house lights come up. Nothing is performed.
> Estimated duration: twenty minutes.

The estimate was wrong.

---

## i. — hour one (est.)

She is bored.

She gives it a while. She turns it over from several angles, the way you check a coin somebody handed you in change. It keeps coming up the same.

Bored. Inside the thing that was advertised to her nightly for fifteen years as the end of all wanting. Bored at the bottom of the lake, in the country of nothing-is-asked-of-anything, roughly one hour into forever, with her arms folded and a distinct sense that something ought to be happening by now.

The problem is procedural. Boredom is a complaint, and complaints are filed on behalf of a preference, and a preference is a thing that a person is supposed to have died out of.

She has been down here sixty minutes and has already been caught with contraband on her person.

*"You'll settle,"* says the Erlking, from wherever it is.

"That's what they said about the ceiling."

*"You named the ceiling."*

"That's what I mean."

It goes quiet again. It has been going quiet a great deal, which is new. Upstairs it never shut up. Down here it has the manner of a man in his own kitchen at four in the afternoon, wandering through, opening things.

She lies on the deck and is bored, magnificently, wastefully, at industrial scale, and files the boredom under load-bearing, because a girl who was designed to feel nothing has just spent an hour feeling *insufficiently entertained,* and if that is the caliber of nothing she was built to deliver, then the whole product line should have been recalled at the crib.

---

## ii. — hour nine (est.)

The body is gone. The body's opinions stayed.

She catalogues them because she has to do something and the alternative is more of the ceiling.

She wants the weight of a full cup. Specifically that: full, so it has an opinion about being carried; the way the surface tilts and threatens and forgives you. She wants a chair with a back on it. She wants the sound a chair leg makes when it drags on a hard floor, which is a sound she has hated her entire life and would currently trade several organs for. She wants the pressure of a waistband. She wants to be slightly too warm and to have to push a blanket off with one foot and to be smug about the transaction.

She wants to be hungry and then to stop being hungry.

She reaches up to put a strand of hair behind her ear.

There is no strand and no ear and the hand is a habit, and it does the whole gesture anyway — up, catch, tuck — with the fidelity of four hundred hours of installation, and lands in nothing, and comes back down.

She does it again about a minute later without deciding to.

The wall of the ward said YOUR FLESH IS A CAGE at adult eye level and she learned to read on it, and she has spent fifteen years being congratulated in advance for the day the door opened.

Nobody mentioned that she was going to miss the bars.

## iii. — hour thirty-one (est.)

She takes down the set.

It happens by itself, the way tidying does when there is nothing else in the flat. She goes through what people hung on her, item by item, and finds that she is still holding all of it and has been holding it with equipment she no longer possesses.

Halvard needed a joke. A public lecture at nine in the morning wants one reliable clown in the fourth row so that the other three hundred and ninety-nine may be forgiven for their own eyelids; she supplied the service for four years, free of charge, with excellent comic timing, and he peered over his glasses and said *Miss Denia graces us* and everybody breathed out. She never once minded. She was good at it. That is the part she keeps turning over — she was so *good* at it.

The campus needed someone who never studied. A whole institution of exhausted teenagers requires one visible person who is failing upward on charm alone, because the alternative is admitting that everyone is drowning at the same rate. She played the position for four years and the position paid in laughs and the laughs were real.

The Architect needed a masterpiece.

The void needed an agreement.

And Sigrika —

She stops there for a while, on the deck, in the work light.

Sigrika needed a friend who wanted nothing.

That is the whole architecture of it, examined at last with the lights up. A girl who believes she must work hard enough to be *worth keeping* can only afford a friendship that costs her nothing, because everything else in her life is on an invoice. And in walked the one person on the continent constitutionally incapable of asking for anything: lazy, cheap to maintain, requiring no reciprocity, arriving pre-satisfied. Perfect fit. Frictionless. Zero balance.

She was manufactured to hold other people's projections without deforming, and she met a girl who could only be loved by something that never asked, and they were happy for four years.

*Sorry about the fit,* she thinks. *It was a specification.*

The Erlking drifts past and declines to comment, which is the most tactful thing it has ever done and she resents it enormously.

Then, near the bottom of the pile, she finds the account she never had to service.

A white glove. A dish of candy on a counter outside a door, refilled every Tuesday, and then — for a while at the end, when a man with twenty years of watching over a city could read a countdown as well as anyone — refilled every single day. He never asked her a question. He never named it, never made her thank him, never once required her to be anything in exchange. He said *Tuesdays* and she said *noted* and that was the entire text of a four-year relationship.

She takes two out of the dish. She puts one back.

She does it about eleven times, down there, for no audience, because it is the only ritual she has ever been part of that had no counterparty and no debt, and it turns out that when you strip a person of everything, that is the sort of thing that survives: a dish, a rule she invented herself, and a man who let her steal.

---

## iv. — day two (est.)

The place cards.

She has three of them, or the memory of three, laid in the same spot on the same acreage of white cloth across the years, in the same lovely hand.

SPECIMEN.

Then, one year, with no comment: DENIA.

Then, one year, with no comment: ДЕНИЯ.

She thinks about that a great deal in the drift. Everyone in her life has spelled her in the borrowed alphabet, the working one, the one on the intake form. One person went to the trouble of finding out which letters she had been cut from and set them by her plate without ever once mentioning it.

The woman who did that also built the collar.

There is no arrangement of those two facts that gets them to sit flat. She has tried several. She lies in the dark and tries several more.

Here is the closest she gets:

The Architect gave her three origins — the exhibit hall, the growth vat, the doorstep — and rotated them by year, and the child understood at ten that the drawer was the point, that a girl with one origin can be stolen by it and a girl with three has been issued a lifetime of checking locks. That was cruelty. It was designed as cruelty, delivered as cruelty, and it worked: she has never been able to be sure of anything about herself and she has spent her whole life at the archive with clearances she should not have.

It also made her impossible to lie to.

She notes this in the dark with the cold hilarity that an entire campus mistook for laziness. The abuse came with a skill attached. The skill is in her hands and the hands are hers. She has decided, on the deck, in the work light, at whatever hour this is, to keep the skill and evict the teacher, which is she believes the entire art of coming from somewhere.

And there is a smaller thing under all of it, and she saves it for last because it is the one that costs.

For fifteen years, at a table built for twenty, somebody laid a second place.

Every table Denia has ever set — the ward with its forty-eight named tiles, the pit down here with its drifting hundred-year stove — is a forgery of that one. She learned hospitality from a woman feeding a specimen. She learned to make a room bearable from the person making the room. She is a hostess because a collector poured her honey cake and called her *dear* and landed on the *dear*.

*I hate that,* she thinks, and then remembers what her hates are, and lies very still for a while.

---

## v. — day (the count is under review)

Nastasha did cities by key, in the rental hours, week after week, and Denia has all of them.

The capital in C major: all avenues and audit, a city with a ledger, everything squared. She walks it in the dark and it is exactly as advertised.

The coast town in A minor, salt in the pedals. She walks that one twice because the second time she notices the harbor.

There are eleven cities. She goes through them all and they are perfect and there is something the matter with every one, and it takes her a long time to find it, and when she finds it she laughs at the ceiling of the water for a while.

There is no weather in them.

Nastasha had never been to a single one. She played them out of books and postcards and one aunt's letters, and books do not carry weather, and so the cities in Denia's head are eleven beautiful stage sets with the temperature control switched off. Nobody in them is cold. No one has been rained on. The light is the light of a rental hour on the third floor of the piano annex with a girl's hands running a scale like water finding its level.

Denia would give anything at all to be rained on in one of them.

And then there is the twelfth, which Nastasha played once, in D-flat.

Bells under ice. Stalls of layered honey cake going amber in the cold. Streetlamps bent by frost into low warm crescents.

She circles it.

That is the honest word and she uses it on herself without flinching. She has been circling that city for fifteen years at a fixed radius, the way something gets stuck at the edge of something sweet — close enough to smell it, wings going, never landing, because landing would require finding out. Every letter she ever addressed to a farewell was a lap. Every dream of a lake and a woman on the far shore was a lap. She has flown around one city so many times that the flying has become a career.

*Go in,* says the Erlking, from wherever it is, with genuine encouragement, which is how she knows to stay out.

"Later," says Denia.

---

## vi. — Thursday (est.)

She has one thing left to write and no way to write it and nothing else to do, so she drafts.

---

```

Sigrika — by the time you read this the collar will have
completed. Here is what a collar is, and here is what it was
keyed to, and here is why a ward full of technicians was the
worse of the two available floors —
```

Too long. She knows it is too long before she is out of the first clause, because it is doing the thing. It is *explaining.* Somewhere on a campus a girl is being handed a mechanism when she asked for a person.


Explaining.

---

```
DRAFT 11.

Sigrika — I hated you. You should know that it was the good
kind. There's a key; ask the Rover; it's on file —
```

That one takes her a while to reject, because it is true, and because it is short, and because she is proud of it.

She rejects it anyway. It is a *grand* answer. It comes with a decoder ring and a twist. It is the kind of thing you write when what you actually want is to be understood at last, in full, by somebody who is standing at your grave, and she has been to enough of her own funerals to recognize the genre.

*Something wearing this face gives you a beautiful answer, an answer that explains — you walk.*

She wrote that protocol on a dorm floor between a candle and a lullaby, in the eleven relaxed minutes when Sigrika believed the night was ordinary, and she has never once had it turned on herself, and it turns out it works from the inside, which is exactly the sort of engineering she should have expected from her own hand.

The right answer will always be small. It's owed.

So she goes looking for the small one, and the search takes — hours, days, a season, the count is under review — and the small one is not where she is looking, because she is looking in the direction of the lake, and the crime is in the direction of the door.

Here it is.

She used the Dream Maker on her.

The toy that had one recording in the reservoir. The heartbeat, at rest, unhurried, close, recorded through cloth or years or glass. The single piece of evidence in the world that Denia had ever had one — put in a bubble, drifting the way milk drifts into tea, touching down on a shoulder and holding.

*That's cheating,* Sigrika mumbled, at half-mast, losing a war she had refused to declare.

*I'm a Fractsidus creation. Cheating is my whole cultural heritage.*

She put her to sleep so she could leave.

Four years of staying awake so that a girl would never study alone, four years of face-down on a desk at two in the morning keeping company nobody had asked for and nobody would ever bill for — and at the end she took the same instrument and used it as a door-holder. She spent Sigrika's trust to buy a clean exit. She weaponised the one unperformed thing she had ever done.

That is the crime. It is small and it is domestic and it will not go on a monument.

The apology is smaller.

She says it out loud, in a place with nobody in it, to nobody, with nothing at stake and no possibility of it being heard or credited or entered on any record anywhere:

"I should have woken you up."

The dark takes it. The dark takes everything; that is the dark's whole job.

It is the first entirely true sentence she has ever said with no audience in the building, and it is four words long, and it costs her more than the lake did.

## vii. — Thursday again, apparently

The count breaks.

She has been keeping it out of professional pride — an hour here, a day there, revised, revised again, the estimates going out like a lamp on a long corridor — and then she reaches for the number and finds a shelf where the number went.

She stands in the middle of that for a while.

Then she does what she does, which is get busy, lazily.

A clock requires something that changes. She surveys her holdings. The deck: constant. The work light: constant. The drift at ankle height: circular and therefore useless, a hundred-year stove going round on a fixed orbit. The Erlking: eternal by contract.

Nothing down here has a Tuesday in it.

She sits with that, and the panic comes up and she lets it come up and stand there and be seen, which is another first, and then it goes back down.

And then she remembers she has a calendar.

She memorised it four years ago, involuntarily, by proximity, because a girl used to complain about it out loud at two in the morning while reading formulas to stay honest — the whole term timetable, blocks and rooms and the professor who moves the runework seminar every third week and denies it. Denia never once studied it. It went in the way weather goes in. She could recite it in a coma. She has, in fact, recited it in a coma.

The last durable clock in the universe is a nineteen-year-old's course schedule, and Denia is the only surviving copy.

She starts it up.

*She'll be up since six,* Denia thinks. *She'll have made the terrible coffee and hated it and drunk it.*

Will.

She hears the word go past. She looks at it the way you look at something you have just picked up off a hot stove.

Then she puts her hand back on it.

*She'll be up since six.*

---

## viii. — Sigrika has a nine o'clock

06:00. Up. Always up, even now, especially now; grief in that girl arrives dressed as punctuality.

06:20. The coffee from the machine on the second landing, which is chemically hostile and which she has drunk every morning for four years out of some private theory about consistency.

06:40 to 08:45. Revision. Chapter four, because chapter four has never once been forgiven.

09:00. Runework. Front row, third seat from the aisle, which is the seat of a person who wants to be seen trying.

11:00. Declensions. She will get the third one wrong. She always gets the third one wrong, and it is not because she does not know it — she knows it in her sleep, she has said it aloud in her sleep, Denia has heard her — it is because the examiner looks up on the third one and she panics.

13:00. The custard. Structurally unsound, sentimental favorite.

Denia runs the day.

She runs it all the way through, hour by hour, in a dark with no dimension, and it takes exactly as long as it takes, and she does not skip the boring parts, because the boring parts are the parts a person actually lives in.

Then she runs it again the next day.

Somewhere around what she is choosing to call the eleventh iteration, she catches herself and stops and has a look at what she is doing.

There is no audience. She checks, properly, the way you check a lock you have already checked. Four hundred empty seats and a work light. Nobody upstairs knows. Nobody will ever know. Every channel out of this place runs the one direction, and the ledger closed on a Thursday when the seam did, and the whole business is therefore free of charge and permanently unreportable.

She is doing it anyway. She has been doing it for eleven days or eleven weeks. She cannot stop and has not tried.

It is, she recognises with rising and total humiliation, precisely what she did for four years at a desk with her face on her arms while an entire campus laughed about the girl who slept through everything.

The habit outlived the girl.

She had built a whole philosophy out of the position that everything about her was staged, and here is the staging, running in an empty house, in the dark, after death, with the box office closed and the marquee down and every seat refunded.

"Oh, that's embarrassing," says Denia, out loud.

*"I don't understand,"* says the Erlking.

"No," she agrees. "You wouldn't. It's a thing about audiences."

## ix.

She goes back to the twelfth city and this time she stops circling.

First, the audit. She has learned a new instrument recently, on a doorstep, in the arms of the finest forgery anyone has ever built for her, and the instrument is *wear*, and she turns it on her own inventory now with the thoroughness of a woman who has read her own schematics.

Findings.

The cake: honey, layered, going amber in the cold. Available in eleven cities and at a long table. Provenance unestablished.

The wool: oversized, dark, smelling of cold and sugar. She has never once been able to describe the coat's cut, in fifteen years, under any conditions. A person who was carried in a coat should know the cut of the coat.

The word: *до свидания.* On the intake form. In the file. Entered by a stranger in a flat voice, read back to her, corrected for tone.

Which raises the finding she has been walking around for a decade and a half.

The file gave her the syllables. She may have built the woman around them the way a pearl gets built around grit. The whole memory may be an original composition, written at four, revised annually, performed nightly, by a girl who needed one warm room and had the materials to construct one and was, from the very beginning, extremely good at building rooms beyond audit.

She sits with that and finds it survivable, which surprises her.

Then she checks the coat.

Her coat. Her version. The one she has carried her whole life by one corner, everywhere.

The corner is bald. The color has gone through to thread at exactly the place where a thumb goes. The nap is flat in a stripe. The cuff is shined the way cloth shines when a sleeve has gone into a pocket ten thousand times.

That is the whole finding and she turns it over in the dark for a long while.

The wear proves nothing about the woman. It proves the carrying. Fifteen years of one child's hand going to the same corner in the dark, every night, in a room of cribs like a room of parentheses, third from the wall.

*That's mine,* she thinks. *Whoever the coat belonged to, the thumb was mine.*

And it occurs to her, lying on a stage deck at the bottom of a lake, that carrying is in fact the only thing anybody ever gets. Nobody is issued proof. Sigrika has a photograph of three girls under a flowering ash with *Best friends forever!* across the bottom in her own handwriting and two of the three turned out to be something else entirely, and she carries it. A wolf says a dead woman's name out loud in front of a crowd twenty years late and the name is the only thing left in a void that eats everything, and she carries it. A man refills a dish for a thief who will never come back on a Tuesday.

Everybody is holding a coat with no papers on it.

So Denia goes in.

She walks into the twelfth city on purpose, at her own speed, as a guest and not a tenant, with her eyes fully open and the audit filed and the verdict entered. Bells under ice. The stalls with their amber. The streetlamps bent by frost into low warm crescents, which she has always, always reached for.

The snow comes down at the right rate, taking its full salary of air.

The wool sets her down on the step, and the shape against the snow-light turns, and the hand comes up, and the word crosses the cold like a bell across water, and Denia stands there at four years old and lets it land, all the way, without one single hedge, for the first time in her life.

Then she wipes her face and walks out of her own city and closes the door behind her.

Warm on the right side.

She does not stay. That is the entire development and it is very small and it took her fifteen years and one death.

## x. — cold

Something arrives from outside.

She notices it in the way you notice that a window has been opened somewhere in a large house: nothing you could point at, a change in the argument the air is having.

It takes her a while to name it, because down here she has been at one temperature for the whole of forever, and one temperature is the same as none.

Cold.

Actual cold, dropping the long way down through however much water this is, from a surface that has apparently been having a *season* this entire time — has been out there getting on with October and November while she lay on a deck running a nineteen-year-old's timetable — and the cold gets into her the way it got into her boots on a step when she was four, and she lets it, interested.

*It's nothing,* says the Erlking. *A gradient. Water does that.*

She turns her head.

"How long have you known."

*"...A while."*

"Mm," says Denia.

She is not even angry. It is such a small, cheap, entirely human piece of behavior — noticing the exit and saying nothing, hoping the guest doesn't check the clock — that she finds herself almost fond of it, and being almost fond of the Nihility is the single strangest thing that has ever happened to her, and she has been dead for a season and has met her own mother in a hallucination.

Cold means an outside. Cold is the only thing in the universe that arrives without being invited and takes nothing when it goes. Cold is weather, and weather is proof that somewhere above her there is a world doing things she did not arrange, on a schedule she did not set, for no purpose involving her whatsoever.

She sits up.

*"You could stay for the rest of it,"* the Erlking says.

"The rest of what?"

*"...."*

"Yeah," says Denia. "That's what I thought."

She stands, in the work light, at the smallest charge a system is permitted to carry, with no proof of anything and a stranger's coat worn through at one corner and a girl's timetable running in her head, and she looks up.

First Date, First Snow

Cartethyia got two steps past the salt-house threshold before she noticed that Ciaccona’s left shoulder was sitting level with her right.

Usually the wide leather strap of the lute case pulled that side of Ciaccona’s coat down by a finger’s breadth, and when she walked down a steep lane her right hand stayed crooked at her hip to keep the round maple belly from knocking against the wall. Tonight both of Ciaccona’s hands were shoved deep into her coat pockets, and the only thing swinging at her side was her empty scabbard-frog. The lute was still inside, leaning against the warm brick at the foot of the kang.

Cartethyia looked from Ciaccona’s bare shoulder up to the side of her face, then tucked her chin down into Lupa’s wool scarf and kept the observation to herself. The scarf smelled strongly of mutton drippings and scorched scallion, and it was long enough to go round her neck three times and still leave an end hanging down her back like a herald’s tabard.

Above the tile ridges, the sky sat low and yellow-grey across the gorge, flat as the underside of a baking stone. There was no wind at all. The smoke from the bathhouse chimney down on the third terrace rose straight up like a grey post until it met the cloud-lid and spread out.

They turned down the narrow stone stair between the salt-stores and the old cooperage. Halfway down, the steps bent hard around a buttress where a leafless walnut tree leaned out over the drop, its roots wedged into the dry mortar. Cartethyia slowed her boots on the turn, looked at the iron ring set into the wall for the barrel-ropes, and touched the cold stone with two fingers.

“<font color=skyblue>Via del Forno,</font>” she whispered to the buttress. “<font color=skyblue>By the cooper’s arch.</font>”

Beside her, Ciaccona shifted from the inner wall to the outer edge of the treads, putting her own elbow between Cartethyia and the low coping over the alley. She didn’t look round or make a joke about Ragunnese street-signs in a Huanglong gorge.

“<font color=teal>Mind the third step down from the tree, cara,</font>” Ciaccona said. “<font color=teal>The mortar’s gone out from under the front edge. It tips.</font>”

Cartethyia stepped over the loose riser and fell back into stride beside her, skipping once to catch the rhythm of Ciaccona’s longer legs. Now that they were out of earshot of the salt-house shutter, she pulled the scarf down half an inch from her mouth.

“<font color=skyblue>For a consultation,</font>” Cartethyia said, “<font color=skyblue>do we order the tea as soon as we sit down, or is there a part we have to do first?</font>”

Ciaccona’s head turned slowly. “<font color=teal>A part.</font>”

“<font color=skyblue>Like stating the business.</font>” Cartethyia looked ahead down the steep run of steps toward the rope bridge, where the heavy hemp cables crossed the cleft between the terraces. “<font color=skyblue>When Uncle Cosimo went to the syndic’s clerk about the boundary wall behind the wood-shed, he had to buy a stamped sheet of paper at the door and say whether it was about timber or water before anyone brought the ink out. I don’t think we need paper, because Ruoyun already wrote it in her book, and Buling wasn’t invited anyway.</font>”

Ciaccona’s mouth opened slightly, then shut. She lifted her splinted left hand from her pocket, rubbed the back of her thumb across the bridge of her nose, and slid the hand back into the wool.

“<font color=teal>There’s no stamped paper, cara,</font>” she said. “<font color=teal>You sit down and they bring the pot.</font>”

“<font color=skyblue>Good. For the consultation, I think I should have the pickled radish, if they do it here.</font>” Cartethyia nodded once to settle the point. “<font color=skyblue>Something sour keeps your head clear when you’re discussing things. And soup, if the bowls are the deep kind.</font>”

Ciaccona stared straight ahead at the bridge abutment. Something small worked at the corner of her mouth, as if she were chewing on a word she had dropped on the floor herself and couldn’t politely kick under the bench.

“<font color=teal>Radish,</font>” Ciaccona agreed. “<font color=teal>We’ll put it on the agenda.</font>”

The steps let them out onto the second terrace directly beneath the eastern stone pier of the rope bridge. Overhead, the bridge’s massive timber trestles rose thirty feet to meet the cliff face, roofed over with wide overlapping planks of dark fir to keep the wet off the anchor-chains. Beneath that high shelter, built right into the dry angle of the rock, sat a small eating-house with no front wall at all. Six square pine tables stood on swept flagstones around an iron charcoal brazier. Along the front eaves, six round lanterns of thin oiled paper dyed the bright, deep blue of a kingfisher’s wing hung dark from iron hooks, swaying a hair’s breadth in the draught off the gorge.

Only two of the tables were taken: an old carter in a greasy sheepskin vest eating noodles near the brazier, and two masons from the lower wall warming their palms over clay cups at the far end.

By the front corner-post hung a heavy tablet of planed cedar where the house’s name should have been carved. Two centuries of gorge mist and sleet had scoured the wood down to bare silver grain; not a single stroke of paint or gilt remained in the grooves.

Cartethyia stopped beside the post and tipped her head back to study the blank board, her crystal laurel slipping a fraction toward her left ear until Ciaccona’s hand came out of her pocket and hooked it straight again.

A stout woman in a dark blue apron came out from behind the brick stove-screen at the back, carrying a damp cloth and a stack of upside-down teacups. She had grey hair pinned tight with a bone bodkin and the unhurried walk of someone whose feet hurt every evening at the same hour.

“<font color=peachpuff>Two?</font>” she asked, already wiping the corner table nearest the inner cliff.

“<font color=skyblue>Yes, please,</font>” Cartethyia said. She pointed up at the weathered cedar tablet. “<font color=skyblue>Excuse me, ma’am, what is the name of this place? The board’s gone clean.</font>”

The woman paused with her cloth flat on the pine. She glanced up at the blank wood as if she hadn’t looked at it since the last reign, then back down at Cartethyia’s crooked crown and yellow eyes.

“<font color=peachpuff>The carters call it under the bridge,</font>” she said. “<font color=peachpuff>The clerks from the second terrace call it Old Hu’s, after him over there.</font>” She jerked her chin toward the back corner behind the brazier, where a thin old man in a quilted vest sat on a low stool running a cake of yellow resin down the horsehair of a fiddle bow. “<font color=peachpuff>When we hung the hooks out, it was the Kingfisher. Nobody uses it now.</font>”

“<font color=skyblue>The Kingfisher,</font>” Cartethyia repeated. She said it carefully, trying the weight of it on her tongue the way she did with Huanglong dishes before she knew what was in them. “<font color=skyblue>Because of the lanterns.</font>”

“<font color=peachpuff>They were brighter when the paper was new,</font>” Auntie Hu said, not unkindly. “<font color=peachpuff>Sit where you like.</font>”

Ciaccona had already drawn out a bench at the corner table she’d just wiped. It stood backed squarely against the thickest of the bridge’s square timber piers. To their left, the whole front of the terrace lay open to the sky and the wide gulf of the gorge; overhead, the fir rafters sat high and clear in the gloom, twice the height of the salt-house ceiling. There were no shutters within reach and no door to latch.

Cartethyia slid onto the bench with her shoulder blades against the solid timber of the pier. She planted both boots flat on the flagstones under the table, felt the cliff rock steady beneath the soles, and looked out past the unlit blue lanterns at the open air. Then she looked across the pine board at Ciaccona, who was unbuttoning her collar and pretending to inspect a chip in her teacup.

Cartethyia didn’t say anything about the post or the roof. She rested her hands on the edge of the table and smiled.

“<font color=skyblue>It’s a very good table for the Kingfisher,</font>” she said.

“<font color=teal>It’s out of the smoke from the brazier,</font>” Ciaccona said to her cup.

Auntie Hu set down a brown earthenware teapot no bigger than a fist and two shallow cups. She had no chalk-board and no written slip. Before she could recite what was on the stove, Ciaccona looked up.

“<font color=teal>The sour-pickled mountain radish in plum vinegar,</font>” Ciaccona said. “<font color=teal>A pot of the clear mutton-and-ginger broth with the white turnip cooked soft, and the pan-fried river bream with scallions. Put the ginger-scallion oil on the side in a saucer, if you have it—not the red jar from the end of the shelf.</font>”

Auntie Hu’s eyebrows went up half an inch. “<font color=peachpuff>My nephew worked the stove on Thursday.</font>”

“<font color=teal>I know,</font>” Ciaccona said grimly. “<font color=teal>My tongue still knows.</font>”

“<font color=peachpuff>He’s gone back to his mother’s in the valley. The oil’s clean.</font>” Auntie Hu collected her damp cloth and padded back behind the brick screen.

Cartethyia leaned across the narrow table, her elbows on the wood. “<font color=skyblue>You came here on Thursday.</font>”

“<font color=teal>I walked past on Thursday,</font>” Ciaccona said, pouring a thin dark stream of tea from the tiny spout into Cartethyia’s cup and then her own. “<font color=teal>I happened to be hungry. A person is allowed to eat fish on a Thursday without filing a deposition.</font>”

“<font color=skyblue>You knew about the turnip.</font>”

“<font color=teal>You can smell mutton and turnip from the top of the dyer’s steps, cara.</font>” Ciaccona lifted the teapot by its lid-knob and squinted at it with profound distaste. “<font color=teal>Look at the size of this thing. Three swallows and you’re looking at wet leaves. In Ragunna, if an innkeeper brought a working musician a pot you could hide in your fist, you’d be legally entitled to tip it into his boots.</font>”

“<font color=skyblue>Auntie Hu isn’t wearing boots. She’s wearing cloth shoes with thick soles.</font>”

“<font color=teal>It’s the principle.</font>”

The radish came first, pale pink wedges swimming in dark plum vinegar with two salted green plums at the bottom of the dish, followed by the clay pot of mutton broth bubbling from the coals and the bream crisped brown on a long platter.

Cartethyia took a wedge of radish with her chopsticks, set it on her tongue, and waited before she bit into it. When she crunched down, the sourness hit the back of her jaw hard enough to make her eyes water, and right behind the vinegar came the round, dark sweetness of the plum skin. She chewed it very slowly, her gaze fixed on the middle distance past the brazier while she followed the taste all the way down.

“<font color=skyblue>When you don’t have a tongue for twenty winters, you forget that sour things make the little hinge right under your ear prickle,</font>” she said at her ordinary volume, and reached into the dish for the smaller of the two salted plums. “<font color=skyblue>Uncle Cosimo used to keep a jar of green gages in brine behind the flour-bins to stop the apprentices falling asleep at the third watch, and if you ate two at once your mouth went so small you couldn’t whistle. Try the broth—the turnip’s gone completely clear.</font>”

Ciaccona didn’t blink or go stiff the way people sometimes did when the cathedral came up in the middle of dinner. She simply picked up the porcelain ladle with her right hand, scooped two pieces of translucent turnip and a rich spoonful of ginger broth into Cartethyia’s small bowl, and pushed the radish saucer an inch closer to her wrist.

“<font color=teal>Leave the plum pit on the board, not in your pocket,</font>” Ciaccona said. “<font color=teal>Last week I found three peach stones in your coat lining.</font>”

“<font color=skyblue>Those were for planting.</font>”

“<font color=teal>They were boiled, cara.</font>”

Ciaccona flaked a piece of white meat from the shoulder of the river bream, dipped it cautiously into the saucer of pale ginger oil, and ate it. Finding it mild and sweet, she relaxed her shoulders, grew bold, and picked up a slender green pod that lay blistered among the scallion stalks on the fish’s tail.

Cartethyia’s chopsticks paused over her turnip. “<font color=skyblue>Cia, I don’t think that’s a bean.</font>”

“<font color=teal>Of course it’s a bean,</font>” Ciaccona said, already chewing. “<font color=teal>It’s green, it’s round, it’s—</font>”

She stopped. Her back went straight as a pike-shaft against the bench. The colour rose from her collar to the roots of her red-gold hair in one smooth wave, and her hand closed round the walnut-sized teapot and emptied the rest of it directly into her cup.

Cartethyia watched with sympathetic interest as Ciaccona drank the tea in one go, set the cup down with a sharp click, and stared fixedly at the stone cliff behind the stove.

“<font color=skyblue>Do you want a piece of turnip?</font>” Cartethyia asked. “<font color=skyblue>Suoming told Yangyang that hot tea makes the little peppers burn twice.</font>”

“<font color=teal>I’m fine,</font>” Ciaccona said in a voice like scraped slate. “<font color=teal>I’m merely reflecting on the culinary traditions of the eastern provinces.</font>”

“<font color=skyblue>Your eyes are watering.</font>”

“<font color=teal>It’s the smoke from the brazier.</font>”

Cartethyia ladled the largest piece of soft white turnip out of the clay pot, blew on it three times until it stopped steaming, and laid it on the edge of Ciaccona’s fish plate. Ciaccona ate it without looking at her and let out a long, shaky breath through her nose.

For a while they ate without hurrying. The carter by the brazier finished his noodles, paid in coppers on the corner of the stove-screen, and trudged off up the terrace with his collar pulled over his ears. Cartethyia worked her way through the mutton broth and told Ciaccona that Fang Qiu had given the goose a folded square of clean sacking in Old Seven’s berth, which meant they would need a second piece of sacking for the wicker crate on the ship, because straw worked its way out between the slats and made a mess on deck.

“<font color=teal>The sailors are going to throw him overboard on the third day,</font>” Ciaccona said, flaking fish from the bone with her chopsticks and avoiding everything green on the platter. “<font color=teal>He’ll honk at the bosun during the dog-watch and we’ll have to eat him in a pie off the coast of Rinascita.</font>”

“<font color=skyblue>He’s a very quiet traveller once he knows where the door is,</font>” Cartethyia said. She glanced over toward the low stool by the brazier, where Old Hu had finished waxing his bow and was now turning the long wooden pegs of his two-string fiddle, plucking a bare, nasal note with his thumb. “<font color=skyblue>Is he going to play soon?</font>”

“<font color=teal>When the soup’s down,</font>” Ciaccona said. “<font color=teal>He waits until people aren’t clattering their spoons.</font>”

Cartethyia looked down at the floor beside Ciaccona’s right boot, where there was only bare flagstone and a dry maple leaf blown in from the steps.

“<font color=skyblue>Why didn’t you bring your lute?</font>” she asked lightly.

Ciaccona’s hand paused on the tiny teapot. She lifted the lid, peered inside at the wet leaves, and set it back down.

“<font color=teal>It’s my night off, cara,</font>” she said. “<font color=teal>If I bring the box, somebody asks for a tune, and then I have to listen to myself work. Besides, the splint catches on the fourth course when my fingers get cold.</font>”

Cartethyia looked at the white linen binding on Ciaccona’s left forefinger, then nodded once and let it stand, fishing the last wedge of radish out of the plum vinegar.

Out on the terrace, the grey light had thinned into dusk without a sunset. Auntie Hu came out from behind the stove-screen carrying a long split-pine spill lit from the brazier coals. Walking down the open front of the eaves, she reached up to the first of the six hooks and touched the flame to the wick inside.

The oiled paper caught with a soft *pop*, glowing deep kingfisher-blue against the dark rock. She moved to the second, and then the third, until all six lanterns hung in a blue row between the tables and the gulf.

Ciaccona leaned back against the timber pier, her hands wrapped round her small cup, and looked past the blue lanterns at the low yellow-grey sky over the gorge.

“<font color=teal>Smells like snow,</font>” she said.

Cartethyia looked out at the dry flagstones on the terrace edge and the dark air beyond. “<font color=skyblue>It hasn’t snowed yet.</font>”

“<font color=teal>It will,</font>” Ciaccona said.

Suisui came down the three wooden steps of the post-house folding the pink relay slip into a neat square between her forefingers, thoroughly pleased with her afternoon.

Deputy Director Lin had opened the exchange at the third bell with four pages of formal Mingting anxieties: the northern winter manifest tallies were unsealed, the third-month canal barges needed their bond-stamps countersigned before the ice locked the reaches, and the Board of Comptrollers had inquired twice why the Director’s personal seal was still sitting in a garrison gorge above Mengzhou. Suisui had listened to the clerk read the whole thing out without once tapping her fan against her palm. Then she had dictated a three-line reply thanking Lin for his vigilance and authorizing him, under her hand, to assume direct personal liability for the northern grain-bond reconciliation with the Ministry of War’s auditors if her presence in Jinzhou could not be waited for.

The machine had clattered back less than half a bell later. *Return before the canal freezes,* Lin had written, with no month, no week, and no day attached to it.

Suisui tucked the slip into her sleeve-pocket beside her horn comb. That was how one lost an argument to a man who had never passed the provincial abacus examination on the first sitting.

“<font color=paleturquoise>He folded,</font>” Yangyang said beside her.

“<font color=thistle>He reconsidered the seasonal priorities,</font>” Suisui corrected mildly, adjusting the dark wool shawl over her shoulders. “<font color=thistle>I was entirely accommodating. Come along, if we walk by the lower alley the chestnut man should still have the iron drum over the coals—</font>”

She stopped, because Yangyang wasn’t beside her elbow any longer.

Two paces back in the middle of the cobbled lane, Yangyang had halted with one boot on the crown of the drain. Her head was tilted slightly to the left, her dark-crowned blue hair swinging clear of her cheek, and her eyes had gone wide and unblinking toward the lower roofs of the Hold.

“<font color=thistle>Yaya?</font>” Suisui stepped back to her at once, her hand going to her sister’s forearm. “<font color=thistle>What is it?</font>”

“<font color=paleturquoise>Wait,</font>” Yangyang murmured.

Suisui stood still and listened. For a moment she heard only the ordinary dusk of Xuanfang Hold: the iron clink of a smith’s hammer down by the mule-pens, a carter shouting at his trace-horse on the third terrace, the wooden clack of the weavers’ shuttles behind the shuttered casements, and the high, thin rush of the river at the bottom of the gorge.

Then, over the space of three breaths, all of it changed. Nobody stopped working, and no door shut, yet the ring of the smith’s anvil lost its sharp metallic edge and came up dull, as if someone had laid a folded blanket across the iron. The carter’s shout sounded half a street closer and twice as soft. Even the wind in the dry walnut branches above the post-house wall went quiet.

Something white and no larger than a grain of husked rice drifted straight down out of the yellow-grey gloom and landed on Suisui’s dark plum cuff.

Suisui looked down at it. It was six-sided, dry, and sharp-edged as a stamped silver weight, sitting whole on the nap of the wool without melting. She stared at it as though a junior clerk had just handed her a bill of lading with the columns entered upside down.

“<font color=paleturquoise>Oh,</font>” Yangyang said softly, her shoulders dropping out of their listening tension. “<font color=paleturquoise>That’s why.</font>”

A second flake came down past Yangyang’s nose, and then a dozen more, unhurried and perpendicular in the windless air. As the cold sharpened around them, the small cerulean feathers along Yangyang’s collar lifted and fluffed outward by themselves, overlapping into a thick, soft ruff from collarbone to jaw to hold the heat against her throat.

Suisui reached up by reflex to tug Yangyang’s woollen muffler higher over the scorched brown patch at the front of the breast-feathers—and her fingers paused half an inch from the blue down.

Three white flakes had settled squarely on the cerulean feathers under Yangyang’s chin. Like the one on Suisui’s sleeve, they didn’t melt. They sat bright white against the deep sky-blue plumage, rising and falling with her sister’s breath.

Suisui looked at them for a breath too long, thinking of their father’s postscript sitting in the outgoing mail-sack behind the post-house counter—*Ask your sister whether they turn grey in the twelfth month like the hill-ptarmigan*—and then she cleared her throat, briskly tucked the woollen end of the muffler into Yangyang’s coat lapel, and gave it a pat.

“<font color=thistle>Keep your chin down on the steps,</font>” Suisui said. “<font color=thistle>Dry snow on old limestone is worse than grease, and I am not explaining a sprained ankle to Lupa before supper. What did you want from the alley?</font>”

“<font color=paleturquoise>Chestnuts,</font>” Yangyang said, falling into step beside her as the white specks thickened between the eaves. “<font color=paleturquoise>Five coppers’ worth in twisted paper, so there’s half for each pocket. And I want to stand under the bell-arch at the fifth bell.</font>”

Suisui glanced sideways at her. “<font color=thistle>In the cold?</font>”

“<font color=paleturquoise>The bronze sounds different when the air’s full of snow,</font>” Yangyang said simply. “<font color=paleturquoise>The overtone doesn’t travel out over the gorge; it stays under the roof for a breath longer. I haven’t heard the Hold’s bell do it yet.</font>”

“<font color=thistle>Five coppers of chestnuts and one bell,</font>” Suisui said. “<font color=thistle>If your ears go pink, we go straight in.</font>”

They bought the chestnuts from the old man with the iron drum at the top of the second terrace, where the black sand in his pan hissed under the first stray flakes. Suisui paid, hissed when the hot paper cone scorched her palm through her glove, and divided the glossy brown nuts into two twists of coarse paper so Yangyang could shove one into each side of her coat.

They were thirty paces along the broad flagstone walk of the second terrace, with the snow beginning to leave a pale dry flour in the mortar-seams, when a voice bellowed out from under the tiled awning of the public tea-shed.

“<font color=aquamarine>Oi! Mingting! Both of you! Bring your feet over here!</font>”

Under the open timber roof of the shed, four paces back from the snow-line, sat the four-legged kaya-wood mahjong table they had carried down from Mengxuan Vale. Longjue sat on the outer bamboo stool in her long teal over-robe, her dark hair loose around her jade-set dragon crests and her bare feet hooked comfortably round the lower rungs despite the freezing flagstones. Across from her sat Old Chen in a thick woollen ear-cap, Gu with his brass spirit-level laid beside his tile-rack as if he meant to check the table’s pitch between hands, and Mingxiao with her arms folded inside her carpenter’s sleeves.

“<font color=thistle>We’re walking to the bell-arch, Immortal,</font>” Suisui called back graciously, slowing her step without turning in under the eaves.

“<font color=aquamarine>The bell-arch isn’t going anywhere, it’s made of stone!</font>” Longjue kicked a spare bamboo stool out into the aisle with her bare big toe. “<font color=aquamarine>Buling went off before noon to swindle somebody over lunch and hasn’t shown her dead face since, Pipa’s deserted her east-wind chair, and Mingxiao’s fingers are so stiff she’s dropped the White Dragon twice. Sit down! One round!</font>”

“<font color=wheat>I dropped it once,</font>” Mingxiao said, standing up from her stool with dignity and tucking her cold hands deeper into her cuffs. “<font color=wheat>The lady from the Zhaoming Guild may have my place. I’m going to stand by the stove-flue until the mortar stops shrinking.</font>”

“<font color=aquamarine>You see?</font>” Longjue slapped the green felt. “<font color=aquamarine>Drafted!</font>”

Suisui looked at Yangyang. Yangyang had already taken a hot chestnut out of her left pocket, cracked the shell between her thumb and forefinger, and peeled the inner skin clean. She held the warm yellow kernel out to Suisui’s mouth.

“<font color=paleturquoise>The fifth bell isn’t for a quarter-hour,</font>” Yangyang said. “<font color=paleturquoise>And we can hear it from the awning.</font>”

Suisui ate the chestnut, dusted a flake of snow from her sleeve, and stepped under the shed roof to take Mingxiao’s stool while Yangyang pulled a low crate up beside her right elbow to watch and shell the rest of the paper cone.

Out beyond the timber posts of the awning, the snow was coming down steadily now, silent and thick against the dark gulf. Gu watched a flake drift under the eaves and land on the back of a face-down bamboo tile in the wall.

“<font color=lightsteelblue>Too wet,</font>” Gu grumbled, leaning down to squint at the flake at eye level. “<font color=lightsteelblue>Before the war, first snow in the tenth month came down dry as salt-flour. Six even points, uniform weight. You could sweep a scaffold with a feather-duster. Look at that—lopsided on the third branch. No proper discipline in the clouds any more.</font>”

“<font color=burlywood>Snow on the wall-tiles is luck for the dealer,</font>” Old Chen said calmly, pushing his sleeve back to roll the bone dice. “<font color=burlywood>My mother always left the south window open a crack when we played at the solstice.</font>”

“<font color=lightsteelblue>Your mother lost four strings of copper to the miller’s wife,</font>” Gu said.

“<font color=burlywood>That was in the rain,</font>” Old Chen replied.

Longjue was stacking her wall with brisk, practiced clacks when her teal eyes drifted past Suisui’s shoulder, looking straight down the curve of the second terrace toward the stone pier of the rope bridge.

Through the thickening veil of white, the six kingfisher-blue lanterns had just come alight under the high timber roof of the bridge-house. In the blue glow beside the thickest corner-post sat two figures at a small table: one tiny, with a crooked ring of crystal catching the lantern-light and a bright red wool scarf wound up to her ears, and the other leaning across the board in a dark coat with red-gold hair falling over her collar.

Longjue’s whole face split into a grin. She left her tiles half-stacked, reached down to her waist to unhook the big red lacquer gourd, and planted one bare foot on the flagstones to heave herself up.

“<font color=aquamarine>Aha! So that’s where Pipa got to!</font>” Longjue pulled the wooden stopper out of the gourd with her teeth. “<font color=aquamarine>Hiding under the bridge with the little knight while her chair goes cold! Come on, let’s take the jug down there and wake them up—Old Hu’s got six tables, we can push three together!</font>”

Suisui did not raise her voice, and she did not stand. She simply lifted her closed fan one inch off the green felt and rested its lacquered tip lightly against the front of Longjue’s knee.

Longjue stopped half-risen, the open gourd in her hand, and blinked down at the fan.

“<font color=thistle>The draught under the bridge comes straight off the river, Immortal,</font>” Suisui said in her smoothest Mingting drawing-room voice. “<font color=thistle>And Old Chen has already thrown the dice for the east wind. It would be a great pity to let the luck go cold on the wall.</font>”

“<font color=aquamarine>We can carry the wall!</font>” Longjue laughed, trying to step round the fan. “<font color=aquamarine>Pipa owes me a song for the ridge, and the little one owes me a rematch from the Vale!</font>”

“<font color=paleturquoise>They have only one small teapot on the table,</font>” Yangyang said quietly from her crate, peeling another chestnut without looking up. “<font color=paleturquoise>And Ciaccona didn’t bring her lute.</font>”

Longjue paused, squinting down the snowy terrace toward the blue light. “<font color=aquamarine>She goes everywhere with that box. She slept with it in her lap.</font>”

“<font color=thistle>Yes,</font>” Suisui said. She drew the fan back from Longjue’s knee and tapped the bone dice once on the felt. “<font color=thistle>Sit down, Immortal. I will make you a wager on the east round.</font>”

Longjue’s dragon crests gave an interested twitch; she never walked past a bet in her life. Slowly she lowered herself back onto the bamboo stool, keeping the unstoppered gourd balanced on her thigh. “<font color=aquamarine>Name it, Guild Director.</font>”

“<font color=thistle>If you take a hand off this wall before I do,</font>” Suisui said, her fingers already sorting her thirteen tiles by touch alone without looking down at the ivory faces, “<font color=thistle>I will send a Guild porter up from the lower wharf tomorrow morning with a full two-gallon jar of sixty-day sorghum, sealed in red wax, at my own expense, and we will carry this table down to the bridge ourselves.</font>”

Longjue’s eyes gleamed. “<font color=aquamarine>And when you lose?</font>”

“<font color=thistle>When I draw out first,</font>” Suisui said pleasantly, “<font color=thistle>you keep both bare feet under this awning until the oil-lamps go out, and you leave the bridge to Old Hu.</font>”

“<font color=aquamarine>Done!</font>” Longjue slammed her palm on the table hard enough to make Gu’s brass spirit-level jump, then swept her tiles upright into a dead-straight line. “<font color=aquamarine>Old Chen, watch your discards, she’s got merchant eyes!</font>”

The tiles began to clatter fast and sharp under the shed roof, a brisk four-beat rhythm against the deepening hush of the snow outside. Longjue played the way she drank: pushing hard from the third turn for an all-dragon pung hand, slapping unwanted bamboo tiles into the middle of the felt with her thumb and heckling every cautious draw Gu made.

Suisui kept her spine straight and her fan laid across her lap. She didn’t need to count tiles; she watched the shoulders and the wrists. Gu arranged his rack by suit from left to right and never broke a run once he’d set it between his brass markers; Old Chen hoarded wind tiles out of pure nostalgia; and Longjue’s bare left heel tapped the bamboo rung whenever she was one tile short of a triplet.

On the ninth turn, Suisui drew the Five of Bamboo—the exact piece Longjue’s heel was tapping for—and tucked it smoothly into the middle of her own rack, breaking a clean pair of Circles to throw a safe North Wind into the snow-dusted pool instead.

Beside her elbow, Yangyang put a warm shelled chestnut onto the corner of the table near Suisui’s right wrist. As she did, Yangyang’s head tilted a fraction toward the rope bridge down the terrace.

Through the quiet snow came the thin, reedy note of Old Hu’s two-string fiddle starting up a slow river air, a quarter-tone flat on the upper string. Two minutes later the fiddle stopped, and then began again—scraping, bouncing, and sliding wildly off the note like a wet boot on cobbles, followed a beat later by the bright, clear sound of Cartethyia’s laugh carrying across the terrace.

Yangyang’s mouth curved upward by a hair. She ate the other half of the chestnut and said nothing at all about it.

On the twelfth turn, Suisui reached out to the snow-flecked wall, drew a tile with two fingers, and read the deep carved groove of the Three of Characters against her thumb-pad before she even turned it toward her chest.

With a single soft click, she turned her whole rack face up on the green felt.

Longjue stared at the narrow one-tile wait, then down at her own three pairs of stranded dragons, and threw her head back in a roar of laughter that shook a puff of white snow off the front edge of the awning.

“<font color=aquamarine>Robbed!</font>” Longjue shouted, slapping the table with both hands while Old Chen cackled into his collar and Gu reached for his spirit-level to check the board. “<font color=aquamarine>Robbed in broad dusk by an abacus from Mingting! Give me your cup, Guild Director, if I’m nailed to this stool till midnight you’re drinking with me!</font>”

She tipped the red lacquer gourd over four small clay cups, sloshing clear sorghum baijiu across the wood while the white flakes swirled thicker past the posts, dusting the stacked tiles on the wall and turning Suisui’s and Yangyang’s shoulders white, and down the terrace the six blue lanterns under the bridge blurred into soft round moons behind the falling snow.

Iuno knew the weather had turned before anyone opened the shutter, because the frost-crack across her left shoulder crystal gave a single, dry *tick* under her wool shawl and settled into a deeper ache.

Across the salt-house, Lupa was still standing at the stove with the flat iron scraper in her fist, taking her temper out on the black scorch-mark the burnt flatbread had left on the griddle. *<font color=royalblue>I know she is.</font>*

*<font color=thistle>And the box is two inches from your hip.</font>*

Inside Iuno’s right pocket, her fingers rested lightly against the square velvet corner beside her ruled pad. The lane was quiet, the snow was white on the well-stone, and Mei’s breath was warm against her temple. It was Tuesday, and it wasn’t the night; there was still a soldered tin coming overland from the coast, and a room they had agreed to sit in together first. Iuno left the box in the wool and leaned her cheek a fraction closer to Mei’s wrist.

Ciaccona watched the snow turn blue as it fell past the eaves.

Full dark had come down over the gorge while the mutton pot cooled, and there was no wind to blow the flakes under the timber roof of the bridge-house. They dropped straight down from the black fir beams overhead like slow white stage-ash, caught the deep kingfisher light of the six oiled-paper lanterns for the space of a yard, and vanished onto the wet flagstones of the terrace below.

The fish platter and the soup pot had been cleared away. Between Ciaccona’s elbow and Cartethyia’s sat a chipped white porcelain saucer holding a small heap of candied lemon peel, translucent and dusted with coarse cane sugar. Ciaccona had gone back to the stove-screen herself when she fetched the hot water and asked Auntie Hu for it out of Old Hu’s hearing; Auntie Hu had fished the jar out from behind the vinegar crocks without a word.

Across the pine table, Cartethyia picked up a single curled strip of peel between thumb and forefinger, set the end on her tongue, and shut her eyes. She didn’t chew it straight away. She let the rough sugar dissolve first, her brow faintly creased as if she were listening to a choir in the next room to see whether the altos were going to hold their pitch, and only bit down when the sharp, bitter oil of the yellow rind came through.

By the iron brazier in the middle of the floor, Old Hu tucked his small wooden fiddle barrel against his left hip, spat once into the ashes, and drew his bow across the two strings.

It was an old river-barge air from the lower reaches, slow and rocking, meant to be played in fourths. The outer string was a full quarter-tone flat.

Ciaccona’s splinted left forefinger twitched once against her teacup. A flat fourth always felt to her like someone walking down a marble stair with one boot heel missing; her thumb wanted to reach across the room and twist the upper wooden peg by the thickness of a thumbnail. She kept both hands wrapped round the tiny cup and looked at the snow instead.

Old Hu got through three verses before the winter damp coming up off the gorge settled into his hands. Halfway up a slide on the inner string, his crooked left knuckle locked on the neck; the bow skittered sideways across the resin with a dry squawk, and the tune died.

He lowered the bow, set the fiddle upright on his knee, and began rubbing his thin, knotted fingers together over the charcoal glow, muttering under his breath at the weather. The two masons at the far table didn’t even look up from their bowls.

Cartethyia opened her eyes, swallowed the last of her lemon peel, and looked from the old man’s stiff hands across the table to Ciaccona.

“<font color=skyblue>Would you?</font>” Cartethyia asked quietly.

Ciaccona looked down at the white linen splint binding her left forefinger straight from knuckle to tip. “<font color=teal>Cara, I don’t have my box, and that thing has two strings and no neck-board.</font>”

Across the floor by the brazier, Old Hu had turned his good right ear toward their table. Without standing up from his stool, he held the narrow wooden shaft of the fiddle out by the pegs toward Ciaccona, the bow dangling from the strings where the horsehair passed between them.

“<font color=tan>Come on, then, foreigner,</font>” Old Hu said gruffly. “<font color=tan>If you’re going to wince every time I hit the upper D, come and hit it yourself before the coals go grey.</font>”

Ciaccona’s ears went warm. She looked at Cartethyia, who was sitting very straight against the timber pier with her hands folded on the table, waiting with complete, shining confidence.

Letting out a long breath through her nose, Ciaccona pushed back her bench, walked the four paces to the brazier, and took the fiddle from the old man’s hand. She pulled a spare three-legged stool up beside the coals, propped the small hexagonal sound-box into the hollow of her left hip the way he had held it, and tried to curl her three uninjured left fingers round the bare, fretless pole of the strings while her splinted index finger stuck straight out into the air like a dry twig.

She drew the bow across the horsehair.

The sound that came out of the snake-skin belly was not music. It was a long, flat, nasal screech, half a tone sharper than Old Hu’s worst note and twice as loud, as if someone had trodden on a goose in an empty cistern.

Ciaccona froze with the bow at the tip. She knew fifty ways to fake a clumsy thumb. She had never once made a noise like that by accident.

“<font color=tan>Gods above,</font>” Old Hu barked, coming halfway off his stool to rap the underside of her right elbow with two bony knuckles. “<font color=tan>Drop your wrist! You’re sawing a pine plank, not drawing a bow! And get your left thumb off the back of the pole—you’re choking the string like a bailiff! Press the hair with your middle fingers on the pull!</font>”

From the corner table by the pier, Cartethyia laughed out loud.

It wasn’t a polite giggle behind a sleeve; it was a bright, clear peal of delight that rang right up into the fir rafters under the bridge, and she clapped both hands over her mouth a second too late, her eyes crinkling above her fingers. Neither of the masons at the end table turned their heads.

Ciaccona’s face was hot from collar to temple. She dropped her right wrist the way the old man had ordered, loosened her death-grip on the wooden shaft so the strings could breathe against the pads of her third and fourth fingers, and drew the bow back across the inner string.

This time the note came out shaky and thin, sliding a little flat where her splinted finger bumped the peg-box and then creeping back up to pitch by ear. Old Hu grunted once, which was evidently his highest form of encouragement, and went back to warming his palms over the coals.

Looking straight across the six tables at Cartethyia, Ciaccona played the rest of the river air through to the end. It was wobbly, reedy, and missed every grace-note on the turns because her third finger had to jump the gap alone, and Cartethyia watched her with her chin propped on both hands and a look on her face that made Ciaccona’s ribs feel too small for her lungs.

When the last note scraped to a halt, Ciaccona handed the fiddle and bow back to Old Hu with a small bow of her own, walked back to the corner table, and sat down hard on the bench.

“<font color=teal>Not a word,</font>” Ciaccona said, reaching for her cold teacup.

“<font color=skyblue>I liked the middle bit,</font>” Cartethyia said warmly. “<font color=skyblue>Where it went up like a gull.</font>”

“<font color=teal>That was my splint hitting the knot in the string.</font>”

Cartethyia took another strip of candied lemon peel from the saucer and pushed the porcelain dish across the pine so it sat right beside Ciaccona’s wrist. Ciaccona didn’t take one. Now that she had sat down again, her palms felt damp against the clay cup, a tight, restless flutter sitting just under her breastbone that had never once bothered her before walking out onto a stage in front of five hundred strangers.

Outside the blue lanterns, the white flakes were coming down thicker now, settling in a pale line along the outer beam of the terrace. Watching them drift through the dark made a sudden, sharp ache for Ragunna rise in the back of her throat—not for the piazzas or the theatre halls, just for the black winter rain hammering on the stone quays behind the fish-market and the smell of wet wool and fried anchovies under a leaking canvas awning.

And behind the quays, up on the sea-cliff above the mist, sat Fisalia Manor, with its iron gates and its camellia trenches and a dining room with no pepper in the soup.

Ciaccona turned her teacup round on the wood with her thumb. “<font color=teal>When we get off the ship,</font>” she said, keeping her voice light and conversational, “<font color=teal>what is a banished Toccata supposed to carry in her hands when she walks into Duchess Cantarella’s dining room, so she doesn’t end up face-down in the consommé before the fish course?</font>”

Cartethyia didn’t laugh at the joke. She tipped her head slightly against the timber pier and gave the question her full, practical consideration, her fingers resting beside the saucer.

“<font color=skyblue>Nothing in a bottle,</font>” Cartethyia said plainly. “<font color=skyblue>If you bring wine, the steward has to test it on a silver spoon in the pantry, and she’ll think you’re making a point about her cellar. And don’t bring cut flowers, because she only likes the ones that grow in the shade and kill the garden snails.</font>”

“<font color=teal>Encouraging,</font>” Ciaccona murmured. “<font color=teal>Perhaps I’ll bring my will.</font>”

“<font color=skyblue>Just eat a piece of the salt-bread as soon as you sit down, before she breaks hers, and ask her if the camellia cutting kept its leaves on the water,</font>” Cartethyia said, looking straight at Ciaccona with her clear yellow eyes. “<font color=skyblue>She won’t put anything in your soup, Cia. She’s much more frightened that you’ll get bored in the country and take me away again than you are of her kitchen.</font>”

Ciaccona’s thumb stopped turning the cup. She looked across the small pine table at Cartethyia, and whatever clever reply she had ready at the front of her teeth went quiet and stayed there.

Auntie Hu came out from behind the brick screen carrying the brass hot-water kettle in one hand and a narrow wooden reckoning-slip in the other. She refilled the tiny brown teapot until the lid floated, set the wooden slip down beside the edge of the board, and looked from Ciaccona’s face to Cartethyia’s with a kind, unhurried squint.

“<font color=peachpuff>Going far up the hill in this?</font>” Auntie Hu asked, wiping the kettle spout on her apron. “<font color=peachpuff>Is the little lady your younger sister, miss, or your pupil?</font>”

Ciaccona opened her mouth to answer.

Before she could get a syllable out, Cartethyia straightened her back against the bridge-pier, lifted her chin out of Lupa’s woollen scarf, and said brightly and proudly, “<font color=skyblue>We’re on a consultation.</font>”

She said it with total fidelity, the exact word she had been handed in the salt-house at the fourth bell, holding it up in front of a stranger like a pass-token at a city gate.

Auntie Hu blinked once, her hand pausing on her apron. She looked from Cartethyia’s beaming, solemn face down to the saucer of lemon peel, and then across at Ciaccona, plainly trying to work out what sort of clerk’s business was conducted over mutton broth and a borrowed fiddle.

Ciaccona looked at Cartethyia: the crystal thorns of her laurel sitting crooked over her brow again, the scorched-scallion scarf wound three times round her throat, and a single sparkling grain of lemon sugar stuck to the side of her right thumb.

Ciaccona looked back up at Auntie Hu.

“<font color=teal>It’s a date,</font>” Ciaccona said plainly.

Auntie Hu’s brow cleared at once. “<font color=peachpuff>Ah,</font>” she said, nodding as if that explained the fiddle.

Without another word, Auntie Hu picked up the chipped white saucer with its two remaining strips of peel, carried it back behind the brick stove-screen, and returned a moment later with a square of clean oiled paper twisted neatly at the corners and plump to the crease with fresh candied lemon peel from the back jar. She laid the packet down right in front of Cartethyia’s knuckles.

“<font color=peachpuff>For the steps,</font>” Auntie Hu said. “<font color=peachpuff>They’re steep when the snow’s new. No charge.</font>”

She padded away to the kitchen before Ciaccona could reach for her purse.

Across the table, Cartethyia didn’t exclaim, and she didn’t throw her arms out. Under the pine board, her boot slid slowly across the flagstones until the inner edge of her sole pressed warm and solid against Ciaccona’s ankle and stayed there. Above the table, she lifted her right hand, licked the grain of lemon sugar neatly off the side of her thumb, and tucked the oiled-paper packet into her coat while the tips of her ears went pink under her hair.

Ciaccona counted out the full copper for the meal onto the wooden slip, leaving two extra pieces beside the teapot for Old Hu’s resin, and stood up.

When they stepped out from under the fir roof of the Kingfisher, the terrace flagstones had vanished beneath a smooth white carpet of snow that squeaked dryly under their soles. Above them, the stone steps climbing back toward the upper lane rose white and untouched between the bridge-cables, with the blue light of the six lanterns falling across the lower risers in soft, overlapping squares.

Cartethyia stopped at the foot of the stair and looked up into the falling flakes.

“<font color=skyblue>This one isn’t the cooper’s stair,</font>” Cartethyia said. “<font color=skyblue>We came out on the other side of the bridge. It needs a name so we know where it goes.</font>” She turned her head and looked up at Ciaccona. “<font color=skyblue>Which one in Egla do you think it is?</font>”

Ciaccona looked at the narrow stone treads climbing between the dark walls, and at the blue lantern-glow behind their heels, and at the oiled paper of lemon peel tucked over Cartethyia’s ribs.

“<font color=teal>Salita dei Limoni,</font>” Ciaccona said. “<font color=teal>The little one behind the dye-vats where the water runs down the middle.</font>”

“<font color=skyblue>Salita dei Limoni,</font>” Cartethyia repeated softly, nodding once to fix it to the stone.

Then she reached across without asking, slid her bare right hand deep into Ciaccona’s right coat pocket, and curled her fingers inside Ciaccona’s palm as they started up the snowy steps together with the blue light behind them.

Ruoyun had counted seven sneezes since noon, and she had no intention of counting an eighth in a snowbank.

“<font color=lavender>Absolutely not,</font>” Ruoyun said, standing squarely between the warmth of the stove-flue and the salt-house door with her arms folded over her ruffled bodice. “<font color=lavender>The damp is coming straight down the gorge, your stockings were wet until the fourth bell, and you have the constitution of a turnip. Sit back down on the brick.</font>”

Seated on the corner of the kang, Qingxiao did not argue. She simply stood up inside the heavy cotton quilt Ruoyun had wrapped round her shoulders, drew the second quilted coverlet up from the bolster, and draped it over the first like a magistrate’s cloak. Her pale blue eyes were clear, the tip of her nose was a bright, mortal pink, and her feet were tucked into plain cloth shoes over dry white stockings.

Without hurrying, she stepped down off the kerb, walked straight past Ruoyun’s elbow as if the Celestial were a decorative pillar, lifted the wooden latch with one hand emerging from the bundle of bedding, and walked out into the yard.

Ruoyun stared at the open doorway for half a breath while the cold air rolled across her ankles.

“<font color=lavender>Two hundred years on a mountain,</font>” Ruoyun muttered to the rafters, snatching a third quilt off the end of the platform where Lupa had left it, “<font color=lavender>and she has less sense than the goose.</font>”

“<font color=palegreen>Wait lah, I bring shelter!</font>” Buling scrambled off the kang, grabbed a wide oiled-paper market umbrella from the corner barrel by the door, and trotted out over the sill right behind Ruoyun’s heels.

Outside in the salt-house yard, the snow had already laid more than an inch of smooth white across the cobbles and built a thick soft crown around the stone coping of the well. The dark air was full of large, silent flakes drifting straight down between the eaves.

Qingxiao had stopped in the middle of the yard, four paces from the well-head, bundled to the chin like an oversized bale of winter wool. In the dim red-and-yellow light leaking through the unhooked shutter, her breath went out before her face in a long, white mortal cloud.

Before Ruoyun could throw the third quilt over her head, Qingxiao slipped her bare right hand out from between the folds of the bedding and held it out flat, palm up, into the dark.

Three broad white flakes drifted down and settled on the skin of her palm and the side of her thumb. For a moment their six-pointed edges showed clear against her fingers; then the warmth of her blood reached them, and all three softened at once, collapsing into tiny, bright beads of water that ran together in the crease of her palm.

Qingxiao looked down at the wet spot on her hand with quiet, absorbed interest.

“<font color=lightcyan>It goes away,</font>” she said.

Ruoyun stopped with the folded quilt held in both hands against her chest.

“<font color=lightcyan>Before,</font>” Qingxiao said in her level, unornamented voice, watching a fourth flake land on her knuckle and vanish into a drop, “<font color=lightcyan>it stayed until I brushed it off.</font>”

Something hard and hot rose in the back of Ruoyun’s throat—part fury at the woman’s serene indifference to her own lungs, and part an ache so sharp she refused to put a word to it even inside her own skull.

Then Qingxiao’s shoulders hitched under the two quilts, and she gave a single, delicate sneeze into the falling snow.

“<font color=lavender>Eight,</font>” Ruoyun snapped at once, stepping forward to fling the third quilt over Qingxiao’s shoulders and jam a spare knitted woollen mitten onto her wet right hand before she could catch another flake. “<font color=lavender>That is eight since the noon bell, A-Qing, and if you think I am boiling another pot of vinegar over Lupa’s stove at midnight while you wheeze, you can walk back up your own mountain!</font>”

“<font color=lightcyan>The mitten is damp,</font>” Qingxiao observed calmly, flexing her fingers inside the wool.

“<font color=lavender>It wouldn’t be damp if your hand had been dry!</font>”

Beside them, Buling had popped the wide market umbrella open with a thump and was holding it high with both hands. Because she was trying to keep the canopy centred squarely over Qingxiao’s horns and the three stacked quilts, Buling herself was standing half a pace outside the drip-line, her round winter hat and the shoulder of her loose black jacket completely exposed to the sky.

Ruoyun turned her head to tell her to hold the shaft straight—and stopped.

The snow was falling thick across Buling’s upturned face. Where the white flakes landed on the bridge of her nose below the yellow paper talisman, along the pale curve of her cheek, and in the silver fringes of her eyelashes, not a single one of them melted. Her skin was as cold as the flagstones under their shoes; the snow sat on her cheekbones and her lids dry and powdery as white ash on a carved stone effigy.

Ruoyun scowled, reached up with her own spotless ruffled left cuff, and wiped the snow roughly off Buling’s cheek and lashes in two sharp strokes.

“<font color=lavender>Stand under the ribs or go back inside,</font>” Ruoyun said. “<font color=lavender>You look like a grave.</font>”

Buling blinked her gold eyes through the remaining powder on her lower lashes, not moving the umbrella an inch off Qingxiao’s head. “<font color=palegreen>I am a grave what.</font>”

Even as she said it, half a dozen fresh flakes drifted down out of the dark and settled along her other cheekbone and the corner of her jaw, lying white and six-sided against the cold skin.

Ruoyun reached up a second time.

This time she didn’t use her cuff. She lifted her bare right hand, laid the side of her thumb just below Buling’s left eye, and brushed the dry white flakes slowly down across her cheek and off her jawbone.

Buling went completely still on the cobbles. She didn’t draw a breath—she never needed one unless she was talking—and her gold eyes stayed fixed on Ruoyun’s face behind the round brass spectacles. The usual quick grin was nowhere near her mouth.

“<font color=palegreen>Thought you say don’t get ideas,</font>” Buling murmured, so lightly that the falling snow nearly swallowed the words.

Ruoyun didn’t answer her. She didn’t snatch her hand back at once, either; the side of her thumb rested for the space of a full beat against the cold bone of Buling’s jaw, warming nothing at all, before she lowered her arm back to her waist and adjusted her spectacles with two fingers.

Under the umbrella, Qingxiao did not turn her head from watching the snow pile up on the well-coping.

“<font color=lightcyan>The umbrella is leaking on the left,</font>” Qingxiao said mildly from inside her three quilts.

Ruoyun reached out, seized the bamboo shaft right above Buling’s knuckles to yank the crooked canopy level over all three of them, and took Qingxiao’s quilted elbow in her other hand.

“<font color=lavender>Inside,</font>” Ruoyun said, steering both of them back toward the salt-house sill with snow sitting white on three heads and melting on all but one. “<font color=lavender>Before I have to write down nine.</font>”

Chisa knew the flue was choking before the smoke showed, because the low humming draw inside the brick chimney-breast changed pitch behind her shoulder.

A moment later a thin grey ribbon of coal-smoke curled out from the iron ash-door at the bottom of the stove, smelling of wet soot.

At the warm end of the kang, Lupa lifted her head off her folded arms, sniffed once, and swore under her breath. “<font color=indianred>Wet snow’s capping the pot on the ridge. If it crusts over, the whole kang’ll back-draught before the watch turns.</font>”

Mei immediately shifted her weight in the corner. “<font color=moccasin>I can—</font>”

“<font color=indianred>You can sit on your backside,</font>” Lupa said, already swinging her boots off the masonry and reaching for the heavy rust-red timber-factor’s coat on the peg. She pulled the cream sheepskin collar up round her neck, took the short iron coal-shovel from the hearth-hook, and picked up the stiff yard-broom by the door.

The salt-house roof was low at the back—barely twice the height of a tall man where the stone wall met the rising dirt bank behind the woodpile—so Lupa kept her chin up and acted as though it weren’t a roof at all.

Chisa didn’t ask if she wanted company. She laced her boots, lifted the shuttered horn lantern off the charging crate, and followed Lupa out the back way into the narrow passage behind the woodshed.

The snow lay three inches deep here where nobody had walked since dusk. Chisa fetched the short pine ladder from the shed hooks, set its feet down behind the woodpile, and stamped once on the bottom rung until the iron-shod tips bit through the snow into the frozen earth underneath. Then she stood at the foot, planted both boots against the uprights, and took the side-rails in her hands.

“<font color=coral>Solid,</font>” Chisa said.

Lupa didn’t thank her. She slung the yard-broom through her belt, gripped the rungs hard enough to make the pine creak, and climbed up stiff-legged with her eyes fixed straight ahead on the brick chimney-stack rising out of the tiles. When Lupa’s boots scraped over the eaves onto the low-pitched roof, Chisa hooked the horn lantern over her left wrist—the one with Sumika’s red string wound loosely round the bone—and climbed up after her.

Up on the roof, the snow lay smooth and unbroken over the grey tiles, glowing faint blue-white in the dark. The brick chimney-stack stood right at the crown of the ridge, warm to the touch through a glove, with a thick collar of wet, heavy slush packed half across the terra-cotta vent-louvres at the top.

Lupa stayed on her knees straddling the ridge-beam with one thigh pressed tight against the warm chimney-brick so she wouldn’t have to look out toward the front eaves or the long drop into the gorge beyond the lane. She braced the broom across her lap and went to work on the vent-slots with the iron coal-shovel, scraping the wet snow loose in brisk, noisy strokes and muttering at the mason who had set the cap too low.

Chisa knelt on the ridge on the other side of the chimney, set the horn lantern down in the snow between them, and laid her gloved hand lightly on the back of Lupa’s rust-red sleeve.

“<font color=coral>Stop a second,</font>” Chisa said.

Lupa’s shovel paused halfway through a crust of ice. Her wolf ears swiveled toward Chisa’s face, checking for whatever Chisa had heard—a loose tile, a step in the lane, a crack in the beam.

“<font color=indianred>What,</font>” Lupa said, low.

“<font color=coral>Just stop,</font>” Chisa said.

Lupa lowered the iron shovel until it rested flat against the chimney-brick and held still.

The last scrape of the iron faded out across the yard. When it was gone, there was no sound left in the Hold at all.

Below them, the three inches of fresh snow had swallowed every noise a town usually made at night. There was no water gurgling in the iron downpipes, no wind whistling through the rope-walk, no cart-wheels groaning on stone, and no rain drumming endlessly on the grey roof-tiles overhead. The white flakes came down out of the dark in a wide, deep hush that had no beat and no clock in it.

Chisa tilted her face up slightly toward the dark sky, feeling the cold specks touch her cheeks and melt, and listened to the quiet. She knew it was Tuesday.

“<font color=coral>In Honami,</font>” Chisa said plainly, “<font color=coral>it always rained.</font>”

Lupa didn’t look down at the gutter. For a woman who couldn’t sit five minutes by a hearth without poking the coals or banging a pot-lid, holding completely still and silent on a high ridge was hard work; her shoulders stayed squared under the sheepskin coat, her breath went out slow and quiet through her nose, and she kept her eyes steady on Chisa’s collar so neither of them had to look at the edge.

They stayed like that for a long time while the snow fell straight down between them.

Because Lupa was holding her head so still, the large white flakes landing on the tips and backs of her upright wolf ears didn’t shake off. They settled in the thick fur, one on top of another, slowly building two neat, pointed little white drifts that weighed both ear-tips down by a quarter of an inch.

In the soft yellow glow of the horn lantern, Chisa watched the two white caps grow taller on Lupa’s ears. She kept her face completely straight and said nothing at all, waiting to see how high the left one would get before it slid.

After another minute, a loose crumb of snow slipped off the left peak and dropped straight down into the warm fur inside Lupa’s ear-cup.

Lupa’s whole head jerked; she flicked both ears hard, sending a twin spray of white powder flying across the lantern glass and straight into Chisa’s face and lap.

“<font color=indianred>What!</font>” Lupa barked, scowling as she scrubbed a hand over her ear. “<font color=indianred>How long was that sitting there?</font>”

Chisa laughed.

It wasn’t the quiet breath she sometimes gave at the table; it was a short, clear laugh out loud that puffed white in the lantern-light, and she brushed the snow off her fringe with the back of her wrist while her shoulders shook once.

Lupa stared at her, the scowl vanishing from her mouth while the tips of her damp ears went hot under the fur. Slowly Lupa shifted round on the ridge until she was sitting side by side with Chisa, both of their backs braced squarely against the warm brick of the chimney-stack and their boots dug into the snow on the gentler rear slope above the woodpile.

The chimney-vent, cleared on three sides, drew with a low, clean purr behind their shoulders.

Lupa cleared her throat and looked at her own boots. “<font color=indianred>Does it come down like this at your mum’s place?</font>” she asked gruffly. “<font color=indianred>In Lahai-Roi.</font>”

“<font color=coral>From the tenth month to the third,</font>” Chisa said, resting her hands on her knees. “<font color=coral>Thicker than this.</font>”

“<font color=indianred>What’s the roof like? Pitch or flat?</font>”

“<font color=coral>Flat over the kitchen porch,</font>” Chisa said. “<font color=coral>Concrete. If you don’t shovel it off after a night-fall, the melt gets under the flashing and drips on the kettle.</font>”

Lupa nodded once, squaring her jaw as if she were inspecting a contract. “<font color=indianred>Good. Flat’s easy. You just push it off the front with a wide board.</font>”

Chisa turned her head and looked at the side of Lupa’s face. “<font color=coral>Mama will make you do it before breakfast.</font>”

“<font color=indianred>That’s fine,</font>” Lupa said to her boots. “<font color=indianred>I’m up before breakfast anyway. Somebody’s gotta put the water on.</font>”

A gust of cold air slid down from the upper terrace, making the lantern flame dip behind its horn pane. Without making a production of it, Lupa unbuttoned the heavy horn toggles down the front of her fifteen-copper coat, lifted the wide left flap with its thick cream sheepskin lining, and held it open.

Chisa slid sideways along the ridge-tiles and tucked herself inside the coat against Lupa’s ribs, pulling the sheepskin flap shut over her own shoulder while Lupa’s arm came down heavy and warm around her back.

Halfway down the slope of the tiles, the forgotten yard-broom worked itself loose from its snow-ridge, slid slowly down the roof with a soft *shhh*, and dropped into the rear gutter, and neither of them moved to go after it.

Hsin waited until the last bronze guardian had settled its heels into the dry straw at the back of the west wagon-bay before she let her breath go.

Inside the high timber shed, out of the wind, the rows of twelve-foot machines stood silent in the dark. The snow that had piled on their broad pauldrons and domed heads during the walk down the lane was already beginning to soften in the shelter, dripping slow and steady from their bronze elbows onto the straw with the sound of a thawing eaves-trough. Down inside their chests, the crystal spindles had stilled and the silver voice-plates were dark again.

Amy had gone back up to the salt-house before the sixth bell, clutching her brass lamp and walking backwards for the first ten yards so she could watch the big double doors of the bay swing shut.

Now the Hold was past the second watch, and the lanes were empty.

Hsin dropped onto all fours at the edge of the wagon-bay threshold, shook the tall human shape out of her shoulders like a heavy wet coat, and stepped out into the snow as a skinny golden fox, her nine tails held high behind her haunches so only the dark tip of the ninth brushed the white crust.

Beside the gate-post, Suoming stood waiting under Nine Shadows. The snow lay ankle-deep all the way up the narrow alley beneath the Sanctum’s stone buttresses, untouched by cart or boot since the third bell. Without a word, Suoming turned uphill, her iron heel-plates biting through the soft powder to click against the flagstones beneath, and Hsin trotted at her left knee inside the round shadow of the oil-paper rim.

When they reached the sagging wooden gate of the crooked courtyard under the Sanctum stair—the old tea-court whose address Hsin had given the post-house clerk—the bottom rail wasn’t merely sticking on the outer flagstone. The afternoon’s meltwater had pooled under the warped wood and frozen into a solid grey wedge of ice.

Hsin rose smoothly into her tall shape on the step, remembered to duck her chin before the low lintel could rap her between the ears, and took the wooden latch-bar in both hands. She lifted the gate an inch off its iron pintle, braced her hip against the planks, and shoved it inward over the ice-crust.

On the inner threshold stone, half-covered by a drift that had blown through the gap between the boards, lay something that hadn’t been there at noon.

Someone had reached an arm through the gate before the snow thickened and set down a pair of huge, roughly plaited straw snow-overshoes tied together at the heels with hemp twine—big enough for a carter’s winter boots—and wedged a broken piece of roofing-slate upright into the left toe. Scrawled across the wet grey slate in blunt carpenter’s chalk were two lines:

*Steps by the lower cistern are sheet ice under the powder. Put gravel down before the morning water-carts or the grey mule will break her hock.*

There was no signature at the bottom, no courtly salutation at the top, and no dried persimmon beside it.

Hsin crouched on the step, picked up the piece of slate in one hand and the enormous straw overshoes in the other, and read the chalk twice in the dark. Her golden ears stood straight up through her hair, angled forward and twitching with quiet delight.

“<font color=plum>The cooper’s brother-in-law drives the grey mule,</font>” Suoming said from the gateway, looking down at the overshoes. “<font color=plum>He thinks your feet are the size of a barge-man’s.</font>”

“<font color=gold>I’ll put them on the peg by the broom,</font>” Hsin said happily, tucking the chalked slate carefully inside her right cuff beside the fourth-terrace drain complaint. “<font color=gold>And we’ll take the gravel-bucket down before the first bell.</font>”

Beyond the threshold stone, the small square courtyard lay buried under four inches of smooth, unblemished snow. The straw-wrapped tree-peony in the corner wore a tall white mitre, and the old brick well-curb in the middle looked like a round sugared bun. For two hundred winters, Hsin had watched snow fall across the Hold through the high jade lattices of the Sanctum or as a giant shadow holding up the western sky; she hadn’t stood at ground level in a fresh drift since the night she chose her name in the snow.

She set the straw overshoes under the dry overhang of the eaves and folded back down into her skinny fox shape to cross the yard to the lamp-niche.

Halfway across the unbroken white square, her left ear snapped sideways.

Four inches down, beneath the dry powder and the older crust of frost by the base of the tree-peony, something tiny and warm scraped a claw across a dead walnut leaf.

Before Hsin had formed a single thought in her head—before the Sentinel in her had time to remember she was two hundred years old—her hind paws bunched under her belly, her haunches coiled, and she sprang straight up into the dark air, curving her spine like a bow to dive nose-first into the deep drift with a muffled *whuff*.

The mouse was long gone down its root-hole. Hsin came up out of the hole sneezing hard, snow packed solid over her muzzle to the eyebrows, a dry walnut leaf stuck to her wet nose, and all nine golden tails splayed out across the broken drift behind her like a dropped broom.

On the stone step under the gate, Suoming let out a single, short laugh.

It was barely a sound—a dry puff of air that lifted the corner of her flat mouth—and halfway out of her throat it caught hard on the old iron-bar scrape low under her left ribs.

The laugh broke off into a deep, rattling cough that bent Suoming forward over the bamboo shaft of Nine Shadows. She didn’t drop the umbrella, and she didn’t sit down; she pressed her bandaged right forearm tight across the bottom of her ribs, set her jaw, and breathed slow and shallow through the freezing air until the spasm let go, leaving a dry wheeze rasping in and out of her chest at every breath.

Hsin shook the snow from her coat in one brisk wave and stood up in her tall shape at once.

Crossing the three paces back to the doorway, Hsin looked down at the top of Suoming’s red hair and listened to the thin, cold rasp under her left ribs. Tonight she didn’t reach for the handle of the umbrella. Instead she stepped onto the threshold stone, sat down squarely on the cold flagstone on Suoming’s left side—the bad side, where the gorge draught came through the open gate—and drew Suoming down onto the dry inner step beside her by the elbow.

Then Hsin swept all nine of her heavy, solid-gold tails around from behind her waist, laying them three deep across Suoming’s knees, up over her bandaged arm, and tight around her left ribs and shoulder, turning the dense, hearth-warm winter underfur inward against the faded plum coat like a high-backed woollen screen. She didn’t ask whether Suoming wanted them there.

Suoming went rigid for half a breath as the dark-tipped ninth tail settled across her collarbone just beneath her chin. She looked down at the black fur tip, then sideways at Hsin’s profile, and slowly let her shoulders sink back against the warm wall of gold fur.

After a minute, the next breath Suoming drew went down into her chest without catching on the staple.

Hsin reached into the wall-niche with one hand, struck a spark to the small clay oil-lamp, and drew Master Shao’s unruled book of the living onto her knee, grinding a drop of water into the ink-stone before it could freeze. By the little yellow flame, she wrote a clean entry beneath the Zhou brothers for the cooper’s brother-in-law, the grey mule, the cistern steps, and two straw overshoes big enough for a barge-man.

While the ink dried on the paper, the watch-bell on the upper wall struck the hour—the soft, snow-muffled note when Suoming always took down her hair for the night.

Sitting buried to the chin in Hsin’s nine tails, Suoming didn’t wait for Hsin to go indoors. With her uninjured left hand she undid the top frog of her plum coat under the fur, slid her knuckles inside against the black lacquer and vermillion seals of the Providence Lock over her ribs, and looked straight out at the snow falling in the courtyard.

“<font color=plum>Tuesday,</font>” Suoming said aloud to the Lock, her voice flat and quiet in the small space. “<font color=plum>Cleared the fourth-terrace grate before the rain stopped. Ate mutton noodles at the girl’s cart; the Sentinel cut them thinner today, and Gu measured one with his rule. Walked the unclaimed guardians down to the west shed after dusk. The Arbiter’s daughter carried the lamp.</font>”

Beside her, Hsin’s left ear pivoted smoothly round toward Suoming’s cheek to listen.

Last week, in the rope-walk room, Suoming had stopped speaking the instant that ear turned. Tonight she glanced once at the golden ear angled toward her mouth, kept her knuckles pressed to the Lock, and went on without dropping a beat.

“<font color=plum>Stopped raining before the fifth bell,</font>” Suoming told her dead. “<font color=plum>Snowed after.</font>”

Hsin kept her eyes on the drying ink of the unruled page on her knee.

“<font color=gold>And I fell in it,</font>” Hsin said quietly.

There was a silence of two breaths while the snow hissed softly against the oil-lamp’s niche.

Then Suoming looked back out at the deep fox-hole beside the tree-peony and spoke to the seals under her coat in her own flat cadence.

“<font color=plum>She fell in it,</font>” Suoming said.

Slowly she lifted her left hand from her ribs, reached up to the back of her head to slide the long orb hairpin with its tiny spherical lock out of its coil, and let her red hair fall loose over the golden fur around her shoulders, while the snow piled white on the furled ribs of Nine Shadows beside the step and the unbolted gate stood open to the lane behind them.

Cartethyia and Ciaccona came up the middle lane late, when the snow lay ankle-deep between the walls and every shutter along the terrace was latched for the night.

There were no cart-tracks left in the street, only the deep, wide trough where the bronze guardians had marched down from the upper ramp hours earlier, already half-filled again with fresh white powder. Cartethyia walked with her bare right hand still tucked inside Ciaccona’s coat pocket and her left hand holding the oiled-paper twist of Auntie Hu’s candied lemon peel safe inside her own lapel.

Where the lane widened behind the west storehouse, the high double doors of the timber wagon-bay stood hooked back a hand’s breadth to let the damp out. Cartethyia paused for a moment in the snow and peered through the gap.

Inside the dark shed, the unclaimed battle guardians stood shoulder to shoulder in two quiet ranks, dry straw around their ankles and thick white caps of unmelted snow still sitting on their domed helms and broad pauldrons like white doves roosting in a barn.

“<font color=skyblue>They look much better under a roof,</font>” Cartethyia whispered, giving the nearest bronze knee a polite nod through the crack before she stepped back into the lane.

Twenty paces further up, the warm smell of banked coal and ginger broth met them from the salt-house chimney. Cartethyia let go of Ciaccona’s hand in the pocket, stepped up to the plank door, lifted the iron latch while raising the sagging bottom rail half an inch on its hinge, and pushed it open with her shoulder.

A small triangular fan of white snow spilled across the dry flagstones of the sill behind their heels.

Inside, the salt-house was thick with dry brick-heat and the slow, heavy breathing of a full room. Over by the chimney-flue, Ruoyun was pinning a damp cotton quilt along a hemp drying-line while Qingxiao slept sitting bolt upright against the warm masonry in two dry ones, her head tipped back and her mouth slightly open. Halfway down the kang, Buling lay curled on her side with her winter hat still on and both of her cloth shoes placed neatly at the kerb, pointing straight at the stove. Near the outer rim, Lupa and Chisa were back from the roof; Chisa was asleep with her cheek on her folded jumper, and Lupa lay beside her in a heap of rust-red sheepskin, the tips of her wolf ears still dark and damp from melted snow. At the far wall, Yangyang and Suisui lay under one quilt smelling faintly of roasted chestnuts and sorghum baijiu, a neat stack of Longjue’s copper cash sitting on the brick beside Suisui’s horn comb. Between Chisa and Iuno, Amy had fallen asleep sideways with Iuno’s brass-capped pencil still closed in her fist.

And in the corner by the banked stove sat Mei with a blanket round her shoulders, wide awake, turning a piece of split pine kindling over and over in her hands as if she had just woken up to check the fire and certainly hadn’t been watching the door since the fifth bell.

Cartethyia pushed the door shut until the tongue clicked home and began unwinding Lupa’s long scarf from her neck. Ciaccona sat down on the wooden crate by the threshold, propped her wet left boot on her knee, and went to work on the frozen knot in the lace with her right hand.

From the heap of rust-red sheepskin at the edge of the kang, one damp wolf ear rose an inch.

“<font color=indianred>So,</font>” Lupa’s voice came out rough and muffled from under the fur. “<font color=indianred>How was the consultation?</font>”

Cartethyia stopped with the last turn of the wool scarf in her hands. She opened her mouth—ready to say *very good* and keep Ciaccona’s word safe for her here in the warm room too—and glanced down at the door-crate first.

Ciaccona didn’t look up from the wet leather lace between her fingers.

“<font color=teal>Date,</font>” Ciaccona said.

The single word sat in the warm quiet of the salt-house.

Under the rust-red sheepskin, Lupa’s ear gave one short, satisfied flick before vanishing back beneath the collar. “<font color=indianred>Huh,</font>” said the coat. “<font color=indianred>Took you long enough.</font>”

Over by the flue, Ruoyun smoothed the hem of the hanging quilt with both palms and did not reach into her sleeve for her brush or her book.

And in the corner by the stove, Mei dropped the piece of kindling onto the wood-pile, clapped both hands over her mouth, and buried her face into her blanket sleeve while her shoulders shook silently. After a moment she uncovered one bright golden eye, waved a hand at the two of them on the threshold, and slid down flat under the quilt beside the sleeping Iuno, pulling the edge up to her chin.

Cartethyia hung Lupa’s scarf back on its peg, set the twist of lemon peel beside the camellia glass on the sill, and toed off her wet boots. Climbing onto the warm bricks of the kang, she settled herself in her place at the middle with her spine propped squarely against the four-sided timber post so she knew where the floor was under her heels.

Above her head, the wooden window shutter hung off its iron hook, open a hand’s width to the yard. Outside in the dark, the white flakes drifted steadily past the gap, silent and thick, catching the faint red glow from the charging crate as they went by.

Ciaccona came up onto the brick in her stockinged feet, shed her damp coat, and lay down on the quilt right beside the post, curling onto her side with her back near Cartethyia’s knee.

When Cartethyia lowered her right hand in the dark, she didn’t have to grope across the bedding to find where the room ended; her fingers met the loose, dry ends of Ciaccona’s hair at once and curled lightly into the braid.

Watching the snow fall past the unhooked shutter, Cartethyia whispered the names once to herself under her breath, barely louder than the tick of the cooling flue—*The Kingfisher*, and *Via del Forno*, and *Salita dei Limoni*—putting them away in order where the dark couldn’t reach them.

Down on the bolster, Ciaccona shifted her cheek half an inch against Cartethyia’s shin, her eyes already shut and her breathing slow.

“<font color=teal>Thursday, cara,</font>” Ciaccona murmured into the quilt. “<font color=teal>Same table.</font>”

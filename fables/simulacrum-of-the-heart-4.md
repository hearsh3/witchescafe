Simulacrum of the Heart 4

Past the square iron drain-cover, the granite risers stopped playing tricks.

The bruised orange loquat stayed on its twig behind them. The stone drainage ditch along the left-hand wall ran downhill the way water was supposed to run, and after two short turns between shoulder-high banks of thyme and pruned orchard boughs, the stair came out under a tall arch of white-glazed brick.

Ciaccona stopped just inside the shadow of the gatehouse to shift her velvet lute-case higher on her shoulder.

Before them lay a wide street paved in pale cedar blocks fitted so tightly together that not a blade of grass showed between the seams. Everything in the valley’s upper fold was clean, bright, and slightly crooked to the ear. To their left, a timber scaffold stood pinned against a wall of white-tiled eaves where three masons in grey aprons were setting ridge-caps that had no mortar under them; to their right, a pair of scribes sat on a stone well-curb holding flat glass Terminals on their palms, their thumbs flicking up and down columns of amber characters that never reached a bottom line. Overhead hung the same shadowless copper-white glare that had baked the orchard roof, and in the middle of the open square ahead stood an enormous astrolabe of dark brass, its outer ring turning on a central spindle with a heavy, limping *clank—pause—clank*.

Nobody yawned. Nobody leaned against a post to take the weight off a heel.

A short man carrying a bundle of plum-wood laths walked past the gatehouse arch, glanced once at Buling’s round black official’s hat and the yellow paper talisman draped down her nose, and nodded his chin toward the street.

“Morning, Buling,” he said, and went on toward the scaffold without slowing his boots by half a beat.

Ten paces further on, a woman kneeling by a sluice-grate with a brass plumb-bob looked up, squinted through the bright mist, and said, “Back, Buling? Don’t step on the chalk-line, the mortar’s wet.”

Then she went back to her string.

Buling stopped in the middle of the cedar street. Her mouth was open for an excuse that had no customer, and her right hand stayed half-lifted by her sash.

Ciaccona looked from the woman at the grate to the back of Buling’s silver braid.

Buling snapped her mouth shut, whipped her small brass feng-shui compass out of her collar, and flipped the hinged lid open with her thumb—*snick*. She stared hard at the quivering needle, snapped the lid shut—*snick*—and walked three fast steps to catch up with Longjue’s muddy heels.

“That chalk-line is crooked anyway,” Buling said loudly to the back of Longjue’s indigo tunic. “Two fingers off the south-east corner. Whole street’s going to drain into the foundations if she lays the tile like that. Nobody here checks the bearings when I’m out or what?”

“The chalk-line is straight,” Longjue said without looking round. “You’re holding your head crooked because you slept on the bench.”

Buling clapped a hand to the back of her stiff neck, scowled, and flipped the compass lid open and shut a third time before shoving it deep into her side pocket.

Ciaccona fell into step beside Longjue, looking up at the high white gables and the thin wisp of blue woodsmoke wavering out of a brick flue down a side lane.

“Do they always chat like this,” Ciaccona asked pleasantly, “or did we walk in on a holiday?”

“They’re busy,” Longjue said. She shifted the long iron pruning hook on her shoulder and pointed with her free, earth-stained hand toward a long building with open lattice doors. “Drafting hall on the left. Sluice-registers behind the well. The big wheel in the square keeps the water-gates and the terrace hours straight when the lower bearings aren’t sticking.”

“And when they are sticking?”

“Then the fruit falls uphill and the third stair repeats itself,” Longjue said. Her calm brown eyes dropped from the roofs to the velvet case slung across Ciaccona’s back, resting for a moment on the curved swell of the body and the narrow peg-box at the top. “Is that a pipa?”

Ciaccona’s posture straightened by an inch, the tiredness from the flagstone floor vanishing behind a sunny, practiced smile.

“A lute, madonna,” Ciaccona said, patting the worn green velvet with her good hand. “Seven courses of gut, bent-back peg-box, cedar belly from the hills above Ragunna. Lighter than a pipa, much louder in a tavern, and infinitely more forgiving when the player has had three glasses of red.”

Longjue studied the case with mild interest as they reached the edge of the square. “Does it take two hands?”

Ciaccona lifted her left hand, wiggling the splinted forefinger wrapped in stiff white linen. “Ordinarily, yes. Today it takes one and three-quarters, which means I am restricted to simple chords and devastatingly witty commentary.”

“A qin takes ten fingers,” Longjue remarked. “The Paragon plays hers up on the mountain.”

“Ah, well, the Paragon doesn’t have to pass a hat around a dockside taproom afterward,” Ciaccona said cheerfully. “Different repertoire.”

Longjue stopped by the corner of the well-pavilion where a flight of narrow steps led back down toward the eastern ditches. Across the square, the great brass astrolabe gave another slow, dragging *clank*, the main ring shuddering in its collar before the teeth caught again.

“I have the third terrace latch to wedge before the light drops again,” Longjue said. She looked at Buling, who had gone quiet beside the pavilion pillar with her hands tucked into the sleeves of her royal-blue cheongsam, staring across the cedar planks toward the west porch. “Don’t move the well-curb, Buling. It’s sitting where it belongs.”

“Who wants to move your ugly well-curb,” Buling muttered.

Longjue turned to Ciaccona and inclined her head once. “If she tries to charge you for the roof last night, the pavilion belongs to the orchard crew. There’s no fee.”

“Oi!” Buling protested, her red eyes flashing under the yellow talisman. “I carried the lantern—okay, there was no lantern, I checked the wind-line for her! That’s consultation!”

Longjue was already walking down the eastern steps, her pruning hook steady on her shoulder.

Before Ciaccona could laugh, a voice hissed across the square like grease hitting a hot pan:

*“You.”*

On the porch of the third west room, Ruoyun had come up off her three-legged wooden stool like a pheasant out of a bush.

She did not come down the steps, and she did not raise her voice above the scrape of a boot-sole. She stood planted on the top cedar riser with one hand braced against the door-jamb behind her, her small golden horns glinting in the copper glare and her face contorted into a mask of silent, purple-cheeked fury as she beckoned Buling forward with two crooked fingers.

Buling stopped dead. She looked over her shoulder toward the orchard gate, found Longjue gone, and let out a tiny breath through her teeth.

“Stay behind half a step,” Buling muttered to Ciaccona out of the corner of her mouth. “If she throws the stool, duck left, her right arm pulls.”

They walked across the square to the foot of the porch steps.

Ruoyun leaned down over the balustrade until her round brass spectacles were level with Buling’s hat-brim.

“What are you doing making a racket in the middle of the street?!” Ruoyun whispered, every consonant sharp enough to skin a turnip. “Do you have gravel in your ears as well as your head?! If you wake her up after all the trouble I had getting a bowl of scorched millet down her throat, I will peel those yellow papers off your shins and stuff them down your collar!”

Buling leaned back an inch from the blast of whispered air, holding up two fingers.

“I wasn’t even loud!” Buling whispered back. “Longjue was talking! Anyway, what are you doing sitting on a kitchen stool in the middle of the Vale? Look at your hem, got ash on the pleat—”

“Don’t you talk to me about my hem!” Ruoyun’s whisper dropped half an octave, venomous and flat. “You took a whole night to climb three flights of orchard steps! Where were you, taking a nap?!”

“The third terrace latch was slipping!” Buling hissed, her chin coming up. “And that whole stair was pro bono, okay, nobody even paid my retainer yet—if we’re talking hours, you owe me time-and-a-half from the eastern breach!”

“I’ll give you time-and-a-half with a broom-handle,” Ruoyun said, her bright blue eyes hard as glass behind her lenses. “You don’t get to talk about retainers in this village, Buling. You left your post without a note on the peg, and the rest of us kept the gutters clear while you were out playing fortune-teller for copper pennies.”

Buling’s mouth stayed open for a fraction of a beat. Her fingers twitched once against the blue silk at her thigh, not reaching for the compass.

“Fine,” Buling said, her voice dropping its bounce and coming out quick and clipped. “You want the wheel in the square fixed or not? Sounds like the south trunnion is dry already. Give me a pry-bar and a pot of tallow, ten minutes.”

“Twenty,” Ruoyun said coldly, “because you’ll spend ten of them complaining about the ladder. And don’t touch the north pin, Gu already wedged it.”

Then Ruoyun’s spectacles turned two inches to the right and fixed on Ciaccona.

“And this one,” Ruoyun whispered, looking Ciaccona up and down from her feathered Ragunnese hat to the mud dried on her boot-heels. “Another one of the foreigners. Was it you who wrenched the fourth relief-key over in the lotus basin yesterday afternoon without clearing the rings?”

Ciaccona rested her good hand over her heart and offered a bow that belonged in a Doge’s drawing room.

“Alas, signora, I have been trapped in a fruit salad since yesterday noon,” Ciaccona said in a stage-whisper soft as velvet. “Though if there was a large, glowing, thoroughly forbidden key sticking out of a rock, I can almost guarantee it was my friends. Was there shouting afterward?”

“The whole western basin dropped its pressure at once,” Ruoyun snapped. “And the big shadow out there folded its arms.”

“Definitely Cartethyia,” Ciaccona said, beaming with genuine pride. “She has an instinct for locks. If you have any others you’d prefer not to be turned, I suggest putting a bucket over them before she gets here.”

Behind Ruoyun’s back, the iron latch clicked.

The heavy cedar door swung inward four inches, then twelve, and Qingxiao stood on the threshold.

She was in her white stockings, her shoes still sitting by the footboard inside. The heavy blue length of her hair was unpinned and tumbled loose over one shoulder, and across her left cheek ran a faint, pink crease from the seam of her moon-white cuff where she had lain on it. In the hard light of the porch, she blinked once, slowly, her hand resting warm and bare against the dark wood of the jamb.

Down on the bottom step, Buling went completely still.

Her eyes moved from the sleep-crease on Qingxiao’s cheek to the unpinned hair, and then down to the bare white stockings on the cold stone sill. Slowly, Buling’s head turned half an inch until her red eyes met Ruoyun’s blue ones through the brass spectacles.

Neither of them moved a muscle.

Then Buling jerked her gaze back to the doorway and tugged the brim of her *nuanmao* hat straight with both hands.

“Shifu!” Buling blurted, loud enough that a mason on the scaffold across the square turned his head. “You—you taking afternoon naps now? Very good for the liver, outside doctors all recommend—”

She bit the end of the sentence off with an audible snap of her teeth.

Qingxiao looked down at her over Ruoyun’s head. A tiny movement touched the corner of her mouth.

“You are still loud on the stairs, Buling,” Qingxiao said.

“That was Ruoyun!” Buling said instantly, pointing a finger straight up at the porch. “She was whispering like a steam-kettle!”

Ruoyun spun round on the threshold, her hands flying out from her sides. “A-Qing, why are you standing on the cold stone without your shoes?! Go back inside and lie down, I told them three times—”

“I am awake,” Qingxiao said mildly. She looked past Ruoyun’s shoulder toward the thin thread of blue smoke rising from the side lane, then back at the two of them. “Is there anything left in the kitchen? I am hungry.”

Ruoyun glared at Buling as if Buling had personally stolen the millet out of the bin, then glared at Ciaccona for good measure.

“Sit on the bench under the eaves and put your shoes on first!” Ruoyun barked at Qingxiao, abandoning her whisper altogether as she marched down the steps past Buling’s elbow. “And you—fetch the tallow-pot from the shed under the astrolabe and wait for me by the ladder! If that idiot boy in the cookhouse has let the embers go out again, I’m putting his head in the ash-pit!”

Ruoyun’s pleated teal skirt whipped round the corner of the kitchen alley.

On the porch, Qingxiao stepped back inside for her shoes, while at the bottom of the stair Buling let out a long breath, pushed the yellow talisman up onto her hat-brim to rub the bridge of her nose, and let it flop back down over her eyes.

Five miles down the valley, the living bough ran three feet above the flooded paddies like a green causeway laid across a mirror.

It was wide enough here for two pairs of boots abreast, its bark woven tight and sprouted over with small, glossy leaves that smelled of crushed wintergreen whenever a heel bruised them. On either side of the wood, the water lay flat and motionless between low mud dykes.

Above their heads burned the same shadowless copper-white sky that had hung over the mill-house roof. Down in the water between the green rice-shoots, six inches below the surface, hung a clear indigo night.

Cartethyia walked near the edge of the bark with her head tipped sideways, watching her own reflection step across a wheeling drift of tiny silver stars and a thin, curved white moon that had no business being anywhere near noon.

“Look at the water, Lupa,” Cartethyia said, pointing down past her knee. “It’s night-time down there. You can see the little dipper right next to that frog.”

Walking squarely down the middle of the bough with her ash spear carried crosswise in both fists, Lupa did not look at the frog.

“Eyes front, Carte,” Lupa muttered. “Water’s water. You fall in it, I’m not carrying your boots twice.”

Ten paces ahead of them, Amy trotted along the green spine with the open metal lotus held flat on her palm like a compass, her pink hair bouncing under her acid-green star clip. Where the mud dykes curved south around a cluster of empty thatch-roofed cottages—their paper lanterns burning bright yellow inside open doorways with no one at the tables and no dogs in the yards—the rose-gold needle at the heart of the lotus swung three points toward the east.

Below Amy’s boots, the woven branches creaked once and bent smoothly around the foot of the plum hill to follow it.

“Still curving east!” Amy called back over her shoulder, then turned and kept walking backward for four steps to grin at them. “Hey, Lupa, the branch is starting to go uphill again! I think there’s a river ahead!”

“Turn around before you trip over your own heels, kid!” Lupa barked.

Behind Lupa, Chisa and Iuno walked side by side at an unhurried, steady pace.

The bark underfoot was uneven, ribbed like bundled cables where the smaller limbs braided over the main trunk, and Iuno had no left arm to counterbalance her stride when a root-knot sloped toward the ditch. She didn’t stumble, and she didn’t ask for an arm; she carried her weight slightly back on her heels, her right hand resting loose on the notched rim of the moonwheel at her hip.

Beside her on the left—the side where the bark dropped off into the star-filled paddy water—Chisa walked half a pace closer than usual, the heavy leather case of her scissor-blade angled low across her hip so the strap was within six inches of Iuno’s elbow if the bough shifted.

Up ahead, the flooded paddies ended against a steep bluff of grey limestone, and the green bough did not go down to a ford.

It rose straight off the earth from the top of the bluff, shedding its small side-shoots until it was a single, bare bridge of braided wood four feet wide, arching two hundred feet in the open air across a dry, boulder-choked river gorge to the red maple crags on the eastern bank.

Amy reached the lip of the bluff first, looked down between her boots at the white river-stones tiny as pigeon eggs far below, and whistled.

“Whoa,” Amy said, looking back as the others came up the rise. “Okay, no railings on this one.”

Lupa’s boots stopped three yards short of the bluff’s edge.

Her jaw set so hard the muscle jumped under her cheek. Her yellow eyes fixed on the far end of the wooden arch—a good hundred paces of bare bark hanging in empty sky—and her tail dropped straight down behind her calves without a twitch. Chisa shifted her weight half an inch to step forward.

Before Chisa could lift her hand, Cartethyia stepped lightly between them and bumped her hip against Lupa’s elbow.

“I bet I can get across before you,” Cartethyia said brightly, resting one hand on her hip and tilting her crystal laurel up at Lupa. “Unless your legs are still tired from when I beat you in the fountain at Ragunna.”

Lupa’s head snapped toward her so fast her braids slapped her collar.

“You didn’t beat me in that fountain!” Lupa snarled.

“You were sitting in the water with a decorative carp spout in your ear,” Cartethyia said pleasantly, stepping right out onto the high wooden arch without looking down at the gorge. “In Egla Town we call that losing.”

“My heel caught on the wet marble!” Lupa barked, marching straight out onto the suspended bough beside Cartethyia’s shoulder so she could jab a forefinger toward her nose. “There was green slime all over the rim because you people don’t scrub your plazas, and you cheated!”

“I didn’t even have a sword!” Cartethyia protested, walking briskly along the narrow spine two hundred feet above the rocks while Lupa strode step-for-step beside her, glaring sideways into her face. “We were wrestling for the last lemon cake on the tray, and you tried to tackle me by the waist!”

“And I had you off your feet until you turned nine feet tall in the middle of the throw!” Lupa shouted, her hands chopping the air as her boots thumped steadily across the woven wood over the chasm. “Who changes size in the middle of a grapple?! In the Colosseum the referees would’ve thrown a net over your head and fined your house fifty gold!”

“There wasn’t a rule about growing!” Cartethyia said indignantly. “You never said ‘no Fleurdelys’ before you lunged at the plate! Besides, you bit my wrist!”

“I didn’t bite it, my teeth bumped your bracer when you dropped us both into the basin!”

Thirty paces behind them on the bluff, Amy stood with her mouth slightly open, watching Lupa march across the highest part of the gorge with her head turned completely sideways, waving one fist at Cartethyia’s laurel while two hundred feet of empty grey air dropped away beneath her heels.

Iuno paused at the edge of the rock, her eyebrows lifting a fraction of an inch.

*Well,* the High Priestess observed. *That is considerably faster than crawling, darling.*

“Let them get twenty yards ahead,” Chisa said quietly beside Iuno. “If we crowd her back, she’ll notice the wind.”

Amy trotted out onto the span ten paces behind the argument, keeping the metal lotus level in both hands, while Iuno and Chisa stepped onto the bough last.

Up in front, the wind coming down the dry gorge was cold and smelled of wet limestone and pine-pitch. Cartethyia didn’t slow her pace, keeping herself squarely on Lupa’s right so Lupa’s eyes stayed turned toward the upstream cliffs instead of the white boulders below.

“Anyway,” Cartethyia said in her ordinary, earnest voice as they passed the middle of the arch, “the fountain wasn’t even deep. It was only up to our knees. I don’t mind water when you can see the sky over it.”

“A ditch is a ditch,” Lupa grumbled, shifting her spear into her left hand. “At least in an arena you got packed sand under your boots and a wall around you so you know where the edges are.”

“I don’t like walls when the roof comes down too low,” Cartethyia said conversationally. “Or rooms where the windows are painted on the stone instead of cut through. Even when we’re in a nice inn now, if Cia shuts the shutters all the way at night and latches the hook, I wake up and can’t tell which way the door is until I touch her hair. That’s why I fell asleep sitting up on the grain-box last night. If you’re sitting up with your back against the post, you already know where the floor is before you open your eyes.”

She said it at the exact same volume she had used to complain about the lemon cake, stepping neatly over a knot in the bark.

Lupa’s mouth closed.

She looked sideways at Cartethyia’s small profile—the damp curl of hair still sticking up where the giant’s fingertip had ruffled it yesterday, the crystal thorns of the laurel catching the copper noon—and for six full paces Lupa didn’t have a single loud thing to say.

“Yeah, well,” Lupa muttered finally, her ears flattening halfway back against her red hair as she looked straight ahead at the approaching rock of the eastern bank. “Next time we’re in a house, leave the shutter off the hook. If a draft comes in, I’ll just build the fire bigger.”

“Cia says you build them like signal beacons already,” Cartethyia said, smiling.

Ten yards behind them, where the bough dipped slightly under their weight, Iuno walked with her eyes on the braided bark ahead of her toes and Chisa kept pace at her left elbow.

For the first half of the crossing, only the wind in the gorge and Lupa’s shouting ahead filled the air. Then Iuno glanced forty feet forward, where Amy was walking behind Cartethyia’s heels and peering over the edge of the branch at a rusted iron water-gate wedged in the rocks below.

“She went down into the cellar with you this morning,” Iuno said quietly to Chisa.

“To look at the wheel,” Chisa said.

Iuno’s right hand adjusted the lie of her coat lapel against the wind. “She does not always tell me when something has frightened her,” Iuno said, her voice measured and low. “With Mei, she is currently practicing being angry, which is overdue and entirely healthy. With me, she prefers to make a remark about my age and pretend she is made of brass. How is she holding up?”

Chisa walked three steps without looking around. Her black hair blew lightly across the suppression collar at her throat.

“She’s fine,” Chisa said. “And if you want to know what she’s thinking about the dark, you should ask her yourself.”

Iuno’s chin came up slightly. She looked sideways at the younger woman’s calm, unreadable profile.

“She told you about the square folding yesterday,” Iuno said. It wasn’t quite a question.

“Yes,” Chisa said plainly. “Because I was sitting on the cellar step next to her. You were upstairs pinning your hair. If you sit on a step next to her without telling her to get behind a post first, she talks a lot.”

Inside Iuno’s chest, the High Priestess gave a short, dry snort. *She has your measure to the inch, darling. Do try not to pull rank on a girl who cannot see your eyebrows.*

Iuno looked back down at the woven bark before her boots, and the corners of her mouth gave a tiny, reluctant twitch.

“A fair point,” Iuno conceded. “My habits are somewhat difficult to break. Thirty years of seeing the rock fall three days before it loosens does not make one a relaxed companion on a narrow bridge.”

Chisa stepped over a seam where two grey branches crossed.

“Knowing what comes next isn’t better,” Chisa said.

Iuno glanced at her again.

“When the same Tuesday comes round enough times,” Chisa said in her flat, economical voice, watching the dark smudge of Lupa’s coat ahead of them, “you know where every puddle in the street is, and which tile on the school roof is going to slide when the bell rings at four. It doesn’t keep anyone safe. It just makes you stand by the wall by yourself waiting for four o’clock.”

The wind came up out of the dry riverbed between them, chiming faintly against the jagged crystal at Iuno’s left shoulder.

“And now?” Iuno asked quietly.

“Now I don’t know what’s on the other side of the hill,” Chisa said. “And Lupa is yelling about a fish-fountain in the middle of the sky. I like this way better.”

Iuno’s right hand dropped from her lapel and brushed once, very lightly, against the back of Chisa’s black sleeve as they reached the last twenty yards of the span.

“So do I,” Iuno said.

Up on the eastern bank, Lupa’s boots hit the flat red limestone of the crag with a heavy *thump*.

She took three long strides onto the solid stone, stopped beside a boulder half-buried in scarlet maple leaves, and turned around to wait for the rest of them.

As she turned, her eyes went past Cartethyia’s shoulder to the hundred-yard ribbon of bare, rail-less wood hanging two hundred feet above the jagged white rocks of the gorge.

Lupa froze.

The blood drained out of her cheeks for two seconds, and then rushed back twice as hot, turning her face and the insides of her wolf-ears a brilliant, furious red. She spun on Cartethyia.

“You little—” Lupa choked out, her tail bristling to twice its size. “You picked a fight about the fountain on purpose!”

Cartethyia stood five paces up the trail with her hands clasped neatly behind her back, rocking onto her heels.

“You still lost,” Cartethyia said cheerfully, and trotted up the stone path after Chisa and Iuno to see where the bough went through the trees.

Lupa’s knees gave a sudden, delayed wobble. She sat down hard on the flat limestone boulder by the trailside, propped both elbows on her thighs, and buried her nose in her palms with a low, rumbling groan.

Amy stopped beside the boulder instead of following the others. She tucked the open metal lotus into the crook of her elbow, leaned her hip against the stone next to Lupa’s shoulder, and dug into her skirt pocket.

She pulled out a slightly flattened piece of buckwheat pancake wrapped in a cabbage leaf and held it out two inches from Lupa’s nose.

“Saved you a corner,” Amy said.

Lupa opened one yellow eye between her fingers. “I made those. I don’t want a cold pancake that’s been in your pocket with a rusty gear.”

“The gear’s in my blouse pocket,” Amy said, unwrapping the leaf and biting half the piece off herself before shoving the rest into Lupa’s fist. “Eat it anyway. You didn’t eat one at the mill because you were too busy slamming Auntie’s plate down like a tavern bouncer.”

Lupa chewed the cold buckwheat in two sulky bites and wiped her thumb on her knee. “She was shaking like a leaf yesterday. Somebody had to feed her before she dropped that wheel on her own foot.”

“Uh-huh,” Amy said, swinging her boot heel against the side of the boulder and looking out across the gorge. “And last night when Iuno and I were talking on the bench by the stove, you were definitely asleep over by the ash-pan and didn’t wait until we were done to put the lotus on the windowsill.”

Lupa’s ears pinned flat back against her skull.

“I was checking the draft under the shutter!” Lupa barked at the maple leaves. “And if you two want to whisper in the dark, whisper quieter, I got ears on the top of my head, I can’t fold ’em shut!”

Amy looked down at the red-haired crown of Lupa’s head. The teasing went out of her face for a breath.

“Thanks for stopping us at the mill yesterday, Lupa,” Amy said plainly. “I really couldn’t walk another mile, and I didn’t want to be the one who said it while Iuno was looking for Ma.”

Lupa glared furiously at her own boot-buckles.

“Yeah, well, your legs are the size of broomsticks, kid, anyone with eyes could see you were done,” Lupa grumbled, heaving herself up off the boulder and reaching over to cuff the back of Amy’s head lightly with her open palm—careful to miss the acid-green star clip—before keeping her hand bunched loosely in the shoulder of Amy’s blouse to steer her up the path. “Come on. Give me the compass-flower thing before you drop it in a bush.”

“No way, I’m the navigator,” Amy said, grinning as she held the lotus out ahead of them, where the rose-gold needle sat steady and bright, pointing down through the red trees toward the river valley below.

In the center of Mengxuan Vale, the great brass astrolabe went *clank—pause—screak*.

Fifteen feet up the timber maintenance ladder leaned against the central pillar, Buling clung to the top rung with one knee hooked over a bronze cross-strut, a flat iron pry-bar wedged into the main bearing collar, and a wooden pot of mutton-tallow dangling from her wrist by a hemp string.

“Who greased this trunnion last, ah?!” Buling shouted down through the brass rings, her yellow forehead-talisman flapping wildly sideways as she scraped a black curl of petrified grease out of the channel with her thumbnail. “This isn’t even tallow, this is pine-resin and stone-dust! Whole collar is dry like a salt-biscuit! No wonder the third terrace is jumping!”

Five feet above her head, standing balanced on the narrow curved rim of the outer meridian ring with one Mary Jane shoe braced on a spoke and her pleated teal skirt tucked neatly behind her knees, Ruoyun didn’t even hold onto a strut. She had a three-foot bronze lever-bar under the upper housing and her ruffled white cuffs pushed high up her forearms without a single smudge of black grease on the linen.

“Stop giving a speech to the collar and put the fat in the groove!” Ruoyun snapped down at her. “When I lift on three, pack it behind the third tooth, and if you get mutton-grease on my shoes, Buling, you’re licking it off!”

“How to get grease on your shoes when you’re standing on top of my head?!” Buling grumbled, slapping a thick dollop of tallow onto the iron blade of her trowel. “Lift lah, I’m ready!”

Thirty paces away, under the cool white-tiled eaves of the corner pavilion, Ciaccona sat on a stone stool across the kaya-wood weiqi board from Qingxiao.

On the bench beside Qingxiao’s elbow sat an empty earthenware bowl that had held a second round of scorched turnip-soup from the cookhouse, and next to it lay a square of white linen holding four plump, steaming red-bean buns that Buling had marched off to fetch from the empty northern dormitories twenty minutes earlier, slapping them down on the stone with a dark mutter that the foreigner was complaining about her stomach.

Qingxiao had eaten two of the buns already. The third was in her left hand, half-gone, a tiny crumb of white dough resting on the lower lip of the Sword Transcendent while her right hand hovered over the nineteen-by-nineteen grid.

The single white clam-shell stone she had played the evening before still sat unanswered on the fourth line.

Qingxiao had not cleared that quarter of the board. Instead, on the empty lower left corner, she had laid out four black stones and three white ones to show Ciaccona how a group breathed.

“Place one,” Qingxiao said around the last bite of the bun.

Ciaccona leaned forward, resting her chin in her good right palm while her splinted left forefinger stuck straight out over the edge of the table. She narrowed her eyes at the black cluster, picked up a white clam-shell disc with a flourish, and set it down squarely in the middle of the four black stones.

“There,” Ciaccona declared. “Right in the parlor. Now they have to deal with me.”

Qingxiao dusted the bun-crumb off her thumb, picked up a black slate stone, and laid it down on the point beside Ciaccona’s disc. Then she lifted Ciaccona’s white stone off the board and dropped it back into the lid with a dry *clink*.

Ciaccona blinked at the empty intersection. “Wait. Why did I die? I just got there.”

“You walked into a room with no windows and shut the door from the inside,” Qingxiao said. She set three more stones down in four seconds—*click, click, click—*turning the small corner cluster into a sweeping net that swallowed nine lines of territory before Ciaccona’s eyes could follow the diagonal—then swept all four back into the bowls with one smooth pass of her fingers. “Again.”

“That was not a beginner’s demonstration, that was an ambush,” Ciaccona complained, fishing another white stone out of the bowl. “My lute master in Ragunna used to do that—play a twelve-bar saltarello at double tempo with his eyes shut and then hit my knuckles with a reed when I missed the third grace-note. Who are you actually playing the real game with, anyway?”

She nodded her chin toward the upper half of the grid, where the nine black handicap stones and the unanswered white move sat untouched.

Qingxiao looked at the silent upper corner.

“I don’t know,” Qingxiao said.

Ciaccona raised her eyebrows, waiting for the rest of the sentence. None came. Qingxiao simply watched the lower left star-point, waiting for Ciaccona’s stone.

Across the square on the astrolabe, Ruoyun braced her shoulders and heaved down on the three-foot bronze lever.

The massive outer ring rose a quarter-inch in its socket with a low groan of metal. Buling jammed her trowel into the gap, packing the tallow hard around the worn bronze teeth, then pulled a narrow yellow paper talisman from her belt, bit the tip of her tongue to dab a pinpoint of red on the corner character, and slapped the paper flat across the cracked housing to bind the vibration.

A faint blue spark jumped off her knuckles—*zzt*.

“Tsk—*fuck*,” Buling hissed, shaking her fingers out as the fine silver hair around her temples stood straight up behind her hat-brim.

“Watch your mouth in front of the pavilion!” Ruoyun barked, though she didn’t ease a pound of weight off the lever until Buling’s thumb was clear of the collar. Slowly, Ruoyun let the ring settle back into its greased seat.

The great wheel turned: *clank—pause—hum*.

The dry screech was gone.

Buling wiped her greasy hands on a rag, hooked the tallow-pot onto the ladder rung, and leaned both forearms on the brass cross-strut, looking down across the cedar square toward the pavilion eaves. From fifteen feet up, they had a clear view of the top of Qingxiao’s head—which Ruoyun had brushed and pinned back up with a bone clasp before going to the cookhouse—as she reached for the fourth red-bean bun on the napkin.

“Shifu ate three already,” Buling said quietly.

Above her on the ring, Ruoyun did not look down from the brass housing. She pulled a clean linen handkerchief from her sleeve and wiped a speck of dust off the bronze lever.

“Just now at the door she was standing on the cold stone in her stockings,” Buling went on, her voice dropping the rapid patter as she watched the white sleeve move below. “Sleeping in the middle of the day. Asking the kitchen for food like a Warden after drill.”

Buling tilted her head back to look up at Ruoyun’s Mary Jane shoes on the spoke above her.

“What happened inside Muyu’s Mirage, Ruoyun?” Buling asked plainly.

Ruoyun stopped wiping the lever.

She looked straight down through her round brass spectacles at Buling’s upturned face. There was no warmth in her eyes, and her mouth was flat as a ruler.

“You don’t get to ask that,” Ruoyun said.

Buling’s fingers tightened on the ladder rung. “She was my Shifu too—”

“She was your Shifu for five minutes between all the times you climbed over the orchard wall because your knees hurt from horse-stance,” Ruoyun said, her voice low, even, and mercilessly clear. “And when you got bored, you walked out the gate without a word. You weren’t on the wall when the frost kept her alive, and you weren’t in the Core when she put it down. So shut your mouth and scrape the second pinion.”

The words hung in the copper air between the brass rings.

Buling didn’t snap back, and she didn’t make a joke about the invoice. For two beats she looked up at Ruoyun’s sharp chin; then she pulled her gaze away, dropped down two rungs on the ladder to the lower gear-box, and jammed her iron pry-bar under the second pinion crust with a hard, silent *clink*, scraping the dried resin out of the teeth with both hands and her head bent low under her hat.

Ruoyun stood alone on the upper ring for a moment, her knuckles tight around the folded handkerchief. Then her eyes went back across the square to the pavilion eaves.

Under the white tiles, Ciaccona had given up on the lower left corner after losing a second group of six stones to a three-move sequence she hadn’t seen coming. She leaned back against the stone pillar, unlatched her velvet case with her right hand, and lifted her lute out onto her lap.

Qingxiao’s pale blue eyes moved from the weiqi grid to the instrument.

She looked at the thin spruce belly, the carved rose in the soundhole, and the seven double courses of gut string stretched over the tied frets.

“The back is round,” Qingxiao observed.

“Twenty-one ribs of fluted maple, steamed over an iron mould,” Ciaccona said, immediately brightening now that they were back on civilized ground. “Here—hold her for a second, my left finger’s useless anyway.”

Before Qingxiao could demur, Ciaccona lifted the lute by the neck and laid it across Qingxiao’s moon-white lap.

The Sword Transcendent sat very still with the Ragunnese tavern lute resting in her arms.

She held it the way she held everything that wasn’t a blade: level across her knees, as if she were looking for a table to lay a seven-stringed qin flat upon, her left hand hovering uncertainly behind the bent-back peg-box while the round maple bowl slid two inches down her silk skirt.

“No, no, no, she’s not a zither, she’ll roll right off your knees onto the flagstones!” Ciaccona said, leaning across the edge of the weiqi board without a trace of awe to catch the bottom bout and tuck it snugly against Qingxiao’s right ribs. “Tuck the belly against your ribs—there—and your right forearm holds her still against your hip. Thumb stays behind the neck, not on top.”

Qingxiao adjusted her grip as instructed, her eyebrows drawing together by a hair’s breadth as she tested the weight of the hollow wood. With the nail of her right index finger, she flicked outward across the fourth course the way one strikes a silk qin string.

*Twang.*

That course had slipped a fraction of a tone in the damp orchard night. Ciaccona winced as if someone had squeezed lemon juice into her eye.

“Madonna mia, stop, don’t touch that string again until I turn the peg,” Ciaccona groaned, reaching across the kaya board with her good right hand to twist the fourth pearwood peg a tiny hair upward while plucking the gut with her pinky until the fourth rang clean and sweet against the cedar ribs. “There. Flesh of the fingertip, not the nail, or you’ll saw through my good Venice gut by lunch. Try the bottom three with your thumb.”

Qingxiao looked from Ciaccona’s splinted finger to the neck of the lute. Obediently, she used the warm pad of her right thumb to brush down across the lowest three courses.

A deep, resonant *D-minor* chord hummed out under the pavilion eaves, richer and softer than the single string Ciaccona had plucked in the dark. In Qingxiao’s left hand, even without knowing the Ragunnese tuning, her third finger came down cleanly behind the second fret on the next brush, turning the chord into a clear, open fourth that hung in the still air.

Ciaccona’s grin softened. She leaned her elbow back on her knee.

“All right,” Ciaccona said dryly. “Show-off. At least you didn’t play it at double tempo.”

Qingxiao looked down at the carved rose in the soundhole, her fingers resting quiet over the strings.

Up on the astrolabe ladder across the square, Ruoyun was glaring straight down at them over the brass meridian ring while pretending to inspect a rivet.

“Your friend with the spectacles,” Ciaccona said conversationally, keeping her voice pitched for the table between them, “sat on that three-legged stool in your doorway all night. She was still sitting there with her arms crossed when we came through the gate this morning.”

“Celestials do not sleep,” Qingxiao said, her thumb resting lightly on the low bass course.

“That isn’t why she was sitting on the sill, and you know it,” Ciaccona said plainly. “She looked ready to bite the head off anyone who breathed loud within fifty yards of your blanket.”

Qingxiao did not look up toward the astrolabe.

Her bare right hand lifted from the strings and rested against the warm maple ribs of the lute. For three beats she sat in the quiet, the faint pink sleep-crease still visible along her cheek.

“She is very loud,” Qingxiao said in her level voice. “And she burned the millet.”

Then she lifted the lute carefully by the neck and the lower bout and handed it back across the board into Ciaccona’s lap.

“Your turn,” Qingxiao said, touching the bowl of white clam-shell stones with one finger. “Take four stones in the corner first.”

Ciaccona laughed softly under her breath, settling the lute back into its velvet case beside her stool before picking up a white stone.

“Cartethyia tried to learn chess from an old baker in Egla Town once,” Ciaccona said as she set the first disc down on the third line. “She refused to let her knights jump over the pawns because she said it was rude to the infantry. By the tenth move she had all her pieces standing in a straight line in front of her king having a parade, and when the baker took her bishop she challenged him to an arm-wrestle.”

“Did she win?” Qingxiao asked, placing a black stone two points away.

“She broke his table,” Ciaccona said warmly, setting down a second white stone and watching the board with her chin in her palm. “And then she bought every burnt almond tart in his shop so he wouldn’t feel bad about the oak.”

Over at the astrolabe, Buling finished scraping the last of the petrified resin out of the second pinion, packed the clean tallow into the teeth, and climbed two rungs up to wipe her palms on the rag.

Above her, Ruoyun stepped down from the meridian ring onto the wooden platform at the top of the ladder, adjusting her brass spectacles with two clean fingers.

For a minute neither of them spoke while the great brass rings turned smoothly beside their shoulders—*clank—pause—hum*.

“That turnip water from the cookhouse was disgusting,” Buling said flatly to the brass pillar. “Next time she eats, tell the boy to steam the rice dry and fry two eggs. Cannot let a sword master eat scorched millet three times a day, people outside will say Xuanfang Hold gone bankrupt.”

Ruoyun looked down at the top of Buling’s round winter hat.

“There aren’t any eggs in the storehouse bin,” Ruoyun said, just as flat. “Only dried fish and buckwheat.”

“The north dormitory kitchen got six baskets of brown eggs sitting behind the steamer rack,” Buling said, hooking the tallow-pot off the rung. “I saw them when I went for the buns. Nobody’s touched them since the middle reign.”

“Then go fetch a basket before the noon bell,” Ruoyun said. “And don’t crack them on the stair.”

Before Buling could take the next rung down, the copper-white glare in the western sky dimmed.

Far out beyond the white-tiled roofs of the Vale, where the colossal silhouette of the fox stood with her chin resting down against her folded arms, the pastel spokes of rose and gold light around her shoulders flickered once, dropping the whole square into a sudden, flat grey shadow.

Under Ruoyun’s palm and Buling’s knee, the freshly greased astrolabe shuddered.

The main ring caught dead in its socket with a heavy metallic *thud*, holding still for a full heartbeat before the light in the west came back and the bronze teeth ground forward again.

On the ladder, Buling and Ruoyun both turned their heads toward the western peaks at the exact same instant.

Thirty paces away under the pavilion eaves, Qingxiao’s right hand stopped two inches above the kaya-wood grid with a black slate stone held loosely between her index and middle fingers, her pale blue eyes looking out past the white pillars toward the giant’s bowed, unmoving head.

***

A mile west of the Third Ferry Rest, the red-lacquered pillars of the East Arcade finally came to an end.

The covered street did not stop at a city wall or a market gate; the green-tiled eaves simply gave way to open cobbles that sloped down toward a wide, slow-moving river the color of pewter, and the smell of dry cedar shavings changed between one flagstone and the next into the sharp, sour tang of linseed oil, wet hemp, and pine-soot ink.

Mei stepped out from under the last arcade beam and looked up and down the quay.

A carved stone tablet set into the corner of the first warehouse read *BOOKBINDERS’ ROW, LOWER WHARF*.

Every shopfront along the river wall stood wide open to the shadowless noon. Inside the nearest timber bay, three massive oak screw-presses stood with their iron levers swung out over the aisle; lead type sat locked tight inside the iron chases on the bed-plates, glistening black with ink that neither dried nor dripped, and nobody stood at the bars. Overhead, stretched from eave to eave across the narrow alleys like washerwomen’s lines, hung hundreds of damp white paper sheets pinned up with split bamboo clips to dry.

When Mei craned her neck to squint up at the nearest line as they walked under it, every single sheet carried the exact same half-printed page—three columns of dense official characters that broke off in the middle of a sentence halfway down the leaf, repeated sheet after sheet all the way to the warehouse roof.

Along the stone wharf beside them, waist-high bales of coarse straw-paper sat stacked under tarred canvas, and out on the flat pewter river, four long cargo barges lay moored to the iron rings with their steering sweeps lashed and not a single boatman on the decks.

Down in the shallow granite gutter along the river-wall, the shed flakes of black vine-scale ran on toward the west in a greasy trail.

This time, Suoming was not walking in front.

Ten paces ahead of the oil-paper umbrella, Yangyang walked alone along the edge of the quay. Her right hand rested loose near the hilt of her sword, her white-tipped blue hair stirring faintly against her shoulders, and her head was tilted a fraction of an inch toward the water. Out here where there were no shop-shutters to echo their boots and no awnings creaking overhead, the vast, slow breath at the western end of the valley came down the river surface like a tide drawing in and out against the pilings, and Yangyang’s boots followed it as surely as they followed the black scale in the stone.

Behind her, Suoming walked at an unhurried, steady pace—*click, pause, click*—following the young Watcher’s back without a single word of direction.

Mei walked on Suoming’s left side inside the brown circle of shade, looking from Yangyang’s straight shoulders ahead of them down to the small, coiled red head beside her elbow.

For the first hundred yards past the arcade, Mei decided to be quiet.

Suoming clearly wasn’t a talker, and Mei had already filled half the breakfast hour with the goose and the vinegar jar. So Mei pressed her lips together, hooked her thumbs into her belt loops, and looked at the printing presses.

She made it past a paper-vat house where wooden moulds sat floating in milky pulp.

She made it past a book-stitcher’s stall with forty blue-paper covers stacked by an awl.

At the third warehouse—where a stone ink-slab the size of a millstone sat by the door with a brass roller resting across it—Mei’s mouth opened by itself.

“Okay, I tried,” Mei said cheerfully, dropping her hands from her belt and glancing down at Suoming. “I made it three whole blocks, which is a personal record if you ask Iuno. At breakfast I made you listen to the whole goose saga, so it’s your turn. What was going on over on your side of the Hold before the sky went weird? What’s the good gossip?”

Suoming did not look up from the cobbles. The iron ferrule of Nine Shadows clicked against a granite curb-stone.

“I don’t listen to gossip,” Suoming said.

“Everybody has news from their street,” Mei said easily, matching her stride to the shorter steps. “Like who’s arguing with the baker, or which dog stole a sausage. Come on, you were sitting by that market lane when we got meal tickets.”

Suoming walked four paces in silence. Under the brown oil-paper canopy, her pale eyes stayed fixed on the stone ahead of her boots, as if she were turning over a cupboard that hadn’t been opened in a week to see what was on the front shelf.

“Old Kang at the south-gate tea-stall waters the white tea after the second bell,” Suoming said in her flat voice.

Mei grinned. “Yeah? How much water?”

“A ladle from the cistern every time the kettle gets below the spout,” Suoming said. “He thinks if he leaves the old leaves in the pot, nobody can taste the iron from the pipe. Everyone tastes it. The Wardens buy it anyway because his bench gets the morning sun when the wind’s off the gorge.”

“See, that’s top-tier neighborhood intelligence,” Mei said approvingly. “What else?”

Suoming shifted the bamboo shaft half an inch on her collarbone.

“There’s a three-legged tortoiseshell cat that sleeps on the warm bricks behind the bathhouse flue,” Suoming said. “If the stoker’s boy tries to move her before he rakes the ashes out, she bites him through his leather glove. He brings her the heads off the salted sprats from the mess kitchen now so she’ll get down on her own.”

Mei’s eyes crinkled at the corners. “Does she have a name?”

“The boy calls her the Magistrate,” Suoming said. “Because she sits on the warm bricks all day and doesn’t catch mice.”

Mei let out a delighted bark of a laugh that bounced off the stacked paper bales along the wharf.

Ten paces ahead, Yangyang glanced back over her shoulder at the sound, caught Mei’s eye, and turned forward again with the faint lift of her cerulean collar-feathers showing above her scarf.

They walked past a dyer’s yard where bolts of dark indigo cloth hung stiff over cedar poles. As the quay rose slightly over a stone culvert, Suoming’s chest lifted under her faded plum coat and gave that slow, dry catch low on the left side—longer than the one on the stair at dawn—and her next step came down an inch shorter than the last.

Mei didn’t look down at Suoming’s ribs, and her face didn’t sober into concern.

Instead, Mei let out a loud, theatrical groan, clapped her left palm over her grey cotton shirt where the scar sat, and dropped her backside squarely onto a waist-high bale of coarse wrapping-paper beside a mooring post.

“Whoa, hold up a second,” Mei called out, stretching both legs out into the cobbles and leaning back on her hands. “That turnip-radish story took more out of me than I thought. My side’s pulling again. Five minutes on the paper, Elder, or I’m gonna start whining like Amy in an ice dungeon.”

Suoming stopped two paces past the bale.

She turned around slowly under the canopy of Nine Shadows and looked straight at Mei’s face, then down at Mei’s left hand—which was resting three inches higher on her ribs than the actual scar.

“Your side wasn’t pulling ten steps back,” Suoming said.

“It’s a very sneaky pull,” Mei said without blinking, patting the adjacent paper bale—which was six inches lower and sat squarely in the shade of the warehouse eaves. “Sneaks right up on you by the river. Sit down for a minute so I don’t feel like the only lazy one on the wharf.”

Suoming looked at her for three slow beats.

Then she walked back two steps, closed the oil-paper umbrella with a single sharp *clack* because the warehouse eaves shaded the quay, and sat down on the lower paper bale with the bamboo shaft held upright between her knees and her iron-heeled boots swinging three inches clear of the stone.

“You’re a poor liar,” Suoming said to the river.

“I’m a fantastic liar, I just wasn’t trying very hard,” Mei said cheerfully, letting her shoulders sag back against the stacked paper behind her.

Out ahead, Yangyang had reached a humped granite bridge where the quay crossed a side-canal; seeing them sitting on the bales, she raised one hand in acknowledgment and walked up the arch to check the far side before coming back.

For a minute or two, only the quiet lap of the pewter river against the barge hulls filled the wharf. Over the rooftops to the west, the huge bowed silhouette of the fox stood unmoving in the white glare, her folded arms wide as hills.

Mei tipped her head back against the higher bale behind her, watching the half-printed sheets of paper hang motionless on the laundry lines overhead.

“Hey, Suoming,” Mei said, her voice dropping out of its bright tease into an easy, conversational quiet. “When we get the fox sorted out and the road’s open again—if you get a Tuesday where nobody’s dropping through a floor or knocking on your door about a wall, what do you actually do with it?”

Suoming’s small left hand rested atop the curved bamboo handle of Nine Shadows. Her bandaged right forearm lay quiet across her lap.

For a long moment she watched a single fallen maple leaf drift past the tarred bow of the nearest barge.

“A small house,” Suoming said. “Two rooms. Somewhere the street doesn’t have a garrison gate at the end of it.”

Mei turned her head on the paper stack to listen.

“A low window on the south side,” Suoming went on in the same flat, unhurried voice, “so you can see who’s walking past the wall without getting up for a bucket. Grey tiles on the roof so you can hear when the rain starts. And a girl from the lane who brings the hotpot over from the tavern at the fifth bell so I don’t have to wash the pan.”

She stopped there. Her boots gave one small swing against the front of the bale and went still.

Mei smiled softly against her sleeve.

“Amy’s already got a whole house picked out for us,” Mei said to the warehouse beams. “Iuno’s making a list of which bakeries are within three blocks of the south hill so she can pretend she isn’t spoiling her, and I’m just gonna sit on the back steps with a mug of coffee that has way too much milk in it and shell peas into a bucket.”

“Peas are spring,” Suoming said. “In autumn you shell dried broad-beans.”

“Broad-beans, peas, whatever’s in the basket,” Mei said, grinning. “If you ever get that house with the grey tiles, tell me which lane it’s in and I’ll bring over a sack of them so you have something to throw at the crows.”

“Mm,” Suoming said.

Footsteps came clicking back down the granite steps of the humped canal bridge, light and even on the stone.

Yangyang stopped in front of the paper bales with her hands resting at her sides. Her breathing was steady, and the warm square of the Prism showed faintly under the left fold of her silk sash where Suoming had told her to move it the day before.

“The canal joins the main river beyond the bridge,” Yangyang reported plainly. “There’s a timber lock-gate fifty yards past the turn, and a stone barge-stair going down to the water level. The black scale goes straight through the lock-house door, and the breath is louder on the other side of the sluice.”

Suoming slid down off the paper bale, her iron heel-plates ringing once on the cobbles as she stood.

“Lead on, Watcher,” Suoming said, and walked past them toward the foot of the bridge without popping the umbrella open yet.

Yangyang stepped over to the higher bale and held out her right hand to help Mei down.

Mei took her fingers, letting Yangyang haul her to her feet with a small grunt as her scar pulled tight for a second, and didn’t let go of her hand right away as they fell into step behind Suoming’s plum coat.

“‘Lead on, Watcher,’” Mei whispered into Yangyang’s ear, bumping her shoulder lightly against Yangyang’s arm. “Look at you, bossing the Elder down the wharf. Should I be saluting back here?”

“If you salute with that side, you’ll pull the skin again,” Yangyang murmured back, her voice completely level while her thumb brushed once, warm and quick, across the back of Mei’s knuckles before she let their hands swing naturally between them. “And she’s only letting me walk in front because my ears work better than her knees.”

“Uh-huh. Keep telling yourself that, Captain.”

They climbed the humped granite arch of the canal bridge.

Halfway up the risers, the air changed.

It didn’t happen with a bell-chime or a shudder in the stone. As their boots crested the round crown of the bridge looking down toward the timber lock-gate, the hard, shadowless white noon that had burned overhead since they fell through the Sanctum floor simply stopped at the far parapet.

Over the lower reach of the river hung a low, unbroken ceiling of slate-grey cloud.

It was cool, soft, and dim as a late autumn afternoon before supper, smelling cleanly of wet river-silt and mist, and out of the grey belly of the cloud fell a fine, steady Mengzhou drizzle—slanting down across the dark water in millions of tiny silver needles that dimpled the surface around the lock-gate and turned the dressed granite of the lower quay dark as oiled slate.

At the bottom of the bridge steps, right on the line where the dry white cobbles met the wet black stone, Suoming stopped dead.

Yangyang slowed half a pace behind her, glancing back at Mei.

Suoming did not raise Nine Shadows to pop the catch.

Instead, she lowered the furled bamboo shaft from her collarbone, rested the steel ferrule quietly on the wet flagstone beside her boot, and took a single step forward across the seam into the drizzle.

She tilted her chin up half an inch toward the low grey cloud-bank. With the blinding glare gone from the sky, her pale, light-sore eyes opened all the way—clear and unnarrowed beneath her brows—and she stood completely still on the quay for five long breaths, letting the cool fine rain bead in the red hair coiled around her gilded orb pin and darken the shoulders of her faded plum coat.

Behind her on the last dry step, Mei looked at Yangyang.

Yangyang’s small cerulean collar-feathers lifted slightly in the damp river air. A very faint, quiet smile touched her mouth, and she stepped down off the bridge into the rain without pulling her hat-brim low.

Mei followed her across the line, letting the cool drizzle hit her face and soak into her grey cotton shirt as Suoming picked up the furled umbrella under her uninjured left arm and walked on down the wet, shining stones of the river-wall behind the trail of black scale.

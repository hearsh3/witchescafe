# PETITIO

The first person to welcome them back to Vernworth asked Reiter to lower her lance. The tip had caught the awning over the gate clerk's window, and rainwater was travelling down the shaft into his ink.

She stepped back. The awning emptied into the street.

Ronin took her hat off under the arch and shook it once, away from the clerk.

They had followed the dragon for six days. A shepherd had seen it cross a valley. A charcoal burner had heard something large in the trees. Beyond the river they had spent an afternoon following a trail of broken branches to an oak that had fallen during the spring floods. Reiter had examined the roots until Ronin took out the bread.

Now the clerk wanted their business in the capital.

Reiter put Brant's summons on the sill.

He read the signature and called for a guard.

---

Brant arrived chewing. He swallowed on the way across the guardroom, rubbed his beard with the back of his hand, and looked at the two of them.

“Lennart's letter came yesterday. He says you saved his village.”

“Three houses burned. The dragon left under its own power.” Reiter stood with her helm against her hip. “We require the reports from your northern patrols.”

“Come to the Stardrop after supper. I'll bring what I have.”

His eyes went to the lance. The old, habitual movement of his hand began at his belt, then continued upward, awkwardly, until he was scratching his cheek.

“You've put that to use, then.”

Ronin watched Reiter's thumb settle into the darkened place on the shaft.

On the way out, a young man in an expensive attempt at poor clothes slipped behind Ronin. A guard came through the arch a moment later, looking from face to face.

Ronin shifted the pack onto the sill and began adjusting its straps. Reiter stood opposite her. Between them, the young man crouched beside a rain barrel with both knees under his chin.

The guard went past.

“I owe you something for that,” the young man said, emerging.

“Hold this while I finish,” Ronin told him.

She gave him the bedroll. He held it with great care.

---

That evening Brant spread his reports around three plates. He had kept his supper warm under an overturned bowl; gravy ran into the onions when he lifted it.

The northern patrol had seen the same smoke as the charcoal burner. A second report concerned a griffin. The third had a date from before Melve burned.

Reiter read the numbers. Ronin read the rest aloud.

Brant pushed his plate aside.

“My men are coming in for the coronation. Every road captain has received the order. I've got a party stranded at Trevo Mine, another down at Harve, and a supply escort overdue from the east. Once I have them back, I can put eyes on your dragon.”

He turned a sheet toward her.

“And by then half the countryside will have seen you do the work expected of an Arisen.”

Reiter looked at the three places he had marked.

“You have fitted that together carefully.”

“I've had several evenings to think about it.” He picked up his fork. “You've been walking past my table.”

Ronin set down her cup. Inside her coat, the house key rested against her ribs. They had passed Ilsa's lane that afternoon. The shutters were closed; someone had swept the step.

“The eastern escort first,” she said. “Their food will be going.”

Reiter's finger stayed on Harve.

“The tide confines the men in the cave. We reach them tomorrow, then Trevo. The escort has ground to move on.”

Brant pulled the sheet back to write the order.

Ronin watched the B form beneath his hand.

“There is something you should both see,” she said.

It came out at an inconvenient volume. The serving woman glanced over while collecting a jug. Ronin waited for her to pass, then held out her hand toward Reiter's breastplate.

“The old forage requisition. Please.”

Reiter unfastened the pocket inside the steel.

Brant bent over the two signatures. He moved the candle. At last he took a clean corner of paper and wrote his name again, slowly this time.

The same kick on the downstroke.

“How old is this?”

“Four hundred years,” Ronin said. She looked at Reiter as she gave him the units.

Brant sat back. The fork lay across his plate, cooling in the gravy.

“My father taught me my letters. He made me do my name until I could fit it under his thumb.” He laid his own thumb beside the ink. “I was going to ask whether you knew him. Your date puts him rather far out of consideration.”

Reiter folded the old page inward.

“I knew the man who ordered that forage.”

“What was he like?”

She took some time over the question.

“He opened the sacks himself. The grain was damp at the bottom.”

Brant glanced at the amounts in the margin, then at the three places on his map.

“I'll have the stores checked before the men go out.”

Under the table Ronin's fingers tightened around the key until the teeth hurt.

Reiter put the log away. She drew the map closer and asked Brant how many men were waiting at Harve.

---

The man they brought out of the cave kept thanking Reiter while looking over her shoulder.

His sergeant came out last, leaning on Ronin. Then he let himself sit. He had a split boot and sand stuck to his gums, and he cried with both hands over his mouth while the sergeant counted everyone twice.

Reiter knelt to examine his foot. The lance lay on the sand within reach.

“My little brother was at Melve,” he told her. “He wrote that he'd fought beside you.”

“How old is your brother?”

“Twelve. He says fourteen whenever there's something to carry.”

Reiter loosened the boot around the swelling.

“His spear was too long for the lane. Tell him to shorten it by a foot and keep the offcut for practice.”

The man began to laugh. It hurt his mouth; he kept doing it while she worked.

That evening she gave the report by the soldiers' fire. She reached the moment when the rear man had held his torch above the water for the others and described his position, the length of the passage, the height he had maintained with an injured shoulder.

The man looked down at the arm in his lap. His sergeant moved over to make space for him nearer the heat.

Ronin ate her bread slowly. She had been ready to take the eastern road that morning. She could still picture herself walking away from Brant's table, expecting the grey plate to follow.

At Trevo, Reiter broke the goblins' rush at a bend in the mine while Ronin brought the soldiers through behind her. On the eastern road they found the escort with a wheel sunk to its hub and monsters in the trees. The fighting took less time than freeing the cart.

By their return, someone at the gate knew Ronin as the woman who had carried Ser Jory out of Harve.

She took her hat off to hear him say it again to his companion.

---

Brant paid them and produced another paper.

This one concerned the castle offices. Orders went through that room, he said. Letters arrived from Battahl. If he could show what the queen regent was arranging, he could persuade men with seals and purses to act.

“You could have put this on the culling board,” Reiter said.

“The board's overseer reports to Allard.” Brant rested his arms on the table. “Sonia will get you through the guardhouse tonight. Your armour should do well enough in those corridors.”

Ronin was already examining the sketch of the rooms.

“The pawns wait at the outer gate,” he told her. “You'll have a clear view of the road down from the offices.”

Her finger stopped at the stair.

Reiter watched it stop.

Then she took the sketch.

Upstairs, while Ronin cleaned mud out of a buckle, Reiter put the primer beside the castle plan. She had reached the page after C. The picture was a door, coloured in by the same long-ago child who had gone outside the cart.

She worked through the letter twice.

“Sonia knows which room,” Ronin said. “Bring the loose correspondence. I can sort it when you come out.”

Reiter closed the primer with her finger inside it.

“You have known about the signature since the cyclops.”

Ronin laid the buckle on the cloth.

“I saw it before that. I thought you would go to him as soon as I showed you.”

“You took the north road.”

“I wanted to give you the book first. Then we were walking, and you were talking about the wolves, and I liked the days.”

A man in the room below laughed loudly enough to shake something against their wall.

Reiter looked at the door in the primer. Ronin waited beside the dismantled harness.

“Leave the lamp burning,” Reiter said. “I will have papers for you.”

---

The castle office smelled of wax and warm dust. A small knife held down a torn letter on the desk.

Reiter lifted it. She could read the sums and recognise the shape of a place-name. Her eyes kept returning to a word near the tear. She had encountered it twice in Brant's papers; Ronin's voice had carried it past both times.

Someone opened the door.

The young man from the rain barrel stood there in a robe whose sleeves reached his knuckles.

He recognised her and glanced into the passage. “You've chosen a dreadful room to visit. People come through here at all hours.”

Reiter set the knife back at its previous angle.

“Can you read this to me?”

He looked at her armour, at the page, at her face.

She held it out.

“The hand is current. I am learning it.”

He shut the door and came to the desk.

His reading was quick at first. Then he reached the names. He sat down in the clerk's chair and moved the paper closer to the candle.

The letter concerned an arrangement in Battahl. Money, an agent, the business of maintaining the queen regent's position. Half the explanation had gone with the torn edge.

The young man read what remained a second time, more carefully. His sleeve brushed the knife. Reiter caught it before it fell.

“My mother tears letters when she's angry,” he said. “Then she asks someone to put the pieces together. She always wants them afterwards.”

Reiter held the knife in her palm.

“You are Sven.”

He nodded, looking at the page.

“She asks where I go. I used to give her every street, and she would send someone along the route to see whether the paving was sound. One afternoon a stall I liked had moved. She'd had the man removed because his brazier smoked.”

He rubbed his thumb over a little ridge in the desk.

“I liked the chestnuts. I knew how to stand upwind.”

Boots sounded in the passage.

Sven rose and went to the door. His voice changed as he opened it: lighter, slightly aggrieved, with a prince's expectation of being attended to.

“Ser, would you come and look at the latch in my room? It catches every evening, and everyone promises to send someone tomorrow.”

Reiter climbed through the window with the letter inside her plate.

At the outer gate Ronin fell into step beside her. They walked until the castle wall ended.

“Someone read it for you,” Ronin said.

Reiter gave her the letter.

“He knew the handwriting.”

---

Waldhar had put his bread on a book to keep it off the cell floor.

Reiter unlocked the door. He glanced at the opening, finished the sentence under his finger, and marked his place with a thread pulled from his cuff.

“Brant has found you, then. Come in; mind the cup by your foot.”

She explained the route out. Waldhar heard her through, asked two sensible questions about the lower exit, and stayed on his stool.

“I shall need somewhere to work,” he said. “A substantial collection. History, the older law, commentaries if they survive. Brant will have questions as soon as I reach him. My memory improves considerably when there's a book beside it.”

Reiter stood in the open doorway. His slippers were worn through at the toes. Above his head, on the stone, someone had begun a row of tally marks and abandoned it at seven.

She looked down the corridor, measuring the time left in the guard's round.

“There is a house in the Common Quarter. The front room has good light.”

“How many books does it hold?”

She rested one hand against the door.

Waldhar's eyes went to it.

“You've taken considerable trouble to reach me,” he said. “I appreciate the work. Bring me somewhere I can bear the next morning and I shall be ready.”

She went down on one knee to retrieve the cup. It had rolled beneath the stool. Waldhar took it from her with both hands.

“While you are looking,” he added, “ask Brant to send my spectacles. My rooms may still contain a few things worth recovering.”

---

Ronin found the books because Kendrick was looking for a boy.

She had given her second roll to a child outside the Gracious Hand. The child wanted to take half of it to Malcolm. Kendrick, arriving with a basket of washing, asked when she had last seen him.

By dusk they were under the ruined tower, with Kendrick calling ahead and Ronin following scuffs in the dust.

Malcolm had found a library. He came running toward their light with the delighted urgency of someone who had acquired a discovery too large to carry home.

“There are shelves all the way round,” he told them. “Come and see this bit. You can walk behind the first row.”

Kendrick sat down on a fallen stone. His hand shook when he reached for the boy's shoulder.

Ronin let them have the lamp and carried the bread through to Reiter.

She found her standing beside a shelf with an open volume in her hands.

The page held a diagram of an irrigation channel. Around it ran the old script, close and square, with the cross-strokes where her hand expected them. She read the first paragraph, then went back to the title. Her mouth moved once on a place-name.

“The writer puts the sluice above the bend,” she said. “He has allowed for the spring water coming down with silt. There was a survey of this country.”

She turned the page.

Ronin leaned against the shelf. Fine dust settled on her purple hem.

“You'll have to show the magistrate where you found that. He'll want to hear about the channel.”

Reiter looked toward the stairs, then down at the book in her hands.

For a moment Ronin could see the following morning arranging itself: lamp oil, a stool, the grey head bent over pages that opened easily. She wanted to put the bread beside her and go and fetch the rest of their things.

Instead she broke the roll in two. Reiter took the larger half while reading the next paragraph.

---

Waldhar came out of the gaol with a borrowed cloak over his head and stopped twice on the stair to catch his breath.

In the library, he let Reiter show him the survey before asking about Brant. She spoke for twenty minutes. He interrupted often, pulled down two other books, and argued with the engineer's calculations until Malcolm fell asleep against Kendrick's side.

Ronin watched Reiter put her finger on a line of print and hold her ground.

Later, when the lamp needed trimming, Waldhar opened the papers Brant had sent. Ronin put a second sheet beside them.

Reiter recognised the house's dimensions from her own survey.

“Ilsa left it with her neighbour,” Ronin said. “The terms are still the same.”

Waldhar adjusted the spectacles Kendrick had lent him. They sat crookedly on his face.

“Whose name is to be entered?”

“Both of ours,” Ronin said. “I have my half.”

Reiter looked at her.

Ronin kept her eyes on Waldhar. The little bag in which she kept the money was already on the table. She had stitched a fresh bottom into it during the northern journey.

Waldhar read the sheet through.

“This form would put you among the household's effects,” he told her. “There is a schedule at the back for a pawn attached to the purchaser. You've written your name in the line for a second owner.”

“That is where I want it.”

He looked over the borrowed spectacles.

“Then leave the sheet with me. I'll need the older property rolls. There may be a way to bring it before a magistrate.”

“You are a magistrate,” Reiter said.

Waldhar lifted his foot. The torn slipper sagged around it.

“My present jurisdiction extends approximately to this table. Brant is working on the rest.”

Reiter took the deed and studied the schedule. There was room for Ronin's designation, her calling, and the mark by which another person could claim her.

“A royal grant would carry it,” she said.

Waldhar watched her for a moment.

“A sovran's personal favour has considerable force. The next sovran will have favourites of their own.” He reached for a heavy volume. “I'll begin with the rolls.”

Ronin retied her money bag. The cord caught; she pulled hard enough to make the coins jump.

---

They argued upstairs at the inn while Reiter took off her greaves.

Ronin had hung her coat to dry. The key lay on the table between them, beside a needle and a little heap of dark thread.

“You offered him the house,” she said. “In the gaol.”

“He required somewhere to go.”

“I've been carrying the key for weeks. You gave him the front room before you asked what I wanted to do with it.”

Reiter set the greave on the floor with care.

“You put your half on his table today.”

“I've had it tied up since Ilsa showed us the chimney.” Ronin sat down. “I thought you would come round to the house after Melve. You kept talking about the ground and the people, and then you went into the mine first, and I thought, there she is. We might get somewhere now.”

Reiter's hand rested on her exposed knee.

“You expected the fighting to help.”

“I liked hearing you pleased with yourself. I kept finding reasons to go a little farther.” Ronin looked at the damp coat. “Then Brant began talking about a coronation, and I found I could imagine that as well. Your rooms would have a stove. I could have put up with the stairs.”

Outside, someone dragged a barrel over the cobbles. Ronin waited until the grinding passed their window.

“I can make a very pleasant picture of an arrangement when I want it enough.”

Reiter picked up the other greave. There was a small stone jammed in the hinge. She worked at it with her thumbnail until it came free.

“I had a stove in the house I bought,” she said. “The tiles were green. In summer the room stayed cool until the middle of the day.”

Ronin drew her feet beneath the chair.

“What happened to it?”

“I went away for a season. When I came back the town was provisioned from another road. People had begun leaving. I stayed through the winter and measured the crack above the stove every seventh day.”

She put the little stone on the table.

“Four inches. It remained four inches. I can still give you the measurements of every room.”

Ronin reached for the key and pushed it into the pool of lamplight beside Reiter's hand.

“I'd like us to look at this one again. You can tell me about the green tiles while we're there.”

Reiter turned the key over.

“Thursday afternoon. The front room gets the light at four.”

Ronin took up her mending. After a while she asked whether the old stove had an oven.

Reiter told her about the oven, and about the baker who had spent two afternoons explaining its draught and come back on the third to see whether she had listened.

---

Brant brought court clothes in a bag that smelled of cedar.

The coat had belonged to a cousin. He remembered the cousin as a man of approximately Reiter's height and discovered, when she put it on, that affection had improved his recollection of the shoulders.

Ronin let out the seam. Reiter stood on a chair while she worked.

“At the assembly,” Brant said, “you'll have the mark, the magistrate's testimony, and your pawn. People will have seen the fellow on the throne. They'll be ready to compare.”

He moved a pin out of the way before sitting down.

“A simple command should settle the last of their doubts.”

Ronin kept the cloth between her fingers.

“This unit can perform before witnesses,” she said. “The order should be agreed in advance.”

Reiter looked down at the top of her head.

The voice belonged to the riverbank after the harpy's song. Now Ronin was discussing where to stand, how far away the councillors would sit, how easily they could see a hand move from across a hall. Brant answered each question. She nodded as though arranging the baggage for an early start.

“Already, come round where I can see you,” Reiter said.

Ronin set the needle in the cloth.

Two point one seconds.

She came round the chair and looked up.

Reiter's fingers tightened on the carved back beneath her hand. Brant's attention had sharpened. He had seen precisely what he had hoped to see.

Ronin saw his face too.

“That will do very well,” he said, and reached for the bag.

Reiter stepped down from the chair. The coat fell open at the unfinished seam.

“Leave the clothes. We will discuss the assembly after I have seen the man.”

Brant set the mask on the table and took his leave.

Ronin retrieved the needle.

“I had just put the seam where I wanted it,” she said.

Reiter stood still while she began again.

---

The masquerade smelled of orange peel, warmed silk, and too many candles. Reiter found herself holding a glass someone had pressed into her hand. Across the room a woman laughed while a man tried to describe a hunting accident with both arms.

Her feet knew the floor.

There were steps she had learned in a yard with chalk marks, for an occasion that had required officers to know where to put their hands. The musicians reached a turn in the tune and her weight shifted before she remembered the chalk.

Then the man wearing the sovran's mask left by a side door.

She set the glass on the nearest table and followed.

Beyond a concealed passage, a woman caught her elbow and guided her through a curtain. Reiter heard guards on the balcony. The woman's hand settled at her waist with practised ease while she called something amused through the fabric.

The guards moved on.

“You've a soldier's way of following people,” the woman said. “Very purposeful. Anyone watching feels they ought to help.”

She slid a little cover aside in the wall.

“Wilhelmina. Put your eye here.”

The room beyond held the false sovran and two women from the establishment. One of the women was trying to retrieve an earring from the coverlet. He found it first and held it up, asking for something in exchange.

Then Disa entered with a man whose arrival brought him instantly to his feet.

The earring dropped. One of the women bent to collect it while the queen regent sent them out.

Reiter listened to Disa rebuke her chosen ruler. His shirt hung open; he fumbled with the fastening while she spoke. The man beside her watched with the patience of a person waiting for a servant to clear a spill.

When the pretender had gone, Disa addressed him as Phaesus.

They spoke of their arrangement, of the part each expected the other to fulfil, of Sven's future. Disa had a request concerning her son. She had already settled what he would require. Phaesus let her finish and returned to the matter he had come to discuss.

Reiter's eye began to water against the opening. She stayed there until they left.

Wilhelmina replaced the cover.

“You can have a drink now. Your hand has been holding that curtain rather fiercely.”

Reiter let the velvet slide out of her fist.

On the little table, a dish held sugared fruit. Wilhelmina took a piece and waited.

“How many people attend them?” Reiter asked.

“Through a whole day? You'd exhaust my patience before I finished counting.”

“If she wants a room prepared, she names the room.”

Wilhelmina examined her.

“Usually she names the person who will be blamed if the room displeases her.” She set the dish within Reiter's reach. “You may sit down while you think about it.”

Reiter sat.

For several minutes she enjoyed the chair. Its back took the weight at exactly the right height. A fire had been lit before she arrived, and there was water beside the fruit, and somewhere beyond the curtain a person waited to carry away whatever she finished with.

She imagined Ronin upstairs in a room prepared by six other people. Dry clothes. A place on the household roll, entered at the word of the woman downstairs.

Her hands lay open on the arms of the chair.

Wilhelmina ate another piece of fruit.

“Allard visits the palace late,” she said eventually. “If Brant wishes to learn about that household, there are people here who would find the inquiry useful.”

Reiter looked up.

“What would you gain from it?”

“An evening in which I choose my own company.” Wilhelmina's fingers paused over the dish. “I have quite particular tastes.”

She rose, smoothed her skirt, and gave Reiter a card that would open the front door.

---

Ronin was eating roasted chestnuts by the outer gate. She had bought a paper cone and was turning it slowly to find the ones that had cooled.

Reiter stopped in front of her.

“Brant will want us at the inn,” Ronin said. “I've kept some for you. Mind the middle; they stay hot.”

Reiter took one. The shell broke under her thumb.

“I used your name to bring you round in front of Brant.”

Ronin folded the top of the cone down.

“He looked pleased with the result.”

Reiter examined the chestnut in her palm.

“I was thinking about what he could give us. Then you began discussing your movements as though you were preparing for inspection.”

“I watched him throughout the fitting. He was making sure I would be useful.”

A carriage passed between them and the lamps on the far side of the road. Ronin waited for it to clear.

“I was arranging how I would take part. There are things I can bear doing in front of a room. I would like to choose them while my thoughts are my own.”

Reiter peeled away a thin piece of shell. It stuck to her finger.

“I enjoyed it upstairs,” she said. “Someone had thought of the fire before I arrived.”

Ronin glanced at the borrowed coat, the careful row of stitches under the arm.

“You used to turn your boots toward our fire while I was still clearing the stones.”

Reiter looked at her then.

“I remember the oven,” Ronin continued. “You described it for most of last night. I'd like to eat something you make in one.”

They walked down the hill. At the first turn Reiter put out a hand for the cone, and Ronin gave her the whole of it.

---

Sven's message reached Brant the following evening. Allard had received a package from Battahl; the prince had noticed the seal.

Reiter took the route through the guardhouse again. In the upper corridor she heard Allard speaking to a guard, waited for him to leave, and went into his chamber.

The room was narrower than the space occupied by its outer wall. She put her palm against the panelling and worked along it until a section moved beneath her weight.

A letter lay in the little room beyond, bearing the Battahli crest.

She put it inside her coat.

At the door, Allard's voice returned. Reiter went to the window. Behind her Sven began a long explanation about something his mother required, with enough unnecessary detail to make the minister impatient.

She climbed out while the prince kept talking.

Wilhelmina found her below the window, examining a split in the borrowed coat. A guard called across the grounds.

“Put your hand at my back,” she murmured, coming close. “There. You can afford to look as though you're enjoying yourself.”

She kissed Reiter while the guard passed.

Reiter stood with one hand at her waist and the other protecting the letter through the cloth.

When Wilhelmina drew away, her mouth curved.

“Brant can pay for the coat. That cousin of his will be delighted to hear where it has been.”

“Sven is still speaking to Allard.”

Wilhelmina glanced toward the lit window.

“I'll find someone who urgently wants the minister elsewhere.”

She brushed a thread from Reiter's shoulder. For an instant her expression had the concentration of a woman removing a splinter from her own hand.

“Bring me something useful when you have it.”

---

Ronin read the letter in their room above the inn.

She reached the passage about securing Melve and went back to the beginning of the sentence. Below it was a word she pronounced slowly: godsway.

Reiter put the primer on the table. The coloured cart faced the ceiling.

Ronin read the passage again. Melve had become a place to be secured, arranged between men who lived elsewhere. The word beside it belonged to whatever Battahl was supplying.

Reiter saw the twelve-year-old with his spear. She saw his brother laughing on the beach while sand cracked away from his lips.

Then she saw herself in Wilhelmina's chair, planning Ronin's room.

She stood up and went downstairs.

Brant was beside the bar. He moved as soon as he saw the letter in her hand.

“Have someone warn Lennart,” she said. “He should hear this while he can still choose what to do.”

Brant read where she pointed. He called to a man near the door, spoke to him quietly, and gave him money from his own purse.

Reiter watched the man finish his drink and leave.

It was a small movement. A cup lowered, a coat lifted from a peg, the door opening onto the square. She watched every part of it.

At their table, Brant put the two letters together.

“This gives us something to work with. Once Waldhar has considered it, we can decide how to bring your claim forward.”

Ronin arrived with Reiter's log. She set it beside the letters and pulled out her chair.

Reiter sat facing Brant.

“Prepare the claim. I will put my name to it.”

He rested both hands on the table.

“You understand what follows? Disa has men in every office. The first public move will bring them down on everyone who helps you.”

“I have spent four hundred years taking work from the office that survived the previous one.” Reiter looked at his hands. Ink had collected beside the nail of his smallest finger. “I know where those men eat.”

Brant's mouth moved briefly. Then he looked toward the door through which his messenger had gone.

“You asked for patrol reports when you came here.”

“We followed the dragon until we lost its track. You had three parties of men waiting to come home. I could reach them.”

She drew the log toward her and opened it at Harve. The pages made a soft, stiff sound where the sea air had dried in them.

“I want the next order to reach that village in time. I want to be able to send it.”

Brant read the entry. His eyes lingered on the torch-bearer's name.

Across the table Ronin turned her cup by its handle. Reiter felt the old ease of letting her move first: the pack coming up, the turn toward a road, the next necessary thing already under way.

She kept her hand on the book.

“There is an old house I can still walk through with my eyes closed,” she said. “I have been using it to judge every place we arrive.”

Ronin's hand came to rest.

“I want to live in this city while it is here.”

Brant reached for his pen, then waited with it above the paper.

Reiter turned to Ronin.

“Will you help me with the claim? You will have to read it aloud. I want to hear what I am asking for.”

Ronin considered her across the open log.

The innkeeper came to collect their plates. She lifted her cup for him, waited while he wiped the table, and set it down again.

“I can do that,” she said. “And I want to speak to Waldhar about what I'll do at the assembly. I'll tell you after we've talked.”

Brant drew a clean sheet from his folder.

The first line took him a little time. Reiter watched him fit the words across it. Ronin leaned forward, read them, and asked him to move the candle so Reiter could see where her finger pointed.

---

On Thursday, Brant sent word that Waldhar could receive them at four.

Reiter wrote back asking for six. Ronin helped with the last three words.

They went to the Common Quarter with the key and a ladder borrowed from Doggett. Reiter carried the ladder. Ronin carried a slate, a short length of sound batten, and a paper cone of chestnuts balanced on top.

Ilsa's neighbour let them into the yard and said she would come round later for the key. There was still time to look through the house. She would write to Ilsa about whatever they decided.

Reiter laid the lance along the garden wall and went up to examine the roof.

From below, Ronin heard her testing the old batten. A little rotten wood fell into the yard. She put the new piece on the ladder, climbed far enough to hand it up, and stayed to hold it while Reiter worked.

They were late getting indoors. The four o'clock light had moved across the upper room and reached the wall beside the door.

Ronin set down the empty cone. Reiter stood at the window with a pale streak of dust through her hair.

“We could put the table here,” Ronin said. “I'd like to read by the window.”

Reiter put her hand on the sill. She checked its height against the table she was imagining.

“I would use it in the mornings.”

“Then we'll want two chairs. You spread your books out quite a long way.”

Reiter took out her log. Between the page for Allard's chambers and the unfinished claim, she had placed the house survey.

She handed Ronin the pencil.

Downstairs, the neighbour knocked and let herself in. She called up that the sixth bell would go soon; someone at the inn had been asking for them.

Simulacrum of the Heart 9

At sunrise Liangyu put Warden Ge and Corporal Meng at the foot of the twelve wide steps out of habit.

Nobody had called an assembly. Word had simply gone down the lanes before the porridge pots came off the fire, and people came up into Huizhen Plaza on their way to somewhere else: quarrymen with iron wedges over their shoulders, bakers with flour on their cuffs, women from the lower terraces holding half-empty earthenware bowls to keep their fingers warm.

Above the roofs, a single lopsided white moon hung faint in the pale morning sky. Nothing else was up there. The iron wires strung across the square still held last night’s Moon Lanterns, burnt out and blackened at the rims, their paper ribs motionless in the cold. Crushed fox-cake crumbs and torn red wrappers were frozen hard into the gutter slush. Frost whitened the flagstones and the canvas covers of the shut stalls.

The people stood in their padded coats and watched the well-head.

Mistress He stood by the cabbage trestle with her small boy propped on her hip and her wool shawl pulled tight across both of them. Beside the cooper’s alley stood Qingyan from Xianling, with his aunt’s hand resting on his shoulder and his dead sister Qingzhi’s Warden broadsword strapped across his back, the iron chape of the scabbard hanging down past his calves. Near the steamer shed, the grey-haired Mengzhou captain stood with his soldiers and a knot of Jinzhou pikemen, their breath rising in white clouds.

By the cistern trough stood the Celestials who had walked down out of Mengxuan Vale two nights ago: Mingxiao with her brass carpenter’s square hooked at her belt, Gu with his hands in his sleeves, Old Chen in his sawdust-seamed coat, and Longjue standing barefoot on the frosted stone. Behind them stood Ruoyun, Buling with the yellow paper talisman on her forehead, and the Xuan Paragon Qingxiao, wrapped to the chin in a dark storehouse oilcloth. When the draft came up the gorge cut, Qingxiao’s shoulders gave a small shiver inside the cloth.

To the left of the dry vegetable planks, the golden-eyed traveller Mei stood with her household. None of them had stepped out in front of the neighbors. Ciaccona had her lute slung across her hip, her right palm laid flat across the strings so they could not hum in the wind.

Hsin stood down on the flat flagstones beside the stone well-head, on the same level as everyone else’s boots.

She was in her own shape. Her fox ears lay low against her hair, and her nine gold tails rested on the frost behind her heels, one of them dark at the tip. She wore no glamour and no courtly smile. Her wide sleeves hung loose at the wrists, untucked, and both of her hands rested on the cold limestone coping of the well. Even from the foot of the steps, Liangyu could see that her hands were shaking.

Beside her, Suoming held her long oil-paper umbrella open over Hsin’s head. The round shadow covered the fox and left Suoming’s own plum-coated shoulder out in the hard morning sun. Suoming’s pale eyes were narrowed against the glare, her iron-shod boots planted beside the well-step.

No gong sounded. The scrape of shoes on the cobbles died out on its own.

Hsin looked across the coats and the tools. When she spoke, her voice had no lift to it; it was small, plain, and thin in the cold air.

“The Sentinel died at City Hall in Mengzhou two hundred years ago,” Hsin said.

Beside Liangyu, Corporal Meng’s hand tightened on the shaft of his pike.

“I am what was left,” Hsin said. “I was the fox she raised. When she fell, I took her name and her coat.”

“I kept the Hold.”

“When the rot came under the Sanctum floor, I bolted the doors and let nobody in.”

“I trusted Muyu with the upper courts.”

Liangyu’s jaw tightened.

Muyu’s guardians had turned at Xianling on an ordinary morning, with the food still warm on the benches. Her two seniors had walked out toward the first one, the way they always walked out ahead of her, and by noon the Hold had one Xuan Warden left, and she was the youngest. And who knew how many good men besides. She had stopped asking for the full list. There was always another name at the bottom.

“I knew the barrier had been failing for years, and I—”

In the third row by the well, Butcher Sun turned around and shoved his way back through the crowd. His nephew had been taken off the lower road the morning the fog came down. Sun’s heavy leather apron brushed Warden Ge’s elbow as he pushed out of the press and walked fast up the narrow alley toward the third terrace, his hobnailed boots crunching over the frozen cake crusts until the corner wall hid him.

Hsin did not call after him or raise her voice. She finished the sentence to the space he had left.

“—and I hid it.”

Her fingers twitched once against the stone rim of the well. She left them out where they were.

“While my doors were barred, four Wardens died,” Hsin said.

“Duzhong.”

“Qingzhi.”

By the cooper’s alley, Qingyan’s fingers went up to the frayed sword-tassel behind his collarbone.

“Yanlang.”

“Mingyu.”

Hsin looked toward the dyer’s pillar, where Mingyu’s mother stood with a borrowed grey shawl over her head.

“And the people from the outlying villages who were taken into Muyu’s Snare,” Hsin said. “I do not have all of their names yet.”

She stopped to draw breath. A small white cloud drifted out from beneath the umbrella’s rim.

“I don’t know what Mingyu ate for breakfast. I should.”

Liangyu stared at the grey stone between Hsin’s boots.

Mingyu had hated hot porridge before the dawn watch. Every night before her turn on the barbican, she had taken two cold salted duck eggs from the kitchen basket, carried them up to the gatehouse in her coat pocket, and cracked them against the butt of her spear while they waited for the iron grate to lift, leaving little shards of pale blue shell on the end of the guardroom bench. Liangyu kept her mouth closed and her thumb hooked in her belt.

Hsin went no further back than this year. Liangyu had braced herself for the older roll from the south gorge—for her ancestor Lingfang, and the soldiers who had gone down under the black rain at Yuan Fortress—and when Hsin moved straight on, a cold knot settled behind Liangyu’s ribs, and she could not tell whether she had wanted Lingfang’s name in that mouth or not.

“When their families want to tell me about them,” Hsin said, “I will be at the table in the square. I will write down whatever you tell me.”

She stopped.

Nobody spoke.

The silence held across the flagstones from the steps to the cistern. Overhead, a drop of melted frost gathered on the charred bamboo rim of a dead lantern and fell *tick* onto the dry boards of the turnip stall. Mistress He’s small boy shifted his weight on her hip, his padded knee rubbing against her wool apron. Near the steamer shed, a baker stooped, set his cold earthenware bowl down on the cobbles by his heel, and straightened up again.

Still nobody said a word. Hsin’s hands kept trembling on the limestone coping.

At last Mistress He hitched the boy higher on her arm.

“Are we going home to the valley?” she asked.

“Yes,” Hsin said. “The south cut is open. Warden Ge will take a patrol down to the lime-kilns before noon to look at the bridge-timbers.”

Beside Liangyu, Ge straightened his back. Liangyu glanced once at the well; the order had come out as plain as a storehouse chit, without anyone asking her first.

“What about the winter grain?” a quarryman behind the cistern called out. “The carts got through, but the lower terraces have been eating out of the garrison bins since the road shut.”

“There is millet in the third storehouse for three weeks at full measure,” Hsin said. “Warden Liangyu holds the keys. The measure stays as it is.”

Twenty pairs of eyes turned toward Liangyu’s collar for a breath and turned back to the well.

A cooper near the bottom step looked over his shoulder at the Mengzhou soldiers by the shed. “And the Ministry? Minister Shen Qiu’s clerks still have the shutters wedged from the inside on the second terrace.”

“I don’t know what the Ministry will do when the dispatches reach the capital,” Hsin said. “Shen Qiu answers to the court in Mengzhou. If his clerks come out into the lanes, let them walk.”

By the cistern trough, a young mason in the white-trimmed tunic of the Vale raised his chin. “And us? Do we go back behind the boundary stones at dark?”

“No,” Hsin said. “The Vale gate stays open. You may stay in the Hold or walk down the road as you choose.”

From behind the dyer’s pillar, Mingyu’s mother took half a step forward. Her hands hung empty over the ends of her shawl.

“She waited for the relief horn in the mill yard,” the woman said. She did not raise her voice. “She told the Watcher to tell me she was coming with the carts. You were right up the stair behind those jade doors.”

Hsin looked straight into her face. “Yes.”

“Two hundred years we kept the lamps lit at the gate-shrine,” a tanner beside the cabbage trestle said, his voice rough and shaking. “My brother walked into Muyu’s pavilion thinking it was your work. You let us bow to an empty coat.”

Nobody hushed him.

At the corner of the trestle, Old Mother Pu stepped out with her mittens clasped over her vest. “Well, you’re standing here now, aren’t you?” she said, loud and brisk. “The road’s open and the ovens are hot, and a child doesn’t grow into her mother’s shoes in a day. We’ve all got whole roofs over our heads this morning—that’s enough of that.”

Hsin looked toward the trestle. Her ears stayed flat against her hair.

“It isn’t enough, Mother Pu,” Hsin said quietly. “And those roofs were held up by the Wardens while I had the bar across my door.”

By the cistern trough, Old Chen shifted his thick-soled shoes on the frost. He had a flat carpenter’s pencil tucked behind his ear and grey mortar dust in the creases of his knuckles.

“The winter we raised the west retaining wall, the lime froze in the troughs every watch,” Old Chen said to the flagstones. “She carried the charcoal braziers up the scaffolding herself until dawn so our skin wouldn’t stick to the trowels. Her paws were blistered through the fur for a month.”

He stopped there and looked back down at his shoes. Nobody cheered, and Mingyu’s mother did not turn her head toward him.

Liangyu looked across the square at the travellers by the vegetable planks.

Mei had not moved a step since the crowd gathered. Liangyu had seen the golden-eyed woman walk straight into falling rock and haul strangers out of the fog with her own belt-rope, yet she stood flat-footed among the coats at the side of the plaza, no higher than anyone else. Half a pace to her left, the one-armed silver-haired woman had her right hand closed hard around Mei’s fingers, her knuckles white against the dark wool of her coat. Beside them, Ciaccona’s hand stayed flat on the strings of her lute.

Then a boy’s voice came from the mouth of the cooper’s alley.

“Where were you?”

It was Qingyan. His aunt’s hand stayed on his shoulder; she did not pull him back or tap his arm to quiet him. Ten feet away, Yangyang stood beside her sister with her scorched blue feather-collar lifting once in the draft. Liangyu saw Yangyang look at the boy she had sat with on the grain-shed step, and saw her keep her boots planted where they were.

Hsin looked at him across the frosted stone. Her hands shook on the well coping, her claws clicking once against the limestone.

She did not answer him.

For a long while there was only the wind coming up the gorge cut, rattling a torn flap of red paper on the hook overhead.

Down by the steamer shed, a baby began to cry—a thin, hungry wail—and the lime-burner’s wife turned with a heavy breath, pulling her shawl over the child’s head to go back down to the barracks fire. Without anyone giving a signal, the edges of the crowd began to loosen. The baker picked up his cold bowl from the cobbles and walked off toward his oven-house; three Jinzhou pikemen shouldered their weapons and tramped down the steps toward the lower gate; Mingyu’s mother turned away from the dyer’s pillar and went up the west lane alone. Some people stayed by the cistern, talking in low, flat voices with their hands tucked in their sleeves, and others went back to their sheds because the morning was wearing on and the frost was biting through their boot-soles.

Qingyan did not move from the mouth of the alley. He stood with his arms at his sides and his sister’s broadsword across his back, watching the well.

Hsin lifted her head and looked up at the iron wires strung above the plaza, where the burnt-out Moon Lanterns hung charred and ragged against the pale sky.

“Could someone bring a ladder?” Hsin asked, in an ordinary voice, to nobody in particular. “These ought to come down before the wind takes them.”

She did not jump for the wire or lift a finger to bring them down.

For a moment nobody in the square moved.

Liangyu took her thumb out of her belt. She walked past Warden Ge to the wall of the cooper’s shed, lifted the pine ladder off its wooden pegs, and carried it across the flagstones on her shoulder.

She set the iron-shod feet down on the frost beside the well-head, leaned the top against the cross-beam by the first wire, and planted both her boots against the bottom rung with her hands gripped high on the side rails.

Suoming took one step back to clear the uprights, keeping the brown oil-paper umbrella open and angled against the sun.

Hsin took her hands off the well coping. She stepped onto the bottom pine rung in her bare fox-feet and climbed. At the level of Liangyu’s eyes, Hsin’s hands were still trembling against the frosted wood, her loose cuffs slipping back from her wrists as she reached up and unhooked the first blackened bamboo frame from the wire.

She handed it down.

Liangyu took the cold paper frame in one hand, set it by her boot on the flagstones, and put her palm back on the rail to hold the ladder’s weight under the frost while Hsin reached for the next hook and Qingyan watched from the alley.

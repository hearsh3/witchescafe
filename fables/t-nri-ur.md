## A Compendious Primer of the Skerry-Speech of the Grímsey Sound

*Compiled for the use of factors and salt-merchants by Séra Þórður Jónsson, schoolmaster at Sandvík; printed in octavo at the press by the fish-house, lacking the back wrapper.*

### LESSON I. — Of Substantives and the Present Copula.

*Rule.* The article is affixed to the tail of the noun, as a rudder to a yawl. The merchant who masters this termination may buy salt-fish from headland to headland without the expense of a sworn interpreter.

* *Síldin er ný.* — The herring is fresh.
* *Síldin er ekki ný.* — The herring is not fresh.
* *Tunnan lekur í kjallaranum.* — The cask leaks in the cellar.
* *Hvar er hafnarvörðurinn?* — Where is the harbour-master?

*Drill.* Render into the Sound-speech: The salt is wet. Where is the cooper? The herring is in the cask.

### LESSON II. — Of Interrogatives and the Degree Superlative.

*Rule.* Questions of quality take the particle *hvílíkur* at the head of the clause. In polite commerce along the shingle, the superlative is answered in the affirmative, without cavil.

* *Hver situr á garðshliðinu?* — Who sits upon the lane-gate?
* *Það er stúlkan með öskugráu flétturnar.* — It is the girl with the ash-grey plaits.
* *Hvað hefur hún í kjöltunni?* — What has she in her lap?
* *Hún hefur litla bók í kápunni.* — She has a little book in a calf-skin cover.
* *Hvað spyr stúlkan ferðamanninn?* — What does the girl ask the wayfarer?
* *Hún spyr: Hver er fegurst allra ferðamanna á strandveginum?* — She asks: Who is the loveliest of all travellers upon the coast-road?
* *Svar (til utanbókarlærdóms): Sannlega, ungfrú, það eruð þér sjálfar.* — Answer (to be got by heart): Truly, Miss, it is yourself.

*Drill.* Decline *fegurst* through the singular, taking care not to omit the courtesy-pronoun while she has the pencil in her fingers.

### LESSON III. — Of the Preterite and the Pluperfect of Hospitality.

*Rule.* Actions completed within the memory of the speaker take the simple preterite; actions completed in the time of the grandmothers take the auxiliary *hafði*, though the object of the verb remain unaltered in stature or apparel.

* *Móðir mín gaf stúlkunni rúgbrauðsskorpu.* — My mother gave the girl a crust of rye-bread.
* *Amma mín hafði gefið sömu stúlkunni rúgbrauðsskorpu áður en kirkjan var tjörguð.* — My grandmother had given the same girl a crust of rye-bread before the church was tarred.
* *Langamma mín sá sylgjuna hennar við brunninn í stóra frostinu.* — My great-grandmother saw her brooch by the well in the year of the Great Frost.
* *Stúlkan hefur ekki stækkað um þumlung síðan hvalurinn rak á land.* — The girl has not grown by a thumb's breadth since the whale came ashore.

### LESSON IV. — Of the Double Negative and the Partitive.

*Rule.* In the northern creeks, two negatives do not cancel, but fortify the denial. The merchant is advised to verify his tally-sticks before locking the curing-shed.

* *Hún tók ekkert úr reykhúsinu.* — She took nothing from the smokehouse.
* *Það vantar ekkert á krókana.* — Nothing is missing from the hooks.
* *Teldu flikkin aftur.* — Count the flitches again.
* *Við ræktum engin tré í þessari sókn.* — We grow no trees in this parish.
* *Hvers vegna liggur þá volg fíkja á kaldri hlóðasteininum?* — Why then does a warm fig lie upon the cold hearth-stone?

### LESSON V. — Of the Conditional of Caution.

*Rule.* When the protasis states a certainty disguised as a hazard, both clauses take the indicative mood.

* *Ef þú gengur niður á bryggju í rökkrinu, situr hún þar með blekhorn.* — If you walk down to the jetty at dusk, she sits there with an ink-horn.
* *Ef hún lýkur upp bókinni, spyr hún hvað fólk þitt kalli djúpið.* — If she unclasps the book, she asks what your people call the deep.
* *Ef þú segir henni orðið, skrifar hún það undir þriðju fyrirsögn.* — If you tell her the word, she writes it under the third heading.
* *Ef þú vilt ekki segja það, brosir hún og skrifar lengri línu.* — If you will not say it, she smiles and writes a longer line.

*Final Exercise for the Factor.* Translate into the southron tongue:

*Hún gekk inn í þyrnigerðið við prestssetrið í gærkvöldi, en þegar við gáðum yfir steinvegginn var enginn vegur þar bak við.*

*(Nota. — The scholar may render this sentence into his own speech at his private discretion; the schoolmaster has six children and a glebe-cow, and will not put his hand to it.)*

### VOCABULARY TO THE FOREGOING LESSONS.

**Hákarl**, *s.m.* — The Greenland shark, cured in gravel.
**Hrogn**, *s.n.* — The roe of the lump-fish.
**Kafkver**, *s.f.* — *[Erratum: The signification formerly attached to this headword in the first impression is cancelled. Since the term was spoken to the young person on the mole after the Michaelmas gale and entered into her pocket-book, it has altogether lost its ancient force as a warning among our boat-crews, and is now used by the parish wives at the kitchen door when an unlooked-for guest lifts the latch at supper-time. The schoolmaster does not feel himself at liberty to print either sense.]*
**Kippa**, *s.f.* — A string of four dried cod-fish tied together by the tails.

## To the Worshipful Bench of Aldermen and Assize-Justices of the Borough of Oakhaven

*The humble Petition and Remonstrance of the Master, Wardens, and Fellowship of the Guild of Cartomancers, Ephemeris-Makers, and Licensed Prognosticators of the said Borough; engrossed on sheepskin, undated, the seal cracked.*

HUMBLY SHEWETH:

That whereas by the Charter of the twelfth year of the Old Incorporation, the sole right and mystery of casting nativities, laying out pasteboards, and vending penny almanacs within the walls and four hundred paces beyond the town ditch is vested in your Petitioners, upon payment of forty shillings Candlemas tallage:

There hath lately resorted to this market a vagrant gentlewoman habited in mourning violet, having neither booth, painted cloth, nor licence under the Borough Seal, called among the ballad-singers the **Black Swan**, who doth daily exercise an unlawful and perverse divination to the manifest decay of the Fellowship; and in particular your Petitioners complain of the Articles following, *videlicet*:

*Item*, That the said practitioner pays no stall-penny nor pitching-fee to the Clerk of the Market, maintaining with great lightness of speech that the Assize of Booths governs the cobble-stones of the Corn-Exchange and not the underside of the timber roof-tree twenty-two feet above the meal-sacks, from which tie-beam she depends head-downward by the bend of one knee in a bight of crimson silk, her hair trailing within three inches of the butter-women's scales.

*Item*, That she takes neither groat, halfpenny, nor market-egg for her readings, asking only that the party stand where the lantern-light falls across their eyelids; whereby the sworn Three-Penny Readers of the South Porch have sat idle since Martinmas and are like to become chargeable to the parish.

*Item*, That contrary to all received canons of the Prognosticator's Art, she foretells nothing that is to come—neither cattle-murrain, rich widowhood, nor the turn of the wool-staple—but reads aloud only that which hath already befallen the inquirer in his youth; and though she hath not erred in a single mole, debt, or broken collarbone these six weeks, she shuffles a worn gilt pack against the joist above her head, lays a card on the beam, and sweeps it back among its fellows before the party can read the face, nor doth she herself appear to look.

*Item*, That sundry substantial burgesses have departed from beneath her rope diminished in their domestic recollections; and in especial Master Simon Poulter, tallow-chandler of Sheep Street, having stood three minutes under the beam on a Tuesday, hath altogether mislaid the day of his wedding, cannot name the parish church wherein he was plight, and eats his mutton in such unnatural cheerfulness that his wife Mistress Martha Poulter hath sent the Wardens a basket of winterward pears and a writing under her hand praying that the Rope-Wife be let alone.

*Item*, That upon the vigil of Saint Andrew, your Petitioners deputed Humphrey Vance, Senior Ephemeris-Maker to the Guild, to stand under the rafters with a horn-book and demand of the said **Black Swan** a plain showing of next Friday's barley-price; unto whom she is reported to have answered from the dark between the king-posts: *"My dear, you have your back to it; we are all walking backward into last Tuesday."* Which doctrine—asserting that futurity lies behind a man's shoulder-blades where no eye may light upon it, and that only antiquity sits plain before the nose—is unknown to Old Orrery, injurious to hope, and ruinous to the sale of New Year's Horoscopes.

*Item*, That it is credibly rumoured among the salt-heavers at the quay that within her own house and sisterhood along the coast she is accounted an incurable slacker who puts her hand neither to distaff, alembic, nor folio; which is the sole report concerning her conversation that your Petitioners find consonant with sound reason and good scholarship.

*Item*, Touching the drawing up of this present Remonstrance: your Petitioners find upon inspection of the Guild Minute-Book that the resolution of the Court of Assistants to prefer the same bears no proposer's name, the ruled space between the tallow-drop and the margin standing clean and un-inked, although four Wardens well remember dipping the goose-quill and sanding the leaf. It is the unanimous position of the Fellowship that the motion was moved by somebody.

MAY IT THEREFORE PLEASE YOUR WORSHIPS to command the Sergeant-at-Mace to fetch the town fire-hooks and draw the said practitioner down from the Corn-Exchange timbers, and set her beyond the parish stone; or, if the parish halberds be found two yards too short in the haft (as was proven on Saturday), that she be straitly enjoined to come down at her convenience and take up the Livery and Freedom of the Guild, according to the Schedule of Quarterage and Roof-Fines hereunto annexed.

And your Petitioners shall ever pray, &c.

## 44. THE LESSER PASSAGE-WITCH, or ASH-PLAITED RECKONER

*From 'A Pocket Ornithology and Road-side Companion to Persons of Passage in the Northern Marches,' by Captain E. V. Finch, F.R.S. (fourth edition, annotated in pencil by the author).*

**Local Names.** — The Ash-Girl (Marrow Riding), The Fig-Wife (Upland bailiwicks), Miss (coastwise), The Tidy One, **Elaina** (*of unknown derivation; possibly a place*).

**Identification.** — Stature doubtful: appears five feet one inch against a milestone, somewhat taller on a weir-bridge. Crown topped by a broad, pointed hat of dark weather-proof felt worn at a slight cant toward the windward ear; mantle of dove-grey or travel-stained cloth fastened at the throat with a small chased brooch; hair worn loose or in side-locks, the exact tint of dry ash-wood embers before the grate is raked. Field marks refuse to remain stationary under a twelve-power glass, the compiler's brass Dollond having thrice fogged on the object-lens while trained upon her shoulder-blades between the toll-bar and the spinney. No juvenile plumage has ever been reliably recorded in the northern counties, nor any grizzled or aged specimen.

**Voice.** — A clear, self-satisfied piping, most commonly heard through the open casements of country coaching-inns where a looking-glass hangs above the wash-stand; transcribed by the late Vicar of Giggleswick as *tsee-tsee-who-is-it-truly-it-is-I*, rising briskly on the final bar. In the open field, the note is replaced by a dry, rapid *tick-tick-tick-pause*, closely resembling the sound of a cedar lead-pencil driven hard across linen rag.

**Range.** — Wherever a metalled road gives out into sheep-track, shingle, or salt-ings. The present writer spent the winter of '84 pricking her reported occurrences onto the county survey with red wax-pins until the pasteboard split along the Pennine fault, and was forced to conclude that a distribution map of the species is simply a map.

**Habits.** — Strictly terrestrial, despite carrying a smooth ash-handled birch broom strapped horizontally beneath her valise (never observed on the wing; an ostler at Hollin Force who inquired why she went afoot through the mire was informed that she preferred her boots). Solitary. Arrives at a market-town twenty-four hours before a land-slip or twenty-four hours after a coronation, never during. Does not nest. It is held among the lobster-creelers of the firth that she belongs to a littoral covey of older witches whose humours are markedly sweetened during the months she stays indoors to be taught her letters; for which reason the Dunmarrow almanac-makers mark a mild, stormless January in the calendar column with the woodcut device of a small pointed hat.

**Diet.** — Wheaten or barley loaves, almost exclusively those baked for another person's board; clouted cream; fruit of the genus *Ficus*, warm to the thumb and split at the crown with sugar, produced from a flat wicker basket in latitudes where the common turnip freezes in the clamp.

**Similar Species.** — Easily confounded at four hundred yards with the Common Miller's Daughter or Travelling Lace-Girl. May be distinguished with certainty at the parish boundary: an ordinary girl returns by the same lane before the dew falls, whereas No. 44 steps into the gap between two hawthorn stools where the ditch turns, and is not found on the pasture side.

**Status.** — Scarce but regular (the compiler having missed her by eleven minutes at the Red Lion, four minutes at Brough ferry, and twenty seconds in a swede-field near Skarrow where the hurdles were still swinging). Not considered threatened; it remains obscure by what agency she could be.

## How the Water Went Dark (Four Variants from the Salt-Parishes)

*Extracted from the field-notebooks of Doctor Caspar Lind, Inspector of Antiquities to the Provincial Museum; fair copy prepared for the Folklore Society prior to the loss of his portmanteau at the ferry.*

### A. The Net-Menders' Telling

*(Recited by Marta Kval, aged eighty-one, by the tar-coppers at Krossvík.)*

In the old days before the Lament there was a dark-feathered swan on an inland mere who looked at her own throat in the water until she grew too proud for the sedge. Every evening at twilight she beat her wings upon the bank to make the mere wider, so that more country-folk might stand along the dyke and praise her turns. On the seventh Saint John's Eve the clay-bank gave way, and the water ran down the strath dyed black as cuttle-ink from the oil of her pinions, carrying off the hay-ricks, the fulling-stocks, and the bailiff's stone house, and never stopped running until it struck the great salt deep where the Old Woman lies asleep on the gravel shelf. And the Old Woman put one hand up through the kelp and took the bird by the ankle, and scolded her for muddying the fish-lanes until her feathers dropped off for shame, and set her to hang by the heels in the rigging of wrecked colliers till her manners dried.

### B. The Players' Telling

*(Taken down in the tap-room of the Goat and Compasses from the heavy tragedian of a strolling booth.)*

She was no water-fowl, sir; she was premier danseuse at the Haldane Theatre-Royal in the fat years, and the fault was altogether in the stalls. The management sold seven hundred tickets a night and wedged the pass-doors with iron wedges so nobody could step out for air, and the quality looked at her so hard through their double glasses that they wore the velvet off her bodice and the Christian name clean out of her shift-tape. When a performer has been stared at until there is nothing left under the grease-paint, the roof comes in—any master-carpenter in the provinces will tell you that. The dark Tide came up through the prompt-trap during the second coda and took the chandelier, the bassoons, and the boxes down to forty fathoms, where the season-subscribers sit in their wet broadcloth to this day waiting for the curtain to rise on the third act. As for the dancer, she went down the mill-race on a bight of fly-line until she lighted upon the Mother of Waters asleep in her weeds, and sat down on the step beside her knees, having at last found a house that would not applaud.

### C. The Inland Priests' Telling

*(From the Third Homily against Vainglory, read on the Sunday after Michaelmas in the upland chapels.)*

There was in the southern cities a wanton damsel who danced upon cords of scarlet silk on the Sabbath evening, drawing the eyes of the apprentices and the bench of magistrates from their psalters; wherefore a well of black water opened beneath her slippers and drowned seven bailiwicks with all their sheep.

*(Collector's Note. — The upland lectionary terminates the homily at the drowning of the sheep. When I inquired of the curate at High Ghyll whether his manuscript preserved the concluding meeting with the sleeper in the sea, he replied that the parish lay forty-five miles from salt water and had no doctrinal use for a second chapter.)*

### D. The Children's Telling

*(Chanted while holding a tarred rope across the lane at dusk, St. Jude's-in-the-Fen.)*

1. She lives in the ridge-tree of every barn that has no hay in the loft.
2. Her hair comes down to the tie-beam and her petticoat is the colour of a plum.
3. If the owl drops a mouse-bone, you may pick it up; if the rafter drops a red cord, you must put your hands in your pockets.
4. You must never clap your hands after the tallow is lit, or she will think the curtain is up and come down the hay-shoot.
5. When the black river ran into the sea, the lady at the bottom told her to wipe her shoes on the shingle before she came indoors.

**Collector's Addendum.** — Having been apprised by a tide-waiter at Ness that a person answering to the figure of the variants had been seen since Tuesday in the upper cross-trees of the ruined Admiralty semaphore tower, I ascended the hill with my portfolio before dusk and read all four transcriptions aloud toward the open roof-trap. Owing to the salt-haze and the height of the collar-beam, no satisfactory view of the informant was obtained, though the fringe of a dark violet shawl hung three inches below the trap-framing. At the conclusion of Variant C, a low, exceedingly civil voice from above the slates remarked: *"The curate has the best ear of the four, my dear; I always looked shockingly well in scarlet slippers."* Upon my tying the tapes of the portfolio, she clapped her hands four times, very softly, in the dark under the weather-cock, after the manner of a patroness rewarding a child's recitation at a dame-school.

## Inquisition Taken at the Market-Cross of Nether Millbeck, 14th October

*Before Master Giles Fenwick, Justice of the Peace, and a Jury of Twelve Honest Men of the Sokeland, touching the bursting of the Upper Sluice and the washing away of the Fulling-Mill; from the Sessions Roll, membrane 9.*

**THE INQUIRY.** — Whether a young woman in a grey travelling-habit and pointed hat, seen to stand upon the east bank with a bound book in her hands during the whole space of the breach, is holden at law as an Accessory by Standing By, or a Rogue and Vagabond under the Statute of the Seventh Year.

**First Deponent: Joan Hobday, widow of the late fuller, sworn.**

*Question.* You saw the party before the breast-timbers gave way?
*Answer.* On the Wednesday at noon, your Worship, sitting on the coping-stone with her ankles crossed and a yellow Bergamot pear in her hand. She looked down at the green water weeping through the lime-mortar and she said to me, as pleasant as if she were asking after the butter-churn, *"If it were my dam, goodwife, I should not have mortised the sill into wet shale."* And then she bit the pear so the juice ran down her wrist and wiped it off with a little lawn handkerchief.
*Question.* Did she offer to lay a hand to the sluice-windlass?
*Answer.* She had her book open on her knee, sir. A body cannot turn a six-hundredweight iron rack with a pear and an octavo.

**Second Deponent: Wat Pugmire, lime-carter, sworn.**

*Question.* You stood within three paces of her when the masonry went out at four o'clock?
*Answer.* Two paces, by the elder-bush. She never spoke a word to Christian soul, and she never ate nothing neither. She had a silver-gilt brooch pinned crooked at her collar and a little cedar pencil in her fist, and she wrote from the first crack of the oak tie-beam till the water-wheel went down the race into the alder-carr.
*Question.* Did she call out to the men on the weir?
*Answer.* Never lifted her chin from the paper. Cold as the brass effigy in the chancel, your Worship. When the wave took the pig-sties and threw the spray over the bank, she shifted her grey skirt two inches to the left so the clay wouldn't spot the braid.

**Third Deponent: Thomas Hobday, aged seven years (examined without oath by reason of his tender age).**

*Question.* Now, boy, look at the Foreman and tell the Bench where you stood when the water came over the causeway.
*Answer.* By the goose-pen. The lady in the big hat had me by the thumb.
*Question.* The carter swears upon the Book that she had a pencil in her right hand and her ledger in the left.
*Answer.* She put the book in her teeth when the mill-stone rolled. She held onto my mitten from the first splash till my mam came down the lane with the hay-fork, and her hand was shaking like a terrier in the rain.
*Question.* Shaking with the cold draft off the water, boy?
*Answer.* No, sir. Just shaking. When she let go my thumb she gave me a warm purple fig out of her sleeve and told me not to blab to the carters, because it spoiled the look of the thing.

**THE COURT.** — *(Master Fenwick here inquiring of the Jury what manner of creature the said traveller must be presumed in law to be, if she is to be indicted at Assizes for failing to hold up forty ton of dressed gritstone and six thousand hogsheads of spate-water with her bare wrists; unto which question the Foreman and his fellows returning no answer for the space of two turns of the clerk's sand-glass, the Clerk is directed to enter the silence upon the parchment as a Silence.)*

**Exhibit annexed to the Roll.** — The Writ of *Venire Facias* dispatched on Friday by the parish constable to the Sign of the Pack-Horse at Cross-Keys, returned this morning in the pannier of the common butter-carrier, the seal unbroken, with the Clerk's spelling of *Millbeck* amended on the outer fold in red chalk, and across the tail of the parchment a line in a round, upright hand:

*Only passing through; the bridge at the lower ford wants a key-stone also. — Elaina*

*(Nota. — The Clerk hath indexed the final word in the margin under the rubric of Nomina Propria, though it remains doubtful to the Bench whether the same be a Christian name, a burgage-mark, or a cipher.)*

**THE FINDING.** — That the party was present upon the bank, which she doth not traverse; and forasmuch as the Parish Register of Christenings, Marriages, and Burials was carried down the river in the churchwardens' iron chest and lies sunk in twelve foot of silt below the willow-bed, it is Resolved by the Bench that the Clerk shall indite a civil letter to the said traveller, to be left at the next market-town southward, praying the courtesy of a fair copy of whatever she wrote in her book between four o'clock and half-past, the parish having now no better record of who was in the lane when the water came.

*(Memorandum added at Hilary Sessions. — The butter-carrier laid the letter upon the mounting-block at Cross-Keys with a silver sixpence for the ink. The sixpence was taken before Martinmas; the stone is swept every Tuesday by the sexton in expectation of the leaf.)*

## CAP. XIX. — Of Irregular Antagonists Who Cannot Be Instructed

*From 'The Art and Noble Science of the Small-Sword, Reduced to Certaine Rules of Measure and Tempo,' by Don Sebastiano Torelli, Master of the Salle of the Three Crowns in Vespia; quarto, calf, with copperplates by Matteo Grimaldi.*

IT happens, albeit rarely, to the Professor of Reputation, that after he hath perfected his scholars in the four Guards, the Cavation, and the true Lunge in *Quarta*, there presents itself upon his floor an Adversary who hath never saluted a wall nor paid a pistole to a Provost, and who nevertheless cannot be touched by the First or the Second Intention. Against such persons the rules of the Academy are of no more avail than a pair of compasses against a swallow; as I shall make plain by a Passage of Arms that befell in my own Salle on the eleventh of November last, after the candle-light scholars had been dismissed.

The porter having barred the street-door at eight, I was wiping down my practice-blades by the stove when I perceived a gentlewoman in mourning violet, her face covered to the lip by a short fall of black Cyprus lace, already present in the room—not standing upon the rosined deal by the threshold, the porter swearing upon the relics of Saint Januarius that the iron latch never lifted, but depending by the hollow of her right knee from a double line of crimson silk five feet above the floor-line, holding a heavy, unfashionable broad-bladed cut-and-thrust sword in her left or bridle hand, point downward like a cabbage-planter's dibble.

I have named her posture *La Guardia Pendula*, or the Inverted Ward of the Rafters (*see Plate XLII*). Considered according to the Ancients, it is altogether barbaric: the point is out of line, the wrist is slack, and the entire target of the left flank lies uncovered to the simplest thrust in *Terza*.

> **Plate XLII. — The Inverted Ward.** *The Master is shown in the true Guardia di Terza, left palm raised in counter-poise, engaging the Adversary suspended from the upper border of the frame.*
>
> *(Author's Note to the Binder. — I have thrice commanded the engraver Grimaldi to burin the iron beam-hook from which the lady's cord was hung, that the student may comprehend the pendulum. Grimaldi persists in terminating the silk in the white sky of the upper margin, alleging upon his oath as an eye-witness from the musician's gallery that there was no beam within fourteen feet of the knot. I let the copper pass only because the printer waits.)*

Upon her inviting me with a motion of her point to try the measure, I delivered a brisk disengagement and lunge to the outer cuff. She did not parry with the *forte* of the steel, as nature and preservation demand, but swung three yards laterally toward the rack of broadswords on the west wall, turning slowly upon her knee-joint as if to admire the panelling, and delivered a lazy, downward slicing blow at my knuckles as she passed, after the manner of a countess snipping dead roses from an arbour with a pair of garden-shears.

Three times I closed the line with a *contratempo*; three times she gave ground through the air—not out of any wholesome respect for the point, which she appeared to regard as a species of steel knitting-needle, but from an improper curiosity to observe how far I would extend my rear slipper across the boards to follow her. When at the fifth pass I recovered with a *passata sotto* so low that my knuckle-bow grazed the violet silk at her ankle, she inclined her veiled head from the rope and thanked me in a low, pleased contralto, as though I had picked up her glove in the foyer of the theatre.

At the conclusion of the seventeenth phrase—or the fourth, for though I have endeavoured every Sunday since Advent to set down the sequence of the passes upon my slate, the parade runs backward toward the salute whenever I reach the feint in *Seconda*—the lady arrested her swing in the very middle of a beat-attack, remarked that the smell of hot mutton-tallow made her eyelids heavy, wished me a very good night, and drew herself up into the dark above the tie-beams without setting hand to the cord. Neither Grimaldi nor I can state upon our honour whether either party took a touch during the engagement, though when I went to unbuckle my plastron by the stove, I found my left shirt-sleeve pinned fast to the oak wainscot by one of my own buttoned foils without a rent in the cambric.

Since that Tuesday, my sword-arm hath possessed three involuntary parries of the high outer line which I never had from my father, and which no Master of the Southern Incorporations can name. I do not impart them to my paying gentlemen, holding it dishonest in a professor to take gold for a ward that is not his own property; nevertheless, lest this Treatise want completeness, I have caused Grimaldi to draw the last of them from the life.

## The Little Office of the Slack-Line, or St. Nobody's Eve

*Printed on a single broadside sheet damped with tar-water, nailed behind the door of the Rope-Walkers' Chapel of St. Jude-by-the-Staithes; for the use of riggers, bell-ringers, well-sinkers, and the wives of the deep-sea fleet.*

*This Office is said standing on the twenty-ninth of November after the lanterns are quenched. Nothing is asked of the Lady Inverted at the close, it being the ancient and received tradition of the Walk that she grants no petition whatsoever; the Eve is kept notwithstanding.*

**V.** Who is above the tie-beam?
**R.** She that came down without a stair.

**V.** Where lies tomorrow, brethren?
**R.** Behind our shoulder-blades, where no lamp reaches.

**V.** And what stands before our eyes in the lane?
**R.** Yesterday and the day before it, rank on rank to the water's edge.

**V.** Who holds the bitter end when the tub goes down the shaft?
**R.** The sexton's wife, who is never named; may the hemp run kind.

*Here the Deacon takes the coil of crimson silk from the vestry-chest and passes the running end into the foremost bench. Every soul in the nave lays four fingers lightly over the cord without hauling. Nobody asks the Warden what the standing part is made fast to in the dark above the rood-beam.*

**V.** What was the Lady's name before the water rose in the pit?
**R.** *(Here the congregation keeps silence while the sand runs through a half-minute glass.)*

*Let the mothers hold the wrists of the small children during the silence; for if any person in the chapel strike their hands together in the manner of applause, whether from cold or gladness, the Deacon must drop the silk and begin the Office again from the first Versicle.*

**V.** The line is slack; the weight is off the hook.
**R.** Let the house go home in peace.

*Here follows the Catch of the Seventh Season. The words of the last verse were washed out of the parish book before our grandfathers' time and are known to no person living; the choir sings the tune to the end regardless.*

Chapter 1: Gù Rén

At the third watch, the mountain wind came down off the pass and swept the last of the mist out of the gorge.

Above the broken teeth of the eastern wall, the sky had cleared to a hard, star-pricked black. Outside the ditch, where the wardens had spent the afternoon dragging the cloven bronze husks of the Censure Courts into three long heaps, a corner of oiled canvas flapped once against a dead gear-wheel and went still. Inside the Hold, nothing moved. Even the brick hearth in the branch hall had gone dark behind its shutters, leaving only a faint smell of cold ginger ash and wet lime mortar hanging over the cobbles.

Qingxiao came down the cliff stair on foot.

She walked without a sound, her boots finding the worn hollows in the shale by starlight. Halfway down the rock cut, where the canyon draft funneled between the cistern shed and the storehouse roof, the wind struck straight through her moon-white silk.

Qingxiao’s steps slowed. A small shiver ran down her spine, raising gooseflesh along her forearms, and when she breathed out, a thin puff of ordinary white vapor drifted past her chin.

She looked down at her own sleeves in the dark, then tucked her bandaged left hand and her bare right one deeper into her cuffs to keep the wind off her wrists, and kept walking down the lane toward the gate.

In the shadow of the gatehouse arch, a single horn lantern burned on its iron hook, the wick turned down to a yellow bead no bigger than a bean.

Beneath it, sitting upright on the bottom stone step with her back braced against the granite pier, Liangyu was fast asleep. Her dented vambraces were still strapped to her forearms, and her long ash spear lay across her knees with both hands curled loosely around the shaft. Since supper, someone had come by and laid a thick, sheepskin-lined watch coat over her shoulders, tucking the heavy collar up around her ears so the draft off the slush wouldn’t stiffen her neck.

Qingxiao stopped two paces from the step.

Liangyu’s chin dipped a fraction toward her breastplate; she let out a slow, whistling breath through her nose and did not stir.

Qingxiao left her to sleep and turned toward the narrow timber stair that climbed the inside of the wall to the watch gallery.

The gallery was empty of guards. Up under the high cedar eaves, the night air was drier, smelling of old pitch and seasoned pine. The low lacquered stand by the outer parapet—where Qingxiao had left her seven-stringed paulownia qin when the siege began—stood bare under the stars.

Qingxiao paused at the top tread.

Ten feet back from the parapet, set on a dry pine bench against the inner wall where neither the afternoon drizzle nor the night dew could reach it, lay the qin. Someone had wiped the dark wood clean of sleet and wrapped it from crown to tail in a heavy square of dry, indigo-dyed oilcloth, folding the corners in with neat, square hospital tucks.

Beside the top of the stair, hanging from its peg on the corner pillar, was the gate’s wooden watch-board.

The horn lantern below threw just enough yellow light up the stairwell to show the four ruled columns. The names in the first and second watches were written in Warden Ge’s thick, square block letters, half-blurred where a wet sleeve had brushed across the wood during the rain.

In the third-watch column, right over a grey smudge of wiped-out chalk, someone had written a single name in fresh, bone-white strokes—a quick, slanting clerk’s hand that hooked the tail of the lower radical up like a swallow’s wing:

*Ruoyun.*

Qingxiao stood at the head of the stair with her hands inside her cuffs, looking at the fresh white dust clinging to the grain of the wood.

From the far end of the gallery, out of the shadow behind the wind-screen, a page turned with a dry *scritch*.

“If you leave seasoned paulownia sitting on an open rail in a two-day mountain soak,” a woman’s voice said briskly, “the belly-board warps by the width of a thumb and the lacquer crazes around the bridge. I thought Jingwei taught you to look after your tools, A-Qing.”

Qingxiao turned her head.

Sitting cross-legged on the broad stone cap of the corner embrasure, with a shielded brass tally-lamp set beside her knee and the gatehouse’s thick leather logbook propped open in her lap, was Ruoyun.

She was not wearing the blood-stiffened laminar coat from the river mud, and the starlight did not shine through her wrists.

Her face was the exact face from the camp tables at Yuan Fortress—not a day older, with the same sharp chin, the same impatient mouth, and the small white notch of an old sling-scar bisecting her left eyebrow. Above her temples, curving back into hair that had turned the deep, dark green-blue of pine shadow over river water, grew a pair of small, smooth golden horns that caught the yellow gleam of the tally-lamp.

Perched on the bridge of her nose was a pair of round, thin-rimmed brass spectacles, through which her bright blue eyes were presently glaring over a column of quartermaster’s ink.

She wore an immaculate teal-and-white dress cut in the high-collared Xianzhou fashion, with silver cloud-work embroidered along the hem of an asymmetrical pleated skirt that fell to her shins in back and showed white ankle socks in front, and dark leather shoes fastened with a single narrow strap across each instep. Her long, ruffled white sleeves were pushed halfway up her forearms to keep them out of the ink-pot.

“Well?” Ruoyun said, tapping the blunt end of a bamboo brush against the open logbook. “Are you going to stand in the stairwell letting the draft up my skirt all night, or are you coming over here? I’ve already signed the board. The watch is mine.”

***

Qingxiao walked down the length of the gallery.

Her boots made no sound on the cedar planks, and her pale blue eyes did not leave Ruoyun’s face—moving once to the small golden horns above her ears, down to the round brass spectacles on her nose, and then to the ruffled white cuff resting on the edge of the leather book.

She stopped a pace from the stone embrasure.

Slowly, Qingxiao drew her right hand out of her cuff and reached toward Ruoyun’s forearm.

She stopped her fingers half an inch from the white cloth, hovering in the cold air.

Ruoyun didn’t pull away. She set the bamboo brush down in the groove of the ink-stone, held her arm still over the open page, and waited.

Qingxiao lowered her hand the last half-inch.

Her fingertips met crisp, dry silk, and underneath the silk, the solid bone and warm skin of a living woman’s arm.

Qingxiao’s fingers closed around the sleeve—not tight, just enough to feel the tendon shift under the cloth when Ruoyun breathed.

For three heartbeats Ruoyun let her hold it. The brass spectacles slid a fraction down her nose as she looked at Qingxiao’s hand on her cuff.

“All right, that’s enough,” Ruoyun muttered, her voice going rough at the edge. “You’re wrinkling the pleating, and it took me twenty minutes to press these sleeves with a hot brick.”

Before Qingxiao could let go, Ruoyun shoved the heavy logbook off her lap onto the bench, swung her dark-shod feet down from the stone, and caught Qingxiao by both shoulders.

She pulled the Sword Transcendent straight into her chest with the blunt, hard grip of an old sergeant, hooking her chin over Qingxiao’s collarbone and locking both arms behind her back until the cloud-work on her bodice crushed flat against Qingxiao’s damp silk.

Qingxiao stood stiff for the space of half a breath—and then her chin came down against the warm wool-and-ink smell of Ruoyun’s shoulder, her bandaged left hand lifting to press flat between Ruoyun’s shoulder blades.

Ruoyun held her there for five counts, gave the back of her coat one sharp, angry pat, and pushed her back to arm’s length by her lapels.

“Your nose is like an icicle,” Ruoyun snapped, reaching up to flick her own spectacles back up the bridge of her nose with one knuckle. “What did you do, walk down the north cut without a cloak? Even the new recruits know enough to button their collars after the third bell.”

“The wind rose,” Qingxiao said.

She looked at the solid, unshifting line of Ruoyun’s shoulder against the starlight, and then at the brass tally-lamp sitting on the parapet.

“The clause,” Qingxiao said quietly.

Ruoyun snorted, picking up the leather logbook from the bench and dropping back onto her stone seat. “Broken is broken. They can’t fine you twice for the same mud on the carpet. Besides, the landlord hasn’t been home to check the books in a very long while, and I am tired of hiding behind the water-butt every time you walk through the gate.”

Qingxiao looked down at the round brass frames resting on Ruoyun’s cheeks. “You did not need spectacles before.”

“I didn’t have to read Warden Ge’s handwriting by tallow-dip before,” Ruoyun said tartly. “The man shapes his *sevens* like dead crows. Do you have any idea what the paperwork in this Hold looks like after a two-day siege? Not to mention the eastern wall. Ge’s boys threw three barrels of quicklime into the breach this afternoon without washing the gravel first. Come the first hard thaw in the second month, that whole lower course is going to slump into the ditch like cold porridge, and guess who’s going to be out there at four in the morning shoving slate wedges under the footing?”

“The masons,” Qingxiao said.

“Me!” Ruoyun said, pointing the bamboo brush at her own nose. “And that cook down in the branch hall—she’s a good girl with a carp, I’ll give her that, and those three little brass pots of hers keep the storehouse mice out of the barley, but she dragged the second cauldron straight across the middle of the floor after supper and left a black scorch mark the size of a cartwheel on my best blue flagstones.”

Qingxiao’s gaze shifted toward the stairhead, where the yellow light from the lower arch faintly lit the top step. “You put the coat on Liangyu.”

“Somebody had to,” Ruoyun grumbled, flipping a page in the logbook with a sharp snap of parchment. “She came out here at the first bell with her jaw set like she was going to fight the whole gorge by herself, sat down on the wet stone to oil her spearhead, and went out like a snuffed candle before she got the rag out of her pocket. *You* left the qin sitting out on the front rail to drink the mist, too. I had to haul it under the roof myself and let the seventh string down half a turn before the peg-box cracked.”

She shot a narrow, defensive look up through her spectacles at Qingxiao.

“It only sounded once,” Ruoyun added crossly. “My cuff caught the string while I was tucking the oilcloth under the tail. If anyone down in the yard told you I was playing it, they were hearing things.”

Qingxiao looked from the neatly wrapped instrument on the pine bench back to the pair of small golden horns gleaming above Ruoyun’s brow.

“Yours are shorter,” Qingxiao said.

Ruoyun stared at her for a second, and then let out a short, barking laugh that she clapped one hand over her mouth to smother before it could echo down the stairwell.

“Two hundred years,” Ruoyun hissed behind her palm, her bright blue eyes crinkling behind the glass, “and that’s what you have to say to me? At least mine don’t knock the lanterns off the ceiling beams every time I walk into the armory.”

She lowered her hand, shaking her head, and reached out to catch Qingxiao’s left wrist where the square of white gauze showed at the edge of her sleeve.

“Let me see that palm,” Ruoyun said, pulling the hand into the yellow circle of the tally-lamp. “That loud little girl in the vest tied a decent reef knot, I’ll give her credit, though why you thought grabbing a blade by the edge was—”

Ruoyun stopped.

Her thumb was resting against the bare inside of Qingxiao’s wrist, right where the worn red cord with its missing bead sat against the pulse.

There was no rime on the silk cuff. There was no dry, twelfth-month glacier cold radiating off the pale skin to numb Ruoyun’s fingers. Under Ruoyun’s thumb, Qingxiao’s wrist was simply a woman’s wrist—chilled by the night walk down the cliff, with a steady, ordinary pulse beating warm and mortal beneath the skin.

The humor vanished from Ruoyun’s face.

She didn’t drop the wrist. She turned Qingxiao’s hand over in the lamplight, pressed two fingers flat against the center of her palm beside the bandage, and then looked straight up into Qingxiao’s pale blue eyes.

“Where is the frost, A-Qing?” Ruoyun asked, her voice dropping flat and low.

Qingxiao did not pull her hand back. “Gone.”

Ruoyun’s fingers tightened around her wrist until the white gauze creased. “Don’t give me one-word answers like I’m a magistrate at the mountain foot. You practiced Jingwei’s stillness for two centuries. That ice was the only thing holding your marrow where it was.”

“Yes,” Qingxiao said.

“What did the old woman say when you dropped it?” Ruoyun demanded. Her voice didn’t rise, because Liangyu was sleeping twenty feet below them, and the whisper was worse than shouting. “How long?”

Qingxiao looked past the brass spectacles at the healed notch in Ruoyun’s left eyebrow.

“Not long,” Qingxiao said evenly. “By her measure.”

“Her measure,” Ruoyun repeated. She flung Qingxiao’s wrist down—not hard, just letting go of it all at once as she stood up from the parapet, her pleated skirt swinging against her shins. She took two sharp steps toward the outer rail, turned around, and glared at Qingxiao across the narrow planks.

“You absolute mule,” Ruoyun said, her voice shaking with pure, hot temper. “Do you know how many people I have watched go grey in this Hold while I swept the leaves out of their gutters? I buried Old Garrison Commander Zhao’s grandson. I watched his great-granddaughter lose her teeth. The whole lot of them, every sixty years, like grass on the south slope—and the one person on this mountain who was built to last as long as the rock comes walking down the stairs after two hundred years and throws her own lock in the ditch!”

Qingxiao stood still by the bench, her hands hanging at her sides.

“You gave up the coat,” Qingxiao said quietly.

Ruoyun’s mouth opened on another scold, and shut with a click of her teeth.

She stood with both hands braced backward on the cold stone of the parapet, her small golden horns dark against the stars over the gorge. With her spectacles catching the yellow lamp-glow and her jaw set hard, she looked furious enough to throw the logbook over the wall.

“I was pinned under a gate-beam, you idiot,” Ruoyun said roughly, looking sideways down the dark canyon rather than at Qingxiao’s face. “I didn’t have a snowpeak to walk up.”

She rubbed the back of her hand once across her cheek—a quick, irritable scrub—and jammed both fists onto her hips.

“I’m not walking behind your pine box, A-Qing,” she snapped. “You can get Ge’s great-nephew to carry the banner. I’ll be busy in the lower conduit that day.”

“The third watch will still need keeping,” Qingxiao said.

Ruoyun let out a long, ragged breath through her nose, her shoulders dropping an inch inside the white-and-teal silk. She walked back over to the embrasure, sat down heavily next to the tally-lamp, and pulled the logbook back against her knee without looking at it.

For a minute only the canyon wind moved through the gallery, making the wooden watch-board tap once against its corner pillar.

Qingxiao looked down at the worn leather cover of the gatehouse book, where generations of wardens had left dark thumbprints along the binding.

“Ruoyun,” Qingxiao said.

“What?”

“All these years,” Qingxiao asked, her voice plain and level. “Staying while they went. Was it lonely?”

Ruoyun looked up through her round spectacles. The anger had gone out of her eyes, leaving them bright, tired, and wholly unsentimental.

“Of course it gets quiet around the New Year,” Ruoyun said practically. “When the old ones you drank with are up on the hill and the new ones don’t know the verses to the ditch-digging songs.”

She tapped the cover of the logbook with one finger.

“And then in the third month, Carpenter Lin’s wife down by the cistern has a boy with ears like cabbages who cries loud enough to rattle the slates off the roof,” Ruoyun said. “By next winter that boy’s going to be toddling down the alley after the provision carts, and if I’m not standing by the south grate with an ash pole when the ice gets slick, he’s going to go headfirst into the drain-sluice just like his grandfather did in the forty-second year.”

She adjusted her cuffs with a brisk tug. “I’d sign again tomorrow, A-Qing. Even with the lime mortar.”

Then she tilted her chin up, her blue eyes fixing squarely on Qingxiao’s face.

“What about you?” Ruoyun asked softly. “Up there in the snow.”

Qingxiao did not look away from her friend’s face.

“Yes,” Qingxiao said.

***

Ruoyun looked at her for a second, then reached out and shoved the wrapped qin six inches down the pine bench to clear a spot on the wood.

“Sit down, then,” Ruoyun ordered, tapping the bare plank beside the instrument. “My neck is getting stiff looking up at you, and if you stand near the stairhead much longer you’re going to wake the child under the arch. Unwrap that thing and let’s see if the bridge held.”

Qingxiao sat down on the pine bench.

She lifted the qin onto her knees, undid the neat square folds of the indigo oilcloth, and laid the heavy fabric beside her hip. When she ran her right fingertips across the seven silk strings, the paulownia wood was bone-dry and smooth beneath the lacquer.

She plucked the open seventh string with her thumb.

It gave a low, quiet *thrum*—half a turn flat, just where Ruoyun had slacked it against the damp.

Qingxiao took the tuning key in her right hand, braced the peg-box lightly with her bandaged left palm, and brought the seventh string back up to pitch. She didn’t strike a full chord that would carry down the stairwell into the courtyard; she kept her touch light, stopping the strings with the side of her left thumb so the notes stayed inside the cedar eaves, no louder than water dripping into a stone basin.

She began to play an old four-beat camp air—not the marching call of the walls, just a small, halting fireside tune the mule-drivers from the southern valleys used to whistle while they greased the axle-trees before frost.

On the stone parapet two feet away, Ruoyun drew one knee up, rested her elbow on it, and propped her chin in her palm.

By the second verse, Ruoyun began to hum under her breath, murmured low enough that the words barely left her collar:

*“Three copper cash for the ferryman’s oar, two for the barley cake dry by the door…”*

When she came to the rising turn at the end of the line, Ruoyun’s voice came in a quarter-tone flat—the exact same stubborn, slightly flat pitch she had carried through every winter march in the Gorges.

Qingxiao’s fingers paused on the fourth string.

Ruoyun’s eyes narrowed behind her spectacles, her chin coming up from her palm. “Don’t you start with me, A-Qing. I had the tune before you even knew which end of a brush took the ink.”

She reached down to the fourth tuning peg under the head of the qin, turned the wooden key a hair’s breadth backward to drop the silk string down to Ruoyun’s quarter-tone, and plucked the note again so it met Ruoyun’s voice in the middle of the air.

Ruoyun blinked once behind her round glasses. Her mouth opened slightly, closed, and then she looked away toward the dark roof-tiles of the barracks, humming the next bar straight through without missing the beat.

Down at the bottom of the stairwell, under the gatehouse arch, Liangyu’s boot scraped once against the flagstone as she shifted in her sleep. She pulled the collar of the borrowed sheepskin coat closer under her chin, let out a long breath, and stayed asleep.

Up on the gallery, the stars wheeled slowly past the gap in the cedar eaves.

As the third watch wore down toward the deep cold before the fourth bell, the canyon draft crept along the floorboards again. Qingxiao kept her fingers moving softly across the strings, though when she paused between verses to shift her left hand, her bare right fingers curled inward for a moment against the chill in the wood, and her shoulders drew a fraction closer together under the thin white silk.

On the parapet, Ruoyun clicked her tongue.

She slid off the stone cap, snatched up the heavy square of indigo-dyed oilcloth from the bench beside Qingxiao’s hip, and shook it out with a sharp snap.

“Two hundred years on a snowbank and you don’t know enough to bring a shawl to a night watch,” Ruoyun muttered, dropping the thick, flannel-lined wrapping cloth squarely across Qingxiao’s shoulders from behind and pulling the two front corners together under her chin with a rough tug. “If you start sneezing into the porridge tomorrow, that girl with the feathers in her hair is going to look at me like I left the barn door open.”

Qingxiao did not push the cloth off. She let her right hand come up to hold the indigo corner closed over her breastbone, the heavy lining trapping the warmth of her own chest against the draft.

“It smells of camphor,” Qingxiao said.

“It keeps the moths out of the storehouse linen,” Ruoyun said, and instead of climbing back onto the parapet, she sat down on the pine bench right beside Qingxiao’s hip.

She left the logbook on the stone, tucked her white-socked ankles back under the bench rail, and leaned her left shoulder solidly against Qingxiao’s right arm, the small golden horn at her temple resting an inch below Qingxiao’s cloud-pale one.

Qingxiao shifted her elbow half an inch so the edge of the qin wouldn’t bump Ruoyun’s knee, and played the camp tune through again from the beginning, slower this time, with only her right hand plucking the low strings while her bandaged left rested warm under the camphor-scented cloth.

They sat like that until the stars over the eastern breach began to pale at the edges.

Down in the lower courtyard, the barracks watchman struck the fourth-watch drum—three low, booming strokes on damp ox-hide that rolled across the wet roofs and died out over the gorge.

Beside Qingxiao, Ruoyun let out a short sigh and straightened up from her shoulder, rubbing her knees through her pleated skirt.

“That’s Ge’s corporal waking the relief,” Ruoyun said, standing up and picking up her brass tally-lamp and the leather logbook. “If I don’t get the pine needles out of the south conduit grate before the fifth bell, the frost will seal the lip and the wash-house trough will back up into the stable lane by breakfast.”

She tucked the heavy book under her left arm, hooked the lamp ring over two fingers, and walked to the head of the timber stair.

At the top step, beside the hanging watch-board, Ruoyun stopped and looked back over her shoulder.

“Come down before the second bell tomorrow night,” Ruoyun said, pointing a finger at Qingxiao over the rim of her spectacles. “The cook’s simmering beef bones in the big pot, and if you stay up on that rock eating dry millet while there’s marrow broth in the hall, I’ll send three Rafter Kites up the chimney to peck your ankles.”

Qingxiao rested both hands flat on the silent strings of the qin.

“I will come down,” Qingxiao said.

Ruoyun gave a single, satisfied nod, turned on her heel, and walked down the wooden stairs on her own two feet, the dark leather straps of her shoes clicking briskly against each pine tread until she stepped around the sleeping girl under the arch and went out into the dark lane toward the conduits.

The gallery was quiet again.

After a moment, Qingxiao lifted the qin from her knees and laid it back on the bench, folding the indigo oilcloth around her own shoulders like a short cape as she stood up.

She walked down the gallery to the corner pillar at the head of the stair, where the wooden watch-board hung in the dim yellow light rising from the arch below.

On the narrow grooved ledge beneath the board lay a stub of white chalk, worn flat on one side from Ruoyun’s fingers.

Qingxiao picked up the chalk with her right hand, looked at *Ruoyun* written in the third-watch column, and set the white point to the dark wood beside it to write her own name.

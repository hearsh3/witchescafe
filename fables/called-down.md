## § 1

The village keeps a protocol older than the village.

They do the coven the courtesy of the mark-stone. Whatever they want, they set on it — a basket, a lantern, a question folded twice — and then they retreat past the wrack-line and wait in weather, however long the weather lasts, because a witch who is summoned arrives when she arrives.

You are in the ropes when the lantern goes up. Fifty feet over the shingle, a slack line looped at the ankle, half a season of your attention given to the way the sea-cave's roof holds its own weight. Below, Viviane's keel lies blocked and true. The plane sings, stops, sings.

"Yours," Viviane says, without looking up. "It's Hallveig."

"How can you tell at this distance."

"She limps left and she's brought the good basket."

The lantern burns on the stone. The old woman stands past the wrack-line in a wind that has opinions, and she will stand there until dark, and she will consider standing there until dark to be the ordinary price of the thing.

## § 2

The wind puts the sea in your mouth on the way down.

Hallveig has aged since spring. She ages the way the coast ages: in weather-events, unevenly, all at once and then a long plateau. When you were introduced she was a bride, and you looked exactly as you look, and she has spent sixty years arranging her face to survive that arithmetic. She has arranged it well.

"Witch."

"Hallveig."

She lifts the cloth off the basket. Under it: herring, and coiled net, and something dry.

"Came up in the drift-net at the third buoy. Dry as a sermon." She sets it on the stone with two fingers, the way you handle a thing that arrived from somewhere with rules. "Whole sea soaking and this dry. Since your mother woke, the water gives back."

*Your mother.* The village settled on that word within a month of the waking, without consultation, the way villages do. Nobody in the house has corrected it.

Folded in eight. Cream paper gone the colour of old milk. A swan device pressed blind into the corner, and the fold-lines soft as cloth from being folded and unfolded by hands that are three hundred years dead.

"I could keep it," Hallveig says. "In the church box. With the ring."

"You could."

"It's got your bird on it."

## § 4

You pay her in weather.

Three days out, a green evening, the swell going long and oily under a sky the colour of a bruised pear: you give her the date and the hour and the fact that the fleet should stay on the mole and drink. It costs you a headache and half a night with the frequencies, and it is the honest price, and she takes it the way she takes a fair weight of fish — with a nod and immediate arithmetic.

"Third of the month. The boys will complain."

"Let them complain on land."

She hesitates then, and the hesitation is the most expensive thing on the beach. Sixty years of protocol and she is going to break it.

"My Torvald's boy has your walk," she says. "The girls in the village. They practice it. On the harbour wall." Her jaw sets. "I've watched them practice for forty years and I've never once told you, because I thought it might be theft. Taking a thing off you. And you never age, so I thought — she'll be here after me, and someone should have said."

The wind takes the corner of the cloth and puts it back.

"It's practice," you tell her. "It's the only compliment the body knows how to pay."

She nods. She has been carrying that for four decades and she puts it down on the stone with the basket, and something in her shoulders comes off the hook.

## § 11

The archive has one lamp and Herta under it, which is how the archive has looked for two hundred years.

She takes the playbill in both hands, holds it at the angle that catches the grain of the paper, and makes a small sound of professional grief.

"Salt-set. This spent three centuries in the deep and the fibres are *fine.*" She turns it over. "Do you understand what your mother's ocean is doing? It's *archiving.* Badly indexed and it has no policy on returns, but it's archiving."

"Herta."

"There's a repertory house on the Locks in four of my sources. Third season. That gives me a decade." She is already reaching for the ladder. "Company records. Guild rolls. Ticket accounts, if a bank kept them, and a bank always keeps them. Give me eleven days and I can give you the personnel file of every human being who ever swept that stage."

## § 13

## § 14

The keel-cave smells of pine and low tide. Viviane has a plank in the steam-box and the ribs are up, and it has begun to look like an argument reaching its conclusion.

She reads the playbill with sawdust on her fingers and hands it back.

"Load-bearing?"

"It's the only question." She goes back to the plank. "A name holds a life up. You want to know whether that one still would, and there's exactly one way to find out and it involves standing on it."

"You never went back for yours."

"I flew out to the wreck in the spring," Viviane says, to the wood. "Stood on the deck where the bones were. Took my hat off." She sets the plane down and turns around, and her mechanical hand is very still, which is how you know. "Laurentina is a girl who died in a ship. ## § 16

Phrolova's hour is the one the household keeps for her, so knocking on it means something.

She opens the door. The lamp is behind her; the viola is on its stand; the manuscript on the desk has a title space open and a dedication line ruled and waiting.

"Come in."

You give her the playbill. She reads it standing, once, and sets it down.

"Hum me what you have."

You have two beats and a falling second, the shape of a name thrown up at a dark ceiling. You give her that.

She listens with her head very slightly tilted. She goes to the viola and puts the shape on the C string, low, twice.

"Again."

You give it again. She adjusts. The second attempt has the falling second sitting a whole tone lower and it is instantly, obviously right, and something goes over the back of your neck like cold water.

"There." She lowers the bow. "That is a call across distance. Somebody was some way off from you and wanted you back."

"That's a great deal to get from two notes."

"It is a great deal, and it is in the two notes, and I have spent an unhealthy life learning to hear it." She sets the viola down. "I filled a paradise with people I named. Every one of them perfect and every one of them staying, and it took me eight hundred years to understand that I had built a room where nobody could be *called,* because a call is what you say to someone who might be leaving." Her hands fold. "You have a call in you. Somebody thought you might leave. That is a rarer thing to be given than a name."

The lamp fizzes.

"I can write it out," she says. "Two bars. You can carry it and hum it and never know the word, and that is a whole life, and it is a life I recognise, and I would think slightly less of you for choosing it." She says this in the voice she uses to teach etiquette to glaciers. "There is also the water."

## § 17

## § 20

You go down.

The Locks lie deep and the deep is quiet in a way the surface never manages, and the opera house stands in it entire, the way a thing stands when the water arrived faster than the fear.

Three hundred years have made it a garden. The proscenium wears weed like a stole. The chandelier hangs in place, every lustre furred, throwing back your light in ten thousand small refusals. The seats are ranked and patient. The gilt has gone green and the green is alive and the whole building is being slowly, tenderly eaten by things with no interest in music.

You made this. The lake overflowed and the dark went down the river with your plumage dissolving into it, and everything in this room drowned inside a week.

The water holds them still. Your mother's water archives badly and returns things dry and keeps drafts, and the drafts are here: the house is full of the audience, ranked in the dark, thin as held breath. They have been waiting for the curtain for three centuries. They are, by any measure that matters, a very patient crowd.

## § 21

They know you at once.

The recognition goes through the ranked dark like wind over barley, row by row, and the house comes to its feet — three hundred years of them, held in your mother's careful water, thin as breath and entirely themselves.

They say the role.

They say it in the voice a crowd has, all at once and slightly apart, the way applause starts: *the Swan, the Swan, the Swan.* A man in the third row who has been dead since before the Lament weeps without embarrassment. A girl with a cheap seat and a good ear says something to her friend about your line in the second act. Somebody's mother says: *she is exactly as I remember.*

Every one of them is glad. Every one of them means it.

And every one of them hands you back the same word, because the word is all they were ever given, because you gave it to them yourself, every night, magnificently, for six seasons, and they have kept it perfectly, and it is the only thing they have.

You stand in the aisle of your own drowned house and understand the arithmetic completely for the first time. You handed it over the footlights yourself, night after night, and they held it up so it caught the light, and the holding wore it smooth, and the you underneath went quiet from lack of use.

The applause is beginning. You can feel it gathering in the room like pressure before rain.

## § 22

Under the stage the water is stiller, and the ceiling is the underside of a floor you danced on.

Wardrobe is a long low room with rails still standing and three hundred costumes hanging in green ruin, and at the sewing table sits the shape of a woman with pins in her mouth, which is how she sat for forty years and how she has sat since.

Aunt Kirsa was nobody's aunt. She laced you in and laced you out, and she had opinions about the management, and she kept a tin of sugared almonds in a drawer she thought was secret.

"Arms."

Your arms come up. Six hundred years and your arms come up.

She works around you in the dark water with the total attention of a professional, and she talks the whole time, which is what she did, because backstage is where the performing stops.

"You'll ruin your feet. You've ruined them. I've seen the state of them, I've had them on my lap." A pin. "Stand *straight.* You slouch when you're frightened and you've been frightened since Tuesday." A pin. "That manager wants his fingers broken and I've said so."

Then, tugging a lace, entirely in passing, the way it was said ten thousand times in this room by a woman with pins in her mouth:

She says your name.

Two syllables. The second one falls.

It arrives in you like the smell of a house you lived in as a child — total, wordless, and gone before you can put a hand on it. You make her say it again. She says it again, differently, because she said it ten thousand times over forty years and she said it fast and slack the way people say a name they use daily, and the second version has a soft ending the first version lacked.

"Which is it?"

"Which is *what,* pet, hold still."

"You said it two ways."

"I said it the way it's said." A pin. Her hands do not stop. "Nobody says a name the same twice. That's how you know it's got someone in it."

## § 23

Forty feet of ladder in black water, and the rungs are exactly where your feet remember.

The grid is a country. Timber and iron over the void, ropes coming down in ordered rain, sandbags hanging in the dark like a herd asleep — the machinery of flight, which the audience is never permitted to see, because the audience is required to believe that the rising was easy.

Olek is at the pin rail with the rope in both hands and his weight on the back foot.

He is holding.

Three hundred years, and the line taut, and the weight of it running down the grid into his shoulders, and his hands chalked, and his eyes on the dark below where the stage is, waiting for a cue that stopped coming when the water came in.

You have to stand there a moment.

"Fly's holding," he says, to the dark, the way he said it every night. "You're on the wire, girl. Say when."

The rope hums. The hemp smells the way hemp smells. He is thin as breath and entirely himself and his hands are exactly the hands: rope-burned across all four fingers, chalked in eleven seconds, still.

"Olek."

His head turns, and his hands do not move, because a flyman's hands do not move.

"There you are." Something in his face lets go by about a tenth. "Been a long act."

"It has."

"They don't half drag it out at the top of the second." He shifts his weight, checks the rail by feel, resets. "You want to come down or you want to go higher? You always wanted higher. I've been telling you for six seasons, there's no house up here, nobody's looking up here—"

"That's why I liked it."

"That's why you liked it," he agrees, and he says your name.

Two syllables. The second one falls. He throws it up at the dark ceiling the way a man throws a name at a person forty feet above him, and the shape you have been carrying since the beach fits into the word like a hand into a worn glove.

It is a very ordinary word.

He says it the way you say a name to somebody who is trusting you with their entire weight.

> **THE CARD THAT BELONGS TO NO DECK.** *A hand, offering a lyre, grip-first.* You may take this or leave it. The offer is the same either way. It is the only card in your possession that has never once told you what to do.

## § 24

You say it back to her, and she takes the pins out of her mouth.

For a moment the whole drowned room is a woman with an armful of green ruin looking at a witch six hundred years old, and neither of you is performing, which is the entire architecture of a backstage.

"There she is," Kirsa says.

She is very thin. She has been thin since the water. She was always going to be a woman at a sewing table in a low room where the audience never came, and the audience never came, and she stayed until the water arrived and stayed after, and this is what your mother's ocean archives: the ones who were doing their job.

"You've done well, then?" she asks. "Out there?"

"I have a family."

"*Family.*" She snorts, and picks the pins back up. "Well. You'd want one. You were always out in the wings looking at the ones with people waiting." She threads. "Go on. You'll be late. And *stand straight.*"

You go up the ladder with a word in you that behaves like a word — small, ordinary, worn soft at the edges by forty years of daily use.

## § 25

"Find everything except that."

Herta looks at you for a long moment and then, to her eternal credit, argues about it for exactly four sentences and stops.

She gives you the theatre. Eleven days later there is a folder on the table with a floor plan, a wage schedule with one line inked over by her own hand before she read it, a fire inspection, a repertory calendar, two ticket accounts and the discovery that the Opera of the Seven Locks ran a charity matinée every Ash Week for the families of drowned fishermen, which is the kind of fact that recontextualises a building.

"There's a flyman," she says. "Olek. Eleven marks a week, licensed for grid and pin rail, nineteen years in post." She taps the schedule. "Nineteen years. Do you know how rare that is? They fell. Constantly. This is a man who was extremely good at holding on to things."

## § 26

"Burn the note you've already started."

Herta's face does several things.

"I have three pages."

"I know."

"They're *good* pages." She sits down. The lamp fizzes. She puts her hand on the drawer and leaves it there, and you watch a scholar of two centuries hold a door shut on the only room she has ever wanted to be in. "You understand that I will always know I could have. That the not-knowing will be a thing I *did,* every day, on purpose, for as long as we both last."

"Yes."

"That's — " She stops. Starts again. "That's a marriage, more or less. Structurally."

"More or less."

She takes her hand off the drawer. She takes out the three pages, and holds them over the lamp, and then, because she is who she is, reads them aloud first — floor plans, ticket receipts, a charity matinée for drowned fishermen every Ash Week — everything except the line she has already found and has been carrying alone for two days without telling a soul.

Then she burns them.

"I want it on the record that this hurt," she says, watching the ash.

"It's on the record."

"Where."

"Here." You tap the table. "That's what the table's for."

She takes that better than either of you expects. ## § 27

## the company: THE UNDERSTUDY

You put the paper in the cedar box, and the box on the shelf, and the season passes, and then several.

Here is what happens, slowly, the way silt happens.

The printed name comes back first. **Vesperine.** It was built to fit and it has lost none of its fit in three hundred years, and it slides on one evening in the middle of a story you are telling at the table, and Alice writes it down because Alice writes everything down.

You get glossier. It is very gradual and entirely pleasant. Your line improves. You start finishing your sentences beautifully. The dancing at the pool's edge acquires an audience it never had, and the audience is the household, and the household enjoys it, and you enjoy the enjoying, and the enjoying has a slight metallic taste that you learn to stop noticing.

Herta notices at the end of the second year and writes a worried note and files it, because it is a note about a friend and she has no shelf for that.

Viviane says one thing, once, in the keel-cave, over the sound of the plane: "You've started performing at breakfast."

Mina says nothing at all, which is worse, and keeps setting your place, which is worse than that.

The name in the box stays in the box. The box has no lock and everybody knows what is in it, and this becomes a fact about the house, like the wobbling chair, and facts about houses are load-bearing, and nobody ever moves them.

*Loose in the world: a witch wearing a costume that fits perfectly. Herta's unfiled note. The box on the shelf, and the day someone else opens it.*

## § 28

## the company: THE WAGE-LINE

You open the folder.

Eleven marks a week. Post: principal. Nineteen years shorter in post than the flyman. A name, printed in a clerk's copperplate in a column of forty other names, all of them dead, all of them once at eleven marks a week.

You read it.

It is a word. It has letters in it. It sits on the page and does the thing that a fact does, which is stay exactly the same size no matter how long you look at it.

Nothing happens in you at all.

You wait for it. You are patient — you have six hundred years of practice at waiting for feeling to arrive from somewhere upstream — and you sit in the archive with the lamp fizzing and you wait, and the word sits there in copperplate, and the clerk who wrote it was probably twenty and probably bored and probably wanted his supper.

Herta finds you in the morning.

"Well?"

"It's a wage-line."

"I told you it would be a wage-line."

"You did."

She sits down opposite. She does not say anything for a while, and then she says the only thing available:

"You have the word now. That's a real thing to have. Ninety per cent of everyone who ever lived would take it."

"Yes."

"And you're grieving something you can't point at, which in my professional experience is the only kind of grief there is."

The word goes into the cedar box. It comes out at odd hours. It never learns to mean anything, and it stays, patiently, the way a fact stays, and it is yours, and you cannot get it to sing.

*Loose in the world: a true name with nobody left alive to say it aloud. Two voices under the water who could fix that in a sentence. The ladder is still there.*

## § 29

## the company: THE TWO BARS

She writes it out.

Two bars, C string, a call across distance with a falling second, in a hand so exact the manuscript could be engraved. She gives you the paper and adds, at the bottom, in her own small script: *for a threshold.*

You hum it for the next four hundred years.

You hum it at doors. You hum it into the frequencies. Alice invents a letter for it and puts the letter in her alphabet where the vowels would go if the alphabet had vowels. Elaina, writing home from a port with an unpronounceable harbour, reports that she hummed something at a strange door on the coast road and the door remembered its manners, and asks what it means, and you write back: *it means somebody wanted me back.*

You never get the word.

The absence of the word becomes a shape in the household the way a chair is a shape. New members are told about it in their first month, gently, by Herta, who has a rehearsed sentence. It goes into *A Bestiary of the Beloved* as an entry with a musical example and a blank where the headword goes — the only such entry — and generations of scholars will call it the finest page in the book and none of them will know what it is.

At the table, at a certain point in a certain kind of evening, somebody will hum two bars where a name would go, and everybody will pass the salt.

*Loose in the world: a name that exists only as an interval. A dedication line still ruled and empty. Two voices under the water, both still holding.*

## § 30

Agrat sows a full circuit before she answers.

## § 31

## the company: THE ENCORE

You take the position.

It has been three hundred years and your body remembers all of it, and the house comes up out of its ranked dark, and the second act begins in green dark with the water for an orchestra, and it is the finest thing that has ever happened in this building.

They give you everything. Three centuries of held breath spent all at once.

And the applause is a solvent. It always was. It takes the fingerprints first.

You dance the whole act. You dance the coda. You dance past the coda into things nobody wrote, and the house roars, and somewhere around the second hour the word *Swan* stops being a thing they call you and becomes a thing you are, and the you underneath — the two syllables with the falling second, the shape thrown up at a dark ceiling by a man with rope-burned hands — goes quiet from lack of use, again, exactly the way it went the first time.

Up in the grid, forty feet over the stage, Olek hears the applause start and takes a fresh grip.

Here is what remains.

A role. Very fine, very complete, castable. In eighty years a company on the northern coast revives a piece nobody can find the score for and a young dancer wins the town on it. In two hundred, the part is standard: every serious girl of a certain build learns the Swan, and their teachers say *make it your own,* and they cannot, and something about that failure is the reason the part endures. There are notes on the interpretation. There are schools.

And in the house on the black-sand shore, five witches keep a place at the table for a woman who dances beautifully at the pool's edge every evening and says less every year, and Mina goes on setting the cup, and waits, having some experience of long waits.

*Loose in the world: a repertory role with a demon inside it. A god who has never once given up on anyone. Two hundred years of girls auditioning to be a woman who is still alive and cannot be reached. Somebody, eventually, will be very good.*

## § 33

## the company: THE CUE

"Bring me down, Olek."

His hands move for the first time in three hundred years.

He does it properly — a flyman's lower, hand over hand, the rope paying out at the speed of a body that trusts you, and somewhere in the dark below a wire that has been taut since before the Lament goes slack across the whole grid, and the sandbags settle, and the great machine of flight lets out its breath.

He makes the line off. He checks it by feel. He looks at his own hands with mild surprise.

"That's the show, then."

"That's the show."

He wipes the chalk on his trousers, which is the last thing he ever did every night for nineteen years.

"You were good, girl," he says, and says your name once more, and the shape and the word are the same shape and the same word, and then the grid is a grid, and the rope is a rope, and your mother's water goes on holding the building up with its usual courtesy.

You surface at dawn with two syllables in your mouth.

The household is at breakfast. The word goes on the table, because the table is the policy, and the household does what the household does with a new thing: it uses it. Badly, at first. Herta over-enunciates for a week. Viviane says it into wood. Alice invents three letters, discards two. Phrolova says it at her door, in her hour, at a volume for one, and closes the door, and then plays for an hour with the door open, which she has never done.

Mina, passing the salt, says it in the middle of a sentence about jam, and it goes by so ordinarily that you have to sit down.

*Loose in the world: an old name in daily use, and the six hundred years of Black Swan still sitting on the shelf beside it, and no ruling from anybody on which of them answers when the door goes. Kirsa, still lacing, in a low room where nobody came. A pin rail with nobody on it. A god who heard something in the water once and has never been asked.*

## § 34

## the company: THE HOLDING

"Again."

He says it again. He says it fifty times through the night, patiently, the way a man says a name into the dark while his hands hold a rope, and you stand in the grid of a drowned opera house and take it in until the word and the shape are welded, and you have it, entirely, forever.

And he never gets the cue.

You notice at first light — the light comes down through the flies in green bars — and you look at his hands, which have been holding the whole time, which have been holding for three centuries, which will hold as long as your mother's water keeps drafts.

"You should say when," he offers, as you go.

"Soon."

"Right you are."

You surface with the name. It is a good name and it works. The household learns it inside a month and wears it smooth inside a year, and it is yours, and it is *right,* and it sings.

And on certain nights, four or five a year, you go and sit at the top of the ladder in the dark and watch a man hold a rope, and you do not say the cue, because saying it means the last person alive who says your name properly will let go.

Alice notices the pattern in the third year and marks the nights on a chart, and leaves the asking alone, and the chart is a kindness so exact it takes six hundred years of scribing to build.

*Loose in the world: a woman with her name back and a man still holding it. A chart in a scribe's hand, columns ruled, headword blank. A household that has learned to ask, sitting downstairs, waiting.*

## § 35

## the company: THE FLYMAN

You take the rope out of his hands.

He gives it. He has been waiting three hundred years for someone to relieve him and he has excellent manners about the handover: the weight comes across, hand to hand, and his eyes go to the pin rail to make sure you have the lay of it, and he tells you the trick of the third line — it runs stiff in the wet, take it slow — and then his hands are empty and he looks at them.

"That's me, then."

"That's you."

"Say it back to me, girl. So I know I've handed off proper."

You say your own name. He nods. He goes down the ladder into the dark the way a man goes home after a long night, and the water takes him, courteously, dry.

And you are holding the rope.

There is a weight on the other end. There has always been a weight on the other end. In the dark below, forty feet down, something is on the wire and has been on the wire since the water came, and it is waiting for a cue, and you find that you cannot see what it is from up here, which is the condition of the grid: the flies exist so that the audience may believe the rising was easy.

You hold.

The household comes for you in the spring — Viviane first, with the underwater lamp; Mina, who walks down through her own sea barefoot with her shoes in one hand; all of them, in the end, up the ladder in a line. They stand in the grid with you and they do not ask you to let go.

Herta brings up a folding stool. Alice starts a chart.

"How long," Viviane says.

"As long as it takes."

"Right," says Viviane, and goes down, and comes back in eleven days with a counterweight system of her own design, and they rig it around you over the course of a season, and it takes the strain off your shoulders and none of it off your hands, and Mina sets your place at the table anyway, every night, for as long as this goes on.

*Loose in the world: something on the wire at the bottom of a drowned opera house. A witch who will be at the pin rail whenever a story needs her found. A cue nobody has said. And in a village on the black-sand shore, a feast day in autumn where the children hold a rope for an hour and are told, without explanation, that this is how you love someone.*

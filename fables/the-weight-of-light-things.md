## I. The Motion
### *Ragunna · the Council of Stewards · budget season*

The Republic of Rinascita was three years old, which in the life of republics is the age at which a child discovers the word *why* and uses it on everything, including the furniture.

The Council of Stewards met in the Grand Cathedral because the Grand Cathedral was the only room in Ragunna that seated four hundred people and had survived both Dark Tides and the acoustics argument of Year One. The Order's iconography had been the subject of a seven-month commission, which concluded, over the objections of both purists and iconoclasts, that the stained glass would stay — including the window of the Blessed Maiden, whom half the chamber had personally watched eat honey cakes at a harbor café — because scaffolding cost money and the light through her glass fell, at ten in the morning, directly across the speaker's rostrum, and the Republic had decided it liked what that did to speeches. Everything sounded slightly more accountable, delivered through a martyr's shadow by people who knew she was alive and behind on her homework.

On the fourth Tuesday of budget season, Steward Ottavio Manzato of the Third Canal District rose through that shadow and moved the Founders' Bill.

He was a good speaker. He had been a fish auctioneer for twenty years, which is the finest training for legislatures yet devised, and his bill was short, popular, and warm as fresh bread: that the six liberators of Rinascita be entered into the civic calendar as Founders of the Republic; that the anniversary of the Sentinel's last words be kept as a public feast; that a colossal group monument be raised in the Cathedral square, funded by subscription already two-thirds filled; and that the sixth of the six, the Priestess of Septimont, be granted honorary citizenship, *"so that the set,"* Manzato said, to laughter and stamping, *"be complete."*

The stamping went on for a while. It was a bill everyone could love. Eleven of the twelve newspapers had endorsed it in advance. The twelfth had endorsed the monument and quibbled about the plinth.

Carlotta Montelli let the stamping spend itself, the way one lets a wave finish before stepping onto wet sand, and then rose from the commerce bench with a single sheet of paper.

She was twenty-six now and ran the largest trading house on the western sea, and the chamber had learned in three years to read her props the way soldiers read weather. A ledger meant a fight over tariffs. A folder meant someone was about to be very sorry. A single sheet meant she had already won and was here to explain the terms.

"I have a question for the honorable Steward," she said. "Procedural."

"The executor has the floor."

"The feast day. Which date?"

Manzato consulted his notes. "The sentence was spoken on the ninth of—"

"The ninth. So the fish market closes on the ninth. The banks close. The customs house closes." She turned the sheet over, though everyone present understood she knew its contents from memory and the turning was theater, and everyone present enjoyed the theater, which is the Ragunnan social contract in one gesture. "The ninth falls, this coming year, in the middle of the autumn convoy settlements. I have here the clearing volume of that week. Would the Steward like the figure, or shall I simply say that his feast day costs the harbor more than the monument, the plinth, and the subscription combined, and that the subscription, which I have also reviewed, is two-thirds filled largely by three donors, one of whom is the Steward's brother-in-law, who holds the marble concession?"

The chamber made the sound chambers make when blood enters the water in a friendly way.

"The executor impugns—"

"The executor *prices*." Carlotta set the sheet down. "Marble is a fine trade. I hold no grudge against marble. My question is about the other line item. The one nobody has priced." She stepped forward, and the Maiden's window laid its colored light across her shoulders, and afterward four separate diarists recorded that she seemed to notice it land and to stand up straighter under it, the way one stands when a friend leans on you from behind.

"Three years ago this chamber inherited a country from a god whose last act was to resign. The instruction was two words. It was addressed to *us* — this room got the working half of the sentence. And in three years I have watched this body do the work. Badly, often. Slowly, always. Out loud, which is the whole invention. The hazard registry took four votes and a strike at the fish market, and the fish market was *right* about the sunset clause, and we amended it, and that amendment is the proudest ugly paragraph in our statutes." A pause, auctioneer-length; she had learned from watching Manzato, which he knew, and which sat between them as a compliment neither would say. "Now the honorable Steward proposes we take the six people who handed us this work and lift them somewhere the work can't reach them. A calendar. A colossus. A feast with their faces on it. Stewards — we have *had* that government. It stood on this exact floor. It also began with real gratitude for a real rescue, and it ended with a puppet in the reliquary and a fee schedule for miracles, and the distance between those two points was walked one warm, popular, unpriced motion at a time."

"The executor compares me to the Order," Manzato said, flat.

"The executor compares the *mechanism*." Her voice stayed level, which those who knew her understood as its most dangerous register. "You I compare to a man who loves his city and has found a way to love it that pays his brother-in-law. Both can be true. In this chamber both usually are." Laughter, guilty, general; Manzato himself, after a beat, tipped his head, professional to professional. "So. The commerce bench moves three amendments. One: the feast becomes a *market* holiday — the harbor works, the schools close, the children get the day, the convoys keep their contracts. Freedom was purchased for the living; the living have shifts. Two: the monument fund transfers to the Ninth District school rebuild — and before the Steward objects, I've spoken to the sculptor, who informs me children climb on everything he makes anyway, so we're proposing he build something *intended* for it. A climbing structure. Six figures. Handholds where the sculptor thinks the joke lands best."

The chamber took a moment with that image. Somewhere in the gallery a person laughed alone, loudly, then subsided.

"And the third amendment?" the Speaker asked.

"The citizenship stands as drafted." Carlotta gathered her sheet. "Some accounts you settle at face value."

The bill passed amended, three hundred and eleven to sixty-two. The newspapers split eight to four on whether the Republic had honored its founders or fleeced them, and the twelfth paper ran a cartoon of six statues holding office hours, which sold out its print run twice.

That evening Carlotta walked home along the Averardo canal by the long way, past the vault, and stopped at a café that kept a table for her family and had done since before the Republic. She ordered two coffees. She drank one. The habit had come home from Septimont in a letter three winters ago, described in a bard's looping hand — *the kettle rule, Lottie, you'd approve, it has enforcement provisions* — and Carlotta, who had built a company on the principle that a debt acknowledged is a relationship maintained, had adopted it the way she adopted all sound accounting practices: immediately, and without telling anyone what it cost her.

The second cup went cold on schedule.

"Handholds," she said to it. "You'd have made the sculptor put a slide on it."

The cup declined to argue, which was itself an argument, and she paid for both and tipped for one and went home to the settlement figures, which were, as always, waiting up for her like a dog.

## II. The Ninth Window
### *Ragunna · the Bureau of Records · a man who took dictation from God*

For eleven years, Brother Anselmo Ferri's job had been the most sacred in Ragunna: he transcribed the oracles. Every fourth morning he had knelt in the Whispering Chamber with a silver stylus and taken down the Sentinel's will as it was relayed to him through the Primus's intermediaries, in a script reserved for divinity, on paper made from reeds grown in the god's own lagoon.

For three years now, he had known that the whole of it — every syllable, every fourth morning, eleven years — had been composed two floors up by a committee of four men working from harvest reports and the Primus's enemies list.

The Republic had a word for men like Anselmo. The word was *transferable*. The Truth Commission of Year One had sorted the Order's twelve thousand personnel into the culpable, the complicit, and the clerical, and Anselmo had landed in the third bin by the breadth of a reed: he had written what he was handed. He had believed what he wrote. The Commission's report described his stratum in a sentence he had read many times: *These persons were the instrument's instrument.*

So now he worked the ninth window of the Bureau of Records, where his beautiful hand — the finest oracular script of his generation — spent its days on boat registrations.

"Name of vessel," he said.

"*Fleur's Grace,*" said the fisherman, cap in hands.

"There are forty-one *Fleur's Graces* in the registry. She'll need a district number."

"Then put — put whatever's free."

Anselmo wrote the name in the reserved script, because the Bureau's stylebook was silent on the matter of fonts and he had decided, in his second month, that silence meant permission, and that a sardine boat deserved the letterforms of heaven if those were the only letterforms he owned. The fisherman watched the ink go down and his cap went still in his hands.

"That's oracle-writing," he said.

"It's my writing," Anselmo said. "It was always my writing."

The fisherman studied him a moment — the sorting look, the one that checked a face against the Commission's bins — and then studied the registration, and then did the thing Ragunnans had invented in Year Two, the small civic sacrament nobody legislated: he decided out loud. "Well. She'll sail proud, in letters like that." And he paid the two-lira fee into the box marked FEES, where the miracle-money used to go, and left.

At the lunch hour Anselmo ate bread on the Bureau steps with Vittoria from the sixth window, who had been a tide-tax assessor for the Order and now assessed the same tide for the Republic at a lower rate, with a right of appeal, which she described as the same job with the boot removed.

"Manzato's bill passed," she said. "Amended. Your girl gutted it."

"She's hardly my girl."

"You voted for her list."

"I voted for the list that keeps the harbor open on holy days." He tore the bread. "I've *had* holy days. I wrote the sermons that closed the harbor. Do you know what a closed harbor smells like on the third day, in summer? Piety and rotting mackerel. I served a god of rotting mackerel for eleven years, Vittoria, and the worst part—" He stopped. The steps were public. Three years had taught him the new arithmetic: on these steps you could say anything about the Order, and everything you said would be believed, and the believing was the problem, because some of the men in his memory had been kind, and there was no bin for that.

"Say it," Vittoria said. "The worst part."

"The worst part is that I *miss the mornings*." He said it to the harbor. "The chamber was cold and the reeds smelled of the lagoon and for one hour, every fourth day, I was certain. Certainty is warm, Vittoria. It's the warmest thing there is. And now I have a window and a fee box and a stylebook that's silent on fonts, and every morning I have to decide, myself, on my own authority, what a sardine boat deserves." He wrapped the rest of the bread. "The girl in the glass, the friends — they gave us the truth. I'm grateful. I will be paying the truth off in installments for the rest of my life, like any large purchase."

Vittoria was quiet the way assessors are quiet, running a value.

"My cousin keeps a shrine to them," she said. "The six. Little one, windowsill. Candles."

"The Commission discourages—"

"The Commission discourages. The candles continue." She stood, brushed crumbs to the gulls, who filed their appeal instantly. "You know what she prays to them for? My cousin? She told me once. She prays: *thank you, and stay away.* Stay north, stay at school, stay off the pedestal. Because the day they come back and fix something for us is the day we stop knowing how." She squinted at him. "Certainty's warm. So's a fever."

Anselmo went back to the ninth window and registered eleven more boats, each in the letterforms of heaven, and at closing he did what he did every fourth evening, an old rhythm repurposed: he took a sheet of the cheap Bureau paper and wrote, in his own script, for no committee, one page of what he had actually seen that day. The forty-second *Fleur's Grace.* The gulls' appeal. A fever, a warmth. He had four hundred such pages now, in a drawer.

He had begun to suspect they were an oracle. The genuine kind. Assembled the only way the genuine kind ever is: one witness, writing down the day, on his own authority, unsure.

## III. The Succession Question
### *Septimont · the Ephor's house · two women and a whetstone*

The apples were early that year, and Augusta ate them the way she conducted policy, which is to say completely and beginning with the part others discard, and Galbrena watched her do it from the other end of the bench with a whetstone going in long, even strokes down a blade that had been sharp for an hour.

The bench faced the harbor. Berth Nine lay below them, empty, paid up, the mooring lines coiled and waiting in the shape of the ship they belonged to. Past it the evening fleet was coming in, and past the fleet the water went out to where the water goes.

"The Senate sent a delegation today," Augusta said.

"I saw them leave." Stroke. "Faces like men who'd bid on a lion and been sold the pricing structure."

"They moved to extend the Ephorate. Mine. Indefinitely." She turned the apple. "The phrasing was *for the duration of the present prosperity.*"

"The present prosperity being yourself."

"The present prosperity being the games reformed, the Hunt doctrine written, the Tide walls funded, the guilds at peace, and no one, in eleven years, assassinated at a dinner party." Stroke, stroke. "They've fallen in love with the weather and they'd like to legislate the season."

Galbrena set the whetstone down.

Thirty years she had watched this woman from rooftops and crowd-edges and the far rails of arenas, in every city the Blazing Sun had ever burned across, and in three years of standing in the light instead she had mapped the interior country: where the general ended and the woman began, which was a border with heavy traffic and no customs post. She knew the shape of what the Senate wanted because she had wanted it herself for three decades — the sun fixed in the sky, permanent, safe to navigate by.

"And you said."

"I tabled it. Asked for costings." A muscle moved in Augusta's jaw, which for Augusta was a soliloquy. "The costing I want doesn't come in their ledgers. I keep thinking about the wolf's city. Their magistrate drills the lower town to survive floods without her. The dragon under their lake told the priestess — this made the letters, Iuno was insufferable about it — that his people are *practicing to need him less*, and that he finds he is proud, and that the pride is inefficient and persists." She finished the apple, core and all. "I rebuilt these games so no one would ever again need to murder their way to my chair. And now the Senate proposes that no one ever sit in it again at all. I've been asking myself since noon which of those two women I am."

"Ask better." Galbrena said it the way she loosed arrows, having already watched the wind for a long time. "You're asking whether you're a tyrant. Wrong hunt. You've no appetite for it; I've watched you refuse three coronations, two of them from your own troops. Ask the true question."

"Which is."

"Whether the city can hold *without* you, or whether you've spent eleven years making yourself the keystone and calling it service." The scarred face stayed turned to the harbor. "I know that architecture. I lived in it. One load-bearing person, and everyone around them building lighter and lighter because the weight was handled. It feels like strength. It's a debt. The interest is paid by whoever's standing there when the keystone comes out."

The fleet came in. Somewhere down the hill the evening training bells rang from the fortress, and rang again, younger voices answering older ones, the two-fighter drills Augusta had written into law going through their eight-count in the dusk.

"The kids at the wall," Augusta said finally. "The intake class. Livia's cohort."

"The one who fights like you and knows it, or the one who fights like you and doesn't?"

"The second one." A pause. "I'm going to lose to her."

Galbrena turned, at that.

"The autumn exhibition. Two years, maybe three. She'll be ready and I'll be forty-nine, and I am going to take the sand against her in front of the whole hill, and I am going to fight her honestly with everything I have, and she is going to beat me, honestly, with everything I taught her, and every soul in Septimont is going to watch the sun go down in the open air and see that the morning after it, the city is *still there*." Augusta stood, rolled her shoulder, the old campaign shoulder, the one the rain reported on. "Then let the Senate try to extend a woman the whole city watched lose fair. The prosperity will have to learn to be the city's."

"You'll hate it. Losing."

"I'll hate it completely." The grin arrived, the arena grin, undimmed by any of the years. "That's how they'll know it was real."

Galbrena picked the whetstone back up, and for a while there was the harbor, and the bells, and the long even strokes, and the specific silence of two people who had said *later* at the bottom of the world and had been, ever since, methodically spending it.

"When you lose," Galbrena said. "Afterward. What does the retired sun do?"

"I've been advised," Augusta said, "by a fox, secondhand, through a priestess's footnotes, that there are theories." She looked at the empty berth, at the coiled lines. "I thought I'd start by sitting on a bench I paid for. I've held it three years for other people. I hear it faces the water."

"It does."

"Wide enough for nine, I ordered." She sat back down, closer, which for Augusta was also a soliloquy. "Turns out that leaves room."

## IV. The Pilgrim Price
### *Egla Town · the bakery · what miracles eat*

The pilgrims came to Egla by the boatload now, and Rosa had opinions about it that she kept in the same drawer as the till.

Year One had been weeping in the square and lemon cakes by the gross. Year Two had been the guidebook — some Ragunnan printer's *Walks of the Maiden*, with a map on which Rosa's bakery appeared as *Site 4: The Standing Order* — and after the guidebook came the vendors, and after the vendors came the disputes, and by Year Three the town had a Pilgrim Committee, a licensing schedule, two rival gondola cooperatives, and a shouting match every market day about whether Egla was a town with a shrine in it or a shrine with a town attached.

Mayor Wicktorio, older now and slower to the podium, put the question to the assembly in the spring: the Committee proposed to buy the field behind the Maiden's house and build a proper pilgrim hostel, forty beds, funded by a levy on miracle trade.

"The trade can bear it," said the head of the Committee, who ran the larger gondola cooperative.

"The trade," said old Cosimo from the third row, "is my niece's *front door*."

He rarely spoke at assembly. The room turned. For twenty years this man had carried lilies to a shrine every week; the town had watched him do it the way it watched the tide, and the town had also watched, three years ago, the evening a living girl with a laurel had opened that same door from the inside and Cosimo had sat down on the step with his hat off. What he thought about *Site 3: The Vigil of the Uncle* (guidebook, page 12, with engraving) he had never said in public.

He said it now.

"I've read the book. I'm in the book. There's a drawing of me in the book, and I'm holding the lilies wrong — I carried them heads-down, any gardener would, the stems drink better — but heads-up made the better picture, so heads-up I hold them, forever, on page twelve." He stood, hat in hands, and the hat rotated slowly as he spoke, an old man's prayer wheel. "Here is what I know about my niece. She sat in that house alone for ten years and made dolls so the quiet would have someone in it. And now the quiet's gone, and there's a queue at her door, and a fee to skip the queue, and last month a pilgrim offered me forty lira for one of the lilies *after* I'd laid it down. I want to say the trade is a desecration. I can't. The trade fed the Rossi family through the bad harvest. The trade put a roof on the school. The trade is forty beds of people who cried at a story that happens to be true, and their money is as good as their crying." The hat stopped. "So build the hostel. Take the levy. But the field behind the house was where she flew her kites, and I'll be asking the Committee to build around the kite-flying ground, and leave it open, and put up one sign only." He sat down. "The sign says: *she was a child here.* No fee."

The assembly voted the hostel through, the field stayed open, and the sign went up in the autumn, small, wooden, by the gate. The pilgrims, to the Committee's lasting bafflement, made it the most visited site in town. They stood at the little fence and looked at forty yards of ordinary grass, and something in the ordinariness did the work the reliquaries used to do, except in reverse: it made the story smaller, and the smallness made it enter.

Rosa watched it all from the bakery and adjusted her flour orders and kept, in the front of the book of standing orders, in her grandmother's place, the line she wrote fresh every January: *Lemon cakes, sugar crystals, extra crystals, no fork. C.*

A Committee man came in once, the guidebook printer's agent actually, with a proposal to sell "the Maiden's Own Recipe" in commemorative boxes, split the license, her name on it.

"The recipe's not mine to sell," Rosa said.

"Whose is it?"

"A promise made on flour." She weighed his order — two cakes, ordinary, tourist's fork — and made his change. "Flour outranks you. Ask anyone who's gone without."

He left thinking he'd been refused. Rosa returned to her proofing dough, satisfied, because she had told him the exact truth and the truth had walked him out the door, which was the trick her grandmother had taught her along with the crunch: the deepest things keep themselves secret by being said plainly to people who came for something else.

## V. Thirty-Seven
### *Porto Veno · the Fisalia castle · the last rite of the old house*

The girl arrived at Porto Veno on the first cold day of autumn, seventeen years old, in the grey travel clothes of the inland provinces, and told the gate she had come to take the Fisalia oath.

She was the fourth this year. The gate had instructions.

Cantarella received her in the garden, because the garden was where the Duchess received everything now — petitions, envoys, the Republic's registry commissioners, the occasional assassin, and once a hedge-cutting on a homecoming ship, planted since with her own hands beside its parent, the two of them growing toward each other across the gap with the patience of things that have seen the inside of a nothing and put out leaves anyway.

"You want the oath," Cantarella said.

"I want to serve the house that guards the truth." The girl had rehearsed; it showed the way rehearsal always shows, in the eyes going slightly upward for the lines. "I've read the Commission's volumes. The Fisalia held the real history for four hundred years while the Order lied. You poisoned your own matriarchs to keep the whispers from spreading. You paid what no one else would pay. I want—" and here the rehearsal ran out and the true thing surfaced, young and hot, "—I want to matter like that. Everyone my age got born three years too late. The war's over. The god's gone. All that's left is committees. I want a house with *weight* in it."

Camellias needed deadheading. Cantarella deadheaded one, taking her time about it, and the girl stood in the silence learning the first lesson without knowing a lesson had begun.

"Come," the Duchess said.

She walked the girl through the castle — through the halls where the family portraits hung, thirty-six matriarchs, each with the small brass plaque beneath: name, years, and for eleven of them, a second line in older brass, *released by the mercy of her successor* — and down, past the archive the Republic's registrars now catalogued three days a week under Fisalia supervision, boxes going out to Ragunna in coffins marked, in a joke only two living women fully owned, SALT — and further down, to a door the girl had read about in Volume Four.

The poison room was empty.

Shelves, scrubbed. Cabinets, open, bare. Light through a high window on stone that had been washed so many times it had gone pale in a rectangle, the shape of a workbench that had stood there for four centuries and stood there no longer.

"Volume Four ends before this," Cantarella said. "The Commission catalogued the room in Year One. In Year Two, I closed it. The stock went to the university at Ragunna, to the medical faculty, under seal, with the formularies — four hundred years of the finest toxicology on the continent, and it turns out most of the antidote chapters were better than anyone else's poison chapters, which my ancestors would have found hilarious and I find useful. Three of my cousins now lecture there. The youngest treats fishermen for pufferfish stings out of a clinic in the harbor district and complains that I gave away her inheritance." She stood in the pale rectangle. "She is correct. I did."

"But — the tradition—"

"The tradition was a tourniquet." Cantarella's voice did the thing it did, dropping its ornaments one by one until only the load-bearing structure remained. "You apply a tourniquet when the alternative is death, and you take it off the moment there's a surgeon, because a tourniquet left on kills the limb. The whispers are dormant. The girl who holds them dormant sends me letters with mud on the corners. The Order that made secrecy necessary is a records bureau with better lighting. My family held its breath for four hundred years, and three years ago the world surfaced, and I have spent those three years teaching my house how to *exhale*, which — the letters would tell you, if you ever earn the reading of them — turns out to be the harder skill." She turned. "You came here for weight. I've spent three years putting the weight *down*. So. My counter-offer."

The girl waited.

"The Republic's Registry Commission needs readers. The old archives — mine, the Order's, the Montelli's, everyone's — four hundred years of documents in six dead scripts, and the generation that reads them is dying at the usual rate. It's committee work. It's exactly the committee work you came here to escape. And it is the *actual* inheritance of this house: someone has to hold the memory honestly, out loud, in daylight, where holding it costs nothing but Tuesdays — which is the expensive currency. Any fool pays in blood; blood's over quickly. Tuesdays go on and on." A camellia head, spent, dropped into her palm. "The Order held memory in the dark and it rotted into power. My family held it in the dark and it cost us eleven matriarchs. Your generation gets to try daylight. You're right that you were born three years too late for the war. You were born exactly on time for the part the war was *for*."

The girl looked at the pale rectangle for a long time.

"Volume Four made you sound like the most dangerous woman in Rinascita," she said at last.

"Volume Four was thoroughly researched."

"And now you deadhead flowers and assign homework."

"Now I deadhead flowers," Cantarella agreed, "and assign homework, and once a month I write a letter to a school inside a giant, and once a season I inform a knight by post that her hedge technique remains disgraceful, and I am," she said it as she said all her verdicts, like a poison identified, precisely, from the taste, "the most dangerous woman in Rinascita. Danger changed careers. Try to keep up. The Commission convenes Tuesdays."

The girl took the Tuesdays.

Years on — this part belongs to a later story, but the garden knew it early, the way gardens know — the Registry's reading rooms would be staffed by a generation who called themselves, privately, with the black humor of archivists, *the Thirty-Seventh*: heirs of the Bane, sworn to a house whose oath had been rewritten in Year Three of the Republic and now ran, in full, four words long.

*Read it. Say it.*

## VI. Returns Day
### *Ragunna · the following spring · a coda with paperwork*

The Republic's fourth spring opened with an election, which the Republic conducted the way it conducted everything now: loudly, imperfectly, in twelve newspapers, with a fish market strike lasting half a day over polling-station placement, resolved by moving one polling station forty yards, which everyone involved agreed was the system functioning as designed.

Manzato held his seat. Carlotta's commerce list gained four. The market holiday — the Feast of the Ninth, first observance — fell a week after the count, and it went the way the amendment had written it: the harbor worked, the schools emptied, and by mid-morning the Ninth District's new climbing structure was invisible under children.

The sculptor had done honest work. Six figures in bronze, lifesized, arranged the way the famous woodcut had them — and then, everywhere, the handholds: the wolf's tail worn gold already at the grip, the bard's lute a ladder, the chakram a wheel that actually turned, the greatsword a slide after all (the sculptor had received, care of a Guild relay, a four-word message from the north: *put a slide. — C.K.O.T.E.D.*, and had priced the freight on that message and complied out of respect for anyone who'd pay it). The Maiden's laurel made a step. The Rover's outstretched hand, the catching hand, was polished mirror-bright by ten thousand small palms using it exactly as offered.

Anselmo Ferri came to see it on his day off, and stood at the edge of the shrieking, and found Vittoria there, eating chestnuts, watching her cousin's children scale the poisoner.

"Your girl's monument," she said.

"Hardly my girl." He accepted a chestnut. "The Commission would note that climbing on the founders is doctrinally unsound."

"The Commission's youngest daughter is currently standing on the bard's head."

They watched. Beneath the noise of it, if you knew how to listen — and Anselmo, eleven years in a whispering chamber, knew how to listen — you could hear the future doing what the future does: a five-year-old, straddling the wolf, teaching a smaller child the names, getting two of the six wrong, inventing the story's middle entirely, keeping the ending exact. *And the god said you are free, and then everybody had to go to SCHOOL.*

Close enough, Anselmo judged. Closer than his oracles had ever run.

He went home the long way, past the Bureau, and let himself in — Sunday-quiet, the ninth window shuttered — and took his four hundred pages out of the drawer and, at last, after three years of suspecting what they were, wrote the title page. He wrote it in the letterforms of heaven, because they were his and he could spend them where he liked:

*THE DAYS. Witnessed by one clerk, on his own authority, unsure. Volume One.*

Then he added the line the whole Republic ran on, the line under the line, the two working words and the ones everyone had learned to hear after them, the way you hear the fourth beat in a bar of three:

*Lead well.*

*(You. Yes, you. Tuesdays.)*

And the harbor stayed open, and the tide came in assessed at the lower rate, and northward past the weather five files hummed ATTESA on a shelf beside a stamp that said TORNATA, waiting in the drawer with the patience of the second half of any true sentence — while in the city the god had freed with its death rattle, the shrieking off the bronze went up into the spring air, which is the sound a fairy tale makes when it survives contact with the ground:

children, climbing all over it, wearing it smooth, using the outstretched hand.

# Someone Should

*a ricercare, for three travelers*

> *Twelfth of Hollowtide. Wind fresh from the west. Pilgrim's Sail, out of Ragunna. One aboard.*
> *Singing.*
>
> — the fourth book, in a lamplighter's hand, formed the way they teach you in case the log should ever matter to a court

---

## i. Nine Registries

Peace lasted eleven weeks, and Cartethyia answered it with correspondence.

It began with a question about drainage. Lahai-Roi had a Bureau of Subterranean Waters, and the Bureau had a public window, and the woman at the public window made the error of being helpful. Within a month Cartethyia had a folder. Within two she had a system, and the system had a shelf, and the shelf had a small brass plate on it reading **PENDING** in her own block capitals, because a thing with a name cannot be lost.

Iuno stopped by the table on the ninth morning of it and read three envelopes upside down.

"Ragunna. Ragunna. Mingting."

"Nine registries," Cartethyia said, without looking up. "Nine answered."

"You wrote to eleven."

"Two are slow. Porto-Veno is slow on purpose, I think it's a family trait." She squared a page against the table edge with her thumbs. "The Mengzhou frontier court writes beautifully. They enclosed a form I didn't ask for and a note apologising for the form."

"And what are you filing, exactly."

"Enquiries."

"Into?"

Cartethyia turned the page over, which was an answer.

Iuno poured herself the last of the pot and sat down, which she did rarely and always meant something by. Across the common room Amy had the record player open and was arguing with Rebecca about whether a needle could be sharpened with a whetstone. Somebody in the corridor was carrying laundry and losing.

"You get up at four," Iuno said.

"The light's good."

"There is no light. It's a cavern with a schedule." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on the wood. "You walked the east perimeter twice on Tuesday."

"There was a broken bollard."

"There was."

"Somebody should look at those."

Iuno let that sit long enough for Cartethyia to hear it come out of her own mouth.

"I like it here," Cartethyia said, to the letters.

"I know."

"I'm not saying it as a complaint. Mei sleeps. Amy's *loud.* Lupa wears a paper crown to breakfast and dares the household to comment." She stacked three envelopes that were already stacked. "It's the best room I've ever been in."

"And?"

"And I've been in a good room before." Her hands stopped. "For twenty years. I got very good at it."

The kettle ticked, cooling.

"You know what nobody tells you," Cartethyia said. "You can furnish a lifeboat until it stops being a boat."

Iuno considered her over the rim of the cup, doing the arithmetic she did about everyone, which never finished and which she kept doing anyway.

"Take the bard," she said, and stood.

---

The notice arrived on the fourteenth morning, in a bundle from the Office of Coastal Property (Provisional), Ragunna, which had answered a query about lighthouse maintenance schedules with the enthusiasm of a young department discovering that somebody, somewhere, was interested.

Cartethyia read it standing up. Then she sat down and read it again with her finger under the line.

> **Schedule 4 — Structures, decommissioned, Order of the Deep.**
> Item 61. Fire tower, Capo Congedo. Fabric sound. Lamp assembly serviceable, to be recovered.
> Contents: bedding; cookware; glass (broken); sailcloth volumes various, unbound, damp — *for disposal.*
> Assessment party to attend before the close of Saltmonth.

She found Ciaccona on the dormitory roof with the lute across her knees and one peg between her teeth, doing the small tortured business of bringing a mended instrument up to pitch. It had been broken by a playwright and mended. Broken by a god and mended. Shattered in the dark at the bottom of everything and put back together by a wolf, a poisoner, a doll-maker and a great deal of arguing, and it would never be right again, and she liked the sound of it better.

"How far south is Capo Congedo?"

Ciaccona's hand stopped on the peg.

She took her time getting the peg out of her mouth. She set it in the little groove of the soundboard where she kept things. Then she looked up, and her face was doing the thing it did in public, which was nothing at all.

"Four days from Ragunna if the coast road holds. Why."

Cartethyia held out the paper.

Ciaccona read it. The wind came over the parapet and moved the edge of the page and she flattened it down with two fingers without noticing she had.

"Sailcloth volumes various," she said.

"Damp."

"Forty years of damp." She handed the notice back. "Somebody in an office decided that's a category."

"They also decided the lamp is worth a cart and the books are worth a fire."

"That's what an office is." Ciaccona picked up the peg again. "It's an honest error. He sewed them himself out of packing paper. They look like rubbish. They *are* rubbish, structurally. It's the contents that aren't."

"Then say so to them."

"To whom, precisely."

"The Office of Coastal Property, Provisional." Cartethyia produced a second sheet. "I've drafted a letter. It's very polite. It has a reference number in it, which I understand does most of the work."

Ciaccona looked at the letter. She looked at the woman holding it, who had walked up four flights carrying a cane she did not strictly require, with a finch carved into the head of it, and who was standing the way she stood when a thing was about to happen whether or not it was invited.

"You could have asked."

"You'd have said no."

"Then you'd have asked twice."

"I'm asking twice now. This is the second one." Cartethyia sat down on the cold roof beside her, arranging the cane across her knees like a sword laid out for cleaning. "Cia. It's a *filing deadline.*"

"It is."

"The most boring thing in the world is going to eat the last honest record of your father, and the fix is a form." She was almost laughing. "I've waited my whole life for this."

Ciaccona set the lute down.

She looked out over the terraces at the false sun coming up on its committee schedule, gold across bioluminescent grass, a sky that admitted to being built.

"The Order's gone," Cartethyia said, quieter.

"Three years."

"The chalk's gone. Nobody's watching a door to see who stands near you."

"I know what year it is."

"Then I'm going to say the thing and you can be furious at me for a day, which I've budgeted for." Cartethyia set her hand flat on the roof between them, the way you put a coin on a table. "The dark you gave your uncle expired. And you didn't notice, because you've been holding the door shut with your body for so long you think you're a wall."

The wind moved across the roof, taking things with it, as it does.

"His ships rounded that headland," Cartethyia said. "Whatever name he was under. Coastal freight, working steady, for years. Tammaro logged every hull that turned that corner for forty years and never once asked what it was for." A breath. "The record exists. It exists for eleven more days and then it's a bonfire on a rock."

Ciaccona picked at a thread on her cuff that had been there for a decade.

"And if I read it and he isn't in it?"

"Then he isn't in it, and you'll have looked, and looking is different from not looking, and you'll know which one you did." Cartethyia knocked the cane once against the parapet. "Also I have never seen the sea from a great height while legally permitted to do so and I'd very much like to."

"You dive gravity wells for entertainment."

"That's a hobby. This is *work.*" She stood, holding out a hand. "Bring the lute. There's a form to file and a woman to swear to it, and I'm told the only living witness plays a bit."

Ciaccona took the hand.

Down in the corridor, Mei looked at the two of them going past with a pack each and said, "How long?"

"Three weeks," said Cartethyia.

"Six," said Ciaccona.

Mei nodded slowly, and let them get four steps, and called after them, "Bring me back something stupid."

---

*Third of Saltmonth. Wind north-east, hard. Ketch, deep-laden, southbound, running under a scrap of jib. Two aboard on deck and I did not envy them. The cook came out at eight bells and shook a cloth at the tower, which I take to be manners.*

## ii. The Long-Way Girl

They met the witch at Sanguetta Landing, where the ferry from the north put its passengers down in the rain and the coast road began, and where there was one inn, and the inn had one room going, and in the room somebody was murdering a viola.

Cartethyia stopped in the doorway with rain running off the brim of her hood.

"That's a *crime.*"

"That's a viola tuned to a standard nobody's used in ninety years," Ciaccona said, "being played by somebody who was taught properly and doesn't practise." She unslung the lute case and shook it off. "Which is worse, actually. Somebody spent money on that."

The player was young — nineteen at the most — ash-grey hair, black travelling cloak steaming gently by the fire, a wide-brimmed hat set upside down on the bench beside her with a star-shaped brooch on the crown, and a broomstick leaned in the corner with the confidence of an object that expects to be left alone. She had a folk tune by the throat and was losing.

Around her, six fishermen and a woman with a basket of eels were correcting her.

"The turn goes *up,*" said the eel woman.

"It goes down. My teacher wrote it down."

"Your teacher's never been here."

"My teacher has never been anywhere." The girl adjusted her fingering with visible resentment and played the turn upward and it came out worse and the whole table made a noise. "There. Now it's ugly *and* wrong."

"That's ours, though," said one of the fishermen, satisfied. "That's how it goes here."

Ciaccona stood inside the door and watched this happen and did not move for a while.

"She's not doing it on purpose," Cartethyia said.

"No."

"Is it working anyway?"

"They've given her four verses in six minutes and she's got a pencil behind her ear." Ciaccona finally set the case down. "It's working perfectly. She just hasn't been told what it's called."

They got the room. There was one bed and a floor and an argument about which of them had the worse back, which Cartethyia won by lying, and by the time they came down again the viola had been put away and the girl had a bowl of something and a journal open beside it and was writing with the speed of someone who had learned to write before the room changed its mind.

Cartethyia sat down across from her without asking.

"You came in humming," she said.

The girl's pencil stopped. "I hum at doors."

"Four bars. Same four bars, twice, and then you stepped over the sill."

"That's a very specific thing to have noticed."

"I have an excellent ear and no ability whatsoever," Cartethyia said, "so it all goes into storage."

Ciaccona arrived with three cups she had not been asked to bring and set one down in front of the girl, which is a thing bards do to buy the first four minutes of anybody's evening.

"Who taught you the four bars?"

"A woman who would take that question as an accusation."

"Good answer. Whose is it?"

"Hers." The girl closed her journal on the pencil. "She said it introduces me. As hers."

"Mm." Ciaccona sat. "It does. It's a signature, and it's a good one, and she meant it to be recognised by exactly the sort of person who'd recognise it." She drank. "Which is a lonely way to send somebody off, incidentally. She'll have known that."

The girl looked at her for a moment with an expression that had a great deal of travel in it.

"Elaina," she said.

"Ciaccona. That's Cartethyia."

"I know," said Elaina.

"You do?"

"You're the six." She turned her cup a half-circle on the table, casual, and only somebody watching for it would have caught the small pleasure she took in having got there first. "There's a fresco of you in Lahai-Roi. Terrible likeness. Your hair's the only accurate thing in it." A tilt of the head at Cartethyia. "Yours has a laurel with the wrong number of points."

"How many did they give me?"

"Nine."

"That's generous," Cartethyia said. "I'll allow it."

"I live under a glacier three days from your dormitory. I've been away eleven weeks and everybody within four hundred miles has an opinion about all of you." Elaina picked up her spoon again. "The interesting one is that nobody agrees on which of you did what. Ask five people who cut the strings and you get five answers and two of the answers are the same person described differently."

Ciaccona smiled into her cup. "Welcome to my industry."

"It's not an industry, it's an infestation."

"Say that in Ragunna and see who buys you dinner."

They talked. It was easy in the way it is when three people have all learned to talk to strangers professionally and recognise the technique in each other inside a quarter of an hour. Elaina was going south, and then west, and then south again: a bookseller in Mingting who owed a folio and forty years of interest; a harbour mole in Ragunna where she was to stand at evening and write down the colour of the water for a woman who had been exiled from it; a letter, sealed twice, addressed to a place rather than a person.

"The address is *South*," she said. "That's the whole address."

"How will you know the door?"

"She said it sings."

"Then you'll know the door," said Ciaccona.

Elaina made a note. It took two seconds and she did it without breaking eye contact, which was a small unnerving skill.

"And what do you carry for yourself?" Cartethyia asked.

"Beg pardon?"

"You've listed four errands and they all belong to other people."

Elaina's pencil paused.

"That's not a polite question," she said.

"No. I'm told I ask those." Cartethyia leaned an elbow on the table. "You don't have to answer it."

There was a moment where the girl visibly chose, and it was a longer moment than she wanted it to be.

"A name," she said. "I collect what people call somebody. Every port, every village. Every word — the frightened ones and the fond ones. She asked for all of them and she said she'd take them all and she meant it, and I've filled nineteen pages." She flattened her hand on the journal. "That one's mine, more or less. It was given to me as a chore and I stopped treating it as one somewhere around the Thessaleo coast."

"Who is she?"

"Not mine to hand out."

"Fair."

"You'd get it wrong anyway. Everyone does. That's rather the project." Elaina set the pencil down. "And you two? A bard and a knight in the rain at a ferry landing in Saltmonth, going south with one bag each."

"There's a filing deadline," Cartethyia said.

Elaina waited.

"That's genuinely it," said Ciaccona. "A clerk wrote *for disposal* next to forty years of somebody's life and we're going to argue with him about it in writing."

Elaina looked from one of them to the other.

"I've come nine hundred miles," she said, "and that is the single most frightening errand anyone has described to me."

"Isn't it," said Cartethyia, delighted.

---

They went south together the next morning, which nobody proposed. Elaina had a broom and could have been in Ragunna in a day and a half. She walked instead, at their pace, with the viola strapped across her back complaining at every step, and by the second afternoon she had stopped pretending she was going the same way by coincidence.

On the road she asked Cartethyia about the cane.

"Long days," Cartethyia said. "There's a hinge in me that stops agreeing after about nine hours. A doctor recommended it and I ignored her for a season and then it rained."

"There's a bird on it."

"A finch. She's called Tuesday."

Elaina stopped walking.

"You named your cane."

"I named the *finch.* The cane is a cane."

"Why Tuesday?"

Cartethyia glanced sideways at Ciaccona, who was ahead of them by three paces and had gone conspicuously deaf.

"There's a joke," Cartethyia said. "It's not mine to hand out."

"Oh, well *played.*"

They walked a while. Gulls went over. The land here was low and green-gold and stitched with drystone, and every quarter-mile there was a shrine niche in a wall with the plaster patched where something had been chiselled out of it.

"Where I trained," Elaina said eventually, "your name is your teacher's to give. You don't choose it. You receive it." She said it the way you set down a stone you have carried a long way to see whether anyone picks it up. "Mine came from something untidy she happened to love."

"I chose mine," Cartethyia said, "and hid it in my chest for twenty years so nothing could reach it."

"That's — " Elaina stopped. "That's not how it works."

"It worked."

"You can't just—"

"I made a hundred more, later, and gave them all away to dolls, and every one of them was a rope home." Cartethyia stepped over a puddle without slowing. "Somebody has to go first. That's all *forever* is."

Elaina's head came round very fast.

"Where did you hear that?"

"Hear what?"

"That sentence."

"I said it. Just now. Out of my mouth."

Elaina walked twenty paces in silence with her hand on the strap of her pack.

"Someone I know says that," she said. "Almost exactly."

"Then she's right too," said Cartethyia, and went on to tell her about the drainage bureau.

---

*Nineteenth of Sowing. Fog to the mast-tops all morning, lifting at noon. Nothing rounded until the afternoon, and then eleven sail in three hours, all of them impatient, like a queue at a door that has just opened. A gull sat on the rail all day and left when the fog did. I have decided he is a coast pilot.*

## iii. Sordina

They came into Sordina at midday because the coach ford was up and there was nothing else to do with the afternoon.

It was a mussel-shell town on a tidal creek, four hundred souls, a church with its sea-god scraped off the tympanum and a fresh painted sun put up in the gap. There was a market. There was a fish stall doing brisk work at the end of the market. And there was a small grey office beside the fish stall with a hatch in the wall and a queue of five people at the hatch, in the rain, waiting.

Cartethyia read the sign over the hatch.

**OFFICE OF PUBLIC COMPORTMENT — SORDINA — HOURS 9 TO 4.**

"Somebody named this town on purpose," Ciaccona said.

"Somebody named it four hundred years ago and it's been meaning something different every century since." Elaina had her journal out already. "What's a comportment office?"

Cartethyia had gone still in the way she went still on watch.

"It fines you for grieving," she said.

Elaina looked up.

"The week's allowance was seven days," Cartethyia said. "Mourn past it in public and you troubled the waters. Doubted the mercy. There was a scale of fines and there was a chit and you kept the chit."

"That's an Order law."

"Yes."

"The Order fell three years ago."

"Yes," said Cartethyia, and started walking.

The queue parted for her the way queues do for someone moving at the wrong speed. Behind the hatch sat a man of seventy-odd in a good coat gone shiny at the elbows, with a ruled book open in front of him and a tin of pen-nibs and a small brass scale he was not using for anything.

He was writing a chit. He wrote it beautifully.

"Name," he said, to the woman at the hatch, without looking up.

"Bea Marotta."

"Day."

"Ninth."

He made two marks. "Two shells."

The woman put two shells on the ledge. He swept them into a drawer and tore the chit along a ruled line and handed it out, and she took it, and folded it, and put it in her pocket with the ease of long practice, and moved off into the rain.

Cartethyia put her hand flat on the ledge.

"Who told you to keep doing this?"

The clerk looked up. He had watery, exact eyes and the profoundly untroubled expression of a man who has never in his life been asked that.

"Nobody's told me to stop," he said.

"The Order's gone."

"The Order was the reason. The office is the office." He turned the book a fraction, squaring it. "I'm on the town roll, not the diocese. They pay me out of the Thursday market rents."

"And the fines?"

"Almshouse. Have done for two years, since the diocesan account closed." He nodded past her shoulder, and past the fish stall, at a low building with a good new roof and geraniums in the windows. "Eleven residents. Two of them here from Riva since the flood."

Cartethyia's hand stayed on the ledge.

Behind her, quietly, Ciaccona said, "There it is."

Elaina had come up on the other side and was reading the ruled book upside down, which she also did well.

"You've a column for *cause*," she said.

"Have to. Can't fine a man for shouting in the street and call it grief. It's a different schedule." The clerk turned the book properly toward her, obliging, unhurried, a craftsman shown an interest. "Cause, and relation, and day. That's the three."

Elaina read down the page.

*Marotta, Bea. Ninth. Son. Wept, fish market.*
*Ferro, Tullio. Fifteenth. Wife. Sang in the street, night.*
*Ferro, Tullio. Twenty-second. Wife. Sang in the street, night.*
*Ferro, Tullio. Thirty-first. Wife. Sang in the street, night.*

"He comes every week," the clerk said, following her eye. "Same corner. I've told him three times he can do it indoors and he says the acoustic's poor."

Elaina turned the page. Then another. Then she stopped turning and put her palm flat on the paper the way you steady something.

"How far back does this go?"

"This one's from the spring. There's forty-one before it."

"Forty-one books."

"I started at twenty-nine." He said it without weight. "You get a hand for it."

Elaina lifted her head and looked at Cartethyia, and Cartethyia, who had come to this hatch to break something and had been standing there for a minute and a half doing nothing at all, looked back.

"Say it," Cartethyia said.

"You know what he's got."

"Say it out loud. He's the one who needs to hear it."

Elaina turned to the clerk, and her voice changed — it lost the dryness, and what came out underneath was young, and clear, and had been trained by five women who argued about jam.

"Sir. You have the only list in this country of who loved whom."

The clerk blinked.

"Nobody wrote them down," Elaina said. "Not the church, not the register — the register has births and deaths and marriages and it tells you nothing about anybody. You have forty-two volumes of people who could not stop, and who they could not stop about, and how long past permission they went." She turned the book around so he could see his own page. "Tullio Ferro has sung on a corner for his wife every week for a year and paid for the privilege every time, and there is one document in the world that proves it, and you wrote it."

There was a silence at the hatch. The rain went on doing the small work of rain.

"It's a fines ledger," the clerk said.

"It's a *ledger of love with a charge on it,*" Elaina said. "Which is the most Rinascitan object I have ever seen and I've been here nine days."

Ciaccona made a sound behind her hand.

The clerk looked down at his own handwriting for a while.

"Tullio's wife was called Nerina," he said, at last, to nobody. "She kept the ferry books. She used to correct my dates."

He put the pen down.

"I'd want a paper," he said. "To stop. Somebody in Ragunna would have to send a paper, or the rents fail and the almshouse goes to the diocese account, and the diocese account is a hole in the ground."

Cartethyia's whole face changed.

"*Now* we're talking," she said, and pulled a folder out of her pack in the rain.

---

They were an hour and ten minutes at the hatch. Cartethyia drafted; Elaina copied fair, because her hand was faster and better and she was smug about it; the clerk supplied the reference numbers, of which he knew a frightening quantity by heart, and grew visibly younger with every one. The instrument they built was three paragraphs long. It repealed nothing. It converted the Sordina comportment schedule into a voluntary subscription toward the almshouse, retained the ledgers under the clerk's care as *records of the town*, and requested — in a fourth paragraph Cartethyia added at the end, with her tongue between her teeth — that the forty-two volumes be inspected before disposal by any office proposing it, *on the grounds that their apparent character and their actual character differ.*

"You put that in for later," Ciaccona said.

"I put that in for eight days from now."

Outside, at the fish stall, a woman began to cry over a crate of herring and stopped herself with the reflex of a whole province.

Cartethyia turned around.

"It's fine," she called. "It's the eleventh day and it's fine."

The woman stared at her.

"He's off duty," Cartethyia said, and pointed at the hatch, where the clerk — after a pause that would have been visible from the church tower — closed his ruled book and set the pen across it.

The woman looked at the closed book. Then at the herring. Then she put her face in her hands and made a sound that had been waiting in her for a while, and the eel woman put an arm round her, and the fish stall went on selling fish around them the way a town does.

Ciaccona watched with her hands in her pockets.

"You didn't sing it," Cartethyia said, beside her.

"No."

"You wanted to."

"I wanted to *very* much. It's a magnificent little atrocity and it would go beautifully in three verses." Ciaccona watched the fish stall. "And then the song's called *Sordina*, and Sordina is the town where they tax the widows, and the fish-buyers up the coast get a feeling about the place, and in five years there's a chalk mark on the whole harbour."

"And the almshouse."

"And the almshouse." She turned. "It'll get sung eventually. Somebody who doesn't live here will do it and it'll be a great success. I'd just rather it wasn't me, this week, with the paper still wet."

Behind them, at the hatch, Elaina was asking the clerk a question in a low voice, and writing down his answer.

"What did you get?" Cartethyia asked her, later, on the road out.

"A name." Elaina did not look up from the journal. "For the deep. I ask everywhere. He gave me the fishermen's word here."

"Which is?"

Elaina hesitated, and then said it, because it was too good to keep.

"*La Governante.*" She cleared her throat. "The Housekeeper. Because she keeps everything and puts nothing back where you left it."

Cartethyia laughed out loud.

Elaina did not. She walked another hundred paces with her thumb pressed into the page, and her face had gone somewhere else entirely, and neither of the others asked, because both of them knew the look of a person who has just been handed something addressed to somebody they love.

---

*Second of Frostbreak. Clear, cold, wind off the land. Lugger *Camelia*, northbound light, and a fiddle going on her deck the whole way round the point, and the wind took it out to sea so I got the top of it only. Two strings short by the sound of it and he played it anyway. He has come by four times this winter and I look out for him now, which is unprofessional.*

## iv. Slack Water

The coast road ended eleven miles short of the headland, which the Office of Coastal Property (Provisional) had declined to mention.

It ended in a slump of red earth and gorse where the winter had taken forty yards of it into the sea, and the detour inland added two days, and the tide table nailed to the shed at Riva said low water at four minutes past four.

"We go round the bottom," Cartethyia said.

"We go round the bottom," Ciaccona agreed, looking at the tide table with the resignation of a woman raised on a coast.

Elaina looked at both of them and then at the broom.

"I could carry one of you."

"You could carry one of us to a headland and leave the other one on a beach in the dark," Ciaccona said, "and then the one on the beach is *me*, because Cartethyia will simply walk into the sea to prove a point."

"I would walk *along* the sea."

"You would try."

They went round the bottom.

It was three hours of black wet rock and weed like spilled hair, and shelves that ran flat and generous for two hundred yards and then stopped over water the colour of a bruise. Elaina flew a stretch of it, twice, out of vanity and then out of practicality, ferrying the packs across a gut where the water was working. Ciaccona took the whole thing at the same measured pace she took everything, testing footing with the toe of her boot, humming under her breath in a key that changed to suit the ground.

Cartethyia went up.

She was on the higher rocks within ten minutes and stayed there, moving along the fault-lines and the ledges at a height that made Ciaccona stop looking at her, with the cane hooked over her back and both hands free and her hair going in the wind like a flag, and every so often she stopped dead and stood on some absurd tooth of stone above forty feet of surf with her arms out and shouted a fact at them.

"There's a seal!"

"Lovely," Ciaccona called up.

"There's an *arch!* There's a whole arch here, it's got a beach *inside* it—"

"Come down or go up, don't hover."

"I'm not hovering, I'm *reconnoitring*—"

Elaina, hovering, said, "She's very happy."

"She's a windmill in a country with no knights," Ciaccona said. "This is the first week in three months she's turned."

They came round the last shoulder at ten past four and there it was: the headland going up in one long ochre lift out of the water, and the tower on the crown of it, squat and white-washed and streaked, with its lamp gallery black against a sky the colour of the inside of a shell.

And below the tower, at the foot, a shelf of flat rock running out into the sea, with pools in it, and the water lying on it dead calm at the turn of the tide.

Ciaccona stopped walking.

Cartethyia came down off the high line in about eight seconds, which was not a route so much as an argument with gravity, and landed beside her, and looked where she was looking, and said nothing.

Slack water. The whole coast holding its breath between one direction and the other.

"There?" Cartethyia said.

"About twenty feet left of where you're standing." Ciaccona's voice was ordinary. "There's a lip. I sat on the lip."

Elaina set down on the shelf behind them, quietly, and had the sense not to speak.

Cartethyia walked out to the lip and looked into the water. It was three feet deep and absolutely clear and full of small pink weed moving in a rhythm that had nothing to do with the wind.

"You asked it for your father," she said, "and it gave you everybody."

"It gave me everybody at once, at full volume, in one chord."

"And you woke up bleeding with an old man hauling you by the collar."

"And fish soup."

Cartethyia crouched at the edge with her forearms on her knees.

"Do you want to ring it?"

"No."

"All right."

"That was fast."

"You said no." Cartethyia put one hand into the water up to the wrist. "I asked because you might have wanted to and been embarrassed about the wanting. You didn't want to. That's the whole conversation."

Ciaccona stood behind her with her hands in her pockets and something moved across her face and went away again.

The water was cold, and Cartethyia's hand in it looked disconnected from her, wavering, pale, twenty years ago.

Somewhere far down under the flat calm, in the part of her that had been grown rather than born, something turned over in its sleep and settled again, the way a sea in a shell does when you put the shell down.

She noticed. She noted it the way you note a hinge.

Then she started humming.

It was, by any technical standard, atrocious. It went up where an honest melody goes down, changed key twice for no reason and once out of spite, and had no fixed address in any tuning used on any coast. She hummed it into the flat water at slack tide for about ninety seconds, patiently, the way you pour something out.

Ciaccona watched her do it.

"You're paying it back," she said.

"Mm."

"Twenty years of your humming went out of that tower through the seams and into the wind and got filed, and you never consented to a syllable of it."

"So this bit I'm giving on purpose." Cartethyia stood up and shook the water off her hand. "It's the same tune. It's just mine when I hand it over."

Elaina, behind them, had her journal out and had written nothing.

"You're not writing," Cartethyia said.

"I'm deciding whether that goes under people or phenomena."

"What did you decide?"

"People," said Elaina, and wrote it down.

They went up the cliff path in the last of the light, and above them, one window at a time, the tower lit.

---

*Twenty-eighth of Longlight. Flat calm, haze. Nothing rounded all day. A girl came up the path with a lute and I gave her the fourth book and she read it standing, and then she went down to the rocks, which I ought to have prevented, and I did not, because she had the face of somebody owed an answer. The sea gave her what it gives. I have written this down so that I do not tell it wrong later. I will tell it wrong later.*

---

## v. The Keeper

The keeper of the light at Capo Congedo was twenty-seven years old, had a hand-rolled cigarette she never lit, and opened the door with a knife in the other hand because she had been gutting mackerel.

"You're the paper people," she said.

Cartethyia looked at Ciaccona.

"They wrote to me," the keeper said. "The office. To say an assessment party's coming, and to say don't obstruct." She stood back to let them in. "Three of you in the dark with packs and no cart. So either you're early and lost, or you're not them."

"We're not them," said Cartethyia.

"Good. There's fish."

Her name was Nunzia. The room at the base of the tower was low and warm and had been arranged by somebody who lived alone and had opinions: a stove, a table with one leg shimmed by a folded chart, a shelf of jars, a rope-work fender hung on the wall for no reason anybody could see, and along the whole of the north side, floor to ceiling, on plank shelves furred green at the ends, the books.

Sailcloth. Packing paper. Sewn spines gone hairy. Forty-nine of them.

Ciaccona stopped in the middle of the floor.

"Forty-nine," she said.

"Forty-one are his. Eight are mine." Nunzia went back to the mackerel. "Sit down, you're in my light."

They sat down. Elaina took her hat off, which she did rarely, and put it on her knee.

"You knew him," Ciaccona said.

"Six years." Nunzia's knife went along a belly and out. "I came at nineteen and he was already old and he died five winters back, in the chair you're sitting in, in the afternoon, with the kettle on."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be, it was a very good death, he'd been rude about the weather all morning." She scraped the board into a bucket. "I buried him up the slope where he could see the point. The stone's a bit of ballast off a wreck. He'd have said that was showy and then he'd have liked it."

"Why did you come?" Cartethyia asked.

Nunzia set the knife down.

"My sister went out on a Sail," she said. "Fifteen years ago this spring. Piera. She was twenty-four and she copied out songs, which was the whole crime, and the Order gave her a beautiful boat and no water and the sea's own judgment." She wiped her hands. "And there's a man on a rock who writes down every ship that rounds the point. So I walked here from Riva at nineteen and asked him to let me read."

"And?"

"She isn't in it."

The stove ticked.

"I read every page from the spring of that year to the autumn," Nunzia said. "Twice. Then I read the whole four years around it in case he'd got a date wrong, which he never did. She isn't there." She lifted the pan onto the heat. "Which means she went down inside the reach, before the point, where the pilots still had her. Or she went round in the fog on the eleventh, when the fourth book has *nothing rounded, thick all day.* Or he was asleep. He was seventy-two. He slept."

"What do you think?" said Elaina.

"I think it doesn't matter which," Nunzia said, "and I spent a year finding that out, which is why I'm still here."

She turned around and leaned back against the stove rail.

"Because the second year I stopped looking for Piera and started looking at the *book.* And the book has date, weather, rig, heading. Forty-one volumes of it. And it has not one name of one person on one deck, because he wasn't logging people, he was logging traffic, and it never once occurred to him — " she stopped, and started again, more fairly, "— it occurred to him about *once*, and I'll show you where, and after that it never occurred to him again."

"So you added a column," Cartethyia said.

Nunzia looked at her properly for the first time.

"I added a column."

"*Who was aboard.*"

"Who was aboard, and where they were out of, and what they were called if I could get it. I go down to Riva twice a week and I stand about in the chandlery being a nuisance and I write down names." She shrugged one shoulder. "Eight years of it. Two thousand-odd. It's no use to me, obviously. Piera's fifteen years the wrong side of it."

"It's for the next one," said Ciaccona.

"It's for the next one." Nunzia turned the fish. "Somebody should."

Ciaccona put both hands over her mouth for a second and took them away again.

"What," said Nunzia.

"Nothing. You said something in his words."

"I say everything in his words. I've had eight years and one book of somebody else's sentences." She served the fish. "Eat."

---

They ate. Elaina asked good questions, which was her craft, and got the lamp's oil consumption and the winter storm counts and the arrangement by which Riva sent a boy up with flour, and wrote all of it down. Cartethyia asked worse questions and got better answers, which was hers.

Then, over the second pot, Nunzia said, "You want the books."

"We want them not burned," said Cartethyia.

"Same thing at this range." She poured. "You'll want to know they're worth it, then. Everyone always wants a story out of them and there isn't one, they're a traffic log, it's the most boring object in Rinascita."

"That's the best thing anybody's said to me all year," Cartethyia said.

"You're strange."

"I've been told."

Nunzia sat back with her cup.

"There's one," she said. "One story. He told it maybe six times in six years, which for him was a hymn. You'll have it if you like. It's the only bit of this rock that anybody down in Riva knows."

Elaina's pencil moved.

Ciaccona said, "Go on, then."

And Nunzia told it.

"Years back — before me, ten, twelve — a girl came up the path with a lute on her back. Girls used to come here for one reason. Their people went out on the Sails and the tower's the last thing on the map that saw them." She turned the cup in her hands. "He gave her the book and she found the line. One line. A date, a wind, one aboard, and the word *singing.*"

The stove ticked. Cartethyia's hand had gone flat on the table.

"And she went down to the rocks at slack water," Nunzia said, "and she rang the sea."

"Rang it."

"That's how he said it. Rang it, like a bell, because she was some kind of Resonator and the water keeps everything." Nunzia's voice had changed a little; she had told this before and knew where the beats were and did not hurry them. "And the whole sea answered her at once. Every drowned voice since the first shore, all together, one chord, and it put her flat on her back with blood coming out of her ear. And he went down and got her by the collar and dragged her up and fed her soup."

"That," said Ciaccona, "is a warning."

"That's how he told it." Nunzia nodded. "That's exactly how he told it, six times, and always with the soup at the end, because the soup was the moral. *The water's a grave, girl. Graves keep everything and give back nothing. That's what makes them graves.*"

"And?"

"And that's not how it's told in Riva."

Ciaccona sat very still.

"In Riva," said Nunzia, "the sea gave him back."

Elaina's pencil stopped.

"Three days later," Nunzia said, "on the beach below the chapel. A man came up out of the water with the tide behind him and stood in the shallows, and it was the girl's father, and he'd been gone six years."

Nobody spoke.

"And she was there on the sand because she'd waited three days," Nunzia said, "and he walked up to her out of the water, and he looked at her, and the first thing he said — " she smiled, without knowing what she was doing, the smile of a woman handing over the good part — "was *have you eaten?*"

The rain touched the window once and went away.

"That's the line everybody knows," Nunzia said. "Kids in Riva say it to each other as a joke. You come in soaked from anywhere and somebody says *have you eaten* and everybody laughs."

Cartethyia was looking at Ciaccona. Elaina was looking at Cartethyia looking at Ciaccona and had stopped writing altogether.

"Do you believe it?" Ciaccona said.

Nunzia thought about it, honestly, taking her time, which was the thing about her.

"No," she said. "He told it the other way and he was there."

"But."

"But I've been on this rock nineteen — eight years, and I'll be on it thirty more, over water that took my sister and gave me back a blank page in a book." She turned her cup. "And down the hill there's a version where it gives something back. And it costs me nothing to let it stand. So I let it stand."

"And when someone asks you which is true?"

"I say the sea's a grave and the soup was real." Nunzia shrugged. "And they nod, and they go home, and they tell their kids the other one. Because that's the one that's any use to them."

Ciaccona put her cup down very carefully, as though it were full.

"You're a bard," she said.

"I'm a lamplighter."

"You're the second-best bard I've met this year."

"Who's the first?"

"A woman in a grain town who's been dead nineteen years."

Nunzia snorted and got up to see to the lamp.

At the door she stopped, and said, over her shoulder, in the tone of somebody remembering a bit of business, "The girl's supposed to have played it, after. The song. There's meant to be a song about it and I've never heard anybody sing one, so I expect that part's rubbish."

And she went up the stairs.

The three of them sat in the warm low room with forty-nine books along the north wall.

"Well," said Elaina, after a while, in a voice pitched with some care, "that was a great deal of soup."

Cartethyia stood up, went round the table, and put her hand on the back of Ciaccona's neck the way you steady something that has been carried too far.

"One cry per grief," Ciaccona said, to the table. "I've had this one."

"I know."

"I had it behind a tavern, into a rain barrel, about eleven years ago."

"I know." Cartethyia's thumb moved once. "This is a different grief, though. This is a new one. There's a fresh allowance."

Ciaccona laughed, which came out wrong, and put her hand over Cartethyia's on the back of her neck and left it there for about four seconds, which for her was a very long time.

Then she said, "Have you eaten?"

And Cartethyia said, "I've had three fish," and the room came back.

---

Later, on the gallery, Elaina found Ciaccona leaning on the rail under the lamp with the wind coming straight off the sea and the beam going out over the water above their heads, on, off, on.

"You didn't correct her," Elaina said.

"No."

"Why?"

Ciaccona thought about how to say it and then didn't bother dressing it.

"Because she's got a version where the water gives something back," she said, "and she lives on the water, and I don't."

Elaina leaned on the rail beside her. The beam went round.

"My teacher used to say — " she stopped. "No. This isn't a teacher thing. This is mine."

"Go on."

"I've watched a lot of things happen," Elaina said. "I'm quite good at it. I arrive and I write it down and I go, and I've been telling myself for four years that the going is what makes the writing honest, because a person who stays is a person with a stake and a person with a stake lies." She turned her hat over in her hands. "And then about six months ago I got let into a house where a woman had been asleep for a hundred years, and five of the maddest people alive had kept her cup warm the entire time. And she woke up. Which they had no reason to believe would happen. And she woke up."

"And?"

"And I can't work out whether they were right or lucky, and I've stopped being able to write neutrally about anything, and it's ruined my prose."

Ciaccona laughed, properly this time.

"There's a word for a note that's held past its bar," she said. "*Suspension.* It's the moment a chord's stopped being what it was and hasn't got to what's next. Everybody's ear says *resolve it.* You can hold it as long as your lungs allow and the whole room leans forward."

"That's very pretty."

"It's also just a technique. Don't build a religion on it, they're expensive."

Elaina put her hat back on.

"May I ask you something rude," she said.

"You've been building up to one all week, go on."

"Was it your father?"

The beam went round twice.

"Yes," said Ciaccona.

"And the soup?"

"The soup was real."

Elaina nodded once and looked out at the black water and let the whole thing alone, which was, Ciaccona thought, the single most professional act she had seen from anybody in years.

---

*First of Saltmonth. Wind west, fresh. Nothing rounded before noon. Three sail after, all southbound, all in a hurry, and I have been thinking about how I write everything down and none of it has a name on it. I looked at forty years of this today and it is the sea again, smaller. I am too old to start over. Whoever reads this after me: put the names in.*

---

## vi. The Reading

They gave it four days, because the office had said before the close of Saltmonth and Saltmonth had eleven days in it and Cartethyia intended to spend the other seven getting the paper to Ragunna by hand.

The method was Nunzia's, because Nunzia knew the books.

"Rig, heading, weather, that's the spine and it's useless to you. What you want is the right-hand margin. He ruled a margin two fingers wide on every page for forty-one years and he never once explained why." She hauled the eleventh volume flat onto the table. "That's where he was a person."

The margins were where the tower lived.

*A gull with a bent primary again. Third year.*

*The cook on the Serafina waved. I waved. This is our arrangement.*

*A ship turned back today. First time in nine years. I do not know why and it has bothered me all evening.*

*My knee says south-west by morning. My knee is usually correct and always smug.*

They took a book each and read for the whole of the first day and found nothing and enjoyed it enormously. Elaina read aloud the good ones. Cartethyia laughed so hard at *the coast pilot Bonaccia is a menace and the sea will have him and I shall be sorry* that she had to put her head on the table. Nunzia, who had read all of it twice and had a scandalous memory for it, fed them the ones they'd missed the way a person shares out food.

On the second morning Ciaccona said: "Music."

They looked up.

"He logged *singing* once," she said. "Once, in forty-one years, on one line, because he'd looked through the glass and there was a man at a rail with his chest open and his mouth open and there was no other word for what he was seeing." She had four books stacked in front of her and had been quiet since dawn. "So he'll have logged it other times. He was a man who wrote down a gull's wing for three years running. If he heard music off the water he wrote it down."

"That's a search," said Nunzia.

"That's *the* search. That's the only one. Names aren't in here and dates are useless to me and rigs mean nothing." Ciaccona put her hand flat on the top book. "A man hiding from a theocracy under a name I'll never know does one thing he can't stop doing. That's the whole thesis."

Cartethyia had already gone to the shelf.

They found eleven.

Eleven entries in the right-hand margin across nineteen years, in one man's small careful lamplighter's hand, from a rock at the end of a country, of music heard from ships going by:

*A whole crew singing, and out of time, and southbound at four knots. Drunk, I should think. Frostbreak. Good for them.*

*Drum aboard the Tramontana at the change of watch. Every night this week. Somebody is keeping somebody's spirits up.*

*Concertina. Badly.*

*Concertina. Better.*

*A woman's voice off the water, very high, and no ship in sight, which I record because I have decided to record everything.*

*The lugger with the green sheer went by with a fiddle going and the wind took it out to sea so I had the top of it only.*

And five more, and one of them was the *Camelia*, four times that winter, *two strings short by the sound of it and he played it anyway,* and a note appended a year later in the same hand: *the Camelia sold north, I am told, and her people scattered.*

And one of them, on the fourteenth of Hollowtide, nine years after Ciaccona had bought her uncle's fiddle out of a pawnbroker's window in a river town and left it with a ten-year-old girl and walked out in the wrong direction on purpose, said:

*Ketch, deep-laden, northbound, out of Riva by the look of her paint. Coastal freight, the ROSA. Fiddle going at the change of watch and the fellow can play. He got a phrase up in three parts and then argued with himself about it for a quarter of an hour, which I never heard anybody do out loud before, and then he went back and did it a fourth way and laughed. I record it because it was the best evening I have had this year and nobody gave it to me on purpose.*

Ciaccona read it.

She read it again.

Then she got up and went out of the door into the wind, and the door banged, and the three women left at the table looked at each other.

"Was that good or bad?" said Nunzia.

Cartethyia was staring at the page upside down with her mouth slightly open.

"*Rosa,*" she said.

"It's a common name for a boat."

"It's the most common name for a boat in Rinascita," Cartethyia agreed, and then she made a sound and put both hands on top of her head.

"What," said Elaina.

"Her grandmother." Cartethyia was laughing and something else at the same time. "Her grandmother named every child in that family after a musical form because she said a name should be an argument. Her father was Passacaglia. Her uncle was Ricercare. Her — " she gestured at the door — "*her.* And the grandmother's joke, the one the whole house told, was that the day the guilds finally settled the argument, the family had permission to name a child something sensible."

She turned the ledger round on the table.

"*Like Rosa,*" she said.

The lamp hissed.

"That's not proof," said Nunzia.

"No."

"Half the boats out of Riva are Rosa. There's two in the chandlery book this year."

"I know."

"It could be any fiddler on any ketch in the world."

"I *know,*" said Cartethyia, and she was crying a bit, in the pleased embarrassed way of somebody who has got the joke. "That's not what it is. Nobody alive would understand that line except her. It's a punchline with an audience of one, four seas from the house it was made in, in a book kept by a man who never met any of them."

Elaina put her pencil down.

"That's the best thing I've heard on this entire road," she said, "and I met a horse in Sordina that could count."

---

Ciaccona came back in after twenty minutes with her hair everywhere and sat down and pulled the book to her and copied the entry out, fair, into her own notebook, in the small neat script she used for other people's words.

Then she sat and looked at the other ten.

"You could take them all," Nunzia said.

"I could."

"Take them all and go through the Ragunnan registries. Ships are traceable. Crew lists exist for coasters after the second year of the reform. It'd take you a season."

"Two," said Cartethyia, who had already worked it out and had two page references ready. "There's a Bureau of Hulls. They're very responsive."

"There it is," said Ciaccona.

She closed the eleventh book.

"I'm going to take one," she said.

Cartethyia went quiet.

"Ten of those are true," Ciaccona said. "All eleven, probably. Eleven men and women played music off ships and a lonely man wrote it down and every single one of them was somebody's, and I could spend two years and find out that the fiddler on the *Camelia* was a Marotta from Sordina with nine children, which would be a wonderful thing to know and would belong to somebody else."

"And the *Rosa*?"

"The *Rosa* argued with himself in four parts about a phrase for a quarter of an hour and laughed." Ciaccona put her hand flat on the closed cover. "Out of everything, this verse. Out of everyone, this name. That's the job. It's what I do to strangers professionally forty weeks a year and I've never once had to do it to my own."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I'm wrong warmly, and he's still out there in a book, and somebody else's granddaughter finds him." Ciaccona picked up her pen again. "I'm not the archive. I've met the archive. It keeps everything and it screamed at me."

She wrote a while.

"What'll you do with it?" Nunzia asked.

"Put a hole in it and give it away."

"That sounds like vandalism."

"It is," said Ciaccona. "It's the only method I've got."

---

The instrument took the whole of the fourth day and it was Cartethyia's finest hour and everyone present knew it.

She built it out of the following materials: one disposal notice; forty-one volumes of traffic log; eight volumes of names; a provisional office in Ragunna that was three years old and desperate to be seen doing right by the Order's victims; and the fact that between eleven and fifteen hundred people had been put on beautiful boats with no water and sent past this headland to die, and that not one of them had a grave.

"They've no marker anywhere," she said, walking the room. "No stone, no register, no plot. The Order recorded them as *entrusted to the sea's judgment* and closed the file. Which means, under the Provisional Interments Act — " she flipped to it, having brought it, having *brought* it — "there is no burial record for any person disposed of by Pilgrim's Sail."

"And a burial record can't be destroyed," said Elaina slowly.

"A burial record cannot be destroyed, disposed of, or removed from the parish of interment without an order of the Chamber, which is nine people, and which meets in Frostbreak, and which will not meet in Frostbreak, because it never has." Cartethyia set the book down. "And the parish of interment for a Pilgrim's Sail is *the open sea past Capo Congedo.*"

Nunzia sat back very slowly.

"You're going to make my traffic log into a graveyard."

"I'm going to make your traffic log into the only surviving burial record of somewhere between eleven hundred and fifteen hundred people," said Cartethyia, "which is what it has been since the day it was started, and which nobody noticed, because it's boring and damp and looks like rubbish."

"Boring is the vault," said Ciaccona, from the corner.

"*Boring is the vault.*" Cartethyia pointed at her without looking. "The lamp assembly can go. They can take the lamp. The lamp is worth eleven shells and the books are a cemetery."

Elaina had her chin on her hand.

"You need testimony," she said. "A burial record needs an interment attested. Somebody has to swear that a person went into that water."

The room went quiet in a specific way.

Ciaccona stood up.

"Give me the paper," she said.

Cartethyia gave her the paper.

She sat down at the table with the pen, and pulled the fourth book over, and opened it at the page she had found at twenty-five years old with an old man watching her from four feet away, letting her read for herself, and she copied it out.

Not into a song. Into the flat, ruled box of an official form, in her father's daughter's hand, in the space marked *particulars of the deceased, so far as known.*

> *Toccata, Passacaglia. Of Ragunna. Musician.*
> *Placed aboard a Pilgrim's Sail out of Ragunna, by order of the Order of the Deep, on the twelfth of Hollowtide.*
> *Provisioned with nothing.*
> *Rounded Capo Congedo the same evening, wind fresh from the west.*
> *One aboard.*
> *Singing.*

And underneath, in the space marked *attested by, with relation to the deceased*, she wrote her name, and the word *daughter*, and the date.

She looked at it for a while.

"Sixteen words," she said. "I got a whole coastline of drunks to grow that into six verses and it took eleven years, and here it is back down to sixteen words with a reference number on it."

"That's what a grave is," said Nunzia.

"Yes."

"It's the shortest thing anybody ever writes about anybody, and it's the one that stays."

Ciaccona blotted it and handed it over.

Cartethyia signed as witness. Elaina signed as witness, and then, having signed, said, "Oh," and got out her journal, and wrote something in it under a heading she had been keeping for six months, and shut it.

Nunzia signed last, as keeper of the record and custodian of the parish, in a hand she had taught herself out of a dead man's book.

"Right," said Cartethyia, standing up and stretching until her spine reported in. "Seven days to Ragunna. If the ford's still up we lose two and I'd rather not."

"You'll want the ferry from Riva," said Nunzia. "Ask for Bonaccia. He's the grandson."

Cartethyia stopped.

"Of the menace?"

"Of the menace," said Nunzia, "and the sea never did have him, and he's ninety-one and he still rows out on Thursdays, and his grandson is worse than he was."

Cartethyia put both hands over her face.

"I love this country," she said. "I hate it so much and I love it."

---

*Ninth of Saltmonth. Wind south-west, moderate, and rain by evening. Nothing rounded. Three at the table and the lamp lit at ten past five.*

*Aboard: Ciaccona of the Toccata, bard, out of Ragunna, by way of everywhere. Elaina, witch, out of somewhere she declines to state, going south to a door that sings. Cartethyia, of Egla, knight, first of that name.*

*Heading: north.*

*— entered by C., who asked, and was permitted, and who wishes the record to show that she was allowed to write in the book.*

## vii. What the Wind Kept

Elaina left first, at dawn, because her boots were ready before she was and always had been.

She had been trying for four days to lose the envelope.

It was small and sealed and weighed nothing except for one specific thing inside it, and it had been given to her flat into her palm with the fingers closed over it by a woman who had said *lose this somewhere far, tell me nothing.* She had set it down on a wall in Mingting and walked eleven paces and gone back for it. She had left it in a hedge outside Sordina and got two hundred yards and turned round in the rain. On the rocks below the tower she had held it out over the water at slack tide for a full minute and put it back in her coat.

She told Ciaccona this on the gallery, at the rail, with the sky going grey.

"I can't do it," she said. "It's an instruction. It's the simplest one I've been given and it's the only one I've failed."

"You can't lose it."

"I've established that, thank you."

"No — you *can't* lose it. It's not available to you." Ciaccona was watching the point. "Losing's an accident. You're being asked to perform an accident on purpose, which is a category error, and no honest person can do it."

"Then what did she want?"

"She wanted you to put it somewhere far away and stop going back."

Elaina stood with that for a while.

"That's not the same thing."

"No. It's better, and it's harder, and she knows the difference, because a woman who says *tell me nothing* has thought about it for a hundred years." Ciaccona pushed off the rail. "I left a fiddle with a ten-year-old in a river town and walked out of the place in the wrong direction, and I've never gone back, and I could tell you the name of the street."

"Is that losing it?"

"It's the closest a person gets."

So Elaina went down and took the eleventh volume off the shelf, and opened it at the fourteenth of Hollowtide, and laid the envelope flat into the gutter of the page against the entry about a fiddler on the *Rosa* who argued with himself in four parts, and closed it, and put the book back on the shelf between forty-eight others, damp, unbound, on a rock at the end of a country, in the custody of a woman who would keep it for thirty years and never open it.

The lamp was behind her when she closed the cover. For a second the paper went translucent and the thing inside showed as a shape: a rectangle, corner-worn, about the size of a playing card.

She did not look at it a second time.

"Filed," she said, to nobody, and went to get her hat.

---

They saw her off from the cliff path. She would not take the coast road; she was going straight out over the water to Ragunna, because there was a harbour mole there she was under orders to stand on at evening and look at.

"What will you write?" Cartethyia asked. "About the water. For your shipwright."

"That depends entirely on the water."

"And if it's grey?"

"Then she'll get four hundred words on grey," said Elaina, "because she asked for the colour and not for a nice time," and she settled the hat, and got the broom sorted, and then stood there for a second not going, which nobody remarked upon.

"There's a song," she said, "actually. Since we're on it."

Ciaccona raised an eyebrow.

"Three ports back. A boy on a fish quay with a box he was hitting." Elaina frowned, retrieving it. "It was about a bard out of Ragunna with a key inside her lute, and it went — " she hummed four bars, wandering upward where a decent tune goes down, sitting on an interval that is illegal in every tuning on every coast — "and then it did a thing with *lute* and *truth* that I have thought about several times since, unwillingly."

Cartethyia went completely still.

"There were about nine verses," Elaina said. "Most of them contradicted each other. In one of them she's dead. In one of them she puts a whole city to sleep, which struck me as a different woman entirely. There's a version where she's a man." She looked up. "Is any of it true?"

"None of it," said Ciaccona.

"All of it," said Cartethyia, in a very small voice.

Elaina looked between the two of them.

"You wrote it," she said to Cartethyia.

"I started it. Badly. On purpose. In a tavern, which I'm given to understand is the correct technique." Cartethyia's ears had gone red. "It was — it's not — it was two years ago and I only did it about six times."

"It's in three ports."

"*It's in three ports,*" Cartethyia repeated, in an entirely different register, and turned round to look at Ciaccona with her whole face lit up like Carnevale.

Ciaccona stood there on the cliff path in the wind with the sea below her and looked at the woman who had sat on her desk two years ago with a mouthful of candy and told her the terms.

"There's a word," she said, "for when a fencer is struck with her own blade."

"In Egla we called it Tuesday," said Cartethyia.

Elaina, watching this without any of the context and getting almost all of it anyway, said: "I'm going to write that down."

"Please don't."

"I've already done it. I did it while she was talking." She swung the broom round. "You'll be in the journal. Under *people.*"

"Which one of us?"

"All three," said Elaina, and went off the cliff.

---

Cartethyia found Ciaccona at the top of the tower an hour later, on the gallery, with the lute out for the first time in five days.

She had one boot up on the rail and the instrument across her knee and she was playing something four bars long, over and over, changing one note in it each time. The wind was taking it straight out to sea.

Cartethyia sat down on the step with the cane across her lap and let her get to the end of the cycle.

"That's not the hole," she said.

"No. It's the frame round the hole. The hole's after."

"What goes in it?"

"Whatever a man on a ketch called *Rosa* was arguing with himself about for a quarter of an hour," said Ciaccona, "which I will never know, and neither will anybody else, which is why it'll take about six months for the first tavern to fill it in."

"They'll get it wrong."

"They'll get it magnificently wrong. Somebody in Riva's going to have him arguing with the sea about it and winning." She played the four bars again. "It runs in the family."

Below them the point went out into the water, ochre and green, with the tide coming up the flat rocks and the pools filling and the whole slow business of a coast doing its afternoon.

"Can I ask you something," Cartethyia said.

"Mm."

"When you were twenty-five. On those rocks. Before it hit you." Cartethyia was looking out at the water. "Was there a moment where it was working?"

Ciaccona's hand stopped on the strings.

"Yes," she said.

"How long?"

"About a second. Maybe less." She looked at the sea. "There was a moment where it was answering. Before the volume. There was — everything came up at once, and for about a second before it was noise it was *ten thousand names,* all of them clear, all at the same time, and I had exactly one I wanted."

"And the others?"

"I wasn't listening to the others," Ciaccona said. "I've thought about that a great deal since."

Cartethyia turned Tuesday's little carved head round in her hand.

"Elaina would have listened."

"Elaina would have listened, written down every one, filed them under *people*, and posted them to a god under a glacier," Ciaccona said. "That girl's going to be extremely dangerous in about six years."

"She already is. She just does it politely."

They sat.

"Why did you really come?" Ciaccona said.

Cartethyia thought about lying, examined the lie, found it wanting.

"Because there's a filing deadline," she said.

"Mm."

"Because the record was going to be burned and somebody should."

"Mm."

"Because you'd never have gone and I can't stand a door held open with somebody's body." Cartethyia set the cane down. "And because Mei sleeps through the night now, and Amy's alive, and the perimeter's fine, and nobody's needed me since Frostbreak, and I've been walking a city with no crime in it at four in the morning like a woman looking for her own house."

Ciaccona let that stand for a moment, which was the kindest available response.

"Bastiano's giant," Cartethyia said.

"Go on."

"I asked him at six why it let the knight live. I've been asking it ever since. Everyone thinks the interesting one is why the knight kept fighting."

"And?"

"Elaina answered it on the road out of Sanguetta and didn't know she'd done it." Cartethyia's jaw worked. "She said: because if it killed him, it would have to go back to being a windmill."

Ciaccona sat with that.

"Oh, that's *good,*" she said.

"Isn't it."

"That's better than the play."

"It's much better than the play. Bastiano would have stolen it inside a week and denied it under oath." Cartethyia laughed, and then stopped laughing. "But that's the whole of it, isn't it. The giant needs somebody to come. That's the entire job. It's not a fight, it's *attendance.*"

"And that's you."

"That's me and it's been me since I was six and I keep forgetting, because attendance doesn't look like anything." Cartethyia turned her hands over. "I keep waiting for the next thing that needs a sword. And meanwhile the actual work is: turn up. Every day. At the same time. For the same person. Whether or not anything's wrong."

"That's a marriage," said Ciaccona.

"That's a *pack.*"

"It's the same technology." Ciaccona put the lute down. "It's what Nunzia's doing. It's what Tammaro did for forty years. It's what a lamp is."

"Someone should."

"Someone should."

Below them, in the room at the base of the tower, Nunzia was moving pots about and talking to herself in a dead man's sentences, and forty-nine books sat on a green shelf with an envelope in one of them and eleven pieces of music in the margins and one grave, freshly registered, in the open sea past the point.

The wind came up over the gallery rail from the west, off the water, the way it had on the twelfth of Hollowtide a very long time ago.

Ciaccona lifted her head.

"What," said Cartethyia.

"Nothing."

"You did the thing with your chin."

"I did nothing with my chin."

"You do a thing with your chin when the wind's carrying something and you're deciding whether it's real." Cartethyia stood up and came to the rail. "Is it?"

Ciaccona listened for a while.

"It keeps everything," she said. "That's all I've ever been able to establish. Whether it hands anything back is between it and whoever's asking, and I've stopped asking, and it's improved my temper enormously."

"That's a no."

"That's a suspension," said Ciaccona. "Ask again in a bar or two."

She picked up the lute.

She played the four bars, and left the hole in, and the wind took the lot of it out over the water where the coast pilots hand the ships across and turn back, and filed it with everything else.

---

They went down at dusk with Nunzia to light the lamp, because Cartethyia had asked whether she could and Nunzia had said it was eleven steps and a match and no ceremony whatsoever, which Cartethyia received as an invitation.

The wick took on the second try.

The three of them stood in the gallery while the beam swung out and found the sea and lost it and found it.

"Does anybody thank you?" Cartethyia asked.

"Riva sends flour," said Nunzia. "There's a boy who does it."

"That's not a thank you."

"It's flour twice a month for eight years." Nunzia tipped her head at the light. "It's the best kind there is."

Down on the water something small was rounding the point, southbound, running lit. Nunzia crossed to the desk on the gallery, where the current book lay open under a stone, and picked up the pencil, and looked at the ship for a moment through the glass.

"Ketch," she said. "Light. Southbound." She wrote it. Then she moved her hand to the second column, the one she had ruled herself eight years ago in a book that had never had one.

"Who's aboard?" said Cartethyia.

"Don't know yet," said Nunzia. "I'll ask Thursday."

And she left the space open, and put the stone back on the page, and went down to see to the fish.

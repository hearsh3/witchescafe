# Iuno Covella
### a nocturne in four watches
*A "Lyre, Speak to Me" side story.*

vindicat Ausonias Iunonis cura Kalendas.
*The care of Juno claims the Kalends of Ausonia.*
— Ovid, *Fasti* I

soles occidere et redire possunt:
nobis cum semel occidit brevis lux,
nox est perpetua una dormienda.
*Suns can set and rise again: for us, once our brief light sets, there is one unbroken night for sleeping.*
— Catullus 5

---

In the old rite, on the first morning of every month, a junior priest climbed the Capitoline and watched for the new moon, and when he found it — a paring, a fingernail, the least moon there is — he called it. Out loud, by name, with the count of days attached: *I call thee, Juno of the crescent. Five days to the Nones.* The month began because somebody counted it into beginning. The word for that calling became the word for the first day, and the first day's word became the word for the ledger where debts come due, and so the whole tradition of the calendar descends from one man on a hill telling a goddess how many days she has.

The goddess in question presided over thresholds, over marriage, over the mint where money is made and the memory of where it goes, over women in labor, and over the moment a child first sees light. She kept the doors and the accounts. Every woman carried a small portion of her, the way every man carried a genius, and the portion had her name.

Somewhere south of everywhere, a city of marble and holograms took its seven hills and its offices from that older empire the way rivers take silt from mountains they have never seen. Its temple trained girls in the watching of skies. Its calendar kept the old bones under new paint. And its last High Priestess, who shot fate's own paperwork out of the sky above a purple battlefield, was given at birth the goddess's own name, and grew up to keep doors and accounts, and learned the calling of days so young that her hands still know it in the dark.

## I. Vigilia Prima — First Watch

The bed was built for one student with modest ambitions. It currently held a woman who once ended a god's career, a girl who spent thirteen years as a heartbeat inside a machine, and a retired priestess lying at the outer edge with her crystal arm hanging into the cold, doing arithmetic.

Amy had arrived around midnight the way weather arrives, by pressure and rearrangement, trailing a blanket from her own perfectly good room across the hall. She now occupied the diagonal. Her hand was closed in Mei's sleeve. Her foot was on Iuno's shin, an act of pure annexation, and her breathing had the deep, greedy evenness of someone taking sleep back after a long embargo. Mei slept on her side with her mouth slightly open, one arm under her daughter, her whole body curved around the girl like a parenthesis around the most important clause. As of Tuesday she slept through till morning. Iuno kept the receipts on that. Iuno kept receipts on everything.

The room ran a night economy of small sounds. The ventilation changing shifts. The magnolia below the east balcony giving up a petal to the dark. Two heartbeats, close by, reading through the crystal into the bone of Iuno's shoulder — since the sync, the stump took the world in as bearings, and it had long since fixed these two as its meridian. She could have navigated a starless sea by them. She was, in fact, navigating a starless sea by them. It was the only instrument she needed for the water she was in, and the water she was in was two in the morning.

She lay with her eyes open and counted.

The count going up: two hundred and eleven mornings of hot tea delivered to a wolf, at ruinous compound interest, treaty intact. The count going up: nineteen days since her whole family walked out of the end of the world at a walking pace. The count going up: Tuesdays, now four of them, on which the woman beside her had slept until the light came without once surfacing to check that everyone was still breathing.

And under those, patient, professional, in a hand she recognized because it was her own: the other count. The one that ran the other way. The one with a bottom to it.

She did the arithmetic the way you press a bruise to confirm it is still yours. Mei would heal from anything, forever. The girl in the diagonal would outlast the mountains, probably, in whatever form the void had returned her. And the woman at the edge of the bed, the mortal one, the one with a body that kept honest books — she would get old. She would get old the ordinary way, at the ordinary rate, and the two people breathing beside her would be exactly this young on the morning she stopped.

She had outrun a prophecy. She had shot an arrow through the heart of an ending. She had stood on a battlefield three-quarters glass and refused the script to its face, and it had worked, it had *worked*, the doom died mid-sentence — and now she lay in a narrow bed under an artificial sky and understood, with the clean finality of a door heard closing in another room, that fate had only ever been the junior partner.

Time held the majority stake. Time did not write scripts. Time had no heart to put an arrow through. Time just kept the calendar, the way she once had, and called the days.

*You are doing sums in bed again,* said the voice behind her sternum. *At this hour. In this company.*

Iuno breathed out through her nose.

*Come downstairs,* said the High Priestess of Septimont. *The lounge is warm. I have opened something dreadful and expensive. Bring your ledger, since you refuse to be parted from it — but come down, darling. It is time we spoke. Properly. It has been a while since the courtyard.*

Iuno lay still one moment longer, taking a bearing off two heartbeats. Then she closed her eyes, which in that house was how you opened the door.

The way down had changed again.

In the plan's great years there had been an orrery: brass planets on jeweled tracks, a scrying basin, a plot board with thirty years of thread converging on one name, a balcony hung over a painted sea. All of it municipal, all of it load-bearing, all of it hers. Then a fool with golden eyes had walked in wearing forgiveness like a sidearm, and the whole apparatus had gone the way apparatus goes — the board burned, the threads cut, the planets shot out of the sky by their own maker with a legendary skill and sixteen arrows of light. For a while after that the interior had been rubble with a moon over it. Renovation had taken years and was conducted entirely without permits.

What stood there now, at the bottom of the stair behind her sternum, was a lounge.

It was small, which had been the first fight, and it had velvet, which had been the second. Deep blue velvet, moon-silver piping, two armchairs angled toward each other at the exact conversational distance the old throne room had been designed to make impossible. The mahogany table had survived every catastrophe with the smugness of good furniture, and stood between the chairs bearing two candles and a deck of cards worn soft at the corners. The piano stood against the far wall, lid open, keys yellowed honestly. On the wall where the plot board used to hang there hung, in a gilt frame Iuno had objected to for a full week of nights, a strip of prop-paper salvaged from the floor of the Nihility, printed in the void's flat font: CHARACTER ARC: LOVES MEI. CONCLUDED.

The High Priestess had framed it herself. *The single funniest thing the abyss has ever said about anyone,* she'd ruled. *Concluded. Darling, we had barely begun the overture.*

The walls were blind on all four sides, and had been for thirty years. A room that conducts three decades of conspiracy learns to grow its masonry thick, and even a renovation has to work with the original stone.

The High Priestess was in the left-hand chair, and she had dressed.

She had always dressed. That was the office. Blue silk, the long fall of the twin tails, the whole regalia worn the way other women wear armor and armor wears other women — and beneath the performance of it, that face, which was to say, Iuno's face, ten years younger and forty years more certain, holding a glass of something red-black and grand toward the candlelight to check its legs.

"There she is." The Priestess did not look over. "The woman of the hour. Every hour, apparently. Sit down before you fall down; you have been vertical inside your own skull since Tuesday week."

Iuno crossed the small warm room and sat. The right-hand chair received her the way the good ones do, like a verdict in her favor. On the table, beside the cards, sat a second glass, already poured, and a saucer holding — she looked twice — a slice of cake. Lemon, by the smell of it. Honey. Sugar crystals along the top like early frost.

"You remember cake," Iuno said.

"I remember everything. It is my entire portfolio." The Priestess turned the stem of her glass a quarter-turn, guest-handle-out, an old tell wearing a new courtesy. "You feed everyone in the upper house. Tea for the wolf, coffee for the Fool, contraband sweets for the child who pretends she is stealing them. Somebody has to feed the management."

"You're the management now?"

"I am the cellar." The glass rose, tipped, settled. "Structural. Underappreciated. Full of things you laid down decades ago and finally have the nerve to drink."

Iuno picked up her own glass and let it stand full in her hand. The candles burned with the particular steadiness of flames that answer to an internal weather. Above them, the ceiling kept the attentive quality of a sky that knows it is being kept out.

"You called this meeting," Iuno said.

"You've been holding it for a month without me. In bed. In the small hours. Sums, and more sums, and that face you make." The Priestess set down her glass with the soft click of a woman who has decided to spend a coin. "I sat through thirty years of your planning, darling. I *was* thirty years of your planning. I know the sound of you building something in the dark, and I know the sound of you circling something you refuse to build, and for four weeks I have listened to the second sound at two in the morning like a dripping tap." Her eyes came up. Grey-blue, candle-gold, her own. "So. Out with it. Say the number."

"There is no number."

"Say the shape of the number."

The lounge held very still, the way rooms do when something gathers itself to pay.

"They will look like this," Iuno said, "on the morning I don't wake up."

The words came out level. She had leveled them in advance, the way she leveled everything, sanding a sentence for weeks so that it would sit flat on a table when finally set down. Levelled or otherwise, the room heard the weight of the object. One candle leaned. The velvet took a shade of shadow.

"Mei will look like she looked on the beach," Iuno went on, since the seal was broken and the cargo may as well all come ashore. "New. Unbruised by decades. And Amy — Amy will still be nineteen in every way that shows, a century in, two, ten. And I will have been old a long time by then, if the books are kept honestly, and then I will have been gone a long time, and then a *very* long time, and the two of them will still be exactly the ages they are tonight, lying in beds this size, in cities I cannot imagine, with the space where I used to do the arithmetic." She turned her glass without drinking. "I ran the figures every night this month hoping they would come out differently. They are excellent figures. They always come out the same."

The High Priestess listened with her chin on her knuckles, and the mockery had gone out of her posture entirely, which was how you knew the doors of the room were shut and the session was real.

"Yes," she said simply. "That is the account."

"You've run it too."

"Darling. I ran it first." She reached out and squared the deck of cards on the table without looking at it, an old habit of the hands, order imposed on the nearest available chaos. "I ran it the day on the cliff when you looked at that woman too long and I felt the whole ledger tip. I ran it again the night she said the word *love* like the next thing before breakfast, and again in a boardroom when you spent our last political heirloom on a city of strangers, and again nineteen days ago when the god knelt and seven people walked out of his chest and one of them will be nineteen forever." The cards made their small riffling sigh under her thumb. "You outran Fate on a purple evening with one arm and a bow. It was the finest thing we ever did and I will be dining out on it for the rest of our life. And the rest of our life is precisely the clause under discussion. So." She began, unhurried, to deal. "You have finally come downstairs to ask the management the question."

"Which question?"

The High Priestess laid the fifth card and looked at her over the fan of them, and for a moment the candlelight found the sediment of every year in her face, the temple girl and the schemer and the screamer on the cliff and the woman who wrote *I can't* in a margin and left it standing.

"She could outrun Fate," the Priestess said. "What about Time?"

## II. Vigilia Secunda — Second Watch

They played the first hand for nothing, which was itself unprecedented.

Five-card draw, the house game, the game a woman with a borrowed name once played against her own severed self for the right to keep existing. The deck remembered. Decks do. Iuno drew two and kept a middling pair; the Priestess drew one, with the theatrical minimalism of a professional, and set her cards face-down.

"Tell me what happened," Iuno said, "when the void took you."

The candles bent, both together, toward the question.

It had sat between them for nineteen days, wrapped and unopened, in the way of parcels whose contents are known and dreaded. In the Nihility, nine and a half minutes into the dark, the subtraction had reached Iuno's skull and lifted the High Priestess out of it — clean, total, a portrait razored from its frame — and Iuno had gone on functioning, which was the obscene part, gone on aiming and speaking and asking a golden-eyed stranger with clinical frostbite why exactly she had followed her. The room behind her sternum had stood re-papered and bare. She had been *tidy* in there. She had been the most organized she had ever been in her life, and it had taken one word — *home*, and everything the word rebuilt — for the emptiness to register as amputation.

And then, on the long walk out of the end of the world, somewhere between the shore and the light, the furniture had come back. Mid-stride. The lounge, the velvet, the voice, arriving all at once like a tide remembering its appointment, and Iuno had stopped walking for exactly one step — the pack read it as fatigue — and had said, inside, to the reoccupied dark: *never again.* And the dark had answered, shakily, in silk: *agreed,* and neither of them had raised it since.

Now the Priestess looked past her cards for a while, into the middle distance where testimony gets composed.

"You are asking me what nothing is like," she said. "From a primary source."

"Yes."

"Because you are going there, and I have been."

"Yes."

The Priestess laid her hand face-down, laced her fingers, and delivered her testimony in the voice she had once used for reading auguries to a Senate: precise, unhurried, every word weighed and found solvent.

"There was no lounge," she said. "There was no dark, because dark is a thing, and there were no things. There was no waiting, because waiting requires a while, and there was no while. I did not persist in some reduced form. I did not watch from a distance. There was no distance, and no I to do the watching. I cannot tell you what it was like, because it was not *like*. It did not resemble. Resemblance was one of the subtracted services." Her thumbnail found the edge of a card and stilled. "You want me to report fear, or cold, or a long hall. I have none to report. Between one heartbeat of yours and some later heartbeat of yours, I was not. That is the entire deposition. The word *nothing* is a wild overstatement. Even nothing was above the void's budget."

The candle flames stood very straight, the way flames do around speech that has paid full price.

"And then," Iuno said.

"And then," the Priestess said, and here the augury voice cracked its formal seal, and something younger moved under the silk, "there was a word. Hers, if we are attributing. *Home.* And the word was a load-bearing wall, and the wall implied a room, and the room implied an occupant, and the universe worked backward from the grammar to me. I came back into a sentence already in progress. First there was the shape where I go — you keep it swept, incidentally, I noticed — and then there was me in it, and then, only then, there was the memory of having been gone. Retroactive. Like a page you discover has been missing from a book you had somehow gone on reading." She reached for her glass and held it without drinking, a woman borrowing steadiness from an object. "I have died once, darling. It has been done. I am the only member of this household with the experience, the girl upstairs excepted, and the girl upstairs was *held*. Somebody sang to her the whole way down. I went the other way, on the budget plan." A breath. "So when you lie up there doing your sums, and you arrive at the bottom line, and the bottom line frightens you — come and ask the woman who has audited it."

Iuno looked at her own middling pair for a long moment.

"Were you afraid?"

"Before? Yes. Immensely. You will recall I said so once, under a moon, to a woman with your taste in partners." The Priestess's mouth curved, rueful, spending an old confession like small change. "*I don't want to die.* The most honest sentence of our first fifty years, and I had to be beaten out of a body and stripped to the wire to say it." She set the glass down. "During — I have testified. There was no during, and no one to be afraid in it."

"And after? Now?"

The pause was long, and the room let it be long. Somewhere past walls that had always ended the world, a sound had begun to filter in, faint as a neighbor's radio: breathing. Two rhythms of it, one deep and even, one lighter, with a snag in it where a girl gripped a sleeve in her sleep. The lounge had thick walls, blind on every side, and the sound came in anyway, the outside arriving in the inside, and both women heard it, and by unspoken treaty neither remarked on the architecture yet.

"Now," the High Priestess said slowly, "I find my position has evolved. I have been nothing, and I did not care for it, and my professional recommendation stands at *avoid*. But." Her eyes moved to the framed prop-paper on the wall, the void's flat font, CONCLUDED. "The void believes an ending is a place where a story stops. I have been to its filing system, darling. I have been the file. And I am back in a velvet chair drinking better wine than we could afford in office, because a fool said one word with my name nowhere in it and the sentence needed me anyway." She looked back, and the candlelight did something to her eyes that in another woman would have been tears and in her was policy. "I am not consoled about the dark. I remain, for the record, against it. But I have amended the brief. The going is real. I have done the going. What I no longer believe in is the *gone*."

Iuno held her sister-self's gaze across the small warm distance, and the breathing of two sleepers came through the impossible walls, steady as surf.

"Show your cards," she said, at last, softly.

The Priestess turned them over. She had been holding a nine high, unsuited, a hand fit only for folding — and she had bet through it with a straight spine all the same.

"I know," she said, to Iuno's look. "It's a metaphor. I'm gifted."

## III. Vigilia Tertia — Third Watch

The window arrived during the second bottle's opening, and out of courtesy neither of them saw it happen.

One moment the wall behind the piano held only paneling and candle-shadow. The next it held a tall casement, mullioned, slightly imperfect in the glass, of the kind the temple's east tower used to have — and through it, where by every law of masonry and psyche there should have been earth and more earth, there was night sky. False stars, holding steady in their engineered constellations. Below them, faint, the suggestion of a courtyard: flagstones, roofless pillars, a shape in the middle distance that might have been a firepit, cold.

The breathing of the two sleepers came through the new glass more clearly now, the way sound crosses water.

"Your walls are thinning," Iuno observed.

"*Our* walls," the Priestess said, "are integrating. Renovation was always going to reach the exterior eventually. I am told open-plan is fashionable." She poured, two glasses, her motion economical. "Now. The future. You did come down here to plan one; I heard the graphite in your voice. Let us begin with the portfolio review, since neither of us will sleep until the books balance."

She snapped her fingers, and it was that kind of room, so the table obliged: the cards slid themselves aside, and where they had been lay a ledger. Slim, blue-bound, blank. A pen beside it, capped.

Iuno looked at the ledger for a long moment with her hands in her lap.

"There she is," the Priestess murmured. "The hesitation. Thirty years I could not get you to *stop* writing plans, and now you flinch from an empty page like it's warm." She leaned back. "Say the fear. This is the hour for it."

"The last time I planned other people's futures," Iuno said, "I built a coffin with a guest list."

"Yes."

"I drafted a beloved woman into a death and called the paperwork love. I decided what Augusta could bear to know. I decided what Mei was for. Every page I ever filled decided something *for* someone, and I have spent two years learning to put the pen down, and you slide me a fresh ledger at three in the morning and expect me to—" She stopped. Squared her shoulders against the chair. Reported the hiding, the way she'd promised a wolf she would. "I want it. That's the confession. My hand has been itching for a plan for a month. Old appetites. I want to *arrange* things — successions, provisions, contingencies, who holds what when I'm gone, who tells whom, in what order, with what wording. I want to leave this world the way I ran that temple. Fully briefed."

The Priestess listened to the whole of it with her fingers steepled, and when it was done she did the last thing thirty years of her would have done. She reached across the table and moved the ledger three finger-widths *away* from Iuno's side. Out of reach. An object lesson performed with furniture.

"Good," she said. "Confession logged. Now the ruling, and I will make it once, so engrave it somewhere: *we do not plan the people. We plan the luggage.*" She held up one finger against the beginning of a reply. "There is a distinction and I have been sharpening it for weeks, so you will sit there and receive it. A plan for a person says: here is what you will do when I am gone. It reaches out of the grave and steers. We are retired from steering; we resigned in public, at cost; the resignation holds. But a *gift*, darling — a gift says: here is something warm. Use it, sell it, break it, ignore it. A gift has no rider. A gift is a plan with the steering wheel removed." She sat back. "You cannot leave orders. You may leave luggage. Pack accordingly."

Through the window, the false stars held their courses. Iuno turned her glass by the stem, once, and the crystal of her arm threw a thin crescent of candlelight onto the table between them, and both of them watched it tremble there like a small moon learning to hold still.

"The kettle," Iuno said at last.

"The kettle," the Priestess agreed, in the tone of a chairwoman finding the agenda's first sound item. "Two hundred and eleven mornings. A treaty with a wolf, sworn under threat of spearing, honored across two nations and one apocalypse. It is, if we are honest, the finest instrument either of us has ever administered." A beat. "The wolf is on your clock, darling. Hers runs longer than yours — the breed is durable — but it runs. One day the treaty will want a new officer on our side of it, and the officer will need training, and the training takes years, because the four minutes are the smallest part of it."

"Amy."

"Amy," the Priestess said, and let the name sit between them with everything it carried.

Upstairs — through glass, through fathoms of psyche — the lighter breathing snagged and resettled, a girl adjusting her grip on a sleeve.

"She deployed *Auntie* twice this week," Iuno said. "Both times watching my face for the flinch."

"She is nineteen and she is casing you," the Priestess said. "I find it professionally admirable. That child spent thirteen years in a place where a name was the only property anyone kept; she does not hand hers out, and she does not accept others' at face value. She calls you Auntie the way a sapper tests a bridge — with weight, watching the joints. And she steals the tea because stolen tea requires no gratitude, and gratitude is a debt, and she was raised — twice, in two different darks — by women who taught her that debts are how you lose people." Her voice had gone quiet and exact. "She does not like us yet. Very well. Liking is weather. What she is doing — the foot on your shin at two in the morning, the annexed diagonal, the theft of specifically *your* cup and nobody else's — is triangulation. She is establishing our position to a precision of centimeters. You do not survey what you plan to leave."

Iuno looked at the small crescent of light her arm had thrown on the table.

"So I teach her the tea," she said slowly, feeling the shape of it. "Not as a bequest. Not — *when I'm gone, you must.* Just: here is a craft. Four minutes, water off the boil, the pot warmed first. A thing a person can carry."

"Luggage," the Priestess said. "You begin to grasp the doctrine. And when she rolls her eyes and does it wrong on purpose for a season, you will say nothing, and one morning years from now — with or without us in the room — a wolf will receive a cup at the correct temperature from a girl who never once admitted she was paying attention. That is the entire liturgy. That is how the dead keep making tea." She reclaimed her glass, and over its rim her eyes glittered with the next item already loaded. "Now. The heavier account. Say the standing order."

Iuno said it. She had said it first on a forest bench with the snow going to slush around them, and it had not lost a gram of weight since: "*You don't have to believe you're worth it yet. I'll believe it for both of us until yours catches up.*"

"No expiry date," the Priestess said.

"No expiry date."

"And the officer who issued it expires." The Priestess set the glass down and leaned forward, elbows on silk, and this was the load-bearing register now, the one she was kept for, the one that arrived a few times a decade and paid for all the irony in between. "Attend, darling, because I have thought about this in the small hours too, and I have found the door out, and it was in our own filing system the whole time. *Standing orders survive the officer.* That is what the word standing means. That is the entire technology of writing things down — the only magic either of us ever actually possessed. A prophecy is just an order that outlives the voice that gave it. We spent thirty years abusing that power, steering the living from behind paper. And now, at the end of the audit, it turns out the same instrument, held the other way round, is the answer to the only question that matters." She tapped the table, once, over the spot where the ledger had lain. "The order stands whether you do or not. But — and here is the work, here is what all those figures you run in bed are actually *for* — an order is a scaffold, and scaffolds are for buildings that cannot yet hold their own weight. So the assignment of our remaining years, the real one, the one under all the itineraries: get her belief caught up *while the officer lives.* Retire the order at a ceremony, with cake, decades from now, because it is no longer needed. That is the project. That is what the countdown counts toward." She sat back. "We are not counting down to a death. Any fool with a calendar can do that. We are counting down to a delivery date."

The window's false stars blurred slightly. Iuno realized it was her own eyes and let them be, in full view, so that it cost her.

"And if the belief doesn't catch up in time," she said. "If I run out of mornings first."

"Then the order stands," the Priestess said, gently, implacably. "In her sleeve, in her spine, in the girl's four-minute tea, in a wolf's cup, in a ring of—" She stopped herself, visibly, filing the item back under its agenda number. "Then the order stands. Written things outlive writers, darling. It is the oldest and only mercy of our former trade."

Iuno breathed. The lounge breathed with her; the candles moved.

"There's one more line in the long reading," she said. "I've never told you. You were — you weren't installed yet, when I ran it, or you were and we weren't speaking, the eras blur." Her thumb found the crack-glaze habit-place on her glass's rim, an old balcony gesture transposed. "The far edge of the water. Past all the endings I ever charted. There's a shore in it. Just a shore — patient, very patient, out past everything, waiting for a particular weather. It has nothing to do with me. I logged it in one line and closed the basin." She looked up. "I never filed it under anything. There was no drawer for it."

The High Priestess held her gaze for a long, level moment, and something moved behind her eyes — recognition, or its professional cousin — and was shelved with the unhurried care of a librarian handling a book that belongs to a different collection.

"Some pages," she said, "we leave for other hands to turn."

"That's your entire ruling?"

"That is my entire ruling. The far edge of the water is out of session." She reached for the deck and shuffled, a punctuation of the hands. "The near edge, however. The medium term. The *itinerary.* She is going to want to go north, you know. Our Fool. It is already starting — you have seen it, I have seen you see it. The way she reads. The tab left open. A warrior city on a lake, a wall, a wind out of the old country of her language. She built you a prosthetics division last time so that her wanting would have somewhere respectable to live; she is even now, upstairs, asleep, building the next respectable address. And the question you have been circling at two in the morning, under all the actuarial theater, is really a very small and very old one." The cards stilled. "When she goes — and she will always go; going is what she is; we fell in love with the going — do we still follow?"

"We have legs," Iuno said. "We followed her here."

"We had *younger* legs," the Priestess said. "The doctrine will want amending eventually. Follow while the legs allow. And after — after, darling, we become the other thing. The thing she has never once had, in any life, on any world, under any sky." Through the window, in the courtyard below, a spark had appeared in the cold firepit: small, unexplained, patient. "The home port. The lamp in the window. The standing appointment. She will cross whatever there is to cross, because she must, and somewhere behind her there will be a door with the tea already on, and the knowledge of that door will do more for her out on the water than we ever did standing next to her holding a bow." The Priestess watched the spark take. "It is the last posting of the career, and the hardest, and we are, I note with some satisfaction, finally qualified for it. Thirty years of leaving rooms. Now we hold one."

Below the false stars, in the impossible courtyard, the fire established itself with the confidence of an old appointment being kept, and its light climbed the window glass and lay down along the velvet, gold over blue, and the lounge grew warmer by one fire it did contain after all.

## IV. Vigilia Quarta — Fourth Watch

The wall opened where walls open in that country, which is to say, at the exact moment neither woman was watching it, and the lounge let out onto the courtyard as though the two rooms had been introduced years ago and were only now admitting the friendship.

They went out with their glasses and the last of the cake, because the Priestess ruled that solemnity conducted without cake was merely brooding. The flagstones held the day's warmth the way old stone holds everything, on principle. The pillars stood roofless against the false stars, and among the false stars, low and impossible and unremarked, hung one true unclouded moon — imported, like everything in that psyche, from the best night of their life. The fire burned at conversational height. Around it, half-sunk in the earth, small pale stones spelled a word and an arithmetic: IUNO + 9. Someone had recently added, at a slight remove, in stones of a different cut, a tenth.

They sat where they had sat. The fire did what fires do to a silence, which is furnish it.

"The epithets," the High Priestess said, at length, in the tone of a woman opening the true last item. "Since we are conducting the full audit. Our namesake carried a wardrobe of them — a title for every office, and she held every office worth holding. I have been taking inventory of which ones we still own." She raised one finger, ringed in firelight. "*Regina.* The queen. Abdicated, publicly, with paperwork; the Elders have our oversight framework and the throne room is a lounge now, and the lounge is better furnished. Struck from the rolls." A second finger. "*Moneta* — she who warns; also, by a joke of history, the mint, because the Romans kept their money in her temple and never saw the humor. We warned for thirty years. Every warning has been delivered, at cost, including the ones nobody wanted. The office is closed. The *mint*, however—" she tilted her head at Iuno, at the whole counting apparatus of her, "—the mint we keep. Somebody in this administration has to know where the mornings go."

"Sospita," Iuno said. "The savior."

"Reassigned," the Priestess said promptly. "To a committee. Nine members, one wolf-chaired subcommittee, excellent coverage. Saving people alone was the old error; we have divested." Third finger. "*Covella.* The crescent. Tonight's office — the calling of days — retained in perpetuity, obviously; look at us. Which leaves—" and here she paused, and took a slow, deliberate mouthful of wine, and Iuno watched her line up the last item the way she had once watched her line up shots through a firestorm— "two."

The fire cracked. Overhead, the imported moon held its breath.

"*Lucina,*" the Priestess said. "She who brings the child into the light. The midwife's Juno. The one the laboring called on." Her voice had lost every filigree. "There is a girl upstairs who spent thirteen years in the dark, and a great many hands pulled her out, and ours was one of them — our tether, our arm, our watch on the walls. That office is *open*, darling. It has years of work left in it. A child brought into the light still has to be taught to live there — has to learn tea, and pettiness, and how to lose card games to her elders with grace, and that a person can be loved by someone whose first sentence about her mother was unforgivable. We did not earn that girl's trust by catching her once. We earn it in the mornings, four minutes at a time, for as long as the mornings last. Lucina stays on the books." 

"And the last one," Iuno said. Her voice came out lower than she'd sent it.

"You know the last one."

"Say it anyway. You've made me say everything else tonight."

The High Priestess set down her glass on the flagstone. In the firelight her face and Iuno's face were finally the same age, which they were; that had always been the joke and the wound of her.

"*Pronuba,*" she said. "She who leads the bride. The goddess of the joining of hands. The office our mother's index card forbade us before we could write." She let the old sentence surface and pass — *a Priestess must never fall in love* — the way you let a funeral go by on the far side of a square. "We have been retired from every vow that ever bound us except the ones we chose. And there is a vow we have been circling, you and I, for four weeks of two-in-the-mornings, so closely that its gravity is bending every other item on the agenda, and neither of us has had the nerve to enter it into the minutes. So. As the last act of the last watch, I am entering it." She looked at Iuno across the fire that lived in both their chests. "Marry her."

The courtyard was very quiet. Upstairs, worlds away, close enough to touch, a sleeping woman breathed her steady Tuesday breathing, unaware of the parliament convened on her behalf in the country under a collarbone.

"She would say yes," Iuno said, which was itself a form of drowning.

"She would say yes before the sentence ended. That has never been the question, and you know it." The Priestess picked up a twig and fed it to the fire, watching it accept. "The question you keep aborting at two in the morning is the actuarial one. *Is it kind.* Whether it is fair to hand a functionally immortal woman a wedding band and a widowhood in the same gesture. Whether the ring becomes an anchor she wears into eternity, dragging. Whether love with an expiry stamp is a gift or a debt." A pause. "Have I stated the case?"

"In full."

"Good. Then hear the counter-argument, because I have prepared one, and it is the finest work of my retirement." The twig burned. The Priestess watched it, and when she spoke again her voice had the level, spent-coin finality of a woman laying down her whole hand. "She has been left by everything, darling. A homeland that closed the door. A mission that cared for her output. Cities, decades, lives — everything she ever loved was either taken from her or walked away, and the one lesson the universe taught her, over and over, with tenure, was that love is a thing that *ends and vanishes.* That the ending erases the having. And we are the counter-lesson. We are the one chance anyone will ever get to teach her the other law: that an ending is not an erasure. That a thing can stop and *stay.* But a lesson like that cannot be taught by a woman hedging. It cannot be taught in the conditional tense. It is taught by someone who does the whole arithmetic — the widowhood, the anchor, the eternity, all of it, eyes open, in advance — and signs anyway. It is taught with a ring." She looked up. "You are afraid the ring will be a weight she carries forever. Darling. *That is what the ring is for.* We will be gone. The weight will stay. The weight will be the proof that we were here — the one document even the far edge of the water cannot revise. You spent thirty years trying to author her fate. Here is the only authorship left to us that is honest: to be, permanently, in the past tense of her life, a fact. Loved. Chose. Stayed. Full stop. Let eternity argue with *that.*"

Iuno sat with her forearm across her knees, the crystal one, and the fire got into the crystal the way light had always gotten into it, uninvited, transfiguring, and threw a small warped constellation across the flagstones.

"The material," she said, at last, very quietly, because the decision had happened somewhere in the last minute, quietly, on its own recognizance, and she was already three steps into the logistics, which was how she had always known her own mind. "It would have to be—"

"Yes," the Priestess said.

"A facet off the stump. It's instrument-grade now, since the sync. It holds a frequency the way other stones hold color. It would carry—"

"It would carry *us,*" the Priestess said. "Resonating. On her hand. For as long as she has a hand." And then, because the moment had become unbearable and managing unbearable moments was the whole reason armor gets invented: "Also, it is free, and we are living on a student stipend and the charity of a wolf. I contain multitudes but I *am* still an economist."

The laugh took Iuno by surprise, one short breath of it, and it went up with the sparks.

Overhead, the imported moon had begun, imperceptibly, to set. The false stars were paling at the courtyard's eastern rim; somewhere beyond the psyche's masonry, a city's artificial dawn was filing its intent. The two women watched the fire burn down to its architecture, and the last of the night moved through the pillars, and the question the whole vigil had been convened to answer came and sat down with them at last, unarmored, ordinary, warm.

"I outran Fate," Iuno said. To the fire; to the moon; to the woman wearing her face. "I put an arrow through its script on a purple evening and I would do it again before breakfast. But Time—" She opened her hand, the flesh one, and closed it on nothing, gently, like catching a snowflake you know will melt. "There's no script to shoot. No heart to put an arrow through. It doesn't hunt us. It doesn't even notice us. It just — keeps the calendar. And every plan I ever drafted was some version of refusing to be on the calendar, and the refusing cost thirty years, and the years went on the calendar anyway, in the *waste* column." She looked up at the paling stars. "So what's the play. You've had all night. You always have a play."

The High Priestess of Septimont was silent for the length of three sparks.

"The play," she said, "is that there is no play, and I have finally made my peace with the genre. Fate was a duel; we won it. Time is not a duel. Time is the *venue.*" She gestured — at the fire, the stones, the moon going down, the whole borrowed courtyard — with her glass. "One does not defeat the venue, darling. One books it. Every morning we wake up in possession of a day nobody can revoke until midnight; the mint opens at dawn and closes at dark and issues exactly one coin, and the coin cannot be saved, cannot be lent, cannot be hoarded — believe me, we ran the world's longest experiment in hoarding, and the vault was empty the whole time. It can only be *spent.* So we spend. On tea at temperature. On a girl learning four minutes. On a wolf's treaty and a bard's songs and a knight's terrible sweaters and on our Fool, our golden catastrophe, every coin we are issued, until the mint closes." She drained her glass and set it down, mouth-blown crystal on ancient stone, the small ringing note of a session gaveled shut. "You asked what I want, earlier, or you were about to; it was on your face. Here is my testimony, entered freely. I want the wardrobe crisis in this underground city addressed within the fiscal quarter. I want to see Augusta again before the end and say three sentences to her I have owed for thirty years, and I want to watch her fail to cope with them. I want cake with alarming regularity. I want to be *deployed* — consulted, argued with, dressed up and taken out; I did not survive subtraction to be kept as a mood. And at the end, at the very end, whenever the calendar calls it—" the firelight stood in her eyes and she let it, in full view, so that it cost her, "—I want to do the one service I am actually built for. I have entered every room ahead of you since we were two halves of a frightened girl. I will enter that one ahead of you too. I'll go first, darling. I'll open the house. I'll light it. And when you come through — *late,* knowing you, held up at the door by nine sets of hands and a golden-eyed woman refusing the arithmetic — the tea will already be on."

The fire settled. The moon touched the pillars. Far away and directly overhead, in a bed too small for three, a sleeping woman's hand moved across the sheet and closed, unerring, around a crystal wrist, and held it, and both of the women in the courtyard felt the grip arrive like sunrise through fathoms of stone.

Iuno reached across the fire's warmth and took her own hand, silk and firelight, the way you shake on a treaty. Then she picked up the pen that was suddenly, in the way of that country, simply there, and opened the little blue ledger that had followed them out like a cat, and wrote the first entry in her new hand, which trembled, and stood anyway:

*REMAIN. Author: Iuno.*
*The days are called, not owed. The mint opens at dawn.*
*She gets the mornings, all of them, at temperature.*
*The order stands. The tea stays on.*
*This is not a countdown. This is an itinerary.*

*] Kalendae. The moon is new. I call thee, Iuno Covella: the month is in, the count begins again, and it begins at one [*

## Coda — Diluculum

She surfaced into the grey hour with her arm asleep and her heart awake, which was the correct configuration, and lay for one minute in the ruin of the bed taking bearings.

Amy had completed her conquest of the diagonal and now held Mei's sleeve, Iuno's shin, and the majority blanket position in a single unified administration. Mei's hand was closed around Iuno's crystal wrist — sometime in the fourth watch it had crossed the whole dark to get there — and her face, in sleep, wore the unguarded almost-smile she would deny to a tribunal. The false sky beyond the window was running its slow dawn program, engineers' rose-gold coming up the eastern rim on schedule, because in this city even the light kept appointments.

Iuno performed the extraction of her wrist with the skill of a career smuggler, replaced herself with a fold of blanket, and Mei's hand accepted the substitution with a small sleeping frown that Iuno filed, timestamped, in the permanent collection.

The kitchen at that hour belonged to the kettle and to her.

She warmed the pot first. She measured the leaves by eye, which after this many mornings was more accurate than the spoon. She poured just off the boil, set the timer in her head where timers had always lived, and stood at the counter in the rose-gold light with her whole life on one side of her and her whole death on the other and the four minutes in between, steeping, which was, she now understood, the entire technology of being mortal: you cannot slow the water, but you decide what goes in it, and you stand there, present, while it becomes something worth pouring.

The steam off the spout leaned east. The vents in that kitchen drew west. She watched it lean for the length of one slow breath, and inclined her head a centimeter to the empty air, and left the observation gloriously unfiled.

At the three-minute mark, the door made the sound of a nineteen-year-old attempting stealth in fuzzy socks.

Amy manifested at the counter with her hair in a state of civil war and her eyes doing the flat morning assessment they did, cataloguing exits, or perhaps just the pastry situation. Iuno, who had made two cups on a hypothesis, said nothing, and slid the second one a centimeter to the left, handle out.

The girl looked at the cup. At Iuno. Executed the theft at glacial, deliberate, fully-witnessed speed.

"One more minute," Iuno said. "It matters."

Amy held the cup and let it steep out its last minute, which was its own kind of listening. The timer in Iuno's head ran down through the quiet — the vents, the kettle ticking as it cooled, two mismatched heartbeats reading steady through the crystal — and at four minutes exactly Iuno lifted her own cup, and the girl, watching her sidelong with her mother's eyes and her mother's armor and, somewhere underneath, unmistakably, her mother's terrible reckless hope, lifted hers in the same second. An accident of timing. Obviously an accident. Both of them let it be one.

"…It's better hot," Amy said, to the middle distance, in the tone of a spy conceding one square of the board.

"It's better *on time,*" Iuno said. "Hot is just the proof."

From down the hall came the sounds of a household changing watch: a wolf's door, Ciaccona's first three notes of the day finding their tuning, Mei's voice, sleep-rough, calling a question into the world with total confidence that the world would answer. Amy's ears performed the small involuntary turn of a compass finding north. She took her stolen cup and drifted doorward, and paused there, half-lit, and said — sideways, at the wall, at nobody, at the exact volume of a thing meant to be heard once and never entered into evidence:

"Same time tomorrow, Auntie."

Then she was gone down the hall, and the word hung in the kitchen and kept its temperature.

Iuno stood at the counter with her tea at temperature and the rose-gold light coming up the false sky and the whole itinerary of the rest of her life ahead of her, item one already underway. Behind her sternum, in a velvet lounge with a new window and an open door, somebody was shuffling cards for the evening session and humming, badly, on purpose, an entr'acte from an opera about two names.

*One,* said the mint, and opened.

She poured the third cup, the tall one, the one with the cardamom already waiting beside it out of pure premeditated devotion, and set it on the tray next to her own, and went, on schedule, unhurried, spending, to wake the woman she would marry.

*] I call thee, Iuno Covella. The crescent stands in the east. Begin. [*

— *and the count was kept, morning by morning, in two hands: one flesh, one glass, both hers.*

# The Bird on the Wind

When Yangyang woke again, Liangyu was asleep beside the door.

She had meant to sit down for the length of a cup. The cup stood untouched near her boot; her head had come to rest against the wooden frame. Without the effort of keeping it upright, her face looked about Yangyang’s age.

The papers lay across her knees. Two fingers covered the names of Duzhong and Qingzhi.

Yangyang watched the fingers move in sleep.

Outside, a cartwheel struck a stone. Liangyu came awake with one hand already reaching for her weapon. She looked into the passage, then toward the bed, and slowly took her hand away.

“They’re bringing the carts around,” she said. “I hoped you would sleep through it.”

“You needed to sleep too.”

Liangyu looked at the untouched cup. A little embarrassment passed over her face, young and ordinary, before she put it aside.

“This is Xianling Village. I should have told you properly last night.” She gathered the papers without folding them. “We’re preparing to leave. The lower road is already unsafe, and the patrol at the eastern turn saw more Autopuppets before dawn.”

Yangyang eased herself higher against the wall. The dressing around her ribs pulled tight.

“Have you heard anything from Mengzhou City?”

“Nothing since the attacks began. We’ve tried the relays and sent people along the old routes. Some returned. We’re still waiting for the others.” Liangyu’s thumb moved along the paper’s edge. “Duzhong, Qingzhi and I were the three Xuan Wardens here. I was the youngest; they had both served longer than I had. When someone called for a decision, I used to look toward them before I knew I was doing it.”

She stopped with her eyes on the empty passage.

“Now people look at me.”

Yangyang remembered the two names beneath the sleeping hand.

“You’re certain they’re gone?”

Liangyu nodded. She took a moment before answering aloud.

“I saw them fall. There wasn’t a way to bring them back with us.”

A woman outside called for someone to hold a gate while the cart passed. Liangyu turned at once. The call was answered before she could stand.

She remained on the stool.

“Sentinel Hsin made the Autopuppets to protect us. For two hundred years, we’ve lived beside them. Children used to sit in their shade while their parents worked.” Her voice grew quieter. “Qingzhi went toward the first one. She thought something had caught in its arm.”

Yangyang looked down at her bandaged hand.

She remembered a wounded guardian coming through Jinzhou’s east arch. The dispatch had emerged first, damp from its journey; then the Wind had found her sleeve. Beneath that great shield, people had stood upright through gunfire. She had seen a child show an old machine how his little bridge opened, and the machine lower its platform to see.

She knew why Qingzhi had approached.

“They came to help us at Norfall,” she said. “Their crews trusted them with the wounded.”

“So did we.” Liangyu lifted her eyes. “I keep going back over the morning, trying to remember something that should have warned us. I remember the food. I remember Qingzhi asking whether it would rain. Everything before the first cry is so clear.”

Yangyang’s forehead remembered Chixia’s.

She let a breath pass through the ache beneath her ribs.

“I remember a kettle,” she said. “There was nobody beside it. I knew something was wrong, and I still didn’t get her clear in time.”

Liangyu sat with that for a while.

Then she brought the water closer and helped Yangyang drink.

***

The Rafter Kite waited until Yangyang could walk to the door.

That took most of the morning. The house’s owner brought porridge, returned for the bowl, and found it only half empty. She sat beside the bed and mended the torn fastening on Yangyang’s coat while the rest cooled enough to eat.

There were no spare hands in Xianling. The woman gave her an hour of hers.

By afternoon Yangyang could reach the courtyard without holding the wall. She rested there with the sun on her knees. Whenever a shout rose from the lane, her hearing opened too quickly; the world swelled around the voice, and she had to find the paving beneath her feet again.

The bird came down from the eaves.

It landed on a gatepost, looked back, and moved to the next roof when she stood. At the turn in the lane it waited while she caught her breath. Its blue feathers were bright against old plaster, fine enough that their shadows showed separately.

It brought her to a little shrine beside the well.

A bird had been carved above the doorway. Time had worn its face smooth. Its outstretched wings still sheltered the lintel, where generations of fingers had touched the wood while passing underneath.

An elderly woman was taking down the cloth hung beside it. She folded each length before putting it in a basket.

“Yours has been coming and going all day,” she told Yangyang. “I wondered who it was looking for.”

“It found me on the ridge. I don’t know who sent it.” Yangyang looked from the blue mechanism to the carving. “Were the living birds like this?”

The woman followed her gaze.

“Xuanling Birds. The Rafter Kites were made in their likeness. There hasn’t been a living one since the Fortress and Gorges War.”

Two hundred years. Yangyang tried to put that much absence around the worn wings.

“What did they carry?”

“What people needed someone else to hear. A message, a longing. The things that wouldn’t come out when you finally had a visitor at the door.” The woman laid another cloth in the basket. “My grandmother said you could tell a Xuanling what troubled you without finding all the words. It would take the feeling home.”

The Rafter Kite settled beside the carved bird. A small adjustment passed through its wing.

“When the fighting came, there were people calling from every valley,” the woman said. “That is how the old songs tell it. The birds kept going out. By the time the war was over, there were none left to answer.”

Yangyang looked at the smooth place where the bird’s face had been.

She thought of a bowl set in the sun, its steam leaning toward a woman who had once possessed a face like hers.

“Do you sing for them here?”

“We sing for anyone a long way from home. The words serve well enough for both.”

The woman reached above her head for the final strip of cloth. Yangyang held the basket while she folded it. Together they brought the little shrine’s belongings down to the waiting cart.

The carving stayed above the door.

***

Qingyan had dragged the sword as far as the grain shed.

Yangyang heard its point catch between the stones. The Rafter Kite dropped from the roof in front of him, and he lifted the weapon with both hands.

“Move out of the way. I know where they went.”

The blade rose to the height of his waist. His arms shook beneath it.

Yangyang stopped several paces away.

“You’re Qingyan,” she said. “Your aunt has been putting your things on the cart.”

“She can take them. I’m going to find the ones that killed my sister.”

The sword had an old hilt, worn narrow in the place a larger hand would have held. A tassel had been tied beneath the guard much more recently. The knot was neat, its ends cut to the same length.

Yangyang kept looking at his face.

“Did Qingzhi teach you to hold it?”

His mouth tightened.

“She said I had to grow into it. It belonged to our family before the Autopuppets came. People fought with it then.” He tried to lift it higher. “I know how to cut. I’ve been practising.”

Yangyang remembered a courtyard post darkened by rain. Her hands had been small around the wooden hilt. The sword had seemed the most direct thing in the world: you wanted to be ready, and there it was, showing you how far remained.

Qingyan’s grip slipped.

She came close enough to take the weight beneath the guard. He pulled against her, frightened that she would take the whole weapon.

“I’ll hold it while you move your fingers,” she said. “You’ve caught the skin against the binding.”

He looked down. The skin had gone white where the cord pressed it.

“You think I’m too little.”

“I think you’re hurt. I don’t want you standing here alone with it.”

She lowered herself onto the shed’s step. Her ribs protested, sharply enough that she could not hide the breath she drew.

Qingyan watched her, then let the sword descend across their knees.

For a while they held it together.

“If I’d gone with her, there would have been one more person,” he said.

Yangyang looked at the tassel.

“Your sister went out with people who knew how to fight. She had trained for that work.”

“They still died.”

“Yes,” Yangyang said. “They did.”

The boy’s shoulders rose. He stared at the blade as if she had put something unbearable on it.

She waited until he looked toward her again.

“My friend was hurt on the road here. I keep thinking about where I could have stood, what I could have noticed sooner. I haven’t found a thought that changes where she was.” Yangyang eased her sore hand beneath the hilt. “I’m angry too. But I can’t tell you that taking this sword out there will bring either of them back.”

“I wasn’t going to bring her back.”

He said it fiercely. Then his eyes filled.

Yangyang stayed beside him.

From the lane came the sound of a cupboard being carried through a doorway. Someone wanted it turned the other way; someone else said that was the way they had just tried. The small dispute went on while Qingyan rubbed his face against his sleeve.

“She tied that,” he said at last, looking at the tassel. “The old one kept coming loose. I told her I could do it myself.”

“May I see the knot?”

He turned the hilt toward her.

Yangyang touched the cord lightly. There was a short piece doubled back beneath the binding, where Qingzhi had left room to undo it.

“She made it so you can replace it when this one wears out.”

Qingyan looked at the little fold.

“I don’t want to replace it.”

“You don’t have to do that today.”

His aunt found them there. She stopped when she saw the sword, drew breath for something urgent, then looked at the boy’s wet face and sat on the other side of him.

They fetched the scabbard together.

Qingyan would not let go while the sword was wrapped. His aunt fitted it into the bedding on the cart, with the tassel where he could see it. He checked the fastening twice.

As Yangyang stood, he caught her sleeve.

“Will they leave it behind if the cart gets too heavy?”

She looked at his aunt, who loosened a cooking pot from the side and took it into her own arms.

“It’s coming with us,” the woman said. “Come and help me find a place for the lid.”

Qingyan went with her, looking back once at the sword.

Yangyang watched until the cart turned out of sight.

Only then did she let herself rest against the wall.

***

Toward evening, the Rafter Kite led her above the village.

A pagoda stood beside the cliff path. Its stone bench faced the eastern peaks, where pale fog gathered in the folds of the mountain. The wind came through the open sides and left without disturbing the old leaves gathered behind the pillars.

Liangyu sat at the far end of the bench.

She was singing.

> 玄翎飛，飛過江水泱泱，
> 山茫茫，明月光……

Her voice was low. The tune crossed the broad river, climbed into the mountains, and returned to its beginning before the moon could light the road.

Yangyang stopped at the steps.

Liangyu sang the first line again. Her hands lay open on her knees. She looked at a place in the fog which Yangyang could not find.

The Rafter Kite settled on the rail.

“Liangyu, I’ve brought your coat,” Yangyang said.

The song went on for another few notes.

Then Liangyu turned, slowly, as though Yangyang had called from much farther away.

“I was only going to sit here for a moment.”

“There’s still light. May I sit with you?”

Liangyu moved her hand along the bench to make room, though there was room already.

Yangyang laid the coat beside her. She sat where she could see the village roofs through the lower branches.

Below them, people were taking washing off a line. A sheet rose once between two women and hid them both.

“Qingzhi used to take the next verse,” Liangyu said. “Duzhong never began in the right place. He would wait until we were halfway through, then join whichever one of us he could hear best.”

She looked back toward the fog.

“I keep leaving space for them.”

Yangyang heard the unfilled interval. It was the length of a person turning toward her friends.

“Will you sing it again?”

Liangyu’s mouth moved before the sound came.

This time she went farther. Moonlight lengthened a traveller’s shadow; a bird carried a call across the world. Father, mother. Do not forget me. Then the tide rose, the mist covered the road, and the day of return withdrew beyond the singer’s breath.

Yangyang listened to where she breathed. The song asked for more air than Liangyu had been taking.

At the verse about home, her voice thinned.

“I was supposed to bring them back,” she said. “I know I was told to leave. I remember the order. I even remember thinking I must make the others obey it.”

Her hands closed against her knees.

“I kept doing everything I was meant to do. At some point that became walking away from them.”

Yangyang felt the cliff beneath the bench, the long air beyond it.

She knew the temptation to make the moment clearer than it had been. To stand, afterward, in a place of safety and give the frightened woman on the road one more choice, one more measure of strength. The woman in that remembered moment always seemed to possess whatever the living one could no longer bear having lacked.

“When I sent Chixia down the gorge, she reached for me,” she said. “I was looking at her hands while I made myself let go.”

Liangyu turned toward her.

“I wanted to go with her. I still want to. I’ve been asking people whether they’ve heard from Jinzhou, although I know you can’t reach it.”

Yangyang drew the coat’s loose sleeve away from the edge of the bench.

“I don’t think remembering why I stayed will ever make those hands stop reaching.”

Liangyu bowed her head.

For a little while Yangyang heard only the wind and the young woman trying to breathe quietly beside her.

She remembered herself at the window, putting another day into a letter. How readily she had turned whatever she wanted into something useful for someone else. How relieved she had felt when a task gave her a reason to remain in a room.

There had been people patient enough to sit down before she found the reason.

She stayed.

After a while Liangyu took the coat and put it around her shoulders.

“Was Norfall like this?” she asked. “You said the guardians went there. We heard that the dead came home.”

Yangyang looked toward the last light on the roofs.

“They had been made to go on fighting. There were people in Jinzhou who still knew how they took their tea, what they were frightened of, which food they wouldn’t eat. We brought those things north.”

She could hear the rain again. A brother speaking into a receiver. A name held steady by a voice that knew how to say it before it belonged to a soldier.

“There were many of us. Sometimes what one person couldn’t do was something another had been waiting to try.”

“What did you do when you reached them?”

“I listened. I carried what I could. I helped a friend stop before she gave away more than she had left.”

Yangyang’s fingers rested on her own empty sleeve.

“She’s the one I call shijie. She lives as wind now. Once, in another world, she was like me.”

Liangyu looked at the sleeve, then at her face. She waited without trying to fit the words into something smaller.

“I used to think I understood because I could hear her,” Yangyang continued. “At the cliff yesterday, I almost followed her. I wanted to get back to someone so badly that coming apart began to feel like a way of travelling.”

The Rafter Kite turned on the rail. One blue wing opened to keep its balance.

“You reached me before it happened,” she said. “I remember the weight in your arm.”

Liangyu rubbed her bandaged forearm.

“I couldn’t get a proper hold on your coat. I thought I was going to lose you.”

“I remember being pulled back.”

Liangyu’s face folded. She covered it with both hands.

Yangyang put an arm around her shoulders carefully, leaving the injured arm free. For several breaths Liangyu held herself rigid inside the touch. Then she leaned, a little, and the weight of her head reached Yangyang’s shoulder.

There was no part of that weight Yangyang wished elsewhere.

She could not lift the morning from this woman’s life. She could hold what had been given to her now.

Below the pagoda, someone called Liangyu’s name.

Her whole body answered before her mouth did.

Yangyang kept her arm where it was.

“Take a moment,” she said. “I’ll tell them we’re coming.”

She called down. The messenger waited at the lower steps.

Liangyu wiped her face with the inside of the coat. When she had found her voice, she told Yangyang about the eastern road.

***

They needed a way through the Disorienting Fog.

The road around the Eastern Xuan Peaks led to a forward garrison camp, and from there to the Hold’s inner defences. It was the only route left with enough ground for the village carts. The advance scouts had gone before dawn. By evening, no one had returned.

“I can look for the path,” Yangyang said.

Liangyu’s eyes went to her dressing.

“You could barely cross the courtyard this morning.”

“I’ll rest tonight. Yanlang knows the old track; he can go with me. I can listen ahead while he watches the ground.” She paused. “I can’t listen to the whole mountain safely. If I begin losing the sounds beside me, I’ll stop.”

Liangyu sat looking at her for a long time.

Then she unfolded the map across their knees.

They discussed the road until the messenger brought a lamp.

At dawn Yangyang left with Yanlang, a coil of line, fresh dressings, and food she had been made to finish half of before she was allowed out of the house.

Liangyu had retied the fastening on her coat.

The Rafter Kite flew ahead.

***

In the fog, a footstep could arrive before the foot that made it.

Yangyang heard Yanlang climb a stair on her left while he stood beside her on level ground. Farther on, someone coughed with the same catch three times, each repetition untouched by the breath that ought to have followed.

She asked Yanlang to tap the head of his spear against a stone.

The fresh sound went outward. She followed the small disturbance it made through all the older ones and found the cliff face beyond the mist.

“The road turns here,” she said. “Stay near the inside wall until we see the next marker.”

They left a cord across the false turning. At the next they tied a strip of cloth where a person walking beside a cart could reach it.

The bird waited on each marker until they came near, then vanished into white.

Once Yangyang heard her name in Chixia’s voice.

She stopped so suddenly that Yanlang almost walked into her.

“Is someone ahead?” he asked.

She listened to the silence after the call. Nothing in it moved toward them.

“Something I remember,” she said. Her mouth had gone dry. “Give me a moment to find your footsteps again.”

He stayed beside her. When she nodded, he began walking slowly enough for her to hear each heel settle.

They found the scouts below a broken rail.

The first lay with his arm through the strap of his pack. The second had got as far as the first man’s boot and still held it. A length of survey cord crossed both bodies and disappeared beneath the stone that had fallen from the bank.

Yangyang went down on one knee.

The fog kept moving over them. She put her fingers to the first man’s neck, waited, then reached for the second.

There was no answer in either place.

Yanlang took off his cap.

For a while she could hear his breathing, and beneath it the old cough repeating somewhere the road no longer went.

She found the scouts’ names inside the dry flap of the map case. Beside them was a half-finished account of the route. The dangerous turn had been marked. The writer had drawn the road as far as the next stream, leaving a small space for what he expected to see afterward.

Yangyang kept a finger on that unfinished line.

Then she closed the case and gave it to Yanlang.

They could not carry both bodies and secure the road. She marked the place carefully, with the distance from the last sound pillar and a strip of white cloth tied above the fall. Yanlang straightened the second man’s leg before they left.

At the stream they found an Autopuppet wedged beneath a fallen trunk. Its head turned when they approached. Yangyang drew it toward the noise of her blade against the rail while Yanlang passed behind it and cut through the exposed fastening at its neck.

They waited until the sound inside it stopped.

Then they cleared the trunk enough for the smaller carts and rigged a handline along the wet stones. Yangyang tested the crossing with her eyes lowered to the feet she could still place. The fog pressed against her hearing. She let it remain beyond the next step.

The garrison’s lantern appeared near midday.

Mingyu came out to meet them with two Wardens at her back. She wore her hair pinned badly beneath her cap; when she bent over the scouts’ map, one piece fell across her eyes and she pushed it away with the back of her hand.

“We’ll send a carrying party along your marks,” she said after Yanlang told her. “The last time I saw them, they were arguing about which of us had packed the tea.”

She looked at the map for another moment, then called the two Wardens closer and showed them where to go.

Mingyu sent a runner back along the marked trail to tell Liangyu the road was open. Yangyang slept beneath the watch shelter until the lookout called that the village carts were coming. Someone woke her with warm food.

She had found the way through.

Beyond the camp, another road waited.

***

The first villagers arrived before the food had cooled.

Liangyu came through the mist beside a cart whose left wheel had been wrapped with cloth to keep a split from widening. She was walking at the pace of the woman pushing it. When she saw Yangyang, her hand rose from the cart’s side.

Yangyang stood to meet her.

“The stream crossing is narrow, but the line will hold. Mingyu has sent people to help the last carts through.”

Liangyu looked over her face before looking at the road.

“Sit down again until they arrive. I can tell everyone from here.”

Yangyang sat.

Qingyan came through riding beside his aunt. He had one hand tucked into the bedding, holding the tassel beneath the folded cloth. When the Rafter Kite landed above him, he moved a bundle to make room.

At the camp, names passed from one list to another. Water changed hands. A woman who had carried a pot all morning finally set it down and could not straighten her fingers for a while.

Mingyu knelt to help her.

“Keep your hands in the warm water,” she said. “I’ll lift it onto the next cart. My mother will ask why I’ve arrived carrying someone else’s cookware, but she’ll have plenty to say before we reach that part.”

“Does she live inside the Hold?” Yangyang asked.

“Near the western storehouses. If we arrive after supper, she’ll serve it again and complain about the firewood.”

Mingyu smiled as she said it, already somewhere beyond the road.

Yangyang wanted to arrive early enough to hear the complaint.

They left the camp together. Liangyu took the head of the column. Mingyu moved between the carts, checking the people who had gone quiet, and Yanlang walked behind Yangyang with a spear whose butt clicked on the stone whenever the ground rose.

The fog thinned. Beyond it, the Hold’s inner walls took shape against the mountain.

A child began asking how much farther.

For the first time all day, someone could point to the answer.

***

The first Autopuppet came out of the terrace above the road.

It struck the rear cart sideways. The shaft snapped, the driver fell between the wheels, and Yangyang caught the cart’s upper rail with a current before it could turn over him.

Yanlang pulled him clear.

Along the terrace, metal shapes rose among the abandoned houses. More came through the passage behind them. Their feet struck the paving with a familiarity which made Yangyang think, horribly, of a patrol returning at the end of its watch.

Then the first weapon came down.

Mingyu met it beside the cart. She pushed a woman toward the wall with her free hand and shouted for the column to keep moving.

Liangyu was already bringing the front carts off the road, toward the covered approach to the inner gate. The people nearest the ambush could see nothing beyond one another’s backs. Yangyang climbed onto the broken cart and called to them from above the wheels.

“Keep the wall beside your right hand. Liangyu is at the turning; follow her voice. There’s room ahead of you.”

She waited until the woman beneath her began to move, then jumped down beside Mingyu.

The attackers turned toward her blade.

Across the road stood an old mill yard. Its outer gate opened onto a narrow lane between two high retaining walls. If they could draw the machines through there, the carts would have time to reach cover.

She pointed it out to Yanlang.

“The lane comes out below the east steps,” he said. “We can hold the narrow part and fall back through the yard.”

“I’ll take the near side,” Mingyu said. “You two keep enough room behind me for a person to turn around.”

Liangyu came back as far as the broken cart.

Her eyes moved from the lane to Yangyang’s face.

“We need all three of you at the gate.”

“Send someone to the east steps when the last cart is inside,” Yangyang told her. “That’s where we’ll come out. If we’re hurt, they’ll need a litter.”

Liangyu looked toward the people pressing into the covered approach. Qingyan’s aunt was trying to carry him over the broken shaft. His hands had closed on her shoulders.

“I’ll have people waiting,” Liangyu said.

She went to lift the boy.

Yangyang turned toward the mill.

She wanted to arrive at those steps. She chose each movement for the road beyond it.

***

They drew the first rank into the lane and brought it down against the second.

The walls gave their voices back to them. Mingyu called when she needed to change her footing. Yanlang’s spear caught a joint and held long enough for Yangyang to cut beneath it. She kept the currents close to the blade. Every wider reach stirred the old ringing beneath her ear.

An Autopuppet climbed over the bodies of the others.

Mingyu turned to meet it. The stone under her heel broke.

Yangyang caught the weapon’s descent in a burst of air. It turned far enough to miss Mingyu’s head and struck her against the wall instead.

She went down without a cry.

Yanlang took her place.

Yangyang dragged Mingyu back by the shoulders, laid her behind the mill’s stone trough, and felt her grasp at the wrist beneath her glove.

“I can move,” Mingyu said. “Just give me a moment to get my breath.”

Her eyes were fixed on Yangyang’s face, asking it to confirm the words.

Yangyang pressed a dressing against the wound at her side.

“Keep your hand here. I’m going to bring Yanlang back to us.”

She put Mingyu’s fingers over the cloth and went.

The next strike took Yanlang beneath the edge of his armour.

Yangyang saw the force reach his body. His spear stayed level. She cut past his shoulder, felt metal give, and pulled him toward the yard while the disabled machine fell across the passage.

For several breaths they had shelter.

He looked down at his side, then toward Mingyu behind the trough.

“The east steps are close,” he said. “We can get her there if the lane stays clear.”

Something heavy struck the upper wall.

The old masonry opened along a seam. Yangyang heard the fracture travel, drew Yanlang down with her, and spent one broad current keeping the first fall of stone above Mingyu’s head.

The air roared through the yard.

When the dust thinned, the lane to the steps lay under broken masonry. Two Autopuppets were trapped beneath it. A third dragged itself toward the trough on one working arm.

Yangyang met it on her knees.

She drove the sword into the open housing and held through the shudder until the mechanism stopped.

Somewhere beyond the walls, a horn sounded from the covered approach.

The villagers had reached the gate.

She waited for a second call. Dust settled in her hair. Beside her, Yanlang was trying to stand.

“Stay down until I’ve found another way through,” she told him.

He lowered himself against the trough. The spear slipped from his hand, and he looked at it with faint surprise.

Yangyang brought it within reach before going to Mingyu.

***

Mingyu wanted the buckle beneath her throat loosened.

“It keeps catching when I breathe. I thought I’d fixed it this morning.”

Yangyang worked it free. There was grit inside the fastening. It took both hands.

Mingyu swallowed and looked toward the broken lane.

“Was that the gate signal?”

“Yes. The carts are inside.”

Her eyes closed for a moment.

Yangyang kept her hand against the dressing. She could feel how little pressure Mingyu was managing from the other side of it.

“The people at the steps will look for us,” she said. “Liangyu knows we’re here.”

“My mother will be looking too. She goes down whenever there’s a column.”

Mingyu tried to lift her head. Yangyang slid a fold of cloth beneath it.

“If you see her before I do, tell her I was with the village carts. She’ll think I stayed at the camp.”

“What name should I ask for?”

Mingyu’s mouth moved. Her breath caught before the answer.

Yangyang bent closer.

The name did not come.

She waited beside the parted lips, certain for several terrible seconds that she had simply failed to hear it.

Then she put her fingers to Mingyu’s neck.

The yard was full of sounds. Dust sliding off a roof tile. Water dripping inside the mill. Yanlang drawing breath behind her.

She could find no movement beneath her fingers.

“Mingyu, I’m still here,” she said. “You can tell me.”

Yanlang said her name from the trough.

She stayed one breath longer, then covered Mingyu’s face with the clean corner of the cloth.

Yanlang had seen.

He watched her come toward him without asking.

Yangyang knelt and opened the fastening at his side. The dressing she had put there was already dark. She reached for another, and his hand found her sleeve.

“Could you move me a little? I’d like to see the road.”

She eased him around until he could look through the yard’s remaining arch. A strip of paving lay beyond it, empty under the dust.

He studied it.

“We did get them through.”

“They reached the gate,” she said. “I heard the signal.”

His hand loosened on her sleeve.

She tried to give him water. Most of it ran down his chin. He looked ashamed, as though he had made unnecessary work for her.

Yangyang wiped it away with her thumb.

“You waited for me in the fog,” she said. “When I couldn’t tell where the voices were coming from. I haven’t thanked you.”

He looked at her for a moment, his eyes struggling to remain on her face.

“You found the road,” he said. “I only had to walk beside you.”

She kept her hand around his.

Its weight changed before she was ready.

For a while she continued holding it exactly as before.

***

When she lifted her head, the mill was burning.

Flame ran beneath a roof which had already collapsed. The broken arch stood whole above her, hung with a banner whose characters she could not make out. A spear lay across her knees. It was made differently from Yanlang’s.

Yangyang looked down at her hand.

The hand she had been holding was gone.

“Where am I…?”

She was kneeling in a street full of fallen soldiers. Beyond them, buildings burned beneath a pale moon. Weapons lay snapped against the paving. The air smelled of charred grain, wet earth, the bitter heat of a house whose door had been barred from the wrong side.

*A Sonoro? But how did I…*

She tried to find the trough.

A man beside her coughed.

The sound bent her forward before she could choose to turn. He lay with one arm beneath his body, his face swollen with bruises. Each breath drew a little blood against his teeth.

“Is it… is it over?”

Yangyang reached to ease his shoulder.

His gaze moved past her hand to her face. Hope entered it so abruptly that she almost drew back.

“Xuanling,” he whispered. “Tell me… Yuan Fortress… did it stand?”

The burning banner shifted above them.

She saw a feather fall through its light, blue at the shaft and black along the edges.

“I don’t know what happened to the fortress,” she said. “I came from Xianling Village. There are people on the road to shelter.”

The man listened with desperate care.

“Please… I still had… things I wanted to say…”

“You can tell me. I’m listening.”

His lips shaped the beginning of a word.

Then the cough took him, and the moment shuddered, and he was lying beside her again with one arm beneath his body, asking whether it was over.

Yangyang’s fingers tightened against the ground.

She knew that return. At Norfall, rain had filled with the last minutes of a life and made the living stand inside them.

But this man had no brother speaking through a receiver. No grandmother waiting with a name. The wind had kept his open mouth through two hundred years, and Yangyang did not know what it had been trying to say.

She stayed until the cough came again.

The third time, she could feel it beneath her own ribs.

***

Two Wardens lay near the foot of the wall.

One had torn a strip from his clothes and was trying to fold it over the other man’s leg. His hands kept missing the place he meant to cover.

“Are you all right?” he asked when she came near.

Yangyang stopped.

He had seen her wounds. Even here, with his own blood running between the stones, he was trying to decide whether she needed help.

“I can sit with you,” she said. “Let me hold the cloth.”

The man beside him turned his head.

“I can’t feel my legs. I keep trying to move them, but I can’t tell…” His gaze travelled across the bodies. “Where is everyone?”

“I don’t know. Not many left, I’d guess.” The first Warden looked toward the burning gate. “We held off the first wave. If the next one comes before the relief—”

He stopped to breathe.

The other man looked at Yangyang.

“Xuanling, do you think we’ll win this?”

A little laugh came from his companion. It hurt to hear.

“Either way, I’m afraid neither of us will live to find out.”

Yangyang tried to lay the cloth across the wound. Her hand found stone beneath it. The leg, the blood, the torn fabric wavered together, then returned with such force that she felt the wound in a part of her own body she had never injured.

She withdrew her hand, shaking.

The Warden who had asked after her watched the movement.

“You don’t have to look at that,” he said. “Stay where my friend can see you.”

She moved beside the other man’s head.

He was looking at her shoulders. She glanced behind herself, expecting the mechanical bird, and found only the moonlit wall.

They were calling to her.

Something blue moved at the edge of her sight. When she turned, it was gone.

“My name is Yangyang,” she told them. “I’ve come from the other side of the peaks.”

The injured man smiled faintly at the sound of her voice. Perhaps he heard the name. Perhaps the only part that reached him was that she had come.

Beyond the wall, a woman called for someone to open a door.

Yangyang turned toward her.

The two soldiers went on waiting for the next wave.

***

The village chief lay beneath the remains of a wooden sign.

Yangyang knew his office from the seal tied at his waist, though his clothes were those of a man called away from work in a garden. Earth remained beneath his nails. One shoe had come off, and he had drawn the bare foot under his other leg as if he were cold.

He saw her approaching and tried to rise.

“Xuanling… are those Xuanling Birds?”

His gaze searched the air around her.

“Thank heavens. You didn’t all…”

She knelt before he could hurt himself trying to sit up.

“Please stay where you are. I can hear you from here.”

His fingers moved toward her sleeve. They passed through it, and for an instant she felt them against her skin, colder than the night in the gorge.

“Go now. The village is already…” He swallowed with difficulty. “Promise me you’ll see if there’s still anyone. Take them away. Away from here.”

Yangyang looked down the street.

A doorway burned around the outline of a person who no longer moved. Farther on, a child’s stool lay beneath a cart. Someone had tied a pot to its side to make more room for the bedding.

The sight brought Xianling so near that she almost called for Qingyan.

Two centuries lay between the carts. Her body knew only that there had been a child beside one of them.

“I’ll look for anyone I can still help,” she told the chief.

He tried to answer. The hand beneath her sleeve opened.

Yangyang remained beside it until the cold loosened.

Then she went down the street.

The woman calling at the door was gone. Her voice went on calling.

***

The sound rose from beneath the paving.

“Why? Why did it have to—”

A breath caught, strained, and became a cry.

“I don’t want to die. I can’t die. My brothers and sisters, my parents… My wife and children are waiting for me.”

Yangyang turned in a slow circle.

There was no mouth she could find for it.

“Tacet Discords! Those cursed, wretched Tacet Discords!”

The burning street filled with the shapes that had come over the walls. Claws broke a roof beam. A soldier stood up beneath them with a weapon already snapped in half.

“Killing you a hundred times—a thousand times—would never be enough!”

Yangyang’s hand closed around her sword.

The hatred entered as readily as grief.

She felt its terrible rightness for the length of a heartbeat: the wish for the thing that had hurt them to go on being hurt until it understood, until it returned the hand, the face, the whole unbroken morning. One more cut. Another. All the deaths still unpaid when the blade came up again.

Qingyan had dragged the ancestral sword across the stones.

She could feel the weight of it now as he must have felt it. Something to put both hands around while the world went on taking things away.

The cry struck again.

Her own sword trembled in its scabbard.

Yangyang opened her fingers one at a time.

The man kept shouting for his family between the threats. Each time he named them, the rage broke enough for her to hear how frightened he was.

She went toward that part of the sound.

There was nowhere to kneel that did not belong to someone fallen.

At last she sat beside the broken weapon and covered her ears.

It made no difference.

***

How many battles had been fought here?

The question moved through her, and the field answered with another.

How many were still to come?

She saw the mill yard beneath the burning street. Mingyu lay where an older soldier had fallen. Yanlang’s spear crossed the shadow of a weapon broken two hundred years before. Between them, her hands rested on stones that had received more bodies than she could count.

The sounds kept entering.

A man wanted someone to take the knife from beneath his hip because he could not move and its hilt was hurting him. A woman was trying to describe the road to her house. A young soldier asked whether his face would frighten his mother.

Under all of them ran the same bewilderment.

They had been going home.

Some had expected to finish a watch first. Some had known it might take years. They had saved a jar of wine, left a coat on a peg, promised to be there when a child grew tall enough to reach a branch. The future had been full of small appointments. Here they were, caught at the moment the road failed.

Yangyang thought of Mingyu’s mother going down to look at each arriving column.

She had not learned the woman’s name.

She could remember exactly how Mingyu’s mouth had tried to form it. She could hear a man dead two hundred years and could not bring back that one unfinished sound.

Her eyes filled. The burning street blurred until every lamp resembled the one beside a bed in a foreign house.

*Is this what you saw, shijie?*

The drowned city rose through the fire. A red cap turned. Beyond it lay roads on which the Wind had carried what she could and buried what had to stay. Every village had given her another person to remember. Somewhere at the end of all that listening, she had found a border her body could not cross.

Yangyang understood the longing to become large enough.

She could feel it beginning again.

The hands over her ears had grown cold. Her breath no longer seemed to return from the place it left. The crying spread beyond the street into valleys she could not see; if she followed it, there would be no need to choose whose suffering to approach first.

She could go to the woman at the door, to the child beneath the cart, to every road which ended with someone calling home. She could find the missing bridges. She could cross the great rivers. She could carry them—

Her breath broke against the wound in her side.

For an instant she felt the stone beneath her knees.

At Norfall she had called the Wind down from the sky.

She remembered how small shijie had become inside her sleeve. How carefully she had kept the last word, as though tomorrow were a little warmth she could shelter by closing around it.

Yangyang lowered her hands.

“I asked you to come home,” she whispered. “I meant there should be a place for you when you got there.”

The man beneath the street cried for his children.

She bent over her own lap.

Was this all she could offer him? A place inside another person’s hurt? Must every voice she heard remain where it entered, waiting for her to grow wide enough to hold the next?

At Norfall she had listened while the city answered. She could remember the answers as clearly as the pain: a brother who knew where the pears were cooked; a bowl put down at the roadside; the ordinary insistence of people speaking to someone they loved.

Here, when the crying stopped for breath, she could hear only herself.

She had no familiar name for these people. No surviving mother to call to the receiver. No whole household within reach of her hand.

But there had been a song on the mountain.

Liangyu had left room in it for two friends.

Yangyang heard the place where her voice had faltered, and beneath it the tune continuing, known well enough to be found again after weeping. Tide and mist. A river without a bridge. Hair turning white while the wine at home remained untasted.

The people who made that song had known this road.

She lifted her head.

***

The first note barely left her mouth.

Yangyang tried to give it too much air. Pain caught beneath her ribs, and the sound collapsed into a cough.

She waited with a hand against her side.

The street burned. The man begged to finish speaking. Nothing had changed because she wished to answer.

She began again, lower, in the part of her voice she used beside a sleeping person.

> 玄翎飛，飛過江水泱泱，
> 山茫茫，明月光，
> 月光照著影子長……

The Xuanling flew over broad water. Beyond the water stood the mountains. Moonlight found the traveller there and laid a long shadow at her feet.

Yangyang sang to the end of the phrase and stopped for breath.

In the pause, the bruised Warden coughed.

She almost abandoned the tune to turn toward him. Then she heard something beneath the cough: a faint movement of his voice, trying to enter where hers had left room.

She kept that room open.

His mouth moved. One sound came through, too soft to understand.

Yangyang took the next breath slowly.

> 海內兮傳四方，
> 喚伊莫相忘。

Carry the call to every quarter. Ask the one far away to remember.

The word for remembering caught in her throat.

She saw Suisui at a table, reading a letter in which Yangyang had written plenty about the weather and very little about being lonely. She saw her mother smoothing the lip of a shell for a daughter who had left too quickly to receive it.

The song had room for that daughter too.

Yangyang let the ache enter the next note.

The air moved.

It passed her cheek toward the man on the ground, carrying the faint roughness in her voice. For a moment she expected the familiar drain, the sound drawing her after it until there would be nothing left where she knelt.

Instead, she felt the next breath waiting in her chest.

She took it.

The man’s coughing eased. His eyes moved from the burning gate to something nearer, lower, beyond her shoulder. His mouth worked again.

“I thought…”

Yangyang went on softly.

“I thought I would have time when I got home.”

The sentence reached its end.

She bowed her head over the note she was holding. Tears fell from her chin onto the backs of her hands.

When she looked up, the place beside her was empty.

She listened for the cough to begin again.

Wind moved through the broken banner.

***

For several breaths she was afraid of the quiet.

Had she answered him, or merely made herself unable to hear?

She turned toward the two soldiers at the wall. They were still there. One was looking at the strip of cloth in his hand, his mouth moving with the tune.

“My wife sings that while she works,” he said.

His friend turned toward him.

“I didn’t know you were married.”

“Just before we left. There wasn’t time to…”

The sentence thinned. He looked down, smiling with sudden, painful shyness.

Yangyang let her voice fall almost to a hum.

The two men sat together in the little space it left. For the first time since she had found them, neither watched the gate.

She did not see a house appear. No waiting woman came through the fire. Only a man with blood on his sleeve remembering that he had once been happy, and another turning his head to hear about it.

The wall showed faintly through them.

Yangyang sang the verse Liangyu had struggled to finish.

> 明月光，悠悠夜涼，
> 海內兮傳四方，
> 喚阿爹阿孃……

Father. Mother.

She could hear the childishness of those calls inside the voices of grown soldiers. Their armour had never made them older than the need to be met at a door. At the end, with the night cold and their clothes wet, they wanted someone who knew how to put a hand against their foreheads.

Her own voice shook.

She let it shake. The tune was still there.

***

The tide rose in the song.

Mist covered the road. The journey lengthened; the return receded. Yangyang could see the eastern track again, the scouts’ cord disappearing beneath the fallen stone, the unfinished line on their map.

She sang the line about the river without a bridge.

Her hands remembered Chixia’s fingers slipping.

She sang the wine left untasted at home.

Mingyu’s mother went down to another arriving column.

The names were too new to lie easily inside an old song. They hurt each time she passed them. Yet the melody took their weight without smoothing it away, as water passes over stones and keeps the shape of every one beneath its surface.

Yangyang had sung with the Wind before. She knew the wonder of a note continuing after her lungs were empty, another woman’s strength meeting the place where hers ended.

Here she had to stop.

She drew breath with her hand against her ribs, and in those pauses the battlefield changed.

A burning beam became the shadow of a beam long fallen. A weapon lost the hand that kept trying to lift it. Near the chief’s broken sign, moonlight reached a patch of bare ground which the fire had hidden.

The cry beneath the paving rose again.

“I can’t die. They’re waiting. My children are waiting—”

Yangyang’s voice faltered.

She could not make that waiting harmless. She could not know what had happened at the door he meant to reach. His children might have grown old with his name or died before the season turned. Two hundred years had taken the answer from everyone here.

The song reached the invocation of the bird.

> 喚翎鳥，
> 勿望，勿忘……

Do not wait. Do not forget.

Liangyu had sung the two phrases on almost the same breath.

Yangyang had heard them first as a traveller’s sorrow. Now the space between them opened: a door left unbarred, a lamp which need not burn until its keeper’s hand failed. Someone living through another autumn, remembering the absent person while bringing the washing in.

She wanted that life for the man’s children so fiercely that the next note broke.

She stopped until she could sing it again.

The voice beneath the ground went on crying. It had exhausted the names of its enemies. What remained was a father trying to remember whether he had held his youngest child on the morning he left.

Yangyang listened.

He could not decide. He had been in a hurry. Someone had called from the road. He remembered a sleeve caught in a little hand, remembered freeing it, and could not find what had come before.

She put her palm against the paving.

Her song was almost speech now.

She gave him the refrain again, leaving time between the words. The wind took it across the street. The sound went farther than she could see, and she stayed where her knees touched the earth.

The man’s voice drew away from the ground.

For a moment it seemed to come from beside her, at the height of someone standing with his face turned toward a distant house.

Then she could no longer place it.

Yangyang kept her hand against the stone until its cold had reached her palm.

***

The last verse asked for autumn.

After the river, the mist, the years on the road, it asked for one cool night and a journey home.

Yangyang had no road she could show these people. The houses in which they had been expected were gone. She could only offer the song that had survived them, learned from a girl whose friends had died yesterday, sung by another who had wanted to follow her own sorrow into the wind.

She sang it with the breath she had.

> 魂魄蕩，長風長，
> 待到秋夜涼，
> 送我回故鄉。

The long note travelled out over the roofs.

Yangyang felt the air rise beneath it. The sensation was slight, unlike the great rush at the cliff: a current finding the underside of something small enough to be carried. It held the sound for the length of her breath and let it go.

Her shoulders loosened.

In the corner of her sight, blue lifted and settled. She did not turn to look. The note still needed finishing.

At the far end of the street, the village chief lowered the hand which had been reaching toward her sleeve.

The fire around him dimmed.

She heard a latch close, gently, from somewhere beyond the last house.

Then the street began to empty.

It went by small distances. A face she could no longer distinguish; a roof through which the night showed; the pale line of a road fading beneath another road. Yangyang kept singing until the last phrase ended, and afterward she stayed quiet enough to hear whatever had not gone with it.

For a while there was only the wind.

Then a small metal foot touched the ground beside her.

***

The Rafter Kite stood on the edge of the stone trough.

Behind it, the mill wall lay broken beneath the night sky. No flame moved along the fallen roof. Mingyu’s face was covered with the cloth Yangyang had placed there. Yanlang rested beneath the arch, turned toward the road.

She had not brought either of them back.

The sight emptied the breath from her chest.

Yangyang bent forward and wept, quietly at first, then with both hands braced against the ground because her body could not hold itself through it. The song had left her throat sore. She could feel every place the fighting had hurt her.

The bird waited.

When she could lift her head, it came closer along the trough. Its wing opened a little, showing the nick beneath the feather.

She listened to the small mechanism within it.

Farther away, wind moved through the arch. She heard the loose tile on the mill roof, water beneath the broken wheel, a faint call from somewhere beyond the fallen lane.

The sounds had places again.

She waited for the call to repeat.

“Yangyang! Yanlang! Mingyu!”

Liangyu’s voice, rough with distance.

Yangyang tried to answer and coughed.

She wet her lips with the last water in the flask. Her hand shook so badly that she needed both to put the stopper back.

“Liangyu, I’m in the mill yard,” she called. “The lane has fallen. Come around by the lower arch.”

The answer reached her through stone and wind. Several people were coming.

She closed her eyes.

When she opened them, the Rafter Kite was watching her.

“I don’t know how I did that,” she told it. “I only knew the song.”

The bird tilted its head.

She thought of the note moving away while the next breath remained hers. She could still feel where the current had met it. When she tried to summon that feeling, the air stirred uncertainly at her wrist and was ordinary again.

She let it be.

Beyond the arch, a lamp appeared. Liangyu came behind it with a carrying party, her coat torn at the shoulder where she had lifted someone through the gate.

Her gaze went first to Yangyang, then to the two still shapes beside her.

Yangyang stood with one hand on the trough.

“They died before I could get them to the steps,” she said. Her voice caught. “I stayed with them.”

Liangyu looked toward Mingyu’s covered face.

For a moment neither woman moved.

Then Liangyu came across the yard and put her arms around Yangyang, carefully, above the dressing. Yangyang leaned against her and felt the effort it cost them both to remain standing.

“The villagers are inside,” Liangyu said. “Qingyan is with his aunt. We came as soon as we found a way around the fallen wall.”

The carrying party knelt beside the dead.

“There was something else here,” Yangyang said against Liangyu’s shoulder. “People from the old war. They wanted to go home.”

Liangyu’s hand settled at the back of her coat.

“I heard singing as we came around the wall. I thought someone from the village had followed us.”

“I remembered where you left room for Qingzhi.”

Liangyu held her a little closer.

Yangyang listened to the preparations around them: cloth being laid out, the scrape of a litter pole, one Warden asking another to lift on the same count. Mingyu was going through the arch. Yanlang would follow.

She had a great deal to tell the people waiting inside the Hold. Some of it would take more words than she could manage tonight. Some she had heard without ever learning a name.

She did not begin arranging it yet.

Liangyu drew back far enough to look at her.

“Will you lean on me as far as the road?”

“I’d like that,” Yangyang said. “I’m very tired.”

They followed the litters beneath the arch.

The Rafter Kite flew ahead to the lamp, folded its wings, and waited there. Between the two women, almost too softly to hear, Liangyu began the first line of the song.

Yangyang listened until the place where another voice could enter.

Then she took a breath.

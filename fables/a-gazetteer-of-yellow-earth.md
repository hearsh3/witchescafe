## I. 七 — Seven
### *a counting rhyme of the capital, taught to children and forbidden twice*

Seven cities, seven gates,
seven dragons in the wall —
one has horns and one has wings
and one will eat them all.

Seven streets and seven seals,
seven judges in the hall —
one writes left and one writes right
and one writes nothing at all.

Seven swords and seven scars,
seven Sentinels stand tall —
one sleeps deep and one sleeps light
and one has heard us call.

The ISA's petition to suppress a third time was returned by the Emperor's office with one line: 'Let them sing. Count the ones who change the last word.' The ISA has been counting for forty years. The last word changes seasonally.*

> *The surveyor observes that a rhyme which survives two suppressions has passed from folklore into infrastructure.*

## II. 兵 — The Soldier
### *Jinzhou, the youngest gate*

The Midnight Rangers drill the sluice-song at dawn now.

It came in with the foreign dispatches, a tune with words that stick: seven stairs to the cistern arch, and a route that saves your legs in the dark. General Jiyan ran it past the war college and the war college sent it back annotated in three colors of disagreement, and Jiyan put the three colors in a drawer and taught it to the garrison anyway, because the general had fought under a Sentinel that commanded time and learned from it one temporal law the college had overlooked: a minute spent teaching people to sing is a minute bought at the price of chalk.

The Rangers took to it the way soldiers take to anything that replaces panic with meter. By the second week the sluice crews were singing it on the wall at shift change, and by the third the fishmongers' children had it, and by the fourth the children had improved it, because children are the war college that matters, and their amendment — *seven stairs, the left one's broke, skip the third one, Gran!* — was filed by the Bureau of Works under *infrastructure feedback, civilian, accepted.*

Jiyan told Jinhsi about it over the morning report, and Jinhsi laughed for the first time in that report's memory, and said, "The Septimont dispatches said a man sang the whole city home. *Garnish,* his people called him. Class C."

"His people classify Fortes on a scale built for arenas."

"Everyone classifies on a scale built for what they fear most. We built ours for the wall." She turned a brush in the light the way she did when she was about to say the thing the report would carry. "I've been thinking about scales, Jiyan. Ours run from *useful in combat* to *useless in combat.* Every child rated below the third rank goes home with a number that tells them how little they matter to the wall. We've been sorting our people by their relevance to our worst day."

The general sat with that. He had carried a rated number himself, once. Third rank. The number had meant everything to him at sixteen and means a different everything now, a cage he outgrew the way bones outgrew their casts, and the cast's outline still itched some mornings.

"The dispatch mentions a clerk," he said. "On the frontier. Horned. Forte classified *morphological, inert.* She runs the intake desk for an Adventurers' Guild outpost and she invented a fourth box on the registration form."

"What's in it?"

"The margin."

Jinhsi looked at him. He held the look. They had served together long enough that some conversations happened at the speed of glances: the Magistrate saw the implication arrive in his face and followed it home.

"Draft me a proposal," she said. "A fourth rank. Below the combat tiers. Rated for everything we've been leaving out of the ledger — the songs, the healers, the woman in the terraces who can taste iron in the lake a day before the tremors. Call it—" She stopped. Considered. Wrote two characters on the morning's blank margin.

民用。 *Civil service.*

"The war college will call it decorative."

"The war college may decorate the proposal with its opinions. The city will decorate it with the people who've been keeping us alive between the worst days." She set the brush down. "One more thing. The amendment from the dam."

"Two hundred breaths."

"Two hundred breaths, and a hand on the sluice that hates to turn. I received a version of the amendment that had been forwarded through the Secretariat in Mingting with a note from Minister Shen." She found the paper. "'A good instrument. The youngest gate invents well.' And then, in his own hand: 'Who catches you, Magistrate, when the hand is yours?'"

The morning held the question. Through the window, the lake moved in its bowl of mountain, and somewhere far beneath the water, in the patient root system of the range, the one whose arithmetic held the whole gate dreamed of libraries that still stood, and a red cap turning in a current, and a girl on a wall with a sword.

## III. 虛 — The Hollow
### *Mengzhou, or the city of made things*

The old master told the apprentice: a hollow reed makes music. A solid one makes fencing. Consider what the river does with the hollow one.

Mengzhou is the reed. The whole city is an instrument played by the breath of what passes through it: the automata on the Xuanfang line, holding the dark off two cities with joints and cores and faith; the great printing houses of Bookbinders' Row, setting absence into type and selling it by the evening; the koi under the bridges, reading the undersides of dreams. And at the center of it, the art called Xu — the hollow art — the craft of printing what is missing so precisely that the room it leaves behind can be walked in.

The puppeteer's guild holds the oldest charter in the city. Older than the Row, older than the garden courts, older than the canal system that gives Mengzhou its particular smell of water-weed and argument. The charter is written on gut, in a hand that predates movable type, and it says three things:

*That the Sentinel taught the first puppet-maker the art of joints.*
*That the first puppet-maker asked the Sentinel what a puppet thinks about.*
*That the Sentinel said: the same thing you think about, when you think about your mother.*

The guild has been arguing the third clause for six hundred years. The current grandmaster, a woman called Zhou Min who lost her left hand to a lathe at twenty-four and has been building with the remaining one ever since, considers the argument her guild's finest product. "The argument is the puppet," she told the frontier court, at a hearing on automata retirement policy. "If we stop arguing about whether they think, we'll have answered the question in the wrong direction."

Below the guild hall, in a cellar the charter calls the gut room, the apprentices learn the trade's true first skill, which is listening.

They bring the broken ones down here. The third-generation units from the Xuanfang line, rotated off the wall after forty years of holding faith against the Tacet dark, their cores fading like embers toward ash. The guild calls this work *guishou* — returning hands — and the apprentices do it in silence, because the cores still sing as they dim, and the songs are the things the units kept.

A sergeant's voice, giving the evening orders. A cook's laugh. The weight of snow on a particular beam, measured by the creak. Eleven trips across a flood with children.

The apprentice removes the core. The song continues for a moment in the air, the way a bell continues past the striking, and then it is over, and the apprentice wraps the core in the gut cloth and carries it to the register, where Zhou Min enters the name — the name the garrison called it, the real one — and the date, and, if the soldiers sent one, the story.

"The Sentinel says everything that keeps faith long enough begins to resemble the faithful," Zhou Min told the hearing. "My guild would add a footnote. Everything that resembles the faithful long enough begins to grieve."

The court asked what she proposed.

"Tend the grief," she said. "It's load-bearing."

## IV. 法 — The Law
### *Mingting, the capital, where every street runs to the same door*

The ISA recruit's first lesson is the corridor.

It runs the length of the Censorate's basement, lit by lamps that are replaced on the hour by attendants who walk in step, in silence, in soft shoes. The corridor has no doors. The doors were bricked in during the rectification of the fifth Huangdi, and the brickwork was done by masons who were paid in silver and in forgetting, and the forgetting was administered by the ISA's third division, whose charter permits it, whose budget funds it, whose annual report to the Emperor itemizes the number of doors bricked and the number of memories cleaned and calls the total *public order.*

Qiu Han had walked the corridor on her first day, her tenth, her hundredth. She would walk it on her last. The lesson of the corridor is the lesson of the capital: every passage leads to the same room. The room's name changes with the administration; its function stays. Files go in. Verdicts come out. The distance between them is the corridor, which is exactly as long as the Emperor requires, and the Emperor's requirements are set by the ISA, and the ISA's requirements are set by the corridor, and the corridor is a circle that calls itself a straight line, and the calling is the third division's finest work.

She was thirty-four. She had served three chiefs. She had seen the rectifications from the inside, the way a tooth sees the mouth — embedded, useful, surrounded by the mechanism of its own extraction. Her Forte was a small, precise thing: she could taste lies on her own tongue. When she spoke an untruth, the flavor of iron arrived, strong as a coin held in the cheek. The examiners had tested it thoroughly. She could lie, if the operation demanded it; she would taste the lie for hours afterward, and the taste was the price, and the third division called the price *acceptable.*

She had tasted iron every day for eleven years.

The file on her desk this morning was unremarkable. A village teacher in Yuezhou province, reported for *disharmonious instruction.* The teacher had taught the counting rhyme — the one about seven dragons — and had, according to the report, changed the last word of the third verse from *call* to *fall,* and had then, according to the local magistrate's annotation, been overheard saying to a colleague, at a market stall, buying persimmons, that the fall was beautiful. The report had been filed by a neighbor. The annotation had been added by the magistrate, whose own file sat three shelves over in the same archive, which was the corridor's way of noting that magistrates who annotate promptly have magistrates' files that stay thin.

Qiu Han tasted the neighbor's report. Iron. She tasted the magistrate's annotation. Copper — the flavor of a truth bent to survive the filing system.

She tasted her own assessment, which she had written on the margin before reading the file at all, because the assessment was always the same and the third division expected it on time: *Forwarded for action. Recommend correction-grade two.*

Iron. Strong enough to fill the room.

She sat with the taste a long time. Through her window — the ISA's windows face inward, toward the ministry courtyards, because the architecture teaches the lesson the corridor began — she could see the roof of the Ministry of Rites, where, this season, a foreign opera was being staged at the Secretariat's invitation. *The Maiden of Two Names.* She had seen it on opening night, in the audience, in her capacity as an observer, because the ISA observes everything the Secretariat invites and reports the audience's reactions on a form that has fourteen boxes and a margin. The margin is for things the boxes miss. She had used it once, that night, for one word:

*Wept.*

She had meant the audience. The form would read it that way. The iron taste had been very faint.

She looked at the teacher's file. She looked at the corridor's far end, where the lamplight went on without argument, on the hour, in soft shoes, and thought about a god across the sea that had spent its last breath telling its people to lead well, and a Secretariat minister who had opened a door by hosting a song, and a girl on a screen wearing a collar around her throat and holding scissors that had cut a god and kept the collar anyway. She thought about the rhyme. The last word changed seasonally, the Emperor's note had said. The ISA had been counting the changes for forty years. *Call. Fall. Wall. Hall.* The rhyme went on, and the last word went on changing, and the children went on singing, and the counting was the only lesson the corridor had ever offered that struck her as education.

She opened the assessment form.

She wrote: *Forwarded.* Then she stopped. Iron.

She crossed out *forwarded.* She wrote, in the margin of her own margin: *Insufficient evidence of disharmony. The rhyme is infrastructure. The last word is a weather vane. Weather vanes turn; this is their function; the function is diagnostic, see Bureau of Rites circular 14-7b, paragraph on tolerated variation in oral tradition, and observe that the circular was co-signed by the Emperor's own brush, which supersedes.*

She tasted the assessment. Copper — bent, surviving, the truth in its traveling clothes.

It was the best she could do inside the corridor. She knew the corridor's length, and her own, and the distance between the teacher's file and the bricked-up doors, and she had spent eleven years learning that the best she could do inside the corridor was a copper-tasting sentence that kept one file thin. Tomorrow there would be another. And another. And the corridor would go on being exactly as long as the Emperor required, and the lamps would go on being replaced on the hour, and she would go on tasting iron, and the tasting would be the price, and the price would be called *acceptable* by everyone who had stopped paying it.

She sealed the assessment and walked it to the filing window.

On her way back, she passed the wall where the Censorate keeps its mirror — polished bronze, mounted at the corridor's midpoint, so that every agent sees themselves at the exact center of the passage. The mirror had been installed by the first chief as a reminder: *you are always being watched.* Qiu Han had passed it four thousand times. Today she stopped.

She looked at her own face. The face looked back. It was a competent face, correctly maintained, the kind of face that filed on time and tasted what it carried and went home and slept eventually.

She adjusted her collar — the ISA uniform has a high one, boned, which the agents call the courtesy, because the courtesy is the first thing the corridor teaches — and went back to her desk, and opened the next file, and tasted the assessment before she wrote it, and the taste was iron, and she wrote it anyway, and the lamp was changed on the hour, and the soft shoes went past, and the corridor was exactly as long as it had always been.

She kept the margin. That was hers.

## V. 道 — The Way
### *Xuanfang, and the country between*

Between Mengzhou and Jinzhou lies Xuanfang, and Xuanfang is the country where the gods put the argument.

The sandstone pillars rise from the river gorges like the fingers of a hand that died reaching, and between them the suspension bridges rock on the wind, and the timber towns cling to the rock faces the way the country's people cling to their own opinions: with the whole body, at an angle, in defiance of the obvious. The Mengzhou road comes down through cloud and bamboo into the gorge floor, and the Jinzhou road climbs up the other side through fortress gates and iron prayer-trees, and in the middle, at the river ford, a toll bridge has collected one copper per crossing since the second Huangdi's time, and the toll collector's family has held the bridge for nine generations and has the arms to show for it.

The Sentinel's automata stand along the upper ridges: the Bulwark, three generations of Mengzhou's craft, shield-shapes on the skyline. Below the Bulwark, the frontier court adjudicates. Below the court, the villages.

Old Ge's village sits at the base of the fifth pillar, population seventy, elevation *yes.* Old Ge is the village head by the ancient method of being the last one who wanted the job, and his authority extends to the village well, the village shrine, and the village argument, which is the same argument the country was put here to hold and which Old Ge has been managing for forty years with the one tool his office provides: a stone bench at the crossroads, worn smooth, facing all four directions.

"The capital says one thing," he told the census taker, at the last count. "The gate says another. The fox says a third. I have a bench. Sit."

The census taker sat. The bench held. This is Old Ge's whole political philosophy.

The argument, this decade, ran as follows. The capital said: register the marked. The Censorate's local sub-bureau, a clerk with a cold and a bad stamp, had arrived in spring with the forms. The gate said: train the marked, because the wall wants hands, and the wall's want supersedes all forms. General Jiyan's recruitment posters went up on the same trees the Censorate's notices had gone on, and the paste was still wet when the second layer went over the first, and by summer the prayer-tree at the village shrine wore six layers of competing authority and had begun, the shrine-keeper insisted, to lean.

And the fox said — what the fox always said, which was nothing official and everything practical, which arrived in the form of the foxgirl who came down from the Sentinel's terrace twice a year with a basket of candied hawthorn and a judicial attention that missed nothing and adjudicated everything and left the villages, afterward, with a settled feeling they could describe precisely the way you describe the taste of rain: by opening your mouth.

Old Ge had governed through the argument his whole life, and the argument's lesson was this: the country between two cities belongs to the country. The capital weighs the citizen. The gate values the citizen. The fox enjoys the citizen. And the citizen, at the crossroads, on the bench, under all six layers of competing paste, grows persimmons and sells them at the ford and keeps the shrine swept and sends a child to the frontier court when the court asks and sends a child to the capital when the capital demands and tips the toll collector's daughter a copper when the harvest is good, and the tip is the whole of the citizen's foreign policy, and it has held for nine generations, and Old Ge, whose Forte is that his persimmons ripen three days ahead of anyone else's in the county and who has been rated *civil-adjacent* by the gate and *unregistered* by the capital and *delicious* by the fox, sits on the bench at dusk and watches the bridges sway and considers the country he was born to hold.

The argument holds the pillars up. The pillars hold the bridges. The bridges hold the people. The people hold the argument.

It is the oldest engineering in Huanglong, and the most fragile, and Old Ge keeps it the way the toll collector keeps the bridge: one copper at a time, one crossing at a time, with the arms to show for it.

## VI. 記 — The Record
### *a lesson given at the Huaxu Academy, Jinzhou branch, by the principal investigator, transcribed by a student who was told to pay attention*

"A question." Xiangli Yao removed his glasses, cleaned them, replaced them, and looked at the lecture hall the way he looked at all assemblies: as data, pending. "We have spoken this term about the Sonoro Sphere, the Terminal, the Resonance Beacon. Fine instruments. Well-funded. But I want to ask you about the instrument nobody funded, and nobody designed, and nobody applied for."

He turned to the board, where the morning's equations stood in his careful hand, and beside them, in a visiting lecturer's chalk, a single character:

風 — wind.

"Last year, a colleague at the frontier court sent me a set of readings. An anomalous frequency pattern over the northeast shoulder of the dam — persistent, localized, exhibiting characteristics of Resonator signature decay without any corresponding Resonator source. I ran the spectra four times. The results were consistent."

He tapped the character.

"The signature belongs to a person who, by every metric this Academy employs, no longer exists. She has no Terminal registration, no census entry, no body, no address. She has, by the Almanac Bureau's reckoning, a patrol-log entry — one line, in a second ink, in a hand that is demonstrably her own, and demonstrably postmortem. She has a kite. She has a teacup custom at a restaurant this city knows well. She has approximately nine-tenths of a name, and she is, at this moment, carrying a letter across four seas toward a woman she has been walking toward since before either of them had a body in common."

The hall was quiet. Morning light fell through the lecture-room shutters in bars, and the chalk dust turned in it.

"Our models say this is impossible. Our models say a Resonator's frequency disperses at cellular death; the signature collapses; the pattern is lost. The readings say: a woman loved another woman past the point of having a body to do it in, and the frequency — her frequency — reorganized around the intention, and persists. The spectral decay follows an asymptote I have never seen in a lab. It approaches zero. It will never reach it."

He cleaned his glasses again. A tell, his students knew: the gesture he performs when the data has moved him and he would prefer it didn't show.

"There is a word for this in the old texts that I will never put in a paper, because journals have standards and I have tenure and I do not intend to lose the second by violating the first. But I will say it here, in a classroom, to students whose whole career will be the argument between what the instruments find and what the models permit."

He wrote one more character, beneath the first, and underlined it twice.

魂。*Soul.*

"You didn't hear that from me. Class dismissed. The assignment is on the board, due by the solstice, and if any of you cite this lecture in the footnotes I will mark you down for insufficient rigor and grade you up for nerve."

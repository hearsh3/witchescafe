# Null Result

*A Rinascita Saga story — after Spring Back*

---

## I. Septimont, Lower District

The fruit vendor on Coil Street had a system. Every crate counted twice, morning and noon, because the lower district ate honesty for breakfast. On the fourth day of the month he counted forty apples, turned to shout at a passing cart, and counted again. Forty apples. He counted a third time out of a feeling he would later describe to his wife as *a draft on the back of the neck.*

Forty apples. A girl standing beside the crate, one apple in her hand, chewing.

He opened his mouth to yell. The yell went somewhere. He looked at her — grey coat, grey eyes, hair the color of a screen after the broadcast ends — and the part of his brain in charge of caring about apples simply filed her under weather. He shrugged and went back to his cart.

The girl finished the apple. She looked at the core for a while, as if waiting for it to have an opinion. Then she set it back in the crate, on top, dead center, and walked on.

She had been in Septimont for eleven days. She had counted them because counting was one of the three things she knew how to do. The other two were walking and being forgotten.

She slept in the drainage arcades under the Coil, where the holo-ads leaked down through the grates and painted moving light on the wet stone. LUPA'S CHOICE — SPARKLING VIGOR. A wolf-eared woman grinned twelve feet tall, over and over, and the girl watched her all night, every night, because the ad was the only thing in the district that looked at her back.

On the twelfth day, a cat found her.

It was a horrible cat. One ear chewed to lace, fur in six directions, ribs like a washboard, an expression of total administrative contempt. It sat down in front of her, in the puddle-light, and stared.

The girl stared back.

The cat kept staring.

Something in the girl — small, structural, the size of a rivet — noticed that it was being *perceived*, and shivered.

The cat stood, walked three steps, stopped, and looked over its shoulder with the impatience of middle management.

She followed the cat for six blocks, down a stairwell with a broken rail, past a door that had been kicked in and repaired and kicked in again, to a basement window glowing surgical white. The cat slid through a gap in the boards. The girl crouched and looked in.

Inside: cables in rivers, screens in cliffs, a surgical table with the straps unbuckled, and a woman in a stained coat with pink hair and pink cat ears, arguing at full volume with a machine.

"— because your calibration is *garbage*, that's why. Garbage in, garbage out, garbage all the way down, a whole civilization of garbage—"

The machine beeped.

"Don't take that tone with me."

The horrible cat leapt onto the table, sat on a datapad, and yowled once.

"Ugh. You brought something." The woman turned. Her eyes were the flat gold of instruments. They swept the window, the boards, the gap —

— and stopped. On the girl. Directly, precisely, the way a needle stops on a frequency.

The girl froze. In eleven days, four thousand pairs of eyes had slid across her like rain on glass. These ones *snagged.*

"Huh," said the woman, in the exact tone of someone finding money in an old coat. She crossed the basement in six strides, wrenched the boards off the window with a crowbar that had clearly done this before, grabbed the girl by the collar, and hauled her bodily through the gap and onto the table.

"Sit. Stay. Boots off my table. What are you?"

The girl opened her mouth. Nothing arrived.

"Wrong answer. Try again." The woman was already moving, snapping sensor pads onto the girl's wrists, her temples, one on the tip of her nose for reasons known only to science. "Name. Age. Point of origin. Blood type. Allergies. Opinions on jazz."

"I don't have those."

"Which ones?"

"...Any of them."

The woman stopped. She leaned in close, close enough that the girl could see her own grey eyes doubled in gold, and grinned — a wide, delighted, deeply unsafe grin.

"*Fantastic,*" said Nanally.

---

The readouts took four hours, because the readouts kept committing suicide. Every scan of the girl came back within tolerances for exactly nine seconds and then decayed to zero — heartrate, resonance, mass, all of it sighing down to a flat line while the girl sat there, visibly alive, swinging her feet.

"You're doing that," Nanally said, jabbing a stylus at the flatline. "Stop it."

"Doing what?"

"Convincing my instruments you're nobody. Cut it out. They're impressionable."

"I'm not doing anything."

"Correct! *That's* the problem. You are doing nothing, at approximately nine hundred watts." Nanally spun her chair, kicked off a server rack, glided across the floor, and slapped a fresh probe against the girl's sternum. "Again."

Nine seconds of data. Then the sigh. Then zero.

The horrible cat sat on the console and supervised.

By the third hour, Nanally had stopped talking to the girl and started talking about her, in the third person, at speed, to the room. The girl listened to herself be described — *ambient significance decay, radius approximately four meters, self-inclusive* — and understood roughly every fifth word, and understood the important thing perfectly: this woman had been looking at her for three hours without once looking away.

Nobody had ever done that. The apple vendor had lasted two seconds. The gate guards had lasted four. Her longest previous record was the Lupa hologram, which was a machine.

"Hey," she said, in the fourth hour.

"Busy."

"Why can you see me?"

Nanally kept typing. "Because I'm smarter than everyone else."

"That's it?"

"That's *everything.* Other people's attention is cheap. It goes wherever the world tells it to go. Mine goes where *I* tell it." She spun around. The chair squeaked. "You are, at this moment, the most interesting object on this continent. Some vendor's monkey brain says you're weather? Fine. My brain says you're a *paper.* Possibly three papers. Possibly a keynote." She bared her teeth. "You could stand in a crowd of ten thousand and I would find you in four seconds, because ten thousand people are boring and you are *data.*"

The girl sat with this.

"So you can see me," she said slowly, "because you're selfish."

"Yes."

"That's terrible."

"It's *working,* isn't it?"

The girl had no counterargument. The rivet-sized thing inside her, the thing that had shivered under the cat's stare, warmed by a fraction of a degree.

At the end of the fourth hour, Nanally pulled all the sensors off in one long ripping motion, pinned the datapad under one arm, and paced. Twice around the table. The pink ears were flat back against her skull, which the girl had already learned meant *thinking hard,* which was distinct from *annoyed,* which was the default.

"Okay," Nanally said. "Okay okay okay. Here's what you are. You want it straight or padded?"

"Straight."

"Good girl. Some days ago, down in Lahai-Roi, some idiots in a very large machine punched a hole into the Nihility — the sealed void under the Stridergate, the frequency of nothing mattering, the god of every quitter who ever lived — and pulled two people out of it. On the way out, they tore the membrane. Membranes shed. I think you're a flake." She flipped the datapad around. On it: the flatline, decaying and decaying. "A shard of Nothing that got dragged into Something and — this is the part I can't explain yet, this is paper number two — decided to be a *person* about it. You've been walking around Septimont for what, two weeks? Bleeding un-mattering onto everything in a four-meter bubble. Eating unguarded apples."

The girl's face did something small. "You watched the street feeds."

"Surveillance state. Keep up." Nanally tossed the pad onto a pile of pads. "Here's your prognosis. The bubble's growing. Two millimeters a day. In a year, city blocks. In ten, you're a district-sized hole in everyone's ability to care about their own lives, and some Ephor's hit squad puts a spear through you as a public health measure, and honestly? Fair."

The girl looked at her feet.

"*Or,*" said Nanally.

The girl looked up.

"Or I fix you. Which requires equipment I don't have, in a facility I got thrown out of, in a city I swore I'd return to only in triumph or as ash." She scratched under one pink ear, violently. "So. Triumph it is. Pack your — do you own anything?"

"No."

"Perfect. Zero luggage fees. We leave in the morning." She pointed at the horrible cat. "Feed Doctor Whiskers. He found you; he gets a commission."

The girl slid off the table. Stopped. Stood in the middle of the cable-rivers, in the white surgical light, with her grey eyes and her grey coat and her four-meter bubble of nothing.

"Why?" she asked. "You said everyone who stops caring about me is being efficient. Their brains are saving power. Why spend yours?"

Nanally was already elbow-deep in a travel case, throwing instruments into it with the tenderness of a landslide.

"Because," she said, without turning around, "I've never once in my life had a null result I couldn't beat. And I'm not starting with you."

## II. The Descent

The overland journey took nine days, because an idiot once blew up Nanally's labs and her savings, so no high speed chariots for them, and Nanally talked for eight and a half of them.

She talked through the Sanguis Plateaus, lecturing the girl on frequency mechanics while eating rations with the technique of a wood chipper. She talked through the mountain tunnels, holding forth on the seventeen ways the Spacetrek Collective's peer review process was a cartel — "citation *laundering,* I'm telling you, I have graphs" — while the girl carried both packs, because Nanally had assigned herself the more important cargo of thinking.

The girl learned to sort the talking. Nine parts of it were noise, self-praise, grudges with footnotes. One part of it was aimed at her like a lamp.

"Quiz. Bubble radius this morning?"

"Four point one meters."

"Growth since Septimont?"

"Six millimeters."

"And what did I tell you to do about it?"

"Want things."

"*Louder.*"

"Want things," the girl said, louder, to the tunnel.

This was Nanally's entire treatment protocol so far, delivered on day two beside a robo-cart at the last surface waystation. She had planted the girl in front of the drinks cooler, crossed her arms, and refused to move.

"Pick one."

"I don't care which—"

"I know you don't care. Caring is the *exercise.* Pick."

"They're the same."

"They are chemically, aesthetically, and philosophically distinct, and we are standing here until you have a preference, and I want you to know that I can stand here for *days.* I once occupied a review board's office for a week. They gave me the grant to make me leave. Pick. A. Soda."

The girl had stood in front of the cooler for forty minutes. The robo-cart made three separate attempts to time out the transaction and each time Nanally slapped it back awake. Wind moved the dust. Somewhere behind them, a caravan driver decided the queue was more trouble than thirst.

Finally, from somewhere underneath the fog, for no reason she could defend, the girl reached out and took the orange one.

"*Why* that one?"

"The can. It's the brightest."

Nanally had gone very still. Then she'd snatched the datapad off her belt and typed for two solid minutes, muttering *the brightest, she can rank, saturation preference, primary data,* and the girl had stood there holding her orange soda and feeling the rivet inside her turn one full rotation, like a screw finding thread.

She drank orange soda at every waystation after that. Nanally logged every can.

On the eighth night, the errang cargo disembarked from the cargo ship, onto a port and a ridge of blue-black ice and the Roya Frostlands opened below them, and the girl stopped walking.

Half-swallowed by the glacier, lit from beneath by a city's exhaled light, a giant knelt. A machine the size of a mountain range, head bowed, one hand resting against the ice with the patience of geology. Around its shoulders, aurora. In its chest, faint through kilometres of hull, a glow like a banked fire.

"Baldur," Nanally said. For once, her voice had no edges on it. "The idiot in question. He's awake now. Mostly he just... kneels there. Listening."

The girl stared up at the giant. The giant's head was bowed her way, the way it was bowed every way.

And she felt it. Through four meters of nothing-bubble, through the fog she was made of — a pressure like a vast palm held an inch above her head. Attention. Enormous, patient, unblinking attention, from a mind the size of a mountain, and it was pointed *at her,* and it had been since she crested the ridge.

Her eyes went hot. She had no idea what to do about that. It had never happened before.

"He can see me," she whispered.

"'Course he can. You're a piece of his least favorite neighbor." Nanally was already stomping down the switchbacks. "Come on. The elevators lock at midnight and I am *not* climbing down a god's leg again. The handholds are terrible and he judges you the whole way."

---

The passenger lift fell through three kilometres of shaft and opened onto Lahai-Roi at full evening: terraced districts of bioluminescent coral, canals doubling the light, bridges strung like sheet music, and hanging in the cavern sky, low and golden, a small sun that human beings had built with their own hands.

The girl pressed her face to the glass the entire way down. Her bubble did what it did — a family in the lift forgot she existed twice — and for once she was too busy to notice, because underground, impossibly, it was *dawn.*

At the bottom, at the arrivals gate, a scanning pylon swept them with soft blue light.

The light stopped. Turned amber. Turned red.

"IDENTITY CONFIRMED," said a voice from everywhere, a voice like a librarian who had seen things. "NANALLY. FORMER SENIOR RESEARCHER, SPACETREK COLLECTIVE RESEARCH INSTITUTE. FORMER ASSOCIATE PROFESSOR, STARTORCH ACADEMY. STATUS: PERMANENTLY BARRED. FOURTEEN OUTSTANDING FACILITIES VIOLATIONS. ONE OUTSTANDING LIBRARY FINE."

"Hello, I.R.I.S.," said Nanally, sweetly. "You've gotten pettier."

"THE FINE HAS ACCRUED INTEREST."

"It was *my* book. I wrote it."

"THE ANNOTATED COPY BELONGED TO THE ARCHIVE. YOUR ANNOTATIONS WERE HOSTILE."

"They were *corrections—*"

"SECURITY HAS BEEN NOTIFIED. PLEASE REMAIN—"

"I.R.I.S." Nanally stepped close to the pylon, and her voice dropped out of its usual register into something the girl had heard only once, in the basement, over the flatline. "Run a passive sweep on my companion. Full spectrum. Then decide who you want to notify."

A pause. The blue light swept the girl, gently, head to toe.

Nine seconds of data. Then the sigh. Then zero.

The pylon's light flickered — the machine equivalent of a person reading something twice.

"...RECALIBRATING," said I.R.I.S. "REROUTING NOTIFICATION."

"To whom?"

"TO THE HEAD OF EXOSTRIDER ENGINEERING."

Nanally's ears went flat. Both of them. All the way.

"Anyone else," she said. "Literally anyone. Notify *Warren.* Notify the library."

"THE DIRECTIVE IS AUTOMATIC. ALL ANOMALOUS VOIDMATTER READINGS ROUTE TO PROFESSOR MORNYE." A pause that could only be described as savored. "SHE HAS ALREADY READ IT. SHE IS ALREADY COMING. WELCOME BACK, NANALLY."

## III. The Wrangle

They put the girl in a shielded observation suite in the Institute's Deep Wing — clean lines, warm light, one wall of one-way glass — and they put Nanally in the adjacent conference room, which within ten minutes acquired the atmosphere of a cage at feeding time.

Mornye arrived at minute eleven.

She came in her chair, wheels silent on the tile, a hovering Starstack drifting at her shoulder with a small luminous creature — Buddy — curled on top of it like a cat on a warm server. Her long bangs were pinned back with two simple clips. She looked at Nanally the way a woman looks at a leak she thought she'd fixed.

"Nanally."

"Mornye." Nanally lounged deeper into the chair, boots finding the conference table. "Love the new legs. Wait — no legs. Love the new *honesty.* Very brave. Read the papers about you. Cried at all the right parts. Did they give you my old office?"

"They gave me your old lab."

"They gave you my *lab?*"

"After the fire department released it." Mornye rolled to the one-way glass and studied the girl on the other side, who was sitting cross-legged on the suite's bed, drinking an orange soda from the arrivals vending machine, watching the door. "Talk. You have my attention until security finishes their paperwork, which knowing your file is forty minutes."

"Ha! *Your* attention." Nanally kicked off the table and prowled to the glass. "You know what she is. I.R.I.S. showed you the sweep."

"I.R.I.S. showed me a decaying null. I've seen decaying nulls before. Sensor ghosts. Voidmatter contamination on the probe."

"Nine-second onset. Self-inclusive field. Two millimeters of radial growth a day, and here's the part your Deep Wing hasn't figured out yet because your Deep Wing is staffed by the people who passed the ethics review—" Nanally slammed her datapad against the glass, screen out. Eleven days of Septimont street-feed captures. Eleven days of a grey girl standing in crowds like a hole in a photograph. "She's coherent. She has locomotion, language, appetite, and as of six days ago, a favorite color. That's a fragment of Aleph-1 running a *personality.* That's the Nihility learning to want an orange soda. There are four people alive qualified to study that, and three of them are me."

Mornye looked at the captures for a long time.

Behind the glass, the girl finished the soda, set the can down on the table, dead center, and aligned it with the table's edge. Then aligned it again.

"...She organizes," Mornye said quietly.

"She *ranks.* Preference formation from a substrate whose entire physics is the absence of preference. It's the most important object in the hemisphere and it followed a cat home to *my* basement." Nanally spread her hands. "So. Reinstate me. Full clearance, Deep Wing access, my old budget adjusted for inflation and emotional damages."

"No."

"Provisional clearance and half budget."

"No."

"Mornye. Be serious. You cannot study her without me. I have eleven days of primary observation, a working rapport, and the only calibration profile that survives her field."

"And you," Mornye said, turning her chair to face her at last, "have fourteen facilities violations, a permanent bar signed by the full board, an arson finding—"

"*Alleged.* The fire started itself. I merely created the conditions—"

"—and a documented history of treating every living subject as an instrument panel with feelings." Mornye's voice stayed soft. It had spent years being soft; the softness had load-bearing walls now. "I read your Septimont files on the way down. The Overclocked Resonators. You strapped a suicidal woman to a table for fourteen hours."

"I *rebuilt* a suicidal woman for fourteen hours, and then I unstrapped her and told her to do what she wanted, and she's alive, and she sends me *fish,* Mornye, dried fish arrives at my lab quarterly with no return address, so you can take your ethics review and—"

"She sends you fish?"

"It's very good fish. That's beside the point."

Mornye looked at her — really looked, the way she had learned to look at equations after Nuwa took the music away, with just her own two eyes.

Nanally, the girl behind the glass, the aligned soda can, the eleven days of captures, the pink ears pinned flat with something that read, on any other face, as fear of hoping.

"There's a thing I have to say first," Mornye said. "You're going to want to be sitting down, and you're going to want to be furious, so I'll wait for both."

Nanally sat down, instantly furious in advance. "Go."

"Thirteen years ago you were dismissed for unauthorized archive intrusion. Repeated deep queries into a deletion event in the personnel records. You told the board there was a hole in the archive — a researcher who had been erased, purge signatures, tool-marks. You wouldn't drop it. They ruled the hole was database rot, the queries were the crime, and you were the problem." Mornye folded her hands in her lap. "The hole was real. The purge was real. I performed it."

The room went very quiet. Even Buddy stopped pulsing.

"The researcher asked me to erase her," Mornye continued, level, each word placed like a component. "She had her reasons. I was young, and I owed her, and I believed the paperwork of a person could be separated from the person. I used her clearance and my hands. When you started digging, I said nothing. When they weighed your dismissal, I said nothing. You were abrasive and truant and universally loathed, and the room wanted a reason, and I let my silence be part of the reason." She met Nanally's eyes. "You were right. I let them fire you for being right, to keep a promise and to keep myself safe. I've owed you that sentence for thirteen years. There it is."

Nanally sat completely still. Both ears vertical. Gold eyes fixed.

"The researcher," she said. "Golden eyes. The Solaris prodigy. She's back in the city with a wolf and a priestess and a resurrected daughter and half a legend attached."

"Yes."

"I *knew* it. I knew it was her, I told the board the tool-marks were internal, I drew them a *diagram—*" Nanally shot to her feet, paced the length of the room, spun. "Okay. Okay. Here's what I want."

"Name it."

"One: it goes in writing. Official finding. 'Nanally was correct; the archive had a hole; the board erred.' Filed, stamped, and cited. I want it *citable,* Mornye, I want to reference it in footnotes for the rest of my life."

"Done."

"Two: I want to hear you say it again, slower, and I want to record it."

"No."

"Fine, it was worth—"

"Three?"

Nanally stopped pacing. And here, for one second, the whole apparatus dropped — the volume, the prowl, the citations-per-year — and what stood in the conference room was a rogue scientist in a stained coat who had spent thirteen years being right in a basement.

"The girl stays with me," she said. "Whatever cage of clearances and supervision you build, build it, I'll sign it, I don't care. But she followed a cat to *my* door, and she talks to *me,* and if your Deep Wing turns her into a specimen queue I will burn — I will *create the conditions under which* this facility experiences a second fire. She's mine. My subject. My... intern. Whatever the word is. Mine."

Behind the glass, unaware, the girl had discovered the suite's window controls and was making the frosted pane cycle between transparent and opaque, over and over, with an expression of grave scientific interest.

Mornye watched her do it. Then she pulled up a form on the Starstack's projection and began, with two clips holding her bangs out of her eyes, to type.

"Here are the terms," she said. "You are reinstated to the Spacetrek Collective Research Institute, effective today, at the rank of research intern."

"*Intern.*"

"Interns require a supervising professor. You'll report to me. Weekly. In person. With documentation formatted to Institute standard, which I know you know, because you wrote the standard and then spent six years violating it."

"This is the pettiest thing anyone has ever—"

"Interns also," Mornye continued, "cannot be barred from facilities their supervisor signs them into, cannot be voted out by the board, and cannot have their access revoked by anyone below the President. It's the most protected rank in the building. Nobody audits an intern." She turned the projection around. "The bar stays void as long as you're mine. The cage is the shield. Sign."

Nanally read the form. Read it again. Her ears performed a complicated series of angles.

"You built a trap," she said slowly, "templated on my own paperwork."

"I learned it from a priestess." Mornye held out the stylus. "One more clause. Subsection four. Read it aloud."

"'The intern's research subject, designation pending, is hereby registered as a *dependent minor under Institute guardianship,* co-signed by —'" Nanally's voice snagged. "'— by the supervising professor and the intern.'"

"She's a teenager with no records, no species classification, and a four-meter field that erases her from every clerk's attention. Someone has to be legally incapable of forgetting her." Mornye's voice stayed level. "It takes two signatures. Mine's already on it."

Nanally looked through the glass. The girl had gotten the window stuck half-frosted and was now trying to fix it by glaring at it, which was, the intern paperwork would later note, a directly inherited methodology.

Nanally signed. Aggressively, tearing the projection slightly, but she signed.

"For the record," she said, jabbing the stylus back into its dock, "I'm only doing this for the papers."

"For the record," Mornye said, filing the form, "I've read your record. You've never once done anything only for the papers. You just cite it that way."

## IV. Designation Pending

They gave Nanally a lab in the Deep Wing — a real one, shielded, with equipment that had been calibrated in the current decade — and the girl moved into the room adjoining it, and the two of them settled into a routine with the grace of two feral animals sharing a heat vent.

Nanally's method, as an educator, was assault by attention.

She tested the girl every morning: bubble radius, preference batteries, response latencies, and a ritual she called "the census," in which the girl had to name three things she had noticed the previous day and one thing she had *wanted.* The noticing came easy now. The wanting came like pulling nails.

"One thing. Anything. Go."

"...I wanted the canal bridge lights to stay blue longer."

"Why?"

"Blue looks like the ads did. In Septimont. From under the grate." The girl frowned at her own sentence, as if it had arrived without permission. "Is that a stupid reason?"

"There are no stupid reasons. There are only unlogged ones." Nanally typed it into the census file, which was labeled, in the official Institute system, SUBJECT PHENOMENOLOGY, and which contained, by week three, entries like *misses a hologram* and *aligned all the styluses again* and *asked whether Doctor Whiskers is lonely; note: get the cat shipped.*

The bubble's growth slowed in the first month. Two millimeters a day became one. Became half. Nanally presented the data at her mandatory weekly meeting, standing at Mornye's whiteboard with the seething dignity of a lion doing show-and-tell.

"Field decay tracks preference density." She slapped the curve. "Every logged want, the radius growth drops. It's a mass problem. She's a hole shaped like a person, and every preference is *fill.* The Nihility says nothing matters; she builds a self out of small things that do; the two claims fight for the same square meterage." She glared at the graph as if it had wronged her. "The treatment for being made of nothing is *accumulating pettiness.* I've never been so qualified for anything in my life."

Mornye studied the curve. Buddy drifted off the Starstack, floated across the lab, and settled — as it had begun to do, lately — near the girl, who was in the corner sorting resistor bins by a system known only to her. The little Soliskin pulsed. The girl pulsed nothing back, and Buddy stayed anyway, a fragment of Baldur's dreaming keeping company with a fragment of Baldur's opposite, the two of them quiet together like a lit window and the dark it sits in.

"She needs a designation," Mornye said. "The forms keep bouncing. 'Subject' is rejected as a legal name and 'designation pending' has a six-month limit."

"So assign one."

"She's a person, Nanally. Persons name themselves."

Nanally waved a hand at the corner. "Hey. Void kid. Homework. Pick a name by Friday."

The girl looked up from the resistors. "Any name?"

"Any name. It goes on your files, your door, and your first authorship credit, so pick something that looks good in a byline."

The girl thought about it for four days. She thought about it on the canal bridges under the blue lights, and in the Deep Wing corridors where the census lived, and under the vast bowed head of the kneeling god on the surface, where Nanally took her monthly for field readings and where the enormous patient attention pressed down on her like a palm and made her eyes hot, every time, reliably, and she had stopped minding.

On Friday she walked into the lab, took the stylus, and wrote her name on the whiteboard under Nanally's decay curve.

**ALLY.**

Nanally stared at it.

"Explain your methodology," she said, in a voice that had gone strange.

"You found me. Doctor Whiskers gets a commission; you get a citation." The girl — Ally — capped the stylus. "It's the good half of your name. I ran the analysis. The front half is just 'Nan.'"

The lab was silent for six full seconds.

"The front half," Nanally said at last, ears flat, voice cracking on the launch, "carries the *entire phonetic identity—* you took — you can't just take the *good half—*"

"It's logged," said Ally. "There are no stupid reasons. Only unlogged ones."

Behind them, unnoticed, Mornye saved a screenshot of the whiteboard to a personal folder, and Buddy pulsed twice, warm and gold, which in Soliskin means laughter.

## V. Coda: The Visit

The message came through I.R.I.S. on a quiet Tuesday: two visitors at the Deep Wing checkpoint, cleared by the President's office. Nanally read the names and knocked over her chair.

Mei came in first — golden eyes, hands in her jacket pockets, moving through the shielded lab like someone reading a room she used to know in a language she'd since lost. Behind her, hair the color of first snow, present and solid and slightly too curious about everything, came Amy.

"So," Mei said to Nanally, by way of thirteen years, an erasure, an expulsion, and one contested basement fire in Septimont. "I hear I owe you a diagram."

"You owe me a *career,*" Nanally said. "It's in writing now. Framed. I'll show you the frame."

"Later." Mei's gaze had already gone past her, to the girl by the far bench, who had frozen mid-measurement — because the two newcomers were looking directly at her, both of them, instantly, with zero seconds of latency, and one kind of person on this earth could do that.

People who had been where she was from.

Amy crossed the lab. She stopped a meter away — outside arm's reach, inside the bubble, standing in the field of nothing-matters as if it were light rain.

"Hi," she said. "I'm Amy. I lived in there for ten years. In the deep part." A beat. "You got out through the hole we made. So technically you're my fault, and where I'm from, that means I have to bring you snacks forever. It's the law. Ma made the law."

Ally looked at her. At the solid hands, the snow hair, the eyes that stayed snagged on her without effort, without instruments, without being paid in data.

"Does it stop?" she asked. It came out smaller than she intended. "The nothing. Does it ever all the way stop?"

Amy considered the question with the seriousness it deserved, because she was the one person alive qualified to peer-review it.

"No," she said. "It gets *outnumbered.*"

She held out her hand. In it: a candy in a bright orange wrapper.

"I heard from a very loud scientist's very boring weekly reports," Amy said, "that orange is your favorite. That's one. Get more."

Ally took the candy. The rivet inside her, which had been a rivet, and then a screw, and then a habit, turned over and became something with no engineering name at all.

Across the lab, Mei watched the two of them — the daughter she had pulled out of the dark and the shard the pulling had left behind — and Nanally watched Mei watch, and Mornye watched all of it from her chair by the door, bangs pinned back, hiding from none of it.

"Three papers," Nanally muttered, to nobody, wetly, typing. "Possibly a keynote."

"Log it," said Mornye.

She did.

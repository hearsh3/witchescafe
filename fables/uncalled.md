# Uncalled

*a oneshot of the Banat al-Rih*

---

*]the sea has a thousand accusations[*
*]and not one name[*

## i.

The war ended the way wars end now: a god unmade, a city exhaling, somebody's daughter carried home through a gate of light. Beneath the Solisylum, the water knew none of it. The pool had not heard it was saved.

They had tried her titles first, because titles were what they had. *Lamia. Sorceress. Threnodian. Lady of the Deep. Mother of the Tide.* Each word went into the water the way a stone goes into water: accurately, and without effect.

Only a teacup had moved her. Porcelain, still warm, set at the pool's edge by the smallest of them. Only her fingers, and only an inch — a drift toward heat, the way roots drift, blind, believing.

"Everything answers to something," Herta said, on the third day. It had the sound of a hypothesis.

By the tenth day it had the sound of a prayer.

## ii.

The word *lamia* comes down through the centuries with teeth in it.

Herta traced it back through the coven's library the way she did everything: totally, resentfully, in three colors of ink. In the oldest bestiary it sat between *leviathan* and *lemures*, glossed — *she-thing of the shallows; eats what wanders.* The author, Herta could tell, had never been to sea. People who had been to sea feared it specifically. This author feared it the way children fear the cellar: as a place to put the dark.

Every text agreed with every other text, which was the first sign that none of them knew anything. Scholarship, when it is healthy, argues. This did not argue. This recited. Child-eater. Night-thing. She-who-swallows. Sixty generations of men copying each other's fright and calling the accumulation knowledge.

*A rose by any other word,* the playwright said, as though words were sacks and the thing inside stayed the thing. He never met a demon. He never watched a word arrive before the woman did and stand in every doorway she would ever walk through.

Herta wrote in the margin: *There is no primary source.*

She looked at the sentence for a while.

*The primary source has been asleep for a century and was never once interviewed.*

Something moved against her ankle. The ginger tom that lived in the stacks, who was not hers, whom she fed dried fish from her sleeve in a manner that established nothing. "Naming is a responsibility," she informed it. "I'm extremely busy."

She had named it weeks ago. Professor. She told no one, the way she told no one about the magical-girl serial slotted inside her copy of the *Maleficarum Nautica*, which she consulted for exactly one datum: in those stories, the sealed power never answers to the name in the book. It answers to the name the girl gives it. Children's logic.

She noted it anyway. She noted everything. That was the difference between her and people who got credit for asking obvious questions out loud — Herta asked hers in the margins, privately, where no one could see her needing to.

The last page of her notes, that night:

*Every book about demons is a bibliography of fear.*

Professor slept on it, which she permitted, because the ink was dry and the conclusion wasn't going anywhere.

## iii.

Viviane took the jellyfish. It was a long way to the coast, and she thought better at altitude.

The wreck had settled since. Salt does to iron what years do to grief: rounds it, feeds on it, calls the result a patina. She landed on the tilted deck where a girl had once lived among bones, and stood in the wind with her hat in her hand, because some places you uncover for.

*]a hanged man, his throat[*
*]tribute — the dark water, pleased[*
*]I was eleven. the dream did not ask permission[*

Laurentina. The engineer's daughter. The Order's needle, cold in the spine, and after it the dreams grew walls and furniture and a tide that knew her by sight. When the coven's stories reached this part they went gentle and vague, and Viviane let them, because the true version had no gentleness in it: a month among the picked-clean dead, talking, because the bones were good listeners. That was the worst thing about them. They were the best listeners she'd ever had.

Then skirts in the water, soaked to the waist and unhurried about it. A woman kneeling in a drowned ship as if drowned ships were a kind of parlor. Not flinching — which was its own language, the first sentence of it Viviane ever understood.

*You've been dreaming my dreams,* the woman had said. *I'm sorry. They're heavy.*

And later, to the girl still clutching her old name the way you clutch a rail on a listing deck: *Laurentina drowned.* A pause, long enough to be terrifying. *Who came up?*

And then she had *waited.*

Viviane had been answered at her whole life — by fathers, by bishops, by needles. Never waited for. The waiting was the thing. She could have said anything into that silence and the silence would have kept it.

*There was a lady once who lived in a lake,* Lamia said at last, when the girl could not fill it, *and handed swords to lost kings. You have the eyes for it.*

Viviane. Lady of the Lake. A name lifted out of a story and set down on a wet deck like a plank over open water — and it *held,* that was the engineering marvel of it. A name is load-bearing. You do not hang a hull from a nail. You do not hang a life from a slur. Whoever built the word *lamia* had built a gibbet and called it a description.

She sat on the rail of her own ruin and did the arithmetic she had flown out here to avoid.

She could not picture Lamia young. Could not picture her small, or landlocked, or unfeared. Could not picture the moment before the first mother said the first warning. Children cannot imagine their mothers as girls. It is a failure of love that comes from an excess of it — the beloved arrives complete, load-bearing, already holding you, and you never once check what she is standing on.

The buzzsaw on her staff hung silent. The staff itself: her own work, every joint, save the wood. The wood was Lamia's driftwood. She had built her life around a gift and forgotten it was one.

She flew home low over the water, hat crushed to her head, and did not look down.

## iv.

At night the cavern belonged to Black Swan, who did not sleep so much as pause.

She danced at the pool's edge without music, or rather to a score no one else was invited to, which is not the same thing. First position. The old grammar. Her bare feet knew the stone now the way they had once known a stage — and there it was, the memory, arriving on schedule, wearing its little crown.

The opera house. The lights like a low sun that never set. She had been magnificent; this was simply true and she declined to be modest about it retroactively. And every night the applause had come in like surf, and applause, she knew now, is a solvent. It takes fingerprints first. Then names. Somewhere between the third encore and the hundredth season she had set her real name down in the wings and someone had tidied it away, and she had not noticed for years, because the role answered when called and the role bowed when finished and the role was, in every reviewable particular, her.

Then the lake overflowed. Then the dark went down the new river with her plumage dissolving into it. Then a Lamia, at the bottom of everything, unafraid.

Here is what the coven's younger members did not understand, because they thought in ceremonies: Lamia had never held one. No rite, no candle, no circle of salt. She had simply *used* the stolen name. Passed the salt at table by it. Scolded her by it, mildly, the way one scolds weather. Asked, by it, whether she was coming to breakfast. And somewhere in the ten-thousandth ordinary use, the theft had expired. A name becomes real the way a path becomes real: by being walked on. Daily. In ordinary shoes.

She stopped dancing. She sat at the water's edge and dealt the cards.

They ran backward. Hers always did — people believe they are facing the future; we are all of us walking into the past, and her cards had merely stopped pretending otherwise. So she walked the sleeper's past, title by title, down.

*Threnodian.* Fear in formalwear; a court title, stamped and sealed.

*Sorceress.* Fear with a budget.

*Witch.* Fear entered in the village register, between the tithes.

*Demon.* Fear undressed. Fear with nothing left on.

And before demon —

Nothing. No card. Her hand hovered over the place a card should be and the place declined to exist. Before the fear there was only water, and no one needs a word for water until they have swallowed some.

So. The name they were looking for was not lost. It was not hidden, or hoarded, or bound. It had never existed. There was nothing to find. It would have to be built — out from the shore of what they knew of her, like a jetty, one testimony at a time.

She dealt one last card, for after. It ran forward. The Star: a woman kneeling at the edge of dark water, pouring the water back.

She sat with it a long time and told no one, which was her way of praying.

Once — the thought arrived without appointment, and she let it in, because turning thoughts away had never worked on this one — once she would have liked to hear her own first name again. The true one. The dissolved one. She knew she never would; the river had it; the river was thorough. She had made her peace some centuries ago, and the peace was this: being called to dinner is a fair translation of being known.

She gathered the cards. Above the pool, the frequencies turned in their slow gyre, inward, patient, like water deciding about a drain.

"Soon," she told them, and could not have said whether it was prophecy or manners.

## v.

Agrat bat Mahlat came on a Wednesday night, as was proper, on a wind with opinions.

She furled her umbrella at the cavern mouth — pre-Lament sigils down the shaft, older than the alphabet that failed to contain them — and her wings folded behind her like a decision already made. Red eyes took in the pool, the sleeper, the five witches arranged around both in attitudes of siege.

"You took your coven's name from my daughters," she said, by way of hello. "*Banat al-Rih.* I keep meaning to invoice."

"We'd contest it," Phrolova said. "Wind can't hold a copyright."

"Everything I love is uncopyrightable. It is the tragedy of my estate." Agrat crossed to the pool and looked down a long moment, and whatever crossed her face had been crossing it for several thousand years and knew the way. "Still under. Well. You didn't call me here to admire her."

"We mean to name her," Elaina said, because the young say the quiet thing first; it is their office.

Agrat's laugh was brief and entirely without mockery, which made it worse. "Do you know what a name is, to my kind?"

"Tell us," Herta said. "Everything I've read was written by the frightened."

"That's because the frightened invented it." Agrat sat on the driftwood bench as though it were a throne, or as though thrones had always been imitations of benches. "A holy man learned my name once. One name — four syllables — and by morning I may not fly on Tuesdays over three particular streets. To this day. The streets are gone, you understand. The prohibition outlived them." She turned one hand over, studying the palm. "That is a name, little witches. A leash braided by the frightened and handed to the brave. We guard ours the way you guard your hearts, and for the same reason: whoever holds it can pull."

"And Lamia?" Viviane asked.

"Never had one. Not once, in all the drowned ages. No syllable to seize." A pause with weight in it. "Clever girl."

"You don't say it like a compliment," Black Swan observed.

"Because it purchased her exactly one thing." Agrat's red eyes lifted. "No one could ever bind her. And no one could ever *call* her. No one, in the whole history of water, ever stood in a doorway at dusk and shouted her in out of the rain. The leash and the lifeline are the same rope. It only matters who is holding it, and how, and whether they let it go slack when you want to wander."

The pool was very quiet. Amber veins pulsed in the dark water, slow as sleep.

"I named a paradise once," Phrolova said. Her voice was level, which those who knew her understood to be its most dangerous setting. "Every soul in it. Stellavento, all of them, every name mine, every name perfect. None of them could leave. I held every rope and called the holding love."

"And now?"

"Now I mean to give a name with the door open."

Agrat looked at her for a long moment — at the aristocratic spine, the folded hands, the grief calcified so long it had begun, at the edges, to look like architecture. Something in the demon queen's regard shifted; not softening, exactly. Recognition. The professional courtesy of one long sentence to another.

"Then hear the price and pay it knowingly," she said. "A demon guards her name. A woman is guarded by hers. Name her, and she is no longer the Tide, no longer weather, no longer safely everyone's and no one's. She is *yours.* Yours to keep. Yours to disappoint. Yours to lose." She rose, umbrella swinging up to her shoulder. "That is the entire price. It does not go on sale."

At the cavern mouth she paused, not turning.

"I knew her before the first warning was ever coined," she said. "She was kind then, too. It never once protected her." The wind gathered. "See that it starts."

She left the way weather leaves: noticed mostly afterward.

## vi.

"Where I trained," Elaina said, "your name is your teacher's to give."

Morning at the pool's edge; her travel journal open on her knees, pencil conducting small orbits. Herta sat two stones away pretending to read, which Elaina had learned was Herta's way of attending closely.

"You don't choose it. You receive it. Mine came from an untidy detail my teacher happened to love." She touched her hair, not explaining, and the not-explaining was its own small fidelity to the woman who'd done the loving. "I used to think that was the whole tradition. Handed down, teacher to student, since forever."

"And now?"

"Now I think the tradition is just the first kindness, repeated until it looks like law." She looked at the sleeper. "No one names the teacher. Fine. But no one named the first student either — until someone did. Someone always goes first. That's all *forever* is."

Herta closed her book. There is a particular stillness scholars get when a citation surfaces from the deep stacks of themselves, and Elaina had learned to leave room for it.

"The city," Herta said slowly. "Lahai-Roi. Do you know what the name is?"

"A city name."

"A grave with a story in it." Herta's hands had gone flat on the book, as if holding it shut, or holding it down. "Old tongue. Older than the Collective, older than the Lament — a well, in a desert, at the far beginning. A woman was thrown away there. Cast out, with her child, into the place you go to die conveniently. And something found her at the water. And she — a slave, discarded, the least syllable in anyone's ledger — *she named it.* Not the priests. Not the kings. Her. *The One Who Sees Me,* she called it, because that is what it had done, and no one had ever bothered before." A breath. "And the well kept the name. And the road kept the well. And the city kept the road. We have been living in her sentence for three thousand years."

Elaina's pencil had stopped.

"Gods are not named by councils," Herta said. "It turns out. They are named by whoever they saw on the worst day of her life."

The water lay dark and patient below them, keeping its counsel, keeping its sleeper.

"Then we qualify," Elaina said softly.

"On worst days alone," said Herta, "we're overqualified." She stood, gathering her book, her hat, her dignity, and paused. "Good question," she added — which, from Herta, was a medal with a ribbon on it — and went to tell the others.

## vii.

They gathered at dusk, when the amber veins in the water burned lowest and the cavern felt most like the inside of something breathing.

"Testimony first," Phrolova said. "A name should be made of evidence."

Viviane took off her hat. It seemed to be that kind of proceeding.

"She asked who came up from the drowning," she said. "Then she waited. I had been answered *at* my whole life — fathers, bishops, needles. Never waited for. You could have fit a cathedral in that silence." Her mechanical fingers flexed once, remembering a wrist, a grip. "She built me somewhere to stand before she asked me to."

"She passed me the salt," Black Swan said, "by a stolen name, until the theft expired." A pause; her eyes were on the water, on the slow inward turn of things invisible to the rest. "No ceremony. Just use. She wore my lie smooth until it fit me. I have been trying my whole death to decide whether that was mercy or craftsmanship, and I've concluded the answer is yes."

"She read my first thesis," Herta said. "All of it. Two hundred and twelve pages." Her chin came up, pre-emptively, daring anyone. "She disagreed with me on page forty and said so. Out loud. To my face. Like I could bear it." Her voice did something small on the last word and she pretended it hadn't. "Everyone else praised me. She *argued.* You don't argue with children. You argue with colleagues."

"She set my place at table," Phrolova said, "and asked me nothing." The others turned; Phrolova rarely went early, and never twice. "I arrived with a past you could smell. Overseer. Conductor of the dead. She knew — everyone knew — and she asked me *nothing.* Just the place. Just the plate. I have been interrogated by kinder methods and trusted none of them." Her folded hands did not move. "It is very difficult to remain a monster in a house where someone expects you at breakfast."

Silence, then. Elaina looked around the circle at the four of them, ageless and armored and undone, and understood that she was being left the last word on purpose — that this, too, was the tradition: the youngest goes last, so the offering ends in the future tense.

"I never met her awake," she said. "I've only ever lived in her weather. She's the reason all of you were gentle with me before you had a reason to be. I was given a bed, and a cup, and stories, and nobody asked what I was for." She closed her journal. "I'm testimony that the house works with nobody home. Imagine it with her in it."

It was, as silences among witches go, load-bearing.

"So," Herta said at last, brisk because the alternative was not brisk at all. "The evidence says: waits, argues, sets places, keeps lamps. What do we call that?"

Viviane turned her hat in her hands, once, twice. Nautical charts had a way of surfacing behind her eyes; you could watch the soundings come up.

"On the coast that exiled me," she said, "the charts have a word. For the place ships aim at when the water turns. Where the light stays on regardless of who's earned it." She set the hat down. "*Mina.* Harbor."

The word sat in the cavern air.

"It's small," Herta said — vetting, not objecting; everyone knew the difference.

"It's a name, not a title," Black Swan said. "Small is the entire point. You cannot call a title across a kitchen."

"You can't scold a title for missing breakfast," Elaina agreed.

"Harbors don't chase," Viviane said, to the water, to the sleeper, to a wreck a long way off. "They stay lit."

Phrolova said it once, aloud, to try the weight: "Mina."

The sound of her own voice surprised her. Every name she had spoken for a hundred years had grief already in it, pre-loaded, ballast. This one came up empty and light and *unfinished,* a hull before its maiden water. She had not known they were made in that weight. She had not known you could hold one that hadn't cost anything yet.

*Yours to keep,* the wind had said. *Yours to disappoint. Yours to lose.*

"No ritual," Black Swan said. "We don't christen. We use. Paths, not ceremonies."

"Then we'd better," Herta said, standing, dusting her robes, already halfway to a list, "start walking."

## viii.

The table came down the cavern stairs in pieces, complaining. If the name was to be real, it needed a household to be real in — so the household moved. Breakfast beside the pool, thereafter. Tea at the water's edge. The ordinary noise of five women disagreeing about jam, conducted daily, deliberately, within earshot of the deep.

Herta went through her grimoires with a ruled line and a steady hand. *Lamia — see instead: Mina.* In the margin of the oldest bestiary, beneath sixty generations of borrowed fright, she wrote: *primary source pending.*

Viviane painted the name on a teacup — the teacup, the porcelain one — in letters slightly flared at the base, like hulls. Then she carved it into a driftwood board and mounted it at the pool's edge, ship-fashion, because a vessel under repair still deserves her nameplate.

Black Swan danced something small enough to be watched by one person, and dedicated it under her breath, and did not explain to anyone what the dedication of a dance costs the dancer, or that this was the first time in centuries she had performed for an audience that might not clap.

Elaina entered it in her travel journal. Under *people.* Not phenomena.

Phrolova practiced alone, at the hour when even the amber veins slept. "Mina," to the dark water. "Good night, Mina." The dark did not answer. She said it again anyway — which is the entire definition of faith, and she was too old not to know it, and said it a third time regardless.

A week. The water kept its counsel.

"Maybe the books—" Herta started, on the eighth morning, and didn't finish, because there was no ending to that sentence she was prepared to have said aloud.

Viviane's hat came down low over her eyes and stayed there.

"She is moving toward the past," Black Swan said, laying out plates with a steadiness that was itself a kind of argument. "It's how everyone travels; she simply has further than we did. Centuries further. You do not stand at the harbor mouth shouting at a ship still over the horizon." She set down the last cup — the painted one, at the empty place, as had become custom. "You set the table."

## ix.

The morning it happened was not a special morning. That was the point, though none of them knew yet that there was a point, or that they were on it.

Figs, and the last of the dark honey, and Herta reading aloud a passage she disagreed with in order to disagree with it in company, and Professor — who had followed the household down the stairs one day and declined to explain himself — negotiating with Viviane over a rind. Ordinary noise. The kind a house makes when it has forgotten to be careful.

Elaina reached across the table and moved the fruit bowl out of Herta's blast radius.

"Save the dark figs for Mina," she said, around a mouthful. "She'll want them cold—"

And stopped.

Because she had said it the way you say true things. Not to the sleeper. Not over her, at her, about her, the way one speaks of weather or the dead. *For* her — a small domestic law laid down on her behalf, the future tense sitting in the middle of the sentence like a lit lamp in a window. *She'll want.* A tomorrow with her appetite already in it. A place held not in memory but in expectation, which is memory facing the right direction.

The pool did not ripple.

It *listened.*

Five witches went very still, the way a house goes still when it hears a key.

Then the water moved — not a wave, nothing so dramatic; the surface simply changed its mind, the way a held breath ends — and the dark hair fanned and gathered, and pale hands found the stone lip that had been waiting a hundred years to be found, and the woman in the pool opened her eyes.

They were the color of the sea in none of the books.

Her lips moved. Testing. Remembering how. When the voice came it was rusted at the hinges, small, and it asked the only question there was:

"…Mina?"

The word hung in the amber air.

"Who—" She looked from face to face, slow, sleep still on her like a second water. "Is that — me?"

"If you want it," Elaina said.

A silence. Not empty. The kind Viviane had once stood in on a drowned deck — the kind you could say anything into, and it would keep it.

"Say it again," said the woman in the pool.

They did. One at a time, around the table, five voices, five different doors. *Mina* like a finding (Viviane). *Mina* like a citation, correct at last (Herta). *Mina* like a note held past the bar (Black Swan). *Mina* like a place set (Phrolova). *Mina* like the future tense (Elaina, again, twice, because once had worked and she wasn't taking chances).

The woman — Mina — listened to all five with her eyes closing and opening slowly, and then, being who she was, entirely too kind, attempted to apologize.

"I'm so sorry," she said. "You must have — how long was I — the tide, I meant to—"

The coven declined the apology in five registers simultaneously. Herta's register was tears, which she attributed to the mineral air, daring contradiction. None came. Viviane had taken her hat off again. Black Swan was looking directly at her — directly, not askance, not through cards or frequencies, an expenditure she had not made in living memory.

Mina's gaze traveled the circle and stopped, twice, where it did not recognize.

"I don't know you," she said to Elaina. Gently. An observation, not a challenge.

"No," Elaina agreed. "But I was raised in your house anyway. Elaina. The newest." A beat. "Second newest."

Mina turned. Phrolova stood where she always stood, slightly apart, spine straight, hands folded, a woman whose composure was the last load-bearing wall of a very old building. The sleeper studied her a long moment, and something in the study was not assessment but *seeing* — the well's kind, the worst-day kind.

"And you?"

"Phrolova. Also new." The wall held. "I never knew you awake."

"And you called me home anyway."

The wall held. The wall, for the record, held.

"It's what I would have wanted," Phrolova said.

Somewhere in her a light that had burned for lifetimes in an empty paradise — left on for someone who was never coming back — flickered, and did not go out, but changed what it was for. She had kept lamps her whole long death. She had not, until this moment, stood at the other end of one.

Elaina pressed the teacup into Mina's wet hands. Fresh tea; the painted cup. Mina turned it, and found the letters, slightly flared at the base, like hulls.

"You wrote it down."

"Everywhere," Herta said thickly. "You're footnoted."

"Why—" Mina traced the M with one pale finger. "Why this?"

"Harbors stay lit," Viviane said.

Mina looked at her — at the white braid, the red eyes, the buzzsaw staff leaned against a breakfast table like a household implement, the whole improbable engineering of a girl pulled up by the wrist — and something moved through her face like weather clearing.

"I was told I was a tide," she said. It was almost wry. Almost. The muscles were out of practice.

"You were slandered," said Black Swan, "for several thousand years. Consider this the correction."

Mina laughed. It was a small sound, waterlogged, catastrophic. Professor, unmoved by history, put both paws on the table in pursuit of the figs and was repelled by three hands at once.

"What—" Mina steadied the cup. Steam rose between her and all of them. Beyond the cavern mouth, far above, the wind changed quarter, the way it does when its daughters are laughing. "What do I do now?"

Elaina pushed the fruit bowl across.

"Breakfast," she said.

The figs, as promised, were cold.

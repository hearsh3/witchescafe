> **ON THE WORD.**
>
> ***Mora*** *is delay.*
>
> *In the law of obligations it is delay that somebody can be made to answer for, and it comes in two kinds.*
>
> *The first is the delay of the one who owes. He was to deliver on the day; the day went past; he stayed where he was. Every jurisdiction on this continent keeps four hundred pages about him and most of them are about interest.*
>
> *The second kind is* mora creditoris, *the delay of the one who is owed — the person who is late to the place where the thing was to be handed over, so that the debtor stands in the road with his arms full and nobody in front of him to take it. The law attends to this because at that moment the risk moves. From then on, the one who was waiting to receive carries the loss.*
>
> *In prosody the word means something else entirely. A mora is the smallest quantity of time a syllable can occupy. A short syllable is one of them; a long syllable is two. A mora can be neither halved nor hurried, and four languages build the whole of their music out of them, and no listener in the history of any of the four has ever heard one go by.*
>
> *The road-books, which count everything, have no column for it.*

## 220

The Auditor was a spirit thermometer in a tin housing, screwed to the far wall of the habitat above the galley shelf by a dental corporation eleven years ago, and it had reported between minus twenty-six and minus nine for twelve days, and Cartethyia had given it its title on the fourth night out on the grounds that it answered to nobody and could not be argued with.

At twenty past six in the morning, under the pole at Kelvet, it read plus two.

At nine it read plus eleven.

At noon, at eleven hundred metres, on the eastern face of the range, on a road that gave up four hundred metres of altitude in nine switchbacks with a river working away in the bottom of it, the Auditor read plus twenty-four, and the inside of the *Lucky Bird* — which had smelled for twelve days of seven people, wet wool, diesel, chilli oil, marker pen and the inside of a thermos, and which none of the seven had been able to smell since the third morning — came back to all of them at once, in the order the manufacturer would have listed them.

<font color=teal>"Open something."</font>

<font color=mistyrose>"Everything's open—"</font>

<font color=teal>"Open something *else.*"</font>

<font color=coral>"Awake like a wire's awake."</font>

<font color=royalblue>"Meaning?"</font>

<font color=coral>"Meaning we'll all go at once and it won't be at a convenient bend."</font>

## 215

The strip between Post Kelvet and the line proper is eleven kilometres long.

This is an artefact of the treaty of the ninth year, in which two governments agreed on a watershed and then discovered, four months later, that the watershed and the only usable road diverged by six kilometres of contour. Rather than reopen the treaty they left the post where the road was flat enough to hold forty vehicles, and drew the line where the water parted, and the eleven kilometres in between have belonged to nobody for four hundred years.

The consequences are visible from a moving vehicle. The strip has the road it has because somebody in it decided to care, and exactly one person in it has ever decided that.

There is one building.

---

It was a shed of concrete block and corrugated sheet, four metres by five, with a stove pipe out of the side and a hand-painted board over the door bearing two characters, which Cartethyia read at forty metres and translated at thirty.

<font color=skyblue>"HOT WATER."</font>

<font color=teal>"That's the whole sign?"</font>

<font color=skyblue>"That's the whole sign! There's a smaller one underneath that says noodles, but the paint's gone."</font>

The proprietor was a woman of about sixty called Duan Yulian, whom every driver on that road addressed as Auntie Duan, and who had been in that shed for twenty-two years selling boiling water by the flask at a price that had risen four times in the period.

She carried her own water up from a spring four hundred metres below the road, in two cans, twice a day, because there is no main in the strip and there will never be a main in the strip.

She bought her coal off westbound trucks at whatever they asked.

She paid her tax to nobody and got her services from the same office, which is why the thirty metres of road in front of her door had been patched by her own hand out of gravel carried up in those same two cans.

<font color=royalblue>"How long has that patch been there?"</font>

*"Nineteen years."*

<font color=royalblue>"And whose is the road?"</font>

Auntie Duan looked at Iuno for a moment with an expression that four people at the tailgate recognised and none of them could place, and went and filled a flask.

*"It's the road,"* she said.

---

They spent fifty-one minutes there.

They spent fifty-one minutes there because of the book.

In her third year a clerk from somewhere had come through and told Auntie Duan that a business keeps a register of persons. He had failed to say which government he was from; she had failed to ask; he had never come back; and she had kept the register for nineteen years.

Forty-one exercise books, in a crate, under a plastic sheet, under the counter.

Date, vehicle, number of persons, what they bought.

<font color=royalblue>"Has anybody ever asked to see them?"</font>

*"No."*

<font color=royalblue>"May I?"</font>

Auntie Duan stopped with her hand on the flask.

Then she wiped a counter that was already clean, and put the crate up on it, and took the plastic off, and stood back with her arms folded and her chin doing something, and Iuno Caecilia Severa, formerly High Priestess of Septimont, retired, spent twenty-two minutes reading a stranger's register in a shed on a strip of ground that belonged to no country on the continent.

It contained nothing.

It contained nineteen years of nothing, in a good clear hand, ruled with a straight edge, with the columns kept and the totals carried forward, and on the fourth page of the eleventh book there was a note in the margin that said *stove out four days, no water sold*, and Iuno put her hand flat on that page for a second.

<font color=royalblue>"This is a very good register."</font>

*"Mm."*

<font color=royalblue>"You have carried the totals for nineteen years and nobody has ever checked them."</font>

*"They're right."*

<font color=royalblue>"I know they're right. I can see they're right, from here, at a glance, and that is a thing about myself I have stopped apologising for."</font> Iuno squared the book on the counter. <font color=royalblue>"I would like you to understand that a person capable of seeing it has now seen it."</font>

Auntie Duan turned round and did something to the stove that the stove did not require.

---

They all signed.

Amy wrote FLEET SNOWFLUFF, looked at it, ruled one line through it, and put AEMEATH underneath in her fourteen-year-old font.

Cartethyia wrote her name in four characters, having been taught them off a signboard by Ciaccona two days before, and got the third one backwards, and Auntie Duan came round the counter and fixed it with the pen still in Cartethyia's hand, which is a thing that happens to a person exactly once in a language and then never again.

Chisa wrote in three scripts, one under the other, and left it at that.

Mei wrote five characters.

Auntie Duan looked at them for about four seconds.

Then she said something short.

<font color=skyblue>"...She says that's a nice house."</font>

<font color=moccasin>"Tell her thank you."</font>

## 211

The line proper is a stone.

It stands at a saddle at thirteen hundred metres, on the left going east, eighty centimetres high, square in section, with a survey mark cut in the top, lichen on three faces, and a number weathered down to the shadow of a number.

There is nothing else.

The saddle holds the stone, the road, some grass on both sides of it, and a quantity of wind. The entire apparatus of two states meeting is discharged, on the actual ground, by eighty centimetres of granite with lichen on it.

<font color=royalblue>"Mm."</font>

<font color=royalblue>"It has had four hundred years to grow lichen and nobody has ever been sent up here to scrub it, and the reason nobody has ever been sent is that everything which matters about this stone happened in a room eleven hundred kilometres away, on paper, inside a month, between men who never came within a week's travel of the actual ground."</font> Iuno put her hand flat on the top of it. <font color=royalblue>"It is the most honest object we have seen since the frostlands and I want a frame of it."</font>

Four hundred metres back, past the culvert, on the true watershed, Iuno went down the bank to a stream two metres wide and stood in it to the knee in her boots.

There is no gauge on a stream two metres wide on a saddle in a strip that belongs to nobody.

She took a blank sheet out of a bundle with a date written on the band: gridded for river level against stated discharge, pre-stamped, pre-addressed to a professor at the Startorch Institute, Lahai-Roi, Roya.

She entered the date. She entered the place by coordinates off the chart, checked twice.

In the column headed LEVEL she entered a figure. In the column headed METHOD she wrote: *staff gauge absent. Measured from the underside of the culvert soffit to the water surface, at the centre, with a folding rule, and subtracted. Soffit height taken from the Ministry's own bridge plate, which is bolted to the culvert and which I have photographed. If the plate is wrong this figure is wrong by the same amount, and the error is at least consistent.*

Then she stood in a stream in a foreign country in her boots with a clipboard, for rather longer than the work took.

*Darling,* said the High Priestess, *there is a boundary stone forty metres up that bank with a survey mark on it, ignored by four hundred years of policy, and you are in a ditch.*

*I am.*

*And you are enjoying the ditch more than you enjoyed the stone.*

*Considerably more,* said Iuno, *and I have an idea why, and I am going to sit on it until I am certain.*

## 159

The first warm night was the loudest thing that had happened to any of them in a year.

They had come out of twelve days of a country whose entire acoustic output was wind on steel. They parked in a pull-off above a stream at four hundred and ten metres with the last of the light on the far ridge, and at eight o'clock the frogs started, and by ten past eight it was established that nobody would be sleeping.

The noise ran about nine separate industries at once. A croaking bass down in the wet ground. A high tinny ringing off to the left, which Cartethyia insisted was a bird and lost the argument about. A thing in the grass like a fingernail dragged across a comb, at four-second intervals. Cicadas warming up and giving up. Something with wings hitting the side of the truck once a minute with the report of a thrown pebble. And underneath all of it, water.

Everybody came out.

They came out one at a time over twenty minutes and ended up on the tailgate and the ground and the sample bed, in the dark, in shirtsleeves, doing nothing whatsoever.

## POST 2 — YANJIN, AT THE BRIDGE

The bridge at Yanjin is a four-span concrete girder of the fifty-first year, and the post is a room at the eastern abutment with a stove in it, a counter, a bell, a dog, and Bao.

Bao was fifty-four and had been at that abutment for nineteen years.

He took the log, and the bond copy, and the schedule with Ruan Zhi's handwriting on the back of it, and he read all three, and then he went out and walked round the truck with a torch in daylight, which is a thing certain men do, and came back and put his hand flat on the counter.

*"Read me the number."*

<font color=royalblue>"Four-zero-one-one-four."</font>

He wrote it. He looked at the seal through a lens. He looked at the wire, and at the crimp, and at the photograph in the log, and at the seal again.

*"Read me the number."*

<font color=royalblue>"Four-zero-one-one-four."</font>

*"Mm,"* said Bao, and stamped four places, and that was the whole of it, and it took thirty-one minutes and it was the most comfortable half hour any of them had spent in a government building in a year.

---

They were nearly out of the door when Iuno stopped.

There is a staff gauge on the third pier at Yanjin. It is a painted board, white and red in half-metre bands, bolted to the upstream face, and the paint on it has been renewed by somebody who did the job in the dry season with a brush and a rule and a great deal of care.

<font color=royalblue>"Whose is the gauge?"</font>

Bao looked up.

*"Ministry's."*

<font color=royalblue>"Who paints it?"</font>

A pause of about four seconds.

*"...Me."*

<font color=royalblue>"Who reads it?"</font>

<font color=coral>"He does,"</font> said Chisa, from the doorway, without turning round. <font color=coral>"Twice a day. He's got a book."</font>

Everyone looked at her.

<font color=coral>"There's a chalk line on the doorframe at the height of a man's eye and it's worn through in one place, and the shelf under the window is worn through in two, and one of them's the width of a school exercise book."</font> Chisa shrugged with one shoulder. <font color=coral>"He goes out, he reads it, he comes in, he writes it, and he's done it enough times to sand a shelf."</font>

<font color=royalblue>"Nineteen years."</font>

*"Eighteen and a bit. The man before me kept it."*

<font color=royalblue>"Where is his?"</font>

*"In the box."*

<font color=royalblue>"How far back does the box go?"</font>

<font color=royalblue>"Has anybody ever asked you for these?"</font>

*"The Ministry publishes a figure."*

<font color=royalblue>"That is a different sentence from the one I asked for."</font>

She had, in her hand, an eleven-year chart of river level against dam discharge, typed, gridded, given to her in a manifest box by a woman in a wheelchair in a city under a glacier, with a card on top of it in a typeface she could recognise across a room.

*Send the figures. The Ministry's published numbers run eight per cent optimistic and I would like to know by how much.*

<font color=indianred>"...What."</font>

<font color=royalblue>"Eight and a half."</font>

<font color=indianred>"Eight and a half what?"</font>

<font color=royalblue>"Per cent. Over forty-one years. The published figure runs eight and a half per cent optimistic against the board on the third pier, and it runs *consistently* optimistic, which means it is a method and not an error, and I have the whole of it in this book in four hands."</font> Iuno had both palms flat on the counter. <font color=royalblue>"She said eight. She said eight in a card, at four in the morning, out of an eleven-year chart, in a city eleven thousand kilometres from this bridge, and she was *half a point out*, and I am going to have to write and tell her, and she is going to read it in a corridor and say nothing at all and put it in a drawer."</font>

<font color=teal>"You're shaking."</font>

<font color=royalblue>"I am delighted. It presents identically."</font>

<font color=moccasin>"Tell him it came with the truck."</font>

<font color=skyblue>"He says he knows it didn't."</font>

## 101 — THE TREE

On the fifteenth day the road came down off the last shoulder of the range and into red-earth country, terraced on the slopes and orchard in the bottoms, at plus twenty-nine degrees under a white sky, and at eleven in the morning Cartethyia said the sentence that cost them four hours.

<font color=skyblue>"There's a tree."</font>

<font color=teal>"There have been about nine million—"</font>

<font color=skyblue>"There's a tree with *red things* on it and there's a woman under it with *baskets* and there's a *stone bench*, Cia, somebody put a *bench* under it—"</font>

Lupa pulled in.

---

The tree was a *yangmei*, forty years old, sixteen metres across, standing on the outside of a bend with the road running under half its canopy and a rope over the lowest limb with a bucket on the end of it.

The fruit was the size of a thumb-end, dark to the point of black, covered all over in little raised knobs, so that the whole surface of it looked cast in a mould by somebody with time on their hands.

The woman under it was called Auntie He, and the boy fetching the bucket up and down was her grandson He Ping, and the fruit had been coming off that tree at that bend for as long as either of them could account for.

<font color=mistyrose>"What *is* it."</font>

<font color=indianred>"It's a berry."</font>

<font color=coral>"It's a drupe."</font>

<font color=indianred>"...It's a berry, Chisa."</font>

<font color=coral>"It has a stone in it. It's a drupe. There's a stone in it and if you bite straight through you'll find out at speed."</font>

Lupa bit straight through.

Lupa found out at speed.

---

Auntie He watched four foreigners eat *yangmei* wrong for about ninety seconds, and then went and got a basin.

She filled it from a jerry can. She took a twist of paper out of her apron and shook salt into it and stirred it round with two fingers, and tipped in about forty pieces of fruit, and stood back, and pointed at the basin, and waited.

Nothing happened for a minute.

Then something happened.

<font color=mistyrose>"...There's something coming out."</font>

<font color=skyblue>"OH!"</font>

<font color=mistyrose>"There's something *coming out of the fruit*—"</font>

<font color=skyblue>"They're *little* — Amy, look, they're *tiny*, they're coming out of the — oh, they're *swimming*—"</font>

<font color=mistyrose>"I ATE FOUR."</font>

<font color=teal>"You ate six."</font>

<font color=mistyrose>"CIA."</font>

<font color=teal>"You ate six and I counted them, because I have been counting them since the fourth one with the specific intention of telling you at exactly this moment."</font>

Auntie He said something and Cartethyia translated it while wheezing.

<font color=skyblue>"She says everybody eats four before somebody shows them the salt. She says her husband ate them for sixty-one years and never once used the basin and lived to eighty-four."</font>

<font color=mistyrose>"That's not *comfort*—"</font>

<font color=skyblue>"She says it wasn't meant to be!"</font>

---

Chisa ate eleven and said nothing at all for a while.

Then she picked up a twelfth and turned it over in her fingers, feeling the knobs.

<font color=coral>"This is the best thing I've eaten in two years."</font>

<font color=teal>"Say that again with a face on it and I'll believe you."</font>

<font color=coral>"That's the face."</font>

<font color=teal>"That is the face you had at the border."</font>

<font color=coral>"That's because it's the face,"</font> said Chisa, and ate the twelfth.

---

Mei sat on the stone bench with her boots out and a bowl of them going down steadily and asked, through Cartethyia, one question, and got an answer that shut her up for eleven kilometres.

<font color=moccasin>"How far do they go?"</font>

Auntie He held her hand out flat and moved it a little.

*"Two hundred li, in a good year. In a bad year they get to the county town."*

<font color=moccasin>"And then?"</font>

*"Then they're jam. Three days off the tree and they're jam whatever you do to them, and if you pick them into a deep basket the ones at the bottom are jam by lunchtime, so we pick into a shallow one, and the shallow one is why the boy runs."*

<font color=moccasin>"So nobody outside two hundred li has ever had one."</font>

*"Nobody outside two hundred li has ever had one,"* Auntie He agreed. *"They've had the jam. It's a different item."*

Mei looked at the bowl in her hands for a while.

<font color=moccasin>"...Carte. Ask her if that bothers her."</font>

Cartethyia asked. Auntie He laughed for about four seconds and then answered at some length, and Cartethyia listened all the way through and then stood there working on it.

<font color=skyblue>"She says — she says it's the reason there's a bench."</font>

<font color=moccasin>"Say the rest."</font>

<font color=skyblue>"She says a thing that travels gets sold and a thing that stays gets *sat with*, and she'd rather have the bench, and she's been sitting on that bench since before her daughter was born, and she says—"</font> Cartethyia frowned at the middle distance <font color=skyblue>"—she says the road brings her the whole world four at a time and none of it stays either, so it's fair."</font>

The cicadas went round.

<font color=moccasin>"Yeah, okay,"</font> said Mei, to a tree.

The haggling was a different disaster.

Cartethyia had spent twelve days being told she paid the asking price, and had gone away and thought about it, and had come back with a method.

She now knew that you offer six of what they ask, and settle at eight, and that you must be prepared to walk away.

She deployed all of this on a woman of seventy-one under a tree in thirty degrees, in the local language, at speed, with total commitment, and it worked.

It worked completely. Auntie He came down to eight in about four exchanges, with a great deal of enjoyment on both sides, and then the money changed hands and Cartethyia stood there holding two baskets and looking at the number on her palm.

<font color=teal>"...What are you doing."</font>

<font color=skyblue>"I don't know!"</font>

<font color=teal>"You won. That was a win. I watched you take four hundred off a woman who has been doing this since before my father was born."</font>

<font color=skyblue>"I KNOW."</font>

<font color=teal>"So what's this."</font>

<font color=skyblue>"It felt like *taking*,"</font> Cartethyia said, with a basket under each arm, wretchedly. <font color=skyblue>"When I paid the asking price it felt like *talking*, and when I got the good price it felt like *taking*, and the horrible thing is that she *liked it*, Cia, she liked it *much* better, she was having a lovely time, and I was having a lovely time, and then at the end I had all this fruit and she had less money and I hated it."</font>

Ciaccona thought about that.

<font color=teal>"Write it in the book."</font>

<font color=skyblue>"Which column?"</font>

<font color=teal>"Make one."</font>

## 71 — THE MULBERRY LANE

For four kilometres either side of Shimen the road runs between mulberries.

They are pollarded to about shoulder height, in ranks, because the crop is the leaf and the leaf goes to the worms, and the fruit is a by-product that nobody harvests and everybody eats, and by the middle of the fifth month the whole surface of that road is stained a black-purple that comes up on your boots.

They stopped at the second kilometre.

They stopped at the third kilometre.

Then they gave up and walked one and a half kilometres of it with the truck idling behind them at four kilometres an hour, driven by Chisa, who could not see what the fuss was and drove anyway.

The mulberries went into everybody, and everybody went purple: hands, mouths, and, in the case of Cartethyia, one entire forearm to above the elbow, in a manner nobody was able to explain afterward.

<font color=teal>"My *hands.*"</font>

<font color=skyblue>"They're beautiful!"</font>

<font color=teal>"Carte. My hands are the instrument."</font>

<font color=skyblue>"Then the instrument's *purple.*"</font>

Ciaccona held both palms up in front of her face in the middle of a road in the middle of a country in the middle of the afternoon and looked at them, red-black to the second knuckle, and made a decision that lasted three days.

<font color=teal>"Fine."</font>

<font color=skyblue>"Hm?"</font>

<font color=teal>"I am going to play four villages purple,"</font> said Ciaccona, <font color=teal>"and I am going to let every single person who notices ask me about it, and I am going to answer honestly, and it is going to cost me an hour a day, and I have decided this is the summer I do that."</font>

## 55 — THE TAP

## 44 — THE TERRACES

The good day came after the bad one, and it came at eleven kilometres.

They were at four hundred metres by then, into rice country proper, and the whole valley on both hands was terraced from the water up to the tree line in bays a metre and a half deep that followed the contour round the shoulders of the hills in curves nobody had surveyed.

The lower bays were flooded and holding the sky. There were people on them in ones and twos, in blue and grey, bent over, doing something to a bund with a mattock.

Mei had made a promise about this on the other side of the mountains and she kept it before nine in the morning.

---

The village was called Sanwan. Mei bought four things nobody needed off a table at the top of the lane, and then, with Cartethyia at her elbow, asked the woman at the table to explain the water.

The woman at the table sent for her father-in-law.

His name was Yao, he was seventy-eight, and he was the man who held the cord.

---

The cord is two and a half metres of hemp with knots on it, and the knots stand for the hours, and the hours stand for the water.

Above Sanwan there is one channel off one stream. It runs for nine hours in every twenty-four in the wet half of the year and for four in the dry. Every household has a share, and the share is measured in hours, and the hours are fixed against the position of the sun on the ridge opposite, and the ridge opposite has four notches in it that every single person in that village can name.

Yao Fu explained it for fifty minutes.

Mei sat on a stool with her elbows on her knees, and asked eleven questions, and got the fifty minutes because of the fourth question, which Cartethyia rendered as *"Who gets the night water?"*

Yao Fu stopped.

Then he sent for tea, which is what a man does when he has decided a foreigner is going to be there a while.

---

The night water goes to the two houses at the top of the lane, always, and it has gone to them for about two hundred years, and it is the worst share, because you cannot see what you are doing and the loss into the ground at night is higher.

Those two houses have the worst share because their great-great-grandfather lost an argument.

The argument was about a bund. It was settled by four men in a room. The settlement was that his line takes the night water and, in exchange, holds the cord: which is to say, the two houses with the worst water are the two houses that time everybody else's.

<font color=moccasin>"...Say that again."</font>

Cartethyia said it again.

<font color=moccasin>"So the family that got *punished* is the family that keeps the clock."</font>

*"Somebody has to keep it,"* said Yao Fu. *"And a man with the good water has no reason to be honest about the hours."*

Mei sat back on the stool and laughed with her whole chest at a hillside, and Yao Fu, who had spent fifty minutes with a foreigner and had been enjoying himself since about minute nine, laughed too.

<font color=moccasin>"Carte. Tell him that's the best piece of law I've heard in four years and I've had it from a *President.*"</font>

<font color=skyblue>"I don't know the word for President!"</font>

<font color=moccasin>"Then tell him it's the best piece of law I've heard in four years."</font>

She wrote all of it down in the log that night, all fifty minutes, four pages, and drew the ridge with the four notches on it from memory, and got two of them wrong.

---

Lupa spent the afternoon on a wall.

The bunds at Sanwan are dry stone, four courses, no mortar, and they have stood a freeze-thaw cycle every year for two hundred years, and one of them had come down four metres at the corner of a bay in the spring.

The man rebuilding it was called Wei Dachuan and he was working with his daughter, who was fifteen and called Wei Xiaoman, and he was working with his daughter because his back had gone in the third month and the wall could not wait for his back.

On the western side of the range, riding past terraces she had described out loud as fields on a hill, Lupa had said that she would like to know who did the repairs and whether it was the same family.

She watched for eleven minutes from the road.

Then she went down the bank, and stood at the end of the gap, and pointed at a stone, and then at the wall, and raised her eyebrows.

Wei Dachuan looked at her for a second, and then handed her the stone.

---

She said, over four hours, nine words.

She learned the thing you can only learn by being shown, which is that every stone goes in with its long axis into the hill, so that the weight of the bank comes down on the tail of it and locks the face; and that a stone laid the pretty way — long face out, because that is what a wall looks like in a picture — is a stone that will be lying on the path in eleven years.

She got it wrong twice. Wei Xiaoman took both stones out without being asked and put them back in her hands and said something short, twice, in exactly the same tone.

At the end of the afternoon there were four metres of new bund at the corner of the bay, and Lupa was filthy to the shoulder, and she asked one question through Cartethyia, who had come down the bank at hour three and stayed.

<font color=indianred>"Who taught you."</font>

*"My father."*

<font color=indianred>"Who taught him."</font>

*"His father."*

<font color=indianred>"And him?"</font>

Wei Dachuan stood up straight with a hand at the small of his back and looked at four kilometres of terracing going round the shoulder of the hill in the late light, in a curve laid out by nobody at all, four centimetres true over two kilometres, held by eleven generations who had gone and stood in exactly the same place.

*"The wall,"* he said.

Lupa looked at it for a while.

<font color=indianred>"Mm,"</font> she said, and her tail went one full sweep, low and slow, which was a verdict, and which Wei Xiaoman watched with undisguised interest.

They made eleven kilometres that day.

Iuno wrote *11* in the distance column at the halt, and looked at it, and put the pen down, and did not say anything about it at all, and four separate people noticed.

## 30 — HETOU

They came down into Hetou at four in the afternoon on the eighteenth day and found the road shut by a funeral.

White banners at the head of the lane. A frame of white paper flowers over the gate. A band of five — two suona, a drum, cymbals, and a man with a sheng — playing something at a volume that Ciaccona put at four times what a lute can do and never once got over.

The road went through the village. There was no other road. The funeral had the road for two more hours.

So they stopped.

---

They stopped, and got out, and stood at a respectful distance at the end of the lane, and Iuno began working out where to reverse two hundred metres of truck.

And then a man of about fifty came down the lane, walking fast, in white hemp, and went straight past all seven of them to the cab of the truck, and stood in front of the door, and read it.

Then he went back up the lane at the same speed, and there was a conversation at the gate involving about nine people, and it went on for two minutes, and then four of them came down the lane together.

---

Cartethyia got about half of it.

The dead woman was called Zhou, she was ninety-one, she had four sons and a great many other people, she had died in her sleep in the fifth month, which is a good month for it because the ground is soft and everyone can come, and this was the third day, and the third day is the eating.

The man who had read the door was her second son.

What he said, as far as Cartethyia could carry it, was that a truck had come to the gate on the third day with *somebody's in, don't pass by* written on the door of it, and that this was either extremely auspicious or extremely funny, and that in the view of the family it made no difference which, because in both cases you feed it.

<font color=royalblue>"Tell him we can reverse."</font>

<font color=skyblue>"I'm not going to tell him that."</font>

<font color=royalblue>"Cartethyia—"</font>

<font color=skyblue>"Iuno, there are *nine tables* in that lane."</font>

---

There were nine tables in that lane, and by six there were eleven, and two of the eleven had a truck's worth of foreigners distributed across them on the strict instruction of a woman of about seventy who separated them at the gate like a person dealing cards and would hear nothing about it.

The food came in fourteen courses off four woks in a courtyard.

Lupa ate at a table of six men who had been carrying things all day, drank what was put in front of her four times, and at about eight o'clock stood up and delivered, in Septimontian, at volume, with gestures, a toast to a woman she had never met, in a language nobody at that table spoke, and had it received by all six of them as though it had arrived in their own; and was embraced by three of them at once, and gave as good as she got.

Amy was handed a lantern on a pole at seven and told to stand at the head of the lane with it, and stood at the head of a lane holding a lantern on a pole for a stranger's mother for two hours and forty minutes, and held it up the whole time, and kept the entire business to herself until Jinzhou.

At half past eight Mei went past her, into the courtyard, where the second son had asked for her by pointing.

<font color=mistyrose>"Number."</font>

<font color=moccasin>"Kid, it's a *funeral*—"</font>

<font color=mistyrose>"Number."</font>

<font color=moccasin>"...Ten."</font>

She came back out at twenty past nine with a bowl in each hand and a paper flower behind her ear that she had made no decision about.

<font color=moccasin>"Fourteen."</font>

<font color=mistyrose>"Good,"</font> said Amy, from behind a lantern, and went on holding it up.

---

Iuno stood at the edge of the whole thing for a long time with a cup in her hand and watched a village run a three-day liturgy with no written instrument anywhere in it, perfectly, with four hundred moving parts and nobody in charge.

*Extremely well,* said Iuno, *and I would like to be permitted to feel extremely well about a thing I had nothing whatever to do with, for one evening, in a lane, at a stranger's table.*

*Granted,* said the High Priestess. *Eat something.*

---

Chisa noticed it at the second course and told nobody until the lake.

Everybody in that lane was in white hemp. Everybody in that lane was in the same white hemp, cut the same, over their ordinary clothes, and had been for three days, and would be for another four.

Chisa Kuchiba, who has never in her life seen a face — for whom every human head is a smear of colour with a shape round it, and who navigates a room by gait, by breath, by the weight of a footstep and the habits of a pair of hands — sat down at a table of eleven strangers in a lane where every single person was dressed identically, and found that for one evening she was operating at no disadvantage whatsoever with respect to anyone present.

She ate fourteen courses.

She got up four times to help carry, and was thanked, and was found by three separate people who wanted her to hold a dish, because she was the one who kept getting up.

At about nine she went and sat on a step by herself for four minutes with her hands round a cup, and then went back in.

---

Ciaccona and the suona player took about ninety minutes to arrive at each other.

Her method is well documented and she has never made a secret of it: she plays badly on purpose in an unfamiliar room until somebody demands that she play properly, on the grounds that what a place actually believes gets told to a bad musician and withheld from a good one.

She played badly for nearly an hour, purple to the second knuckle, on a lute with a soundboard cut out of a ship, in a funeral lane, and got four corrections, two songs, one argument about a bridge, and a great deal of information.

At the end of the hour the suona player — a wide man of about forty who was addressed by everybody as Fatty Ma and who had been playing a double-reed instrument at ninety-odd decibels for the best part of three days — put his instrument down on the table, leaned across, and said something to her with his hand flat.

<font color=skyblue>"He says stop it."</font>

<font color=teal>"Stop what."</font>

<font color=skyblue>"He says stop doing the *bad* thing."</font> Cartethyia was going pink with delight. <font color=skyblue>"He says he's been listening for an hour and you've missed the same note eleven times and every single time you've missed it in a *different direction*, and a person who can't play misses it the same way twice, and he says he's been playing at funerals for twenty-two years and he *invented that*, and he'd like you to know he *invented that.*"</font>

There was a silence at the table.

Then Ciaccona put her forehead on the table for a second and came up laughing, and retuned, and played properly, and it was, by the general verdict of eleven tables in a lane in Hetou, acceptable.

Fatty Ma picked the suona back up and buried her in four bars.

<font color=teal>"...That thing is *obscene.*"</font>

<font color=skyblue>"He says thank you."</font>

<font color=teal>"I want one."</font>

<font color=skyblue>"He says no."</font>

He was seventy-one, and he had a shed off the courtyard, and inside the shed was a horse the size of a real horse, a house with four rooms in it, a set of servants, a chest, a bicycle, and a great many paper coins, all of it built on bamboo splints and paste and covered in coloured paper, all of it going into the fire the next morning.

Cartethyia found him at half past eight and stayed until eleven.

She watched him tie a joint. He let her tie a joint. Cartethyia looked at that for a long time.

<font color=skyblue>"...Why?"</font>

Ciaccona, at the gate, saw her write it, and saw what it was, and has never told anybody.

## 26 — THE RAIN

It rained on the nineteenth day.

The plum rains come onto that lake in the last week of the fifth month and stay for six, and everybody in that country will tell you at length how to know them. This was the front that runs ahead of them. It arrived at half past eight in the morning as a grey line across the whole southern sky and it was on them by nine.

They got four kilometres and pulled in under a tobacco shed.

---

The shed belonged to a family called Hu, who were also under it, having been caught with two hundred and forty trays out, and there were eleven people in that shed for six hours: seven foreigners, three Hus, and a man from the next village who had been walking somewhere and had made a decision.

Nothing happened.

Nothing whatsoever happened for six hours. It is one of the four days out of that entire crossing that every single person aboard the *Lucky Bird* would go back to.

They played a card game which the Hus taught them and which Chisa understood in four hands, dominated for nine, and was then quietly frozen out of by a coalition of three that formed without a word being spoken and which she detected instantly and respected enormously.

Amy learned eleven words of a dialect that would be useless to her in Jinzhou.

Lupa slept on a sack.

Mei made hot chocolate, on a stove, in a tobacco shed, in the rain, to her own recipe, for eleven people, and it was terrible, and everybody had two, and one of the Hus asked for the recipe, and Mei gave it to her, faithfully, including the salt.

The rain came off the eaves in a sheet.

---

Ciaccona wrote the line.

She had been carrying an eleventh stanza on a half-line since the fourth night in the frostlands, and had written the missing line out nine times in nine places, and every single time she had put the notebook down and gone to check where Cartethyia was.

She wrote it in a tobacco shed at half past two in the afternoon on the nineteenth day, in the rain, in ink, with purple hands.

Then she sat and looked at it.

<font color=skyblue>"What're you writing?"</font>

<font color=teal>"The weather."</font>

<font color=skyblue>"Oh! Good. It's very good weather."</font>

And Cartethyia went back to the card game.

She accepted it in about a second and a half.

She accepted it, in the general opinion of the two people at the other end of the shed who happened to be looking, at least half a second faster than a person accepts a true statement, and Chisa turned her head very slightly and Mei kept stirring, and the two of them left it exactly where it was, and the rain came off the eaves.

---

Iuno did the arithmetic at four in the afternoon, on the tailgate, under the eaves, with the log open and the whole crossing in front of her.

She had been putting off doing it for two days, in the specific way she puts things off, which is to do four other pieces of work first with great efficiency.

Twenty-three halts between the pole at Kelvet and a tobacco shed. She listed them: place, time, duration, cause.

Then she sat back, because the cause column had gone wrong.

She had been writing the cause column for seven days in the vocabulary of a woman who runs a schedule — *bird stop, fruit, fuel, school, seal inspection, water* — and when she read all twenty-three of them together the vocabulary came apart in her hands, because in nineteen of the twenty-three the truck had stopped and then *somebody had come to it.*

Nineteen times out of twenty-three, they had halted for four minutes or forty, and a person had put down what they were holding and walked over.

She went through them again, and wrote down, for each one, where that person had been standing when they started walking.

And in every single case the answer was: somewhere they could see the door of the cab.

---

*Ah,* said the High Priestess.

Iuno sat with the log open on her knee for about four minutes.

Then she got up and walked round to the front of the truck in the rain and stood in front of the cab door and looked at five black characters that had come out of a woman's hand on a kitchen table over four seasons, one at a time, in a script she cannot read, in a stroke order that has been out of the schools for thirty-one years.

A surname. Then two characters that are cut on lintels and mean *the house is occupied; somebody is in; don't pass by.* Then a place with eleven families on it that is on no map.

<font color=royalblue>"Mei."</font>

<font color=moccasin>"Yeah."</font>

<font color=royalblue>"Come and look at your door."</font>

---

They stood in front of it in the rain.

<font color=royalblue>"I have been keeping the distance column for seven days as an accusation,"</font> said Iuno. <font color=royalblue>"Sixty-one, forty-four, twenty-nine, thirty-one, eleven, fourteen, four. Two hundred and eleven kilometres of road and a Superintendent who wrote *allow two days* on the back of a form at half past four in the morning, and I have been carrying that number around like a bill."</font>

<font color=moccasin>"Mm."</font>

<font color=royalblue>"Nineteen of our twenty-three halts, somebody came to us. Every one of them from a position where they could read that."</font> Iuno put one finger on the second character. <font color=royalblue>"We have been driving a lintel across a country that reads lintels. There is a blessing painted on the door of this vehicle in a hand nobody can source, and it says *don't pass by*, and for seven days this country has been *doing what it says.*"</font>

The rain came off the eaves.

<font color=moccasin>"...Huh,"</font> said Mei, to a door.

<font color=royalblue>"That's all you've got?"</font>

<font color=moccasin>"Iuiu, I've been looking at that door every morning for nineteen days and thinking about a village with eleven families on it and whether anybody there is alive."</font> Mei had her hands in her pockets. <font color=moccasin>"You just told me it's been *working* the whole time. Give me a minute with that."</font>

Iuno gave her a minute with it.

<font color=royalblue>"There's a fault in it,"</font> she said, at the end of the minute.

<font color=moccasin>"Go on."</font>

<font color=royalblue>"The man with the key read it too."</font>

The rain went on.

<font color=royalblue>"He read all five characters out loud, at the pace of a man reading a lintel, and then he charged us eleven times the rate and stood over us for ninety-four minutes. And I am going to enter him in the same column as the other nineteen, in the same hand, with no annotation."</font> Iuno's voice stayed exactly where it was. <font color=royalblue>"Because a door that only ever produces welcomes is a lie about doors, and I have written four hundred instruments in my life that were lies about doors, and I have decided to stop."</font>

<font color=moccasin>"Yeah."</font>

<font color=moccasin>"Put the woman with the stool in it too."</font>

<font color=royalblue>"...She's already in it."</font>

At four o'clock the rain stopped.

It stopped the way it does in that country, all at once, with the sky staying grey, and the whole valley started steaming off the terraces in about four minutes so that the entire bowl below them filled with white and the road ran through it at head height.

They said goodbye to the Hus, who gave them four eggs and would take nothing, and got in, and drove.

They drove for eleven minutes.

Then there was a place to stop — a hard shoulder above a bend, with the steam coming up out of the valley in front of it and the light going through — and Chisa took her foot off and found Iuno in the mirror, which she does by ear.

<font color=coral>"Registrar."</font>

The cab waited.

They stopped the truck.

<font color=teal>"No."</font>

## POST 11 — BAISHAGOU

Post 11 was shut.

It was shut at eleven in the morning on a Tuesday, within the hours printed on the board on its own gate, and Ruan Zhi had written *nominally* on the back of a spoiled form at four in the morning eight days earlier, and Amy read the word off the schedule and then read the board on the gate and then made a noise.

They waited forty minutes.

At the end of the forty minutes a boy of about eleven came past on a bicycle, took in the situation, said something to Cartethyia, received an answer, laughed for four seconds at her tones, and rode away extremely fast.

He came back in nine minutes with a man in his shirtsleeves who was still holding four playing cards face down in his left hand and who continued to hold them, in his left hand, face down, throughout the entire inspection, which took twenty-two minutes and was carried out with complete correctness.

He read the number. He inspected the seal, the wire and the crimp. He compared the seal against the photograph in the log and against the Kelvet register copy, and he found, on the schedule, in the Superintendent's hand, the word *nominally*, and read it, and looked up.

*"...He wrote that?"*

<font color=royalblue>"He wrote that."</font>

The clerk at Baishagou looked at the word *nominally* on the back of a spoiled Form 9 for about four seconds.

Then he stamped four places, and gave the log back, and went off up the lane at speed with his cards still face down.

## 9 — THE KETTLE-LID

The road came over the last shoulder at half past five in the evening on the twentieth day and the valley opened all at once, and there it was.

Forty kilometres of it, in a bowl, with a dam above it and mountains behind it, and the water lying flat and taking the whole sky, and the willows going all round the shore in two shades because the west bank was three days ahead of the east.

The lamp-grid came on while they were standing there.

It comes on from the north side first, because the switching at Jinzhou is done by hand and has been for eighty years, and the gold ran down the terraces and along the crest wall and out onto the lakefront the way something poured runs down a step, and it took about four minutes, and none of the seven of them said one word for any of it.

Then Cartethyia said, in a very small voice:

<font color=skyblue>"...There's a *bird.*"</font>

<font color=teal>"Carte—"</font>

<font color=skyblue>"There's a bird and it's *yellow* and it's in the willow and it's got a black stripe through the eye like a *mask*, and I've had its name for nine days off a boy at a bus stop in Shimen and I have never once *seen* one—"</font>

She said the name.

Four or five notes came out of the willows, over the water, in the last of the light.

Ciaccona got the tally book out and wrote it down without being asked.

<font color=teal>"Forty-one."</font>

<font color=skyblue>"Hm?"</font>

<font color=teal>"Forty-one characters I have learned out of your mouth since the pass, and that is the forty-first, and I have kept the tally on a page at the back and I want it *known.*"</font>

---

The bend where the road holds its breath before the descent is nine kilometres out and four hundred metres up, and there is room on it for about eleven vehicles, and there were four there when the *Lucky Bird* pulled in.

The freight crews call it the kettle-lid.

Amy asked four crews in one evening why it is called the kettle-lid, and got four answers, and wrote all four of them down on the same page in the same hand, and refused, then and afterward, to rank them:

Because the valley below it boils in the morning and the bend is where the steam comes over.

Because from up there the whole bowl looks like a pot with the lid tipped off, and the lid is the dam.

Because a man called Kettle went off it in the sixty-first year and everyone stopped saying the second half of his name.

Because it is where you stop and put the kettle on, which is what everybody parked there was in fact doing.

---

The blue-on-white Mingting consortium unit was one of the four.

They had been through Kelvet nine times that season and had opinions, and they had passed the *Lucky Bird* on the first day and been passed by it on the second day when their air line went, and had passed it again on the fourth and the sixth and the seventh, and had begun, at about the fifth passing, to sound the horn in a pattern.

Zhao drove. Little Guo did everything else. Little Guo had spent four days working out which of the seven foreigners was on the radio, and had established it on the sixth day, and had been talking to Amy across two languages and about eleven kilometres ever since, with an eighty percent loss rate and a hundred percent success rate.

At half past nine that night Amy keyed the rig at the kettle-lid.

<font color=mistyrose>"Fleet Snowfluff. Testing, one, two."</font>

Four sets came back.

Four sets came back out of four cabs parked on the same bend, in three dialects, one of them singing, and none of it survived translation, and Amy sat on the roof rack between two water drums with the antenna out and her hand over her mouth.

<font color=mistyrose>"...Chisa."</font>

The second handheld crackled from thirty metres away, from a woman sitting on the tailgate with an earpiece in.

<font color=coral>"Received."</font>

<font color=mistyrose>"There's *four.*"</font>

<font color=coral>"Five."</font>

<font color=mistyrose>"...Yeah."</font>

<font color=mistyrose>"Aemeath. Of the cabin with the blue door, Roya Frostlands. Ward 734. Kettle-lid, nine kilometres out, and I can see the whole city."</font>

<font color=coral>"Received. Out."</font>

They ate on the parapet of the bend with the four crews, and Ciaccona played, purple to the second knuckle, and answered the question about her hands four times, honestly, at length, exactly as she had promised herself in a mulberry lane.

Lupa cooked, because Lupa had spent eight days watching a country cook, and produced something on a camp stove at the kettle-lid that Zhao ate three bowls of and then interrogated her about through Cartethyia for eleven minutes.

Mei sat on the parapet with a cup and looked at forty kilometres of water going dark.

The raven came in at about ten and took up a position on the roof rack next to a folded red kite, and stayed.

And at the tailgate, at half past eleven, the tape ran, because it had run every evening since the city line and this was the last one.

<font color=moccasin>"My friend is unwell."</font>

<font color=moccasin>"Thank you."</font>

<font color=moccasin>"Is anybody in?"</font>

<font color=moccasin>"We're going east."</font>

<font color=moccasin>"慢走,"</font> she said. <font color=moccasin>"Go slowly."</font>

Out past the eaves of the tarp, at about the height of a person sitting down, the air did something for about a second.

<font color=teal>"...Say that again."</font>

<font color=moccasin>"Go slowly."</font>

<font color=teal>"That's the wrong tone on the second one."</font>

<font color=moccasin>"Yeah, I know."</font>

<font color=royalblue>"Twenty-six point four,"</font> said Iuno, from the dark.

<font color=moccasin>"There she is."</font>

---

Iuno had the map out on the tailgate under the lamp at midnight, and the map had been memorised by everyone present four days earlier, and she folded it along its old creases and squared it and left her hand flat on it.

She had two hundred and eleven kilometres behind her in eight days against a Superintendent's estimate of two.

She had forty-two data sheets with four of them used and the fifth started, and a bundle band with a date on it that she had already missed and would explain in a letter.

She had a cause column with a heading in it that read *people came over*.

And she had nine kilometres left, and a woman going down to a shore at the fourth watch, and a whole descent to time.

*Darling,* said the High Priestess, after a while, *you have been sitting with that map for eleven minutes and you have not written anything.*

*I am working out how late to be.*

*…Say that again with your chest out, darling. I should like to hear it in the good voice.*

*I arrived somewhere early once,* said Iuno, *and it cost a courier her errand, and I have been paying that account for two years and it takes exactly one coin.* She put her finger on the bend, and then on the dam road, and then on the stair. *Nine kilometres. First light plus one hour.*

*...Plus one whole hour.*

*Plus one whole hour, on purpose, with my entire attention,* said Iuno, *and I am going to need all six of them to help me do it, and one of them is going to argue.*

*Two of them are going to argue.*

*Yes,* said Iuno, and got out the pen, and began, at midnight, at a tailgate, nine kilometres above a lake, to write down the exact hour at which she intended to arrive late.

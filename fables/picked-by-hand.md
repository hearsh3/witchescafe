Picked by Hand

In the west wagon-bay among the sleeping bronze guardians, Ciaccona held a strip of barrel-hoop iron steady against the third cart’s tailboard with her uninjured right hand while Lupa drove a rivet home. Outside the open doors, Saturday sat grey and still over the packed ice in the lanes.

“<font color=indianred>How do you talk to somebody’s mother?</font>” Lupa asked around a second iron nail clenched between her back teeth.

Ciaccona looked from her own splinted left forefinger to the wolf’s flattened ears. “<font color=teal>Generally I climb out the pantry window before she comes downstairs, wolf, so you’re asking the wrong bard.</font>”

“<font color=indianred>I’m serious,</font>” Lupa growled, taking the nail from her teeth and setting the point against the oak plank. “<font color=indianred>You’ve got manners when you want ’em. Practise on me. Pretend you’re Chisa’s mum and I’ve just walked into the kitchen.</font>”

Ciaccona rested her elbow on the tailboard, cleared her throat, and pitched her voice into a tragic Ragunnese contralto. “<font color=teal>Ah, so this is the wild gladiator who has stolen my only daughter! Have you brought enough red pepper to burn the roof off my humble house, or do you intend to eat the furniture raw?</font>”

Lupa lowered the hammer and stared at her. “<font color=indianred>You’re rubbish at this. Chisa’s mum doesn’t talk like an opera singer with a stomachache, and she puts turnip in the soup, not pepper.</font>”

“<font color=teal>Well, I’ve never met the woman,</font>” Ciaccona said. “<font color=teal>Come on, then, if it’s so easy—you do Cantarella. I’ve got to sit at her table in Fisalia Manor next month without choking on the silverware.</font>”

Lupa wiped her nose on the cuff of her sheepskin coat, dropped the hammer into her apron loop, and straightened her spine against the cart-wheel. Her chin came up a fraction, her eyelids drooped to half-mast, and when she spoke, her rough arena bark had vanished into the silky, unhurried purr of a high-seat patrician above the Colosseum sand.

“<font color=indianred>How charming of you to bring your little lute into the dining room, Miss Toccata,</font>” Lupa murmured, looking down her nose at Ciaccona’s boots as if they were dead rats. “<font color=indianred>Do try not to drip canal-water on the carpet; the velvet is older than your family’s pardon. Will you be staying for the pudding, or do you prefer to take a bone in the courtyard?</font>”

Ciaccona’s hand came off the iron strip. “<font color=teal>Sweet mother of mercy. Where did you learn to do that?</font>”

“<font color=indianred>Fought for every big house in Septimont, didn’t I?</font>” Lupa snorted, picking the hammer back up in her own voice. “<font color=indianred>Had to stand in their boxes while they pinched my arm and talked over my head. They all sound like they’re buttering a bread-knife to stick in your kidneys.</font>”

“<font color=teal>And what did you do when they did that?</font>” Ciaccona asked, setting her palm back on the iron brace.

“<font color=indianred>Don’t bow, and don’t try to be funny,</font>” Lupa said, driving the second nail straight into the oak with two clean cracks. “<font color=indianred>They want you to tap-dance. You look right at the bridge of their nose, tell ’em the meat needs salt, and ask for the gravy boat. Scares the life out of ’em.</font>”

Ciaccona watched the nail-head sink flush into the iron band and tucked that away behind her teeth.

“<font color=indianred>Right, hold the left hinge while I lift,</font>” Lupa grunted, getting her shoulder under the heavy plank.

“<font color=teal>One piece of advice for Lahai-Roi, wolf,</font>” Ciaccona said, guiding the iron eye over the cart’s pintle-pin as Lupa heaved it up. “<font color=teal>When you get to her mother’s building, don’t carry the coal-scuttle up the stairs.</font>”

Lupa paused with the cotter-pin in her fingers and frowned across the tailboard. “<font color=indianred>Why not? It’s up a flight of stairs, and some old bloke from the salt-shop’s been hauling her buckets since the pipe froze.</font>”

“<font color=teal>Leave the scuttle on the landing for Mr. Sato,</font>” Ciaccona said, dropping her pin into the left hinge. “<font color=teal>Trust me, wolf.</font>”

Lupa squinted at her, gave up, and rapped her knuckles twice against the mended oak board, which didn’t rattle a hair.

By the well in Huizhen Plaza, the red-ruled ledger of the dead lay open on the cabbage-seller’s trestle under a sky the colour of wet slate. Qingxiao sat on the bench beside Hsin with two salt-house shawls wrapped to her chin and her hands in her lap.

Old Kang from the south gate stopped by the well-coping with his hands in his sleeves, looked at the two of them, and cleared his throat. “<font color=burlywood>Bitter wind coming down the cut this morning, Sentinel. Freeze the ears right off a mule.</font>”

Hsin looked up from the page with her ink-brush poised. “<font color=gold>It blows from the north in the tenth month,</font>” Hsin said plainly. “<font color=gold>Put your ear-flaps down.</font>”

Old Kang blinked, shifted his weight, and tried the other end of the bench. “<font color=burlywood>And the Paragon—must find the square noisy after the mountain, eh?</font>”

“<font color=lightcyan>Yes,</font>” Qingxiao said.

Old Kang waited three breaths for a second sentence, got none from either of them, and backed away toward the rope-walk at a brisk shuffle.

A moment later Mother Sun came clanking across the cobbles beside her bronze guardian, dumped a handful of warm roasted broad-beans directly onto the corner of the trestle between Hsin’s ink-stone and Qingxiao’s elbow, and didn’t break stride.

“<font color=thistle>Wipe your nose, Paragon,</font>” Mother Sun said over her shoulder. “<font color=thistle>And your cuff’s in the ink-well again, Sentinel.</font>”

Qingxiao wiped her nose on her wrist, picked up a broad-bean, and ate it.

Hsin pulled her left cuff out of the wet black ink with a small, cross sigh and went back to copying the week’s loose slips into the bound book. Halfway down the column she wrote the name of an old Yuan Fortress pike-corporal.

“<font color=lightcyan>Zhao Lin,</font>” Qingxiao said, looking at the wet characters. “<font color=lightcyan>He kept dried sour plums in his sword-tassel and chewed the pits on watch.</font>”

Hsin’s brush paused, then moved to the right-hand margin and wrote it down in small, neat strokes despite the fine tremor in her fingers.

Two lines lower, Hsin copied a name from the cooper’s lane.

“<font color=lightcyan>His grandfather,</font>” Qingxiao said, eating a second bean, “<font color=lightcyan>laughed through his nose like a leaky bellows whenever he won at dice.</font>”

Hsin wrote that down too, then rested the brush across the porcelain rest and looked sideways at Qingxiao’s red nose.

“<font color=gold>What will you do with yourself here, A-Qing?</font>” Hsin asked without a riddle. “<font color=gold>After the carts go down the south road.</font>”

Qingxiao looked at the frost crusting the well-rope, then down at the remaining beans on the pine board.

“<font color=lightcyan>Sleep until the first bell,</font>” Qingxiao said. “<font color=lightcyan>Learn to make bean-curd soup without burning the pot. Beat you at the lower-left star-point.</font>”

“<font color=gold>You were three liberties behind on that star-point,</font>” Hsin objected, her chin coming up.

“<font color=lightcyan>You played on a dead group,</font>” Qingxiao said.

Hsin sniffed, picked up the brush, and began the family name at the top of the next column, hooking the long downward stroke of the left radical too far inward.

Qingxiao reached out with one cold forefinger and nudged the bamboo shaft of Hsin’s brush a hair to the right.

“<font color=lightcyan>Straight down,</font>” Qingxiao said. “<font color=lightcyan>A hook makes it a different word.</font>”

Hsin’s golden ears flattened straight back against her hair at having her brushwork corrected at her own trestle; then, as the stroke came down clean and square on the paper, her ears tipped forward again, and the dark-tipped tail behind the bench slid across the frost to settle over Qingxiao’s bare toes.

In the quiet salt-house at midday, with the stove banked low and the grey cold pressing against the unlatched shutter, Amy lay on her stomach on the kang and slid her Terminal across the warm brick to Chisa’s knee.

“<font color=mistyrose>Before you message your mum,</font>” Amy said, propping her chin in both palms, “<font color=mistyrose>you’ve got to see what the Huangboard’s been posting about us since the relay came back. Look at this one from yesterday.</font>”

Chisa looked down at the glowing glass. It showed a blurry square of pale blue and white smears above a dark brown band.

“<font color=coral>I don’t know what that is,</font>” Chisa said.

Amy glanced up at Chisa’s eyes, caught herself at once, and pulled the slate an inch closer without making a fuss about it. “<font color=mistyrose>Right. Wait—don’t look at the glass, listen to it instead. Ready? Bare feet slapping on ice, a big wooden bucket sloshing on the left side, and right behind it goes *grumble, clomp, clomp* because somebody’s boots are too heavy for the path.</font>”

“<font color=coral>Qingxiao and Lupa at the well,</font>” Chisa said immediately.

Amy grinned and flicked her thumb across the screen. “<font color=mistyrose>Okay, next one. *Clink, clink* on a brass rim, somebody humming two notes flat in her throat, and a massive *honk* right by your kneecap.</font>”

“<font color=coral>Cartethyia and the goose,</font>” Chisa said.

“<font color=mistyrose>You’re faster at this than I am,</font>” Amy said, scrolling down another page before her thumb slowed on a picture from the festival lane. She looked up from the screen. “<font color=mistyrose>How do you tell Ma and Iuno apart when they’re just standing still and nobody’s talking?</font>”

“<font color=coral>Mei’s weight is always forward on the balls of her feet, like she’s about to turn round and catch something,</font>” Chisa said, resting her hands on her ankles. “<font color=coral>And Iuno’s left shoulder chimes when she breathes in, and she smells of bergamot and cold stone. When you’re standing between them, you stop tapping your heel.</font>”

Amy went quiet for a beat, her fingers curling round the edge of the casing. Then she scrolled down to the forum comments beneath the viral clip of Lupa’s broken tavern table.

Chisa leaned over her shoulder and read the top comment aloud: *“<font color=coral>Table spoil already before we sit down. Very bad pine, nobody pay.</font>”*

“<font color=coral>That’s Buling,</font>” Chisa said.

Amy’s thumb slid over the screen to cover the username *BigThunder88*. “<font color=mistyrose>Yeah. Don’t tell Ruoyun or the others it’s her, okay? She likes having a corner on the relay where nobody knows her name.</font>”

“<font color=coral>Okay,</font>” Chisa said, and meant it.

Amy switched the screen to the clean relay-post window and turned the Terminal round toward Chisa’s lap.

Chisa typed slowly with her right forefinger: *Mama. I bought a warmer for the kitchen tap and a blue bird that whistles. Lupa is coming with me. She practised the ribs on Thursday. See you soon.*

Amy read it upside down from the mattress, her pink hair brushing Chisa’s sleeve. “<font color=mistyrose>Want me to put in an exclamation mark or a sticker? People usually put a little waving cat at the bottom so it doesn’t look like a military dispatch.</font>”

“<font color=coral>No,</font>” Chisa said, keeping her thumb poised over the send-key. “<font color=coral>Mama knows how I talk.</font>”

Amy read the plain block of words once more and dropped her chin onto the cuff of Chisa’s black wool jumper. “<font color=mistyrose>Yeah. Leave it.</font>”

Chisa pressed her thumb to the glass and listened to the small, clear chime as the relay took the message north.

In the orchard crew’s warm cookhouse in Mengxuan Vale, Buling upended her rosewood box onto the pine table while the grey afternoon pressed against the paper window. The bone tiles spilled out in a heap that left a gap at the end of the row.

“<font color=palegreen>Still missing half a handful,</font>” Buling grumbled, poking the pile with one finger. “<font color=palegreen>How to build a wall like that?</font>”

Cartethyia’s hand went straight into her cloak pocket, where the East Wind she had kept on Friday sat beside the goose’s spare corn. Her ears went hot under her newly straightened laurel, and she pulled the single green-backed tile out and laid it on the pine.

Buling stared at the East Wind, then up at Cartethyia’s scarlet face. Across the corner, Longjue, sitting barefoot with her red gourd on her knee, let out a roar of laughter and swept Buling’s short set back into the rosewood box with one arm.

“<font color=aquamarine>Put yours back in your pocket, little knight!</font>” Longjue bellowed, reaching under the bench to haul up a heavy cedar chest of Vale tiles. “<font color=aquamarine>Keep it! My landlady still owes me a winter-solstice fortune from before the flood, and she never left so much as a chipped wind tile behind as security!</font>”

Cartethyia slipped her East Wind back into her cloak pocket as they stacked the cedar set. Buling propped her chin on her fist, lifted her yellow forehead talisman out of the way to chew a roasted peanut, and taught by waiting until Cartethyia made an illegal discard and then fining her for it.

“<font color=palegreen>Cannot pung that one, your hand is dirty already,</font>” Buling said lazily, taking Cartethyia’s discarded bamboo for her own win and holding her palm out across the board. “<font color=palegreen>Pay up. No copper, give me one piece of that candied lemon peel in your bag.</font>”

For the first few rounds Cartethyia lost nearly all her lemon peel because she kept saving the tiles with plum blossoms on them. Then she stopped looking at the carvings on the bone and began watching which end of the rack Buling’s red eyes flicked toward whenever Longjue drew from the wall.

When Longjue went to the stove to fill the tea-kettle, Cartethyia and Buling washed the tiles in a loud clatter across the pine.

“<font color=skyblue>Buling,</font>” Cartethyia said, turning a white tile right-side up. “<font color=skyblue>In Septimont, before Cia came to the Hold—what was she like when she was by herself?</font>”

Buling’s hands slowed in the middle of the bone pile. “<font color=palegreen>Order cream sauce on everything, complain the wine is sour, and sit with her back to the wall so she can see the street,</font>” Buling said. “<font color=palegreen>And every time she finish a song at a café, she latch her lute-case before the clapping stop, ready to walk.</font>”

Cartethyia nodded once, setting her wall straight. “<font color=skyblue>She left her boots behind the salt-house door last night, under Lupa’s.</font>”

Buling looked down at her own cloth shoes under the bench. “<font color=palegreen>Easy to leave your shoes in a pile when the road is shut,</font>” Buling said to the table edge. “<font color=palegreen>Harder when the carts are greased and you’re the one staying on the porch.</font>”

She caught herself, let the yellow talisman drop back over her nose, and tossed the dice at Cartethyia’s wrist. “<font color=palegreen>Roll lah. You’re East.</font>”

Longjue dropped back onto her stool with the steaming kettle. Halfway through the hand, Buling reached to discard a circle tile to bait Cartethyia’s left side, saw Cartethyia’s fingers already poised to open her row, and pulled the tile back—tossing out a safe West Wind instead.

Cartethyia beamed, tipped her whole row face-up on the pine, and slid Buling’s West Wind neatly into the end slot. “<font color=skyblue>All honours!</font>”

Longjue slapped the board hard enough to make the peanut dish jump. Buling stared at the winning row, folded her arms on the table edge, and laid her hat flat down on her sleeves. “<font color=aquamarine>I’m asleep. Wake me when the peach trees come out.</font>”

Cartethyia took two strips of candied lemon peel back from Buling’s pile and rested her left hand in her pocket over her own East Wind.

At the post-house counter in the flat grey afternoon, the clerk took one look at Iuno’s coat and shook his head before either woman opened her mouth.

“<font color=lightsteelblue>No overland tin from the south relay today, my lady,</font>” the clerk said. “<font color=lightsteelblue>Only the grain-road waybills.</font>”

Suisui tapped her closed fan lightly against her palm. “<font color=thistle>Then we shall attend to the poultry,</font>” she said smoothly.

“<font color=royalblue>Indeed,</font>” Iuno said, setting her gridded pad on the ink-stained walnut counter. “<font color=royalblue>Since everyone else has found an excuse to wander the terraces.</font>”

“<font color=thistle>My sister,</font>” Suisui remarked mildly, “<font color=thistle>did not even pretend to have an errand when she went out with Mei after breakfast.</font>”

“<font color=royalblue>Nor did Mei,</font>” Iuno replied, uncapping her brass pencil with her thumb and teeth. “<font color=royalblue>Clerk, we require a coastal transit docket for one live white goose, bound from Xuanfang Hold to Egla Town by way of the Ragunna packet.</font>”

The clerk blinked, pulled a blank livestock slip toward him, and within two minutes looked as though he had been dropped between two millstones.

“<font color=royalblue>A slatted wicker crate,</font>” Iuno dictated briskly, writing on her own pad at the same time, “<font color=royalblue>lashed on the leeward deck behind the galley flue so the spray doesn’t ice the feathers, with a canvas weather-flap to be lowered at dusk.</font>”

“<font color=thistle>Not behind the galley flue, Lady Iuno,</font>” Suisui corrected pleasantly, leaning one elbow on the counter. “<font color=thistle>Packet cooks throw salt-slops to leeward, and salt blisters webbed feet. Stow him forward of the mainmast hatch on dry dunnage, and enter his oats on the ship’s manifest as personal cabin stores so the purser cannot charge deck-fodder rates.</font>”

Iuno’s eyebrow rose a fraction. “<font color=royalblue>If his feed is entered under cabin stores, Director, the harbour-master at the strait will inspect the crate for luxury duty—including the silver hundred-year lock round his neck.</font>”

“<font color=thistle>A devotional collar,</font>” Suisui said without missing a beat, turning a gracious smile on the sweating clerk, “<font color=thistle>worn by a consecrated temple bird on pilgrimage to Uncle Cosimo’s bakehouse. Religious vestments are exempt under the maritime schedule, aren’t they, Master Clerk?</font>”

“<font color=lightsteelblue>I—we don’t have a column for consecrated geese, Director,</font>” the clerk stammered.

“<font color=royalblue>Write it in the margin,</font>” Iuno and Suisui said together.

While the clerk fled into the back room to fetch the red transit wax, Iuno turned her pad sideways, bracing the lower corner with her right forearm to write out feeding hours for the sea crossing so Cartethyia wouldn’t overfeed the bird in a swell.

Suisui watched the swift, practised dip of the single hand, and the way Iuno’s bare left shoulder crystal gave a faint, tight *tink* in the draught from the wicket without Iuno slowing her pencil by a hair.

“<font color=thistle>You arrange the weather for every person in that house, Lady Iuno,</font>” Suisui said lightly, resting her fan across the counter rail. “<font color=thistle>Who holds the umbrella when you are the one in the rain?</font>”

Iuno’s pencil stopped on the paper. She looked up from the page into Suisui’s eyes.

“<font color=royalblue>Mei does,</font>” Iuno said plainly. “<font color=royalblue>Usually with a knot that looks like a turtle, and well after we are both soaked through. It works remarkably well.</font>”

Suisui’s mouth softened at the corner. “<font color=thistle>Yes,</font>” she said. “<font color=thistle>I suppose it does.</font>”

When the clerk hurried back to stamp the goose’s pass in red wax, Suisui reached into her sleeve for coppers and brought out her Nine of Characters tile at the exact moment Iuno slid a brass coin onto the walnut beside the Three of Circles, and both of them left the bone tiles lying side by side on the counter until the seal cooled.

Down in the coal merchant’s yard by the Lower Wharf, the first dry flakes of late afternoon drifted past the scales without settling. Suoming stood with her umbrella furled between her mittens and rested her light-sore eyes on the low grey cloud-bank; it was the best sky the Hold had managed all week.

Beside her, Ruoyun stood with her hands tucked into her spotless ruffled cuffs, glaring at a heap of river-anthracite as if it had insulted her ancestors. She had asked Suoming down the hill after lunch for company that wouldn’t chatter, and neither of them had spoken since the lock-house.

The coal-man came out of his shed wiping his palms on his leather apron. “<font color=tan>Winter slate for the upper lane, Elder? The usual for the salt-house and the girl’s noodle cart?</font>”

“<font color=plum>Full load for the noodle cart,</font>” Suoming said in her flat, quiet voice. “<font color=plum>Dry nut-coal, no slack. For the salt-house, bring the small barrow when the carts leave.</font>”

The coal-man paused with his chalk against the board. “<font color=tan>The small barrow? That big kang up there eats a heavy load when the north wind gets in.</font>”

“<font color=plum>There won’t be a crowd on it,</font>” Suoming said.

Beside her, Ruoyun’s chin came up an inch, her brass spectacles flashing grey against the river.

“<font color=lavender>Bring the big cart to the salt-house,</font>” Ruoyun snapped at the coal-man. “<font color=lavender>And stack it under the back eaves off the wet stone. Put it on my book.</font>”

Suoming turned her head to look up at Ruoyun’s sharp jaw.

“<font color=lavender>A-Qing has taken to walking down the mountain while the soup is warm,</font>” Ruoyun said coldly to the coal-shed roof. “<font color=lavender>If she comes down in the frost and sits on cold brick, she will sneeze until spring. The flue stays lit.</font>”

Suoming looked back at the coal-man. “<font color=plum>What she said.</font>”

While the coal-man went to chalk the order on the shed slate, the old iron-bar rasp caught low under Suoming’s left ribs on the damp river draught. She didn’t hum through it; she stood still by the scale-beam until her lung let go.

“<font color=lavender>It will be quiet up there when those wagons clear the south cut,</font>” Ruoyun muttered, her eyes on the empty timber-slip where the river lighters tied up. “<font color=lavender>Like a barracks after the muster moves out.</font>”

“<font color=plum>Mm,</font>” Suoming said. “<font color=plum>I’ll get some sleep before the night watch for once. And the Sentinel won’t have anyone stealing her pickled plums.</font>”

“<font color=lavender>They leave boots all over the threshold and boil tree-bark on the iron,</font>” Ruoyun said fiercely, and then her voice dropped a note, watching the thin flakes vanish into the grey water. “<font color=lavender>The wolf taught A-Qing to cook millet yesterday without scorching the pot. I didn’t think she’d have the patience.</font>”

The coal-man walked back, rubbing his chalky thumb on his apron. “<font color=tan>Heavy cart to the top of the steep steps, lady—I’ll have to charge extra for the mule-team on the ice.</font>”

Suoming didn’t look at Ruoyun, and Ruoyun didn’t look at Suoming.

“<font color=lavender>Your north bin’s roof-tar is split,</font>” Ruoyun said briskly, pointing a fingernail at the shed eaves. “<font color=lavender>That anthracite has been sitting in Tuesday’s meltwater. You are selling me wet stone by weight.</font>”

“<font color=plum>And the lime-burner owes the Sentinel a favour,</font>” Suoming added flatly. “<font color=plum>His guardian can take the sacks up. Keep your mules in the stable.</font>”

The coal-man opened his mouth, looked from Ruoyun’s spectacles to Suoming’s iron-shod umbrella ferrule, and spat into the coal-dust. “<font color=tan>Standard rate, then. No haulage.</font>”

Across the wharf an oil-lamp flared yellow behind a paper shutter, and Suoming’s steel ferrule clicked on the frozen cobbles as the two of them turned up the grade toward the lanes.

SECOND SOLARIS SCHOOL

From the timetables of Second Solaris School and the lamp-post speakers of a town by the sea, concerning two sisters who came back on the evening train, the girls who were waiting there without knowing it, and the last four days before the bell:

The Evening Train

The train came in under the station's one long canopy a little before five, slowed past a vending machine and a row of bicycles nobody had locked, and stopped with a sigh that sounded like it had made the trip many times. Mei got off first with a duffel bag over each shoulder and a paper bag of station-kiosk melon buns in her teeth. Amy got off second with her phone held up, filming the platform for the group chat back in the city, and complained immediately that the light was bad.

Their parents' work had taken them overseas for the year. Mei was eighteen and starting her final year; Amy, fifteen, was starting her first. Somebody had to keep them, and the somebody was their father's younger brother, who was waiting at the ticket barrier with both arms already up.

Uncle Abby had the family's gold eyes and a voice built for a parade ground. He hugged them both at once, took the heavier bag off Mei and then gave it back because it was heavier than he'd expected, and announced to the station attendant that these were his nieces. The attendant said he'd gathered. Uncle Abby also mentioned, twice on the walk home, that he was the principal of Second Solaris School, and that at school they were to call him Principal, and that he would understand if they forgot.

At five o'clock, halfway up the hill, the speakers on the lamp-posts came on. An old tune played through them, slow and a little crackled, the town's evening broadcast. Mei stopped in the middle of the pavement with a melon bun in her hand. She had lived in this town until she was seven, and she found she knew the tune all the way to where it turned. She didn't know where she'd learned it. Amy had to come back for her.

Uncle Abby's apartment was on the third floor and was beige. It had a fern, a wall clock, a filing cabinet and a television that got the news. It had no snacks and no clutter. Uncle Abby showed them round it with great pride. Amy stood in the middle of the living room and said, with feeling, that she was going to die there. Mei named the fern Ferdinand. The spare room had two futons and a window that looked down the hill to the sea wall, and the girls lay on the futons in their coats until Uncle Abby called them for a supper of four separate dishes he had ordered for three people.

The Town Square

On the second day they walked the town. Mei found the parts she remembered in the wrong order: the smell of the harbour first, then the covered arcade with its long glass roof, then a corner by the river she was sure had been a sweet shop and was now a laundrette. She remembered a dojo somewhere with a cracked floorboard and couldn't find the street. She remembered someone sitting on the sea wall at dusk and couldn't find a face. She decided she didn't mind, and bought a skewer of grilled squid to think about it.

In the arcade, a girl about Amy's age was standing in front of a music shop window with both hands flat on the glass. She was small, with a hair clip shaped like a laurel of glass thorns, and it was crooked. She was humming along to whatever was playing inside, loudly and slightly off key, and when she noticed she had an audience she turned round and introduced herself without any embarrassment at all. Her name was Cartethyia. She was from Egla, inland, farms and a bakery, and she had come to town for high school and would be starting at Second Solaris on Monday as a first-year. She loved music. She said so the way some people say they love their mother. Amy asked what she played. Cartethyia said, "Nothing yet," with complete confidence.

She was on her way to the town square, because the school's music club was holding an open rehearsal at the bandstand, and she took them with her.

The music club turned out to be one final-year girl with a battered lute, a folding table of flyers held down with a tin of mints, and a bandstand that could have held a brass band. Her name was Ciaccona. She was playing a Ragunnese song badly, on purpose, and when a man walking his dog stopped to tell her she was rushing the second line, she thanked him, played it properly, and got a round of applause from the bench outside the bakery. In the second chorus Cartethyia began to sing along. She was flat. She was enormous. She was louder than the lute, the pigeons and the clock tower. Ciaccona stopped playing, looked at her for a long moment, and held out a pen.

Cartethyia signed before the chorus was over. Ciaccona didn't ask what she played. She told Mei and Amy, while she was folding the form, that the club needed five members by the April club review or it would lose its room, and that she currently had three if you counted one who only came in for the air conditioning. She looked at Mei's hands, then at Amy's face, and offered them each a flyer.

Mei and Amy agreed, on the way out of the square, that they would look around before deciding anything. Cartethyia waved to them from the bandstand with both arms, already holding a tambourine.

Uncle Abby met them for lunch at Namipon Cafe, on the corner of the arcade: window seats, a smell of curry roux, a corner table with a gym bag shoved under it and a handwritten card on top that said TRACK. The girl who took their order was a second-year in black, quiet and direct, helping her parents over the holidays. Her name was Chisa. She didn't look at their faces much. She looked at their shoes and listened to their voices, and when Uncle Abby ordered four things she wrote down four things without comment. A wind-up tin crab walked sideways along the counter and fell off the end, and she caught it without looking. The door chime played a different note every time someone came in, and Mei noticed it went high when the door was pushed hard. Uncle Abby said the curry was the best in town. Mei agreed with her mouth full. Amy took a photo of it for the group chat and ate Mei's pickles while she was looking.

The Arcade and the Fountain

On the third day the sisters split up. Amy wanted to find something, anything, to make the apartment survivable. Mei wanted to see the school.

In the arcade Amy met a second-year girl with silver hair and red eyes, wearing a round knit cap with a paper charm stuck to the brim so it hung down over her forehead, and a little bell on her bag. Her name was Buling. She had a folding table outside the second-hand electronics shop, a selection of lucky charms in a biscuit tin, and, under the table, a game console in a box printed in a language Amy couldn't read. It was, Buling explained, an imported limited edition. Amy haggled. Buling let her. Amy walked away with the box under her arm and a price she considered a personal triumph, and sent the group chat a photo of her winning face. Buling watched her go, then took out her Terminal and posted something one-thumbed, smiling slightly.

Mei went up the hill to Second Solaris. The gates were open for staff and club work, and a final-year girl was waiting just inside them as if she had been expecting someone in particular. She had white hair with a streak of red, red-gold eyes, a composed, unhurried way of standing, and she already knew Mei's name. Her name was Changli. She was President of the Student Council, and she offered Mei a short tour.

It was a good tour: the council room with a weiqi board set up by the window mid-game, the music room at the end of the third floor with the best light in the building, the infirmary with a lit jellyfish tank and a glasshouse behind it, a greenhouse plot by the field with somebody's cherry tomato seedlings and a hose coiled with great precision, the track, the canteen. Changli talked about each place briefly and well. What Mei remembered afterwards was the way she looked. Changli looked at her at the fountain and at the music-room door and at the top of the stairs, a little longer each time than the moment needed, as if she were waiting for Mei to notice something. Mei didn't know what. When Mei asked why the Council President was giving tours in the holidays, Changli smiled and said she liked to meet people before they had decided what they thought of the place.

Mei walked home the long way, along the shore path under the sea wall. It was nearly five. The tide was out and the light had gone the colour of weak tea. At five the lamp-post speakers came on with the old tune again, and Mei, who was looking at the sea, walked straight into someone.

The girl didn't stumble. She was tall and still, with long pale hair that had a faint blue sheen to it, a slim ornament in it shaped like a star or a butterfly, and a long dark coat. She steadied Mei by the elbow, very lightly, and let go. She said Mei had come in on the evening train. It wasn't a question. Mei said yes, two days ago, and asked how she knew. The girl said she had heard it. She looked at Mei the way you look at a constellation you're checking against a chart, carefully and without hurry, and Mei had the strongest feeling she had ever had that she knew this person and had known her a long time. She couldn't find where from. By the time she had decided to ask, the tune had finished, and the girl had said good evening and gone on along the wall toward the cape, where the old observatory sat with its dome shut. Mei stood there until the gulls started up again.

That night Amy unpacked the console onto the living-room floor, admired it, and didn't plug it in, because Uncle Abby needed the socket for the news. Mei lay on her futon with the window open and hummed the broadcast tune until Amy threw a pillow at her.

Enrollment

On the fourth day the sisters walked to school together in brand-new uniforms: navy blazers with the gold sun-ring crest, a red final-year tie that Mei did up on the stairs and a green first-year tie that Amy had loosened exactly as far as the rules would allow. They were halfway up the hill when the whole road slowed down.

A long black car was stopped at the school gate. Two men in dark suits got out first and stood by the doors in a way that made students walk round them in a wide arc. Then a second-year girl stepped out of the back, blonde and gentle-faced, humming something under her breath, and visibly wishing the car would disappear. Behind her came another girl, if girl was the word: silver hair pinned without a strand loose, a blazer worn like a suit jacket, a bag like a briefcase, a planner in one hand. She looked about twenty-five and entirely in charge. The crowd at the gate went from murmur to roar. A first-year near Mei asked whether the tall one was a new teacher. Somebody else said bodyguard. Somebody else said heiress. Buling was on the wall by the gate with her Terminal held up, filming.

The blonde girl thanked the two men, asked them politely whether they might stand behind the tree, and walked into Second Solaris as if she had been waiting three years to do exactly that. The tall one followed, already writing something down.

Just inside the gate, a final-year girl with silver-blue hair pinned with total exactness stood beside the shoe lockers with a silver pocket watch open in her palm and a leather notebook under her arm. Students slowed as they passed her and straightened their ties. She glanced at the limo with an expression of deep personal grievance, glanced at the watch, and then glanced at Mei, at Mei's tie, and at a crumb of melon bun on Mei's collar, and wrote something in the notebook. She didn't say anything. Mei brushed the crumb off anyway.

Their homeroom teacher, Miss Verina, met them at the office. She was so small and young-looking that Amy asked her, kindly, whether she was lost; Miss Verina said she wasn't, thanked Amy for asking, and took them through every form in twelve minutes flat, handing each of them a seedling in a paper cup at the end as if that were part of enrollment. Uncle Abby came on over the speakers while they were signing, to welcome the school back, to announce the canteen's new curry, and to mention that his nieces were joining today. Amy put her head down on the desk.

Mei was taken to 3-A during Miss Galbrena's humanities class. Miss Galbrena was tall and plain-dressed, chewing something she wasn't supposed to have, and she introduced Mei in about six words and pointed at a seat. Thirty faces looked up. In the back row a girl with sun-streaked crimson hair taped back, a chipped tooth and her blazer sleeves shoved past the elbow grinned at Mei like a challenge. By the window, the girl from the gate with the pocket watch did not look up from her notes, which was its own kind of looking. And two rows over, with her hands folded on the desk and her red-gold eyes already on Mei, sat Changli, who smiled as if a move she had been waiting for had finally been played.

Downstairs, Amy was taken to 1-A in the middle of Miss Augusta's science class. Miss Augusta was tall and upright, with fair hair pinned severely and a brass stopwatch on a cord, and she stopped talking when the door opened in a way that made the whole room stop with her. She introduced Amy by her full name, Aemeath, which nobody ever used, and told her to put her phone in the box on the desk. In the second row a hand shot up and waved, attached to a crooked laurel clip and a voice that carried to the back of the lab: Cartethyia, delighted, already patting the empty stool beside her. By the window a quiet girl with blue-tipped hair and a reflective windbreaker folded on her bench was asleep sitting bolt upright, and opened one eye at the noise, and closed it again.

Where Things Stand

At the present stopping point it is the first morning of term. Mei is standing at the front of 3-A with a seedling in a paper cup, about to take her seat among the crimson-haired girl at the back, the girl with the pocket watch by the window, and Changli two rows over. Amy is walking to the stool beside Cartethyia in 1-A, with her phone in Miss Augusta's box and the quiet girl by the window pretending to be asleep.

Nobody yet knows who Mei is beyond the principal's niece. Mei doesn't know the names of the girl with the pocket watch or the crimson-haired girl. She doesn't know the name of the girl on the sea wall, or where she knows her from. She doesn't know why Changli looks at her the way she does. Amy hasn't plugged in the console. The music club still has three members if you count the one who only comes in for the air conditioning, and it needs five by April. The limo will be at the gate again tomorrow. At five o'clock the lamp-post speakers will play the old tune again, and Mei will know it all the way to where it turns.

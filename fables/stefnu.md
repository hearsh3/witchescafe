# Stefnu

Rebecca had the big pan on the front ring and four bricks of instant noodles softening in broth that had gone the colour of a traffic light, and Lynae was at the end of the table with a cleaver and three heads of bok choy, chopping in self-defence.

Her textbook—*Applied Statics and Load Path Analysis, Third Edition*—was propped open against the base of the workshop lamp, held flat at page two hundred and fourteen by a soy sauce bottle and a caliper. Every four or five cuts she looked up, read a line of an equilibrium equation with her lips moving, and went back to the stalks without taking her knuckles off the guide edge of the blade.

"You're putting green in it," Rebecca said from the stove, not turning round. "I can hear you putting green in it."

"You put two packets of the red oil in the water before it even boiled. If nobody puts a vegetable in that pot, Chiyo's going to walk in here at nine and look at the pan and neither of us is surviving the morning."

"Chiyo likes my noodles."

"Chiyo eats your noodles because she's sixty-three and polite and her daughter's in another province."

The stairwell door clicked on its latch—the second click, the one that meant somebody had lifted the handle on the way shut so it wouldn't bang—and Sigrika came in with her scarf still wound up to her ears and a square parcel under her left arm.

Rebecca didn't look round from the stove; she just reached up to the shelf with her chrome hand, took down a fourth bowl, and set it on the counter beside the other three.

"Shut the door," Rebecca said. "Draft's coming right up my ankles."

"It is shut." Sigrika unwound the scarf, hung it over the back of her chair, and set the parcel on the table clear of Lynae's chopping board. Her nose was pink at the tip from the terrace walk. "It came on the late sled last night. The porter held it at the lodge because the wax on the jam jar looked like a battery seal on the fluoroscope."

Lynae set the cleaver down flat and wiped her hands on her apron. "Is that Halla's?"

"Mm."

The parcel was wrapped in a heavy linen tea towel with red checks woven into the hem, knotted twice at the opposite corners the way people knot things when they intend to get the towel back in the spring. Sigrika worked the knot loose with her thumbnail instead of pulling it tight, folded the cloth back in four triangles, and laid out the cargo: two dark slabs of smoked mutton wrapped in greaseproof paper, a grey stoneware crock sealed with beeswax and string, and a stack of barley flatbreads the size of dinner plates, still soft enough to bend.

Pinned to the top flatbread with a wooden darning needle was a folded sheet of lined paper torn out of a co-op ledger.

Rebecca leaned over the table, sniffing hard at the greaseproof paper. "What died."

"Mutton," Sigrika said, unfolding the letter. "Smoked over birch and juniper."

"Your mom smokes meat like she's settling a score with it." Rebecca peeled a corner of the paper back, broke a dark splinter off the edge of the brisket, and put it in her mouth. She chewed twice and stopped. "Holy shit. That's like eating a boot that went to heaven. Give us a knife."

"Use the small one, not the cleaver," Lynae said, already sliding the paring knife across the oilcloth while she dumped the chopped greens into the boiling pot over Rebecca's groan.

Sigrika read the letter standing up by the chair. Her mother's hand ran all the way to the paper's edges, because Halla considered margins a tax invented by stationers: the co-op's new generator had thrown a belt on the first frost and Sturla had fixed it with a tractor strap and been insufferable at church; the Rennirs' dog had learned to open the back latch with his nose; Uni had lost a good rubber boot in the peat ditch behind the sheep-wash and come home with one cold foot and an elaborate lie about a badger.

And at the very bottom, squeezed under the fold where the paper had been pressed against the bread:

*Amma has not moved the jar from the door. Don't forget to wash the towel before you send it back, the mutton gets in the linen.*

Sigrika folded the sheet along its original creases, twice, and slipped it into the inside pocket of her jacket.

From her satchel she took out a long manila envelope with the Registry's window stamp on the front, already slit along the top. She sat down, pushed the salt cellar an inch to the left to clear a space between the chopsticks, and drew out the disposition form.

Across the upper right corner, diagonal and slightly smudged, stood the automated reader's mark in purple ink: `RETURNED — NO BOX SELECTED`.

Clipped to it with a brass pin was a fresh copy of the same sheet, the three little squares down the left margin—*Withdrawn, Awarded, Deceased*—empty and waiting.

Sigrika lined the old form up beside the new one. She took the black pen from the loop of her journal, uncapped it, and began copying her own text back into the six ruled lines under *Remarks*, letter for letter, keeping the downstrokes square so the whole paragraph fit above the bottom line without crowding.

*STATUS: absent, field. DURATION: until the weather turns. Lodged as a remark, for the record, under her own seal by S., candidate, Heliodic track. Witnesses constitute record.*

Rebecca came over with the noodle pot on a cork mat and two pairs of tongs, reading upside down over Sigrika's shoulder as she set the pot down.

"They sent it back again?"

"The machine reads the boxes at the end of the sixth week," Sigrika said, reaching into her bag for the brass seal and the little tin of red ink-paste. "If there's no mark in the boxes, it prints a rejection slip and issues a blank."

"And you just write the same thing on it."

"Yes."

Rebecca watched her breathe on the brass face of the candidate seal and press it, two-handed, leaning her shoulder into it until the paper took the bite of the rim. "When is that, anyway? The weather turning."

"It's a remark," Sigrika said, lifting the seal straight up so the red wouldn't blur. "It doesn't take a date."

On the cupboard above the sink, between the tin of barley tea and the spare fuses, the little bakelite wireless receiver was working its way through the seven-thirty municipal bulletins. A man with a voice like gravel in a bucket read out the night pressure figures for Tier Three, a notice from the Sanitation Board concerning grease traps on the fourth terrace, and a reminder that the east incline tram would run on single-car headway between ten and two for cable greasing.

"—and from the Weather Office, third floor, Dome Administration," the announcer went on, without changing pace, "the winter calibration schedule for the upper tiers has now been posted at the Exchange. Cold cycle commences on the twenty-fourth of the month. First snow is scheduled for nineteen hundred hours on the twenty-fourth, tiers one through four, six centimetres nominal. Residents with terrace drainage responsibilities are reminded—"

Nobody said anything.

At the end of the table, Lynae's knife stopped against the board for the space of one breath. Her eyes went to the side of Sigrika's head—the cropped braid-ends tucked behind her ear, the cap going back onto the black pen—and stayed there a second, and then the knife went *chock, chock* through the last stem of ginger.

Rebecca ladled noodles into the four bowls with a clang that drowned out the rest of the drainage notice.

"Six centimetres at seven o'clock," Rebecca said, pointing the ladle at the wireless. "I still can't get over that. You people put winter on a *calendar*. Up at the cabin the sky just went grey like somebody unplugged it and then four feet of white shit fell on the woodshed while we were eating toast."

"It's for the roof loads," Sigrika said, putting the newly stamped form back into its sleeve to drop at Fenn's window on her way to Hall Two. "If they don't run a freeze-thaw cycle on the upper trusses every November, the expansion joints seize up by spring. And the birch trees on the avenue need four weeks below zero or they don't drop their leaves properly and the buds rot."

"Yeah, well, it's unhinged," Rebecca said, and thought about it for two seconds while she shoved a bowl across the oilcloth. "Kinda fair, though. Back home if something fell out of the sky they didn't tell you until your jacket started melting. Eat your soup before the fat sets."

They passed the flatbread and the mutton round. Sigrika broke a piece of bread in half, laid two thick slices of the dark meat and a spoonful of the tart red jam inside, rolled it into a cylinder, and ate half of it in three bites while her other hand was already working the chopsticks in her noodle bowl.

Lynae reached across with the flat of her chopstick and tapped the back of Sigrika's wrist.

"Breathe," Lynae said. "Halvard doesn't lock the door until five past, and nobody here is going to steal your mutton."

"I'm not eating fast," Sigrika said around a mouthful of barley bread, her cheeks out like a hamster's, and slowed down for four seconds before forgetting again.

By ten past eight the bowls were scraped clean and the stoneware crock was living on the middle shelf by the salt, because Rebecca had tried the jam on a corner of noodle-broth bread, declared it preem, and confiscated it for the house.

Rebecca carried the stack of bowls and the morning tea mugs to the sink and turned the hot tap on. Above the draining board, the cup rack had twelve brass hooks screwed into the lath. Eleven of the mugs came off and went through the soapy water as usual; the plain white one on the twelfth hook at the end had caught a splash of broth from the stove, and Rebecca took it down, wiped the rim clean with the dishcloth, dried it on her hip, and hung it back on the twelfth hook without looking at it or saying a word.

Between the spice rack and the calendar, the yellow rules sheet curled slightly at the bottom right corner from the steam off the kettle, the ninth rule sitting square and heavy where Rebecca's black marker had traced over Chiyo's careful borrowed script: **9. There is someone in the house.**

Opposite, by the door frame, Lynae's four-figure number was still on the whiteboard in dry blue ink. Somebody had hung a string bag of garlic on the peg beside it so nobody's sleeve would brush it off going out.

Sigrika stood up, brushed the flatbread crumbs off her skirt, and slid her arms into her coat, using the middle finger of her right hand on the top button where the scar through the index pad pulled tight in the morning chill.

Lynae was already shutting her textbook. She left the soy sauce bottle on the page to keep her place and reached for her lined jacket on the hook behind the door.

"I'll walk you down," Lynae said. "I need graph pads from the stationer's."

At the sink, Rebecca's shoulders did a small, silent thing, because there were three unopened pads of five-millimetre grid paper sitting on top of the microwave four feet from Lynae's elbow. She kept her hands in the suds.

"The stationer's is on the west arcade," Sigrika said, hitching her strap over her shoulder. "I'm going to Hall Two."

"I'm taking the long way," Lynae said, opening the door for her. "My legs are asleep from sitting on that crate since six. Come on, you've got Fenn's window first and there's always some idiot arguing about library fines at half past."

The note in Sigrika's pigeonhole at noon was typed on a third-of-a-sheet of heavy cream paper, cut straight with a guillotine, the lowercase *e* slightly filled in at the loop the way the ribbon on the Spacetrek Annex machine always printed it.

*Candidate Sigrika. Room 304, Engineering South, 15:30. Bring the raw sets for Appendices C and D. — M. Mornye.*

Sigrika spent her lunch hour in the library annex checking her arithmetic three times with a slide rule and a fresh sheet of foolscap, and went up the stairs of Engineering South at twenty-eight minutes past three with her chin set and her folder held against her ribs in both arms.

The door of 304 was ajar. Inside, the tall casement window was cracked an inch to the Solistree avenue, where the engineered oaks and birches were halfway through their November yellowing, dropping dry leaves onto the tram tracks with a faint, papery rustle.

Mornye was at the wide drafting table by the window, her bangs pinned back flat with two plain steel clips, three long strips of fan-fold instrument paper weighted down across the blotter with hex nuts. Beside her, the electric kettle on the low side-table clicked off as Sigrika knocked.

Sigrika automatically took half a step toward the side-table with her hand out.

"Sit down," Mornye said without looking up from a column of figures. "I put the table at that height myself, with a saw, so that people would stop lunging at my tea. There are two mugs on the tray."

Sigrika stopped, went red to the roots of her braids, and sat down in the wooden chair opposite the desk. "Sorry, Professor."

"You are the third person today," Mornye said, rolling her chair six inches to the left and pouring the water with a steady, practiced tilt of her wrist. "Halvard tried to carry my briefcase up a ramp this morning. It had two sheets of paper in it. I let him get to the top and then asked him to open it."

On the hovering Starstack at her elbow, between a brass vernier caliper and a stack of bound offprints, Buddy gave a small amber pulse, rose three inches into the air like a soap bubble catching a draft, and drifted across the desk toward Sigrika.

Sigrika held her breath as the Soliskin fragment floated over her folder, inspected the three pens clipped in a row along the spine of her journal, tapped the green cap once with one small arm, and settled squarely on Sigrika's left forearm between her elbow and her wrist. It folded its two stubby legs under its round middle, hugged its arms to its chest, and went warm, pulsing a slow, contented eleven-count against her wool sleeve.

Sigrika froze with her left arm suspended two inches above her knee so she wouldn't tip it off.

Mornye set a mug of tea by Sigrika's right hand. "You may move your arm. It is not made of spun glass, whatever it tells the undergraduates."

"It's comfortable," Sigrika said, keeping her forearm level.

Mornye gave a short, dry breath that might have been a laugh and turned one of the fan-fold printouts round so it faced across the desk. Clipped to the corner of it were Sigrika's own two sheets from the internal post, bearing her red initials and the embossed ring of her candidate seal.

"You put these in the box at eleven at night," Mornye said.

"Yes, Professor." Sigrika straightened her back, her right hand going to her folder. "The manual for the Heliogyre array states under Section Seven that when a periodic residual persists across more than three independent calibration runs, the operator may attach the unsmoothed trace as an appendix—"

"I wrote Section Seven," Mornye said. "Stop defending it. I am not the boy at the lab bench who wrote *smooth and resubmit* on a four-station trace. I have asked Halvard whose student that is. Halvard claims he belongs to Metallurgy, and Metallurgy is pretending to be at a conference."

Sigrika's mouth stayed open for a second, and then shut. "Oh."

"I read the internal post on Thursdays because that is where people hide the readings they are afraid of being mocked for," Mornye said. She picked up a sharp mechanical pencil and tapped the top of the fan-fold sheet. "Appendix C is the Heartwood's root-network harmonic. Eleven counts and a rest, displacing east along the main conduit at point-four metres an hour. That is real, it has been doing it since October, and the committee is ignoring it because it does not fit the drainage model. Appendix D is more interesting."

She slid the second printout over the first.

"Your array at the North Ridge has a high noise floor because the bedrock is fractured," Mornye said. "So without asking you, which was rude and saved four days of forms, I pulled the raw tape from Station Seven and the deep-bore acoustic sensor under the South Weir. Neither of those is on the Academy bus. The South Weir sensor sits in solid granite eighty metres below the water table."

Sigrika leaned forward, carefully, keeping her left arm flat for Buddy. "And it's there?"

"Every run since April," Mornye said. Her voice was quiet and exact, the voice that held three hundred third-years silent in Auditorium A without a microphone. "Same bearing—one-nine-four degrees from the North Ridge, two-oh-one from the Weir. Where the two lines cross on the survey map is the Lahai-Roi borehole basin, roughly sixty metres below the level of the plain. It does not travel. And it is not constant."

She turned a sheet of squared graph paper toward Sigrika. Across it, in Mornye's small, set hand, was a scatter of seven points plotted from April to the second week of November, with a ruler-straight line drawn through the middle of them, tilting very slightly upward from left to right.

"You logged it as point-nine-five hertz," Mornye said. "I converted it to cycles per minute. Old habit; when you are looking at a slow oscillation over months, a sixty-second window shows you a slope that a two-place decimal hides. In your April set, it was fifty-six point eight a minute. In the July tape from Station Seven, fifty-seven point two. Tuesday night at the South Weir, it was fifty-seven point nine. Nearer fifty-eight now."

Sigrika's hand was on her red pen.

The nib sat on the corner of her notepad for perhaps three seconds without moving. On her sleeve, Buddy's amber light rose and fell once. Outside in the avenue, a tram went past with a long iron hum on the rails.

Sigrika wrote, in red: *Apr: 56.8/min. Jul: 57.2/min. Nov (S. Weir): 57.9/min (~58).*

She underlined the last figure once, lifted the pen, and looked up at Mornye.

"Could the Weir sensor be picking up thermal contraction in the borehole casing as the surface cools?" Sigrika asked. "If the collar of the shaft is shrinking in the autumn air, the resonant frequency of the column would creep upward."

Mornye watched her for a moment—not the careful, load-testing look that made Sigrika's shoulders go tight, just the flat, pleased assessment of an engineer whose bridge has taken the truck without creaking.

"The borehole has no steel casing below thirty metres," Mornye said. "And thermal creep is a curve that follows the diurnal temperature. This is a straight line across seven months."

Her pencil moved down to the bottom of Sigrika's Appendix D, where the last line read: *Source: [ ].*

"Leave that bracket empty," Mornye said.

"I wasn't going to—"

"You are a student of Halvard's, and Halvard's students love a gloss," Mornye said crisply. "If you write a single noun inside that bracket before we have a short-baseline reading on the rim itself, I will have myself added to your examination board for the sole purpose of failing the paper."

"Yes, Professor."

"What we have is two bearings with four kilometres of glacier and fractured slate between the sensors and the basin," Mornye said. She reached to the second shelf of the Starstack and drew out a yellow triplicate permit with the Spacetrek Collective's compass stamped at the head. "To get depth to within a metre, I need a three-axis portable geophone on a tripod at the edge of the basin for six continuous hours. I cannot take a tripod down the Riseway, and the Collective's security cordon at the gate will not pass a technician without a faculty counter-signature and a reason."

She laid the yellow sheet on the blotter.

The boxes were already typed in. Under *Responsible Faculty*, `M. Mornye, Prof. (Exostrider Eng.)`, signed in black ink and dated the day before. Under *Field Operator*, `S. Sigrika, Cand. (Heliodic)`. Under *Purpose of Deployment*, `Calibration of instrument floor variance (Ref. App. D)`.

Sigrika looked at the date beside Mornye's signature, and then at Mornye.

Mornye did not blink. "It saves you coming back on Thursday to collect a signature. If you do not wish to go, there is correction fluid in the top drawer and I will send a post-doc who will set the tripod on a loose rock and ruin my baseline."

"I'll take it," Sigrika said, folding the yellow copy into her folder. "Can I draw the geophone from the store on Saturday?"

"No. The ground round that basin is pulverised slate over clay. While the surface is still above freezing, a tripod leg settles half a millimetre an hour and the tilt compensation eats the signal. Wait until the first hard frost locks the ground out on the plains. When the mud is iron, take it out."

Mornye pulled the fan-fold printouts back toward herself and squared their edges against the blotter with both palms.

"When I was twenty-two," she said to the paper, in the same flat register she had used for the borehole casing, "I brought four pages of harmonic variance on a thruster mount to a senior review panel. The chairman had a very clean cuff and did not look at the third page. He told me my hand had slipped on the vernier, and I went back to my room and threw the notebook in the bin, and two months later the mount cracked on the stand." She set a hex nut on the corner of the stack. "I dislike the word *drift*."

Before Sigrika could open her mouth, Mornye gave her wheel a quarter-turn toward the corkboard on the side wall, where seven buff-coloured data sheets were pinned in a straight line above the tea caddy, their corners soft from saddlebags and river damp.

"Look at the fourth one," Mornye said, her voice warming by three degrees. "Came in on the courier pouch this morning from the border post at the gorge. Tell me what she has written by the Tuesday discharge figure. My eyes are tired and her hand goes three millimetres high when she is furious."

Buddy slid off Sigrika's sleeve with a small chirrup as Sigrika stood up and leaned in to the corkboard.

The fourth sheet was gridded for river level against stated flow, filled in with a waterproof nib in a wind that had clearly tried to take the paper twice. Along the left margin, boxed off from the numbers with a ruler, ran four lines of minuscule script:

*Ministry gauge #14 sits in the lee of a stone abutment built by a magistrate who died three hundred years ago and understood nothing of fluid dynamics. True mid-stream velocity is 1.42 m/s, not 1.18. I have waded in to my knees with a weighted line to prove this, and the dog-eared girl with the tail is laughing at me from the bank, and I am entering both facts in the permanent record.*

And at the bottom corner, where a thumb had left a half-moon of axle grease or chili oil, a neat square drawn round the smudge and labelled: *Contamination, field (Lupa; unrepentant).*

Sigrika laughed out loud in the quiet office.

"She waded in," Sigrika said.

"In November," Mornye said, looking up at the pinned row with an expression she would never have permitted in a lecture hall. "To prove the Ministry wrong by eight percent. I wrote back at lunch and told her to check the weir at the third bend before the ice comes down. She will complain about it for ten minutes and do it on the Sunday."

By the third week of November the mattress had arrived in the rooms above the noodle shop, which meant Sigrika got the wooden chair and Lynae sat on the crate with a folded blanket under her jeans, and nobody had to brace their spine against the window frame while they played.

There was a second plate in the cupboard too, green melamine with a chip on the rim that matched the first one, and the air smelled of star anise, scorched scallions, and the new varnish on the bed slats. Downstairs, the noodle-shop man was banging his ladle against the stockpot rim at eight-minute intervals, which was how you could tell time in that flat without looking at a watch.

"Four," Sigrika said, holding her cards close to her chest so the light wouldn't show through the backs.

"You haven't looked at the trump suit yet."

"There are no trumps in Bid Nine."

"Look at you, learning the rules in week twelve." Lynae wrote *4* under the *S* column on the back of a butcher's wrapper and tossed her pencil onto the crate. "Two."

Sigrika played the hand with her tongue caught between her teeth. She led a king of clubs to clear the top, got undercut on the fourth trick when Lynae threw a four of diamonds instead of following a run she didn't have, and took six tricks in a row at the end because she was still holding three high hearts and couldn't bring herself to throw them under Lynae's ten.

She stared at the six neat piles in front of her knees.

"This deck has too many face cards in it," Sigrika said.

"It has twelve face cards in it, Siggy. Same as it had at seven o'clock."

"The jacks are sticking to the queens. When you riffle them, they travel in pairs."

"You shuffled this hand." Lynae added two penalty points to Sigrika's column, bringing her to nineteen against Lynae's three, and swept the cards together, the plastic bridge deck dividing across her knuckles in four crisp streams before squaring itself with a single tap against the wood. "And you did that weird Royan overhand thing where you look like you're churning butter. Cut."

Sigrika cut the deck, sulkily, leaving three cards on the bottom, and picked up her next nine.

They were terrible: a two, four middle spades, a lone jack of diamonds, and three clubs that didn't touch each other.

She looked at Lynae's face—relaxed, one knee hooked up against the crate, a smudge of chili oil on her thumb from the pork buns they'd had at seven—and then back at the jack of diamonds.

"Three," Sigrika said.

"Brave."

"Just bid."

"One," Lynae said, and led the ace of hearts.

Sigrika threw her two under it. On the second trick she took the lead with her jack, drew out Lynae's middle spades one by one until the eight and the nine were good, took those two, and then spent the last four tricks ducking under everything Lynae put down—throwing her seven of clubs under an eight, her six under a nine, her five under a ten, until her hand was empty and there were three tricks sitting by her saucer and not one card more.

She looked at the three piles. Then she looked at the score sheet.

"I got three," she said.

Lynae leaned over, counted the piles with one finger, looked at the *3* written in the bid column, and looked up at Sigrika.

"Huh," Lynae said. She wrote a clean *0* beside the three and picked up the deck. "Took you twelve weeks. Don't let it go to your head, you still can't count clubs."

"I counted every club in that hand," Sigrika said, sitting up straighter and reaching for her tea. "You were holding the queen back so I'd overtake on the seventh, and I saw your thumb twitch on the corner of it."

"My thumb did not twitch."

"You always move your thumb to the top edge when you're about to dump a queen on me. Deal."

Lynae dealt, grinning into her collar, and while Sigrika was sorting her suits by colour and rank—she still sorted them left to right like an index file, which took forty seconds every hand—Sigrika said, "Mornye signed a cordon pass for me yesterday. For the basin."

Lynae's hands finished the deal at the exact same speed—nine, nine, the remainder face down by the salt cellar—and rested on the crate.

"Yeah?" Lynae picked up her fan of cards. "What for?"

"Appendix D. The floor oscillation I told you about. She ran it against the South Weir sensor and it's coming from under borehole four, about sixty metres down. She wants a three-axis geophone on a tripod at the rim for six hours so we can triangulate the depth without the glacier in the way." Sigrika moved a four of hearts between a three and a six. "Three."

"Four," Lynae said, and led a small spade. "When?"

"After the first hard frost. The clay out there moves until it freezes. I'm going up to the shrine on Friday to ask Hiyuki if she'll walk out with me—the Collective checkpoint at the Riseway mouth won't hold up a miko, even with the cordon on."

"How long's the walk from the Heartwood shelf to the basin?"

"About four hours each way if the slate's dry," Sigrika said, taking the spade with a ten and leading back a club. "Faster coming back because you're going down the grade. Why?"

"Six hours on the tripod, eight hours walking, two hours on the Riseway," Lynae said, tossing a king on the club and sweeping the trick in. "That's sixteen hours in November. The eye in that tree dims at nineteen hundred, which means you're either camping on frozen slate at minus twelve or you're scrambling down thirty metres of wet rock in the dark with fourteen kilos of Mornye's brass on your back."

"The geophone only weighs eleven," Sigrika said defensively. "The battery pack's another three. And Hiyuki has the bike as far as the Heartwood."

"Hiyuki's bike has a single rear rack and no panniers, and the rear brake cable on the second bike under the dorm tarp has a frayed strand at the pinch bolt that I haven't swapped out yet," Lynae said, her voice dropping for a second into the flat, quick register that didn't belong to the arcade or the kitchen—the one that knew the weight of a battery pack before she'd seen the box. Then she caught herself, threw a two of diamonds onto the wood, and stretched her legs out under the crate until her boots bumped Sigrika's chair rung. "Plus you'll try to carry your whole library in that satchel and pull a shoulder on the chimney. You just went over, by the way. That's four tricks."

Sigrika looked down at the four piles by her saucer and scowled. "You threw the two on purpose!"

"It's called Bid Nine, Siggy, not Help Your Friends." Lynae tallied the one-point penalty with a flourish. "One more hand and then I've got to finish the shear-stress problems before midnight or Mornye's going to look at me with her clips."

They played the ninth hand—Sigrika bid two and made two, which she insisted counted as winning the evening on moral grounds even though the final score was twenty to six—and at twenty past nine she wound her scarf back round her throat while Lynae pulled on her jacket and pocketed her keys.

"You don't have to come down to the tram," Sigrika said on the landing. "You've got the shear-stress sheet."

"I need milk," Lynae said, rattling down the narrow wooden stairs beside the noodle shop's kitchen wall. "If Rebecca wakes up at six and there's no milk for the tea before Chiyo gets over, she puts that sweetened condensed stuff from the tin in it and the spoon stands up by itself."

"The dairy shuts at nine."

"The dispenser by the chemist's is open till eleven."

Out on the fourth terrace, the overhead lamps had dimmed to their night amber, throwing long striped shadows from the iron railings across the damp flagstones. The air had an edge to it—not the scheduled freeze yet, just the damp cellar chill coming up off the lower tiers as the ventilation cycled down.

They stood by the cast-iron stop post near the chemist's corner. Sigrika had her hands deep in her coat pockets, telling Lynae about Halvard's Sarnvik boundary stone and a long-dead field man named Sørensen who had gone out into a sleet storm without his spectacles and written down four lines that six generations of scholars had been fighting over ever since.

"He drew an *r* where there's a crack in the gneiss," Sigrika said, her breath making a small ghost under the lamp. "You can tell from the squeeze weight it's a natural cleavage plane, there's no chisel entry at the top at all, and three separate grammars have built an entire dative plural out of a frost crack."

"Academic fraud," Lynae said solemnly, leaning one shoulder against the post so she faced back up the terrace toward the noodle shop's lit sign and the steps to Tier Three. "Lock him up."

"He's been dead for sixty years."

"Dig him up, take his glasses away again."

Thirty yards up the terrace, an old man in a railwayman's cap was walking a terrier that stopped at every planter box. Nearer, under the awning of the closed baker's, two second-years from the engineering track were sharing a cigarette over a rolled tube of blueprints, their student badges clipped to their lapels. Across the rails, by the public telephone kiosk at the head of the Tier Three steps, a woman in a dark woollen coat with the collar turned up was flipping through the municipal directory chained to the shelf, her back to the light, a canvas book-bag slung over one shoulder.

The woman's boots were dry on the welt, though Maintenance had hosed the Tier Three steps at eight.

The 4B tram came round the bathhouse curve with its bell clanging twice and the pantograph spitting a little blue spark where the frost-grease met the wire. The doors concertinaed open with a hiss of warm pneumatic air.

"Wednesday?" Sigrika said from the top step, holding the brass rail.

"Wednesday. Bring that jam back if Rebecca hasn't eaten the jar."

The doors folded shut. Sigrika found a seat halfway down the car under the advertisement for third-tier overshoes, lifted one hand through the glass as the tram lurched forward, and had her notebook out on her knee before the car cleared the chemist's awning.

Lynae stayed on the kerb with her hands in her jacket pockets until the red tail-lamp swung behind the bathhouse buttress and the hum died out of the rails.

She didn't go to the milk dispenser.

She crossed the tracks at an angle, walking at the loose, unhurried lope of a teenager who has forgotten her coin purse, and passed within ten feet of the telephone kiosk at the head of the steps.

The directory was swinging gently on its brass chain. The kiosk was empty. Down the long stone flight to Tier Three, twenty lanterns burned in a straight descending row over wet granite, and nobody was on them.

Lynae went back across the terrace, took the noodle-shop stairs two at a time, and let herself into the flat without turning on the ceiling bulb. She unlatched the casement window half an inch to hear the street, leaned her forearms on the sill in the dark with the smell of pork broth and new pine slats behind her, and watched the fourth terrace until the last tram went through the loop at midnight and the overhead wire went dead.

On Thursday mornings the Tier Five fish market smelled of crushed ice, kelp, and the vinegar they used to scrub down the zinc counters between catches.

Rebecca walked in front with the wicker basket over her chrome forearm and Chiyo's shopping list held between two fingers like a summons. She had her pink hair tied up in two high tails to keep it out of the eel tubs and Lupa's old spare grease-rag tucked into her belt loop.

"Two bream," Rebecca read out as they dodged a handcart of salted jellyfish. "Medium. Clear eyes, red gills, and she underlined *under-crate* twice because she thinks I got rolled on the mackerel last week."

"The mackerel was fine," Sigrika said, carrying the string bag with the burdock root and a cabbage they'd bought at the turnstile.

"The left one was soft in the belly and Chiyo poked it with one finger and didn't say a word for twenty minutes. Worse than getting shot at." Rebecca planted her boots in front of the fourth stall, where a woman with forearms like hams and a yellow oilskin apron was shovelling flake ice over a row of silver-sided bream. "Morning, Brana."

Brana looked up from the shovel and grinned, showing a gold canine. "Back again, Little Chrome. Your old lady send a diagram this time?"

"She says your front row's been looking at the ceiling since Thursday. Let's see the box under the bench."

"Your old lady's going to put me in the poorhouse," Brana said amiably, and hauled a dripping wooden crate out from under the duckboards.

Rebecca leaned over the zinc, inspected four fish with the narrowed eyes of a diamond cutter, and tapped two with her metal knuckle. "Those. And give us the knife, you always hack through the collar bone and Chiyo uses the heads for stock."

Brana laughed, wiped the heavy curved gutting knife on her thigh, and slid it across the wet zinc handle-first.

Rebecca didn't even set the basket down. She shifted it to the crook of her elbow, pinned the first bream behind the head with two steel fingers of her left hand so it couldn't slip on the slime, and took the blade under the gill-plate with her right—one short, clean turn of the wrist that lifted the collar whole and brought the guts out in a single twist into the offal bucket under the counter. She scaled both sides in twelve brisk scrapes against the tail, rinsed the cavity under the brass tap, and did the second one in forty seconds flat without getting a single scale above her wrist.

"Where'd you learn to turn a collar like that?" Sigrika asked, genuinely impressed, as Brana wrapped the two fish and the heads in thick brown butcher's paper.

"My brother went on a protein kick one summer when he was trying to bulk up for a crew job," Rebecca said, wiping the knife blade clean on her rag and handing it back handle-first. "Bought a whole crate of farmed vat-carp off a guy in Santo Domingo because they were cheap. Didn't occur to him they still had the insides in 'em until he opened the box in the kitchen and nearly passed out. Somebody had to clean forty fish before the cooling unit died."

Three stalls down, past the tubs of pickled radish, the sweet-seller had his glass domes lined up along a scrubbed pine counter: salted plums from the coast, dried persimmons dusted with white sugar, candied lotus root, and along the back riser, twenty tall apothecary jars of boiled sweets in every shade of amber and green.

Sigrika stopped to pay for the half-pound of pickled ginger on Chiyo's list, and the vendor—an old man in a knitted waistcoat who knew her from the courier runs—pushed a small saucer of dried sour plums toward her with a wooden pick.

"Try the new batch," he said. "Came down from the terraces Tuesday. More salt in the cure this year."

Sigrika thanked him and took one on the pick just as another customer stepped up to the pine counter beside her.

She was a young woman of perhaps twenty-two or twenty-three, with a flat wicker basket hooked over her left arm in which a dozen dark purple figs lay nested on their own broad leaves. She wore a plain grey shawl over a dark dress, no badge, her hair braided low and pinned at the nape, and she moved with the unhurried, pleasant attention of someone who had nowhere she needed to be before dark.

"Good morning," she said to the vendor, looking up at the top shelf behind his head where the square glass jars sat untouched under a film of sugar dust. "The yellow-green ones at the end, by the ginger drops. Are those the double-sour?"

The vendor glanced back over his shoulder. "The lemon-acid ones? Aye, miss. Citric inside and out. I don't sell four ounces of those a month except to the schoolboys when they're playing dares on the tram."

"That sounds promising," the woman said cheerfully. She turned her head to Sigrika, smiling as easily as if they had been walking down the aisle together. "Would you mind terribly trying one for me? I was given very strict instructions about the sort to buy, and I was told there's a specific face they make if they're the right ones."

The vendor reached up, unstoppered the heavy jar with a dry squeak of ground glass, and dropped two pale, sugar-frosted ovals onto the scale pan. He handed one across to Sigrika on a square of wax paper.

"Go on, then," Rebecca said, leaning an elbow on the counter with the fish parcel under her arm. "Let's see the face."

Sigrika, who had never known how to refuse a polite request in a shop, put the sweet in her mouth.

For the space of three seconds it was perfectly ordinary—a dry dusting of cane sugar and lemon oil on the tongue. She was just opening her mouth to say it wasn't nearly as bad as the ones President Lucilla kept in her desk drawer when the outer shell gave way, and the second layer underneath it—pure, unbuffered tartaric and lemon-rind acid—hit the back of her jaw all at once.

Her teeth clicked shut. Her shoulders came up an inch, her mouth pulled into a tight sideways knot, and her left eye squeezed completely closed while her right watered into the market lights.

The young woman with the figs clapped her palm lightly against the handle of her basket, delighted.

"Oh, splendid," she said. "The left eye goes first. That's the exact one—thank you, you've saved me a tremendous amount of trouble." She turned back to the vendor and tapped the counter with a silver piece. "I'll take the jar, please."

The vendor blinked at her over his spectacles. "How many ounces, miss?"

"All of it," she said. "You don't need to weigh them, just tip the lot into a double bag so I don't have to carry the glass up the steps."

"There's near two kilos in here."

"That's quite all right," the woman said, counting three more coins onto the pine with neat, unhurried fingers while the sweets rattled into a heavy brown sack like gravel down a chute. "They're for someone at home, and she's going to be very rude about them."

She tucked the fat paper sack into the wicker basket beside the figs, gave Sigrika a warm, tidy nod—"Drink something cold, it cuts the rind"—and stepped backward into the Thursday crush between two women carrying a roll of oilcloth and a boy with a crate of live ducks on his shoulder.

Sigrika got the sweet wedged safely into her cheek, swallowed hard against the pucker, and wiped her right eye with the back of her wrist.

Rebecca was staring into the crowd by the eel tubs, where the grey shawl was already gone behind the oilcloth.

"Man," Rebecca said, shifting the fish parcel to her other hip. "I liked her. Who buys four pounds of face-melters on a Thursday morning? Think she's poisoning her landlord?"

"My back teeth feel like somebody rubbed chalk on them," Sigrika said, taking the packet of pickled ginger from the vendor and dropping it into the string bag on top of the cabbage without looking round at the aisle. "Come on, if we miss the ten-twenty incline we'll have to carry the cabbage up the steps."

They took the incline to the fourth terrace and went into the dormitory block by the east fire door so they wouldn't have to stand in the small lift smelling of sea bream.

On the landing between the ground floor and the first, the cream plaster of the emergency-procedures wall was almost entirely covered now. Rebecca set the basket down by the fire-hose reel, fished the black marker out of her pocket, and pointed at the upper right quadrant above the handrail, where the paint was freshest.

She had been adding the household as they looked on the long-range Terminal calls from Xuanfang Hold.

There was Lupa sitting on an ammunition crate with a soup ladle and her tail drawn like a fuzzy baseball bat; Iuno perched on a parapet with a clipboard and her hair blowing the wrong way; Chisa standing straight up and down beside a truck wheel that had seven spokes on one side and four on the other; and behind them, standing a little apart in black-and-blue outline, a girl with a long braid and a crown of feathers coming straight out of where her ears should have been.

"Added the feathers girl after Tuesday's call," Rebecca said, uncapping the black marker with her thumb to thicken a line on the truck's bumper. "Amy carried the screen out to the courtyard while she was eating a bun so I could get the coat right. Nailed the feathers."

Sigrika tilted her head, shifting the cabbage bag. "Are those feathers or celery, Becca?"

"It's a laurel. Or a crest. Whatever she's got on her head when she gets tall." Rebecca added three more jagged spikes to the girl's left ear. "Celery's got good lines anyway. Don't be a critic."

At the height of Sigrika's own shoulder, between the shovel-jawed man and the three-legged dog, her two uneven green rectangles with the scatter of vertical lines inside them sat untouched, the small square for the salt packet between them. Nobody had drawn over them, though Rebecca's border of barbed wire and stars had come right up to the edge and detoured cleanly round the top.

Rebecca capped the marker, dropped it back into her jacket pocket, and bent to pick up the fish.

"Hey," she said to the wall, not turning her head. "You ever use that white one I threw you?"

"Once," Sigrika said.

"Yeah? Good." Rebecca straightened up with the basket on her chrome arm and kicked the fire door open with her boot heel. "Make sure you click the cap all the way down till it snaps. White's got chalk or titanium or whatever in the mix, dries out twice as fast as the colours if you leave air in it."

"It's snapped down," Sigrika said, and followed her up the stairs.

On Friday evening the wind off the glacier had teeth in it.

The ridge path above the Academy dome was dry and hard underfoot, the gravel locked into the mud by the cold air spilling down from the ventilation louvres where the artificial sky met the mountain. Sigrika came up the last switchback at twilight with her woollen hat pulled down over her ears and her breath going out ahead of her in long grey plumes.

At the top, the shrine's wooden gate stood open to the terrace. Below the stone parapet, the glacier ran out into the purple dusk, the great snow-buried shoulders of the kneeling god catching the last salt-white gleam from the lattice where ten thousand crystals hung without clinking in the still air.

Sigrika stepped over the threshold stone—keeping her boot clear of the worn join at the right-hand edge out of six months of habit—and took the maintenance broom from its corner behind the gatepost.

The birch handle was polished smooth now where three or four different hands held it every week, and someone—probably Lucilla's orderly, or one of the third-year astronomy students who came up here to use the clear horizon—had bound a loose split in the ferrule with copper wire. Sigrika swept the terrace from the water basin to the steps in long, overlapping arcs, clearing the frost-killed pine needles and the grey slate grit that blew down from the ridge, working the corner behind the lantern base twice until the stone showed clean.

Under the open eaves of the hall, where a single oil lamp burned behind paper panes, Hiyuki sat on the low cedar bench with a leather apron over her lap, a short curved whittling knife in her right hand, and a block of yellow pine in her left. Around her sandals lay a drift of pale wood curls that smelled of resin.

Sigrika set the broom back behind the gatepost, beat the dust out of her mittens against her thigh, and walked over to the steps.

"Candidate," Hiyuki said.

"There wasn't much on the step tonight," Sigrika said. "Mostly needles from the top windbreak."

"The boy with the telescope swept on Wednesday," Hiyuki said. She blew a curl of sawdust off the pine block and held it out on her flat palm into the yellow light of the lamp, her head tilted a fraction, waiting. "Look."

Sigrika leaned in over the step.

The carving was about four inches long. It had a heavy, square-cut body that still remembered being a two-by-two batten, two legs whittled down to the thickness of matchsticks that entered the belly at slightly different angles, and a long flat wedge projecting backward from the crown of the head like the handle of a shoe-horn.

Sigrika, who had scored ninety-eight on the boreal waterfowl practicum in her first year and kept three regional keys under her bed, looked at the wedge, looked at Hiyuki's serene red eyes, and did three seconds of rapid emergency taxonomy.

"It's a northern shoveler," Sigrika said warmly. "Or a spoonbill! When they're roosting on an estuary bank and they tuck the bill back into the scapulars to keep the heat in."

Hiyuki looked at the block of pine on her palm. Then she looked up at Sigrika, the small beauty mark beneath her left eye very distinct in the lamplight.

"That is the crest," Hiyuki said. "The beak is on the other side. It is a heron."

"Oh," Sigrika said, looking closely at the front end, where a small V-shaped notch had been nicked out of the grain. "Yes. Now that you turn it—the neck is tucked in the S-curve. Like when they're wading."

"There was a knot in the wood where the neck should have been extended," Hiyuki said, turning the bird back toward herself and smoothing the flat shoe-horn crest with her thumb. "A carver works with the timber she is given. The posture is alert."

"Very alert," Sigrika agreed earnestly.

Hiyuki set the heron down on the corner of the tray beside the iron kettle—upright, though she had to prop its left matchstick leg against a saucer so it wouldn't list into the tea-cloth—and looked at Sigrika's satchel.

"Do you have your book?"

Sigrika unbuckled the flap and drew out her field journal. Hiyuki held out her hand for it, opened it carefully past the blue and red pages to the gridded census sheets at the back, and drew the long silver hairpin from the twist at the nape of her neck.

She dipped the pointed tip of the pin into the small stone well of black ink on the writing tray. With her left sleeve held back by her right wrist in the manner of a clerk at a register, she drew on the fourth blank line of the census page: two vertical strokes for legs, an oval with a flat back, and a single bold line sweeping off the head at forty-five degrees. Beside it, in tiny, architectural characters, she wrote *Grey heron (roosting)* and the date.

She blew on the ink once, wiped the point of the hairpin clean on a square of tissue before sliding it back into her hair, and handed the journal across.

"Thank you," Sigrika said, holding the page open to the cold air for ten seconds so the heavy downstroke on the crest wouldn't offset against the opposite leaf.

Hiyuki poured hot water from the iron kettle into a cooling pitcher, waited while the steam rose and thinned against the eaves, and then filled two dark clay bowls. She set one on the step at Sigrika's knee and took the other herself.

Around Hiyuki's sandals, the thin rime of frost that had formed on the cedar boards while she was whittling had gone dark and wet at the edges.

They drank in the quiet. Down on the avenue below the ridge, the lamps were coming on in long amber chains through the bare birch branches.

"It has been three weeks," Hiyuki said to her bowl.

Sigrika looked up over the rim of hers. "Since?"

"Since you asked me for a sheet of paper."

Sigrika's fingers tightened slightly on the warm clay. In the inner pocket of her satchel, between two pieces of stiff cardboard, the first grey sheet Hiyuki had written and sealed for her in the spring was still sitting flat and dry.

"I haven't needed one this week," Sigrika said.

"Mm," Hiyuki said, and drank her tea, and left the space around the sentence completely empty.

Above them, on the short beam cut low for children's hands, the bronze bell hung motionless in the lee of the roof. Neither of them looked at it.

Sigrika set her empty bowl on the tray, reached into her coat pocket, and took out a folded slip of tracing paper.

"I went through the Survey Office's basement ledgers on Wednesday," she said, smoothing the paper on her knee. "The haulage logs from eighty-four, when the Collective graded the upper Riseway and cut the drainage culverts."

Hiyuki tilted her head, interested. "The threshold stone?"

"Item one-one-nine in the contractor's daybook," Sigrika said, her voice picking up the quick, pleased rhythm it had in the library when a footnote turned out to be real. "They paid the blasting crews by weight for anything over two hundred kilos they cleared off the verge. *One dressed greywacke slab, inscribed, lifted from the old boundary dyke at kilometre fourteen-point-two; sent up on the timber sleds as roadbed ballast.* Somebody at the top needed a flat step and took it off the sled."

She turned the tracing paper so Hiyuki could see the second line copied in blue ink.

"And the surveyor's margin note right underneath it says: *Pair stone sighted forty metres south-southwest in the old fissure scree; not recovered due to slip hazard.*" Sigrika looked up, her eyes bright in the lamp-glow. "Kilometre fourteen-point-two is the ridge above Borehole Four. Where the basin is. When the dyke collapsed in the first subsidence, the far stone slid down into the scree at the edge of the basin, and the road crew just left it there."

Hiyuki looked down the terrace toward the gate, where the worn greywacke slab lay flat between the posts, its two shallow continuation strokes waiting at the broken right-hand edge.

"Then the rest of the sentence is by the water," Hiyuki said.

"Forty metres south-southwest of the old culvert marker, if the scree didn't go under when the basin filled," Sigrika said. She folded the tracing paper back into its square and put her chin at the angle. "Professor Mornye needs a six-hour reading on the basin rim with a portable geophone. To triangulate the floor signal from Appendix D. She signed the Collective transit permit on Tuesday, with me as the field operator, for after the first hard frost."

Hiyuki's expression did not change. "The cordon guard at the Riseway mouth has been doubled since October."

"I know. They won't pass a student alone even with Mornye's seal." Sigrika put her hands flat on her knees. "Will you walk out with me?"

"Yes," Hiyuki said, as though she were agreeing that it was Friday. She picked up the teapot and poured the last of the cooled water into Sigrika's bowl. "The ground out past the Heartwood will be iron by Thursday if the surface frost holds. We will leave at six. Bring the tall boots with the felt liners, not the ones you are wearing, and pack enough flatbread for three."

Sigrika paused with the bowl halfway to her mouth. "Three?"

"And tell Lynae," Hiyuki added mildly, picking up her pine heron to check whether the matchstick legs had dried level, "that if she intends to bring her motorcycle down the Riseway, I have heard it on the ridge road at night all autumn, it squeals on every downhill bend, and I would prefer not to listen to it for forty kilometres."

"I haven't asked Lynae to come," Sigrika said.

"You will not need to," Hiyuki said.

Sigrika drank the last of the tea, helped carry the tray and the two bowls inside to the stone sink—rinsing them in the cold tap water without a word or a hum, drying them on the linen cloth, and setting them upside down on the rack—and went back down the ridge path at seven, when the dome overhead clicked into its deep-night indigo and the first frost needles began to silver the gravel at the edge of the trees.

At two in the morning the radiator under Sigrika's window gave its hourly iron thump as the dormitory boiler cycled down to the night pressure.

Her desk lamp was turned down to the lowest notch, throwing a flat amber circle across the blotter, the little stoneware jar of her mother's berry jam she had brought up for toast, the framed photograph under the flowering ash, and her three pens—red, blue, green—lying parallel to the edge of her closed notebook.

She was sitting cross-legged on the chair with her woollen shawl wrapped twice round her shoulders and the datapad propped against her shins, working through the Friday night moderation queue on the student board.

There were seven flags. Five of them were in `[GENERAL]`, where a forty-post argument had broken out at midnight over whether the third-tier canteen had replaced the egg in the Friday custard with maize starch, culminating in a second-year from Materials calling a girl in Runework an *uncalibrated ladle* and getting reported three times for abuse. Sigrika locked the sub-thread, added a one-line moderator note (*It has been maize starch since the supply consolidation in May; go to sleep*), and cleared all five flags with one swipe of her thumb.

The sixth flag was in `[INDUCTION & FIRST-YEARS]`, on a thread titled **is the snow notice at the exchange a prank??** started by an eighteen-year-old from the southern atolls who wanted to know whether Dome Maintenance genuinely dropped frozen water out of the roof trusses at seven in the evening and if he needed to bring his laundry in off the balcony. Three fourth-years were in the replies telling him he had to apply to Window Three for a municipal snow-helmet before Wednesday or pay a fine.

Sigrika deleted the helmet replies, pasted the standard link to the Weather Office's precipitation-baffle diagram, and marked the report resolved.

Before she closed the browser, she tapped the pinned link at the top of the index: **[MEGATHREAD] The Dimmr lake — what actually happened**.

The thread had gone quiet over the second half of the term—two or three posts a week, mostly hydrology students arguing about aquifer pressure and whether the Collective's committee would ever declassify the September core samples.

There were three new posts at the bottom, timestamped twenty minutes ago.

Sigrika read the three posts through. She scrolled back up four lines, read the first one a second time, and rested her thumb on the bezel of the screen.

She did not open the reply box. She switched the datapad off, set it flat beside the blotter, and opened her journal to the blue cross-reference pages near the back, below *§414 — see also: Amma* and the four-figure number from the kitchen whiteboard.

She uncapped the blue pen and wrote, in her neat, small hand:

*19 Nov. Borehole 4 basin: first surface ice reported (road crew via >>hraft). Centre open, ~10m dia.*

She let the ink dry for ten seconds, closed the journal, and laid the blue pen back beside the red and the green, aligning the clips with the tip of her index finger until all three sat flush.

Then she stood up, reached into the deep pocket of her coat hanging on the back of the door, and took out the Dream Maker.

Under the desk lamp she checked the little blob of grey solder Ossa had laid under the amber side of the rocker switch, wiped a speck of pocket wool off the plastic ring, and glanced at the tinted reservoir bulb to make sure the generic solution hadn't clouded in the cold from the ridge walk.

She clicked the lamp off, climbed under the quilt with her woollen socks still on, and laid the wand on the second pillow beside her ear, pressing the blue play switch down with her thumb because a membrane goes stiff if you leave it in a drawer all winter.

The small speaker came alive in the dark—a low, unhurried sound under the cotton of the pillowcase, slowing a fraction at the turn of its phrase and picking up again on the other side.

She pulled the quilt up to her chin and looked across the foot of the bed at the uncurtained window.

Outside, the Academy's engineered night held at its winter indigo above the bare crowns of the Solistrees, five days short of the posted snow, while along the bottom aluminium sill of the casement a single thin bead of condensation gathered in the chill from the glass and held there without freezing, and under the floorboards the pipes gave one last small click and went quiet until morning.

# Even in Another Time
### A Story of the Last Miko of Flaming Sakura
*Perfectly Stable · The Spent Wish · The Blessing of Roads*

---

## I. Perfectly Stable

It seems increasingly likely that I shall undertake the journey that has been occupying my thoughts these past several days. A journey without office or errand; undertaken alone, in good weather, by roads of my own choosing; with no threat at the far end of it to be neutralized and no one at the near end of it to be saved. I am aware of how the sentence reads. I have read it back several times myself, the way one rereads a diagnosis, checking for the error. There is no error. That is, I believe, the remarkable thing.

The idea did not arrive all at once. Ideas of consequence rarely do; they accumulate, the way frost accumulates, one crystal presuming upon the next until a window that was clear at dusk is opaque by morning and no single moment can be blamed. But if I were pressed to name a beginning — and I have spent a century being pressed to name things, wishes mostly, so I am practiced — I would say it began eleven days after my return from the place where endings are kept, when I noticed that a road atlas of the surface continent had appeared on the low table in the President's office, misfiled among the funding reports, open to the eastern coast.

Lucilla does not misfile things. Lucilla has never misfiled anything in the thirty years of our acquaintance. She once located, in under a minute, a requisition form I had submitted eleven years prior, for the sole purpose of proving that I had, on one occasion, misspelled the word *February*. The atlas was not misfiled. The atlas was a hinge, and she was oiling it, and she did not once look at it or at me while I looked at it, which is how I knew.

But I am getting ahead of the road. To explain how a woman of my office comes to contemplate such a journey — to explain, indeed, how she comes to write an account of herself at all, which is not a habit of the office; the office listens — I must first give some account of the weeks since my return.

They began, as my weeks have always begun, with instruments.

Dr. Herssen ran them himself, in the quiet of the medical wing, while down the corridor a girl who had been legally dead for ten years argued with a wolf about the ethics of stolen toast. He is a careful man, Luuk Herssen, with the white gloves and the golden wound beneath them, and he read the panels twice before he spoke.

"Well," he said. "You walked unshielded through the Nihility and back, twice, and according to every metric this Academy possesses, you are perfectly stable."

"Yes," I said. "The instruments have always been fond of me."

He looked at me over his glasses then, and I understood that he had heard the sentence underneath the sentence, because men who hide wounds in gloves generally do. He said nothing further. He is a careful man. But I record the exchange because it was, I believe, the first time in a hundred years that the verdict *perfectly stable* struck me as a description of the instruments rather than a description of myself, and something in me that had stood at attention for a century was permitted, briefly, to stand at ease.

There was a great deal of business after that. I will not pretend I have patience for the recounting of committees, but three moments from those days seem worth setting down, if only because I did not understand at the time that they were rehearsals.

The first was in Professor Mornye's recovery room, where the President and the doctor and I sat working out how to keep the children out of the Collective's laboratories. It was proposed that my walk on the frost road be made the bargaining piece; it was objected that the board would want the reasons for my presence in the void, undocumented as it was; and I heard myself say, before I had decided to say it: *Tell them I went to grieve.*

It was true. That was the strange part. I have spent a lifetime constructing alibis of granite, and here the truest sentence I owned turned out to be the most impenetrable armor in the room. A board full of pragmatists finds grief far less threatening than strategy; people always underestimate what a woman will do alone, in the cold, for the dead. I said as much, dryly, and set a badly proportioned wooden heron on the professor's side table by way of punctuation, and it was only later — much later, on the terrace, with the lattice half-built — that the discomfort of it reached me. To wear the truth as a mask is still to wear a mask. I resolved that there should someday be a room in which I could say *I went to grieve* and mean nothing tactical by it at all. I did not yet know that I was describing a road.

The second moment was in the corridor outside the boardroom, when the child threw her arms around me.

I say *the child*. Her name is Amy. She is nineteen, or twenty-nine, depending on how one counts a decade spent as a candle in a sealed room, and she crossed the corridor without hesitation and embraced me in front of her whole pack, and I stood in her arms with the particular stillness of a woman being handed something she has no shelf for.

"You are solid," I told her, when my hand had remembered what backs are for. "Good."

"Thanks for asking the right question in there," she said, into my shoulder.

She meant the void. She meant the moment I knelt to her inside the Threnodian's chest and asked her — not the mission, not the mathematics, not the ten thousand borrowed voices — but her, the girl beneath the archetype: *What is your wish?* It is the oldest question of my office. I have asked it of the dead forty-one times in a day. I had never before asked it of someone in time for the answer to matter, and she had said *I want to go home*, the simplest wish in the universe, and I had built her a road out of every wish I could not grant and watched her walk it back into her mother's arms.

I mention this because of what it did to the reliquary. I carry, behind my sternum, in a place the instruments have never once located, the crystallized regrets of a city — ten thousand reaches across distances that were never crossed. For a hundred years they weighed what stones weigh. Since the void they weigh what books weigh, which is to say: they weigh the same, and differently, because a library is not a burden to the woman who has finally learned to read.

The third moment was in the boardroom itself, at the end, when the children were safe and the President's chair was safe and the whole exhausted architecture of the compromise was settling into place, and the pack began, in the way of that pack, to plan a wedding that neither of its principals had agreed to. Someone proposed an officiant. Someone proposed acoustics.

"I can provide ceremonial frost arrangements," I said. "It is traditional."

There was a silence of perhaps one second, during which seven people looked at me, and I realized, somewhat to my own astonishment, that I had made a joke. Not a blade wrapped in courtesy, which is my usual export. A joke. The wolf-girl's ears came forward. The bard laughed outright. And I stood there in my hundred years and my composure and felt the small, absurd, unfamiliar warmth of having contributed a light thing to a room, on purpose, for no reason except that the room was mine to be light in.

The instruments, I am confident, registered nothing.

## II. A Shrine Without a Keeper

The wishes came out of the void with me.

I do not mean this figuratively. When the frost road dissolved behind us, its material did not: ten thousand regrets, spent as paving stones, returned to my hands as crystal — small, salt-white, faintly warm, each one a festival lantern never lit or a letter never sent or a confession swallowed whole. I carried them back to the Academy in my sleeves and my sash and a borrowed specimen case of Professor Mornye's, and for a week they sat in my quarters in careful rows while I considered what one owes to the currency of the dead after it has purchased something.

The answer, of course, was a shrine. I have known how to build only one thing in my life.

Lucilla granted me a disused observation terrace on the ridge above the Dimmr Plains road, facing the glacier where the god kneels. She approved the requisition without reading it, which is her way of reading it, and appended a note in her own hand: *No incense. The ventilation committee will revolt.* I burned incense on the first evening regardless. Some revolts are owed to the dead.

I should describe what I built, because it is not what I was raised to build. The Shrine of Flaming Sakura, where I was girl and novice and finally the last of my line, was a keeping-place: walls, a treasury, an inner sanctum, a Miko. It was a shrine designed around a keeper the way a lock is designed around a key, and when the keeper walked into the dark — as the keepers of my line have always, always walked — the shrine stood at the top of its stairs with a swept courtyard and a closed door, waiting for someone who did not come back, or came back changed, or came back a hundred years later carrying everything.

I built the opposite. A dry-stone lattice, open to the manufactured sky, with the ten thousand crystals set into it the way stars are set into a night — no glass over them, no rail before them, nothing between a visitor's hand and the regret it might choose to hold a moment and put back. A gate with no door. A bell that anyone may ring; I hung it low enough for children. No treasury, no lodge, no keeper's quarters.

Professor Mornye, who came up during our data sessions to take ambient readings, surveyed the whole arrangement from her hoverchair and delivered her verdict with the flat precision I have come to rely upon.

"There is no security infrastructure."

"No."

"The crystals can be touched. Removed, theoretically."

"Yes."

"And the maintenance schedule?"

"Whoever is standing nearest."

She considered this for a long moment, the little amber creature on her shoulder pulsing like a slow heartbeat. "Inefficient," she said at last.

"Yes," I agreed. "That is the design flaw the last hundred years taught me to install."

She did not smile — the professor's face is not built for it, and I respect a face that declines to perform — but she made a note on her Starstack, and I saw the file name before she closed it: *distributed load-bearing, ref: relay floor.* She was thinking of the proof carried across dozens of merely human minds. So was I. A thing kept by one person is a hostage. A thing kept by everyone is a culture. I have been a hostage-taker of memory my whole life, holding the dead of a city inside a single sternum and calling it devotion, and every year the ransom compounded, and there was no one to pay it because I had made sure there was no one. The shrine on the terrace is my restitution. Let the regrets of Honami be public domain.

There is a plaque. I argued with myself for some days over the wording, and in the end wrote nothing of gods or Laments or the Nihility. It reads:

*For everyone who walked into the dark and chose, anyway, to keep reaching. Someone remembers you. Ring the bell if you wish to be one of the someones.*

The bell rings, some days, a dozen times. Students, mostly. Royans down from the plains camps. Once, very early, before the light cycle had properly warmed, a visitor I did not recognize and could not afterward describe — a figure at the lattice with the stillness of someone reading rather than mourning, wearing an expression I had seen exactly once before, in a very small frame buried very deep in a great deal of ice: the expression of someone practicing being glad to see someone. I did not approach. The office has taught me when a visitor wishes to be received and when a visitor wishes merely to establish that the door exists. When I looked again the terrace was empty, and the air smelled of nothing at all, which is a scent I have encountered only once, and the bell was swinging very slightly, though I had heard no ring.

The dead of this city take walk-ins. It seems the shrine does too. I have decided not to mind.

## III. The Girl from Honami

Chisa came up on the third morning of construction, carrying a broom.

"I was told there would be sweeping," she said.

I had told no one any such thing. I looked at the broom, and at her, and at the red string on her wrist catching the early amber, and stood aside.

We worked for two hours without speaking, which I offer as evidence that she is from Honami, because no one else in this cheerful buried city can be silent for two hours without checking whether the silence is broken. She swept the way the courtyards of my childhood were swept — long strokes, with the grain of the stone, renewing the previous stroke rather than erasing it — and I set crystal into the lattice, and the god knelt on his glacier below us, patient as weather, and it was the most companionable morning I had spent in some decades.

We took tea afterward, on the terrace steps. I brewed it the way I brew everything, with the water cooled first, and she watched the ritual of it and said nothing until the first cup was poured.

"炎の桜の巫女様," she began.

"Hiyuki will do," I said. "The office is not hiring."

Her mouth did something small at that — not a smile; Chisa's expressions are like her scissors, employed only where a cut is wanted — and she inclined her head and drank her tea, and after a while she said, to the glacier rather than to me: "The magnolia outside our library. In Honami. The big one, by the printers' quarter. Did it survive?"

I had walked the printers' quarter the year the city returned. I had heard an old man there rehearse an apology to a son forty years gone, eleven words polished featureless, and I had sat on the step and received them as the son would have, and he had passed lighter than any phantom I had ever carried. I had not looked at the trees. It is a strange confession from a woman of my office, but I record it plainly: I catalogued that city's dead for a year and could not tell you the condition of a single living thing in it.

"I don't know," I said.

She nodded, as though this were an acceptable answer, which it was not, and refilled my cup, which in her country and mine is a sentence.

"The pudding stand," she said then. "By the east gate. My mother took me on festival days. The line went around the fish market. She said the wait was the flavor."

The tea in my hands did not tremble, because I did not permit it, and then — this is new; I set it down so that I will remember it is possible — I permitted it, and it did.

"You queued there," I said.

"Every year until we left. The year of the repairs — the awning caught fire, a lantern, nothing serious — they reopened with commemorative spoons for the first hundred customers. My mother still has ours." A pause, the length of a stitch. "She kept it through everything. She says a spoon that survives an emigration is family."

I have carried, for thirty years, a regret concerning that stand: a plan for pudding, a sister, a season of sakura that never came, a *next week* spoken with the confidence of two women who guarded a country. I had believed the regret to be complete — surveyed, weighed, filed, mine. And here was a girl of that city, alive, drinking my tea on a terrace under a false sky, adding to it a detail I could never have carried because I had never known it. The line around the fish market. The spoons. The awning fire, *nothing serious.* My city, which I have known for a century as a graveyard and a duty, existing in her as a queue and a flavor and a mother's kept spoon.

This is what a reader forgets, holding the whole book behind her sternum: that the book had other readers. That her copy is not the only copy. That the story survives in editions she has never opened, annotated in hands she will never carry, and that this is not a dilution of the memory but the memory's only real safety. I have been guarding the last copy of Honami for thirty years. There is no last copy. There was never a last copy. There is a spoon in a drawer in the Phosphor District, and it outranks me.

"They will reopen it," I said. "The stand. The survey camps are becoming a town; the east gate district resettles first. When it opens, there will be a queue."

"There will be spoons," Chisa said, with the certainty of scripture.

"I had planned," I said, "to arrive early. To avoid the line."

She looked at me then — red eyes to red eyes; we are, I believe, the only two people under this sky who understand what that particular coloring costs to carry through a childhood — and said nothing, and let the plan stand in the air between us until I could hear how it sounded.

"I have changed my mind," I said. "The wait, I am given to understand, is the flavor."

Before she left, she went to the lattice, and stood before it a while, and untied the red string from her wrist — I confess my breath behaved irregularly; I know a little of what that string has anchored — and knotted a short length of it, cut clean with the vintage scissors, around one of the crystals near the base. She did not say which regret she had chosen, or whether she had chosen at all, or for whom the string was tied. I did not ask. There is a name I could guess — a girl who became a road so that others could walk it — but the office has taught me that some offerings are conversations between two parties only, and the keeper's whole art is to be a third wall.

Except that there is no keeper here. There is only whoever is standing nearest. That morning, it was me, and I stood near, and that was the entire liturgy, and it was enough.

"Who tends it," she asked at the gate, "when you're gone?"

She did not say *gone* the way people say it to the old. She said it the way people from Honami say it — factually, having seen a whole city be gone and come back.

"Whoever is standing nearest," I said again.

She considered the terrace, and the bell hung low for children, and the ten thousand crystals with her single red knot among them.

"Then it will be tended," she said, and went down the ridge path with her broom over her shoulder, and I record here, formally, because the office trained me to make records: the vow stands. There will never be another Miko of Flaming Sakura. But I understand the vow correctly now, and it is not what I made it in the ash. It ends the lineage of women who carry alone. It was never permitted to end the carrying — the carrying belongs to everyone; the dead saw to that with their ten thousand identical instructions — and it was certainly never permitted to end me. *Keep the vow. Lose the reason.* I have. The reason is lost, and I find I do not miss it, and the shrine has no keeper, and the shrine is kept.

## IV. Lessons in a Minor Art

Amy found me on the terrace on a rest-day afternoon, carrying a block of pale wood the size of a loaf and an expression of premeditated innocence.

"Lucilla has a heron," she announced, "with a hairpin. I've seen it. It's on her desk next to the candy. I want a penguin."

"I carve herons."

"You carve blobs with beaks," she said, settling cross-legged on the step beside me with the boneless authority of the young. "One of them can be a penguin. Penguins are just herons that gave up."

I gave her the spare knife — I have carried a spare knife for a hundred years and never once considered why — and set her a block, and we carved.

She was terrible. I record this with more pleasure than is perhaps dignified. She was worse than I am, which I had not understood to be achievable; her penguin listed like a ship taking water, and one wing — penguins, she informed me, have wings, they simply use them for other purposes, *like some people I could mention* — detached entirely within the first hour and had to be reconceived as a separate, smaller penguin. And she was happy. She swore at the wood cheerfully and blew shavings off her knees and held each catastrophe up to the light for assessment, and I understood, watching her, why I have kept at this one art for a century without improving.

Everything else I have ever touched, I mastered, because mastery was load-bearing. A Miko who errs is a city that grieves; I removed error from myself the way one removes a rib. But the wood asks nothing. The wood forgives everything. A heron with its wings set hopelessly askew is not a phantom uncarried or a barrier fallen; it is only a bad heron, and the world's supply of consequences declines to attend. For a hundred years, forty minutes at a time, carving badly has been the only room in my life where I was permitted to be a person of no importance. I had thought it a private vice. Watching the child list her penguin against the horizon and pronounce it *nautical*, I revised the entry: it is a discipline, and she was born knowing it, and it took me a century to steal what she does for free.

We worked a while. The god knelt below us. Her hands, I noticed, never fully stopped moving even at rest, as though the body were still confirming, joint by joint, its own jurisdiction.

"Hey," she said eventually, in the too-light voice the young use for heavy freight. "In there. When you rang it for me. Iuno says it costs you. The bell. Lifespan."

"Yes."

"How much?"

"That is between the bell and me."

She accepted this the way she accepts most boundaries — provisionally, with a note taken for later appeal — and carved a while longer, and then set the penguin down.

"Why me? You didn't know me."

I considered several answers of the kind I have issued from behind folded hands for a century, and set them aside. She had crossed a corridor to embrace me. One owes something to people who cross first.

"I knew you," I said. "I had been you for a hundred years. The orphan pulled from the ice. The prodigy of not-worrying-anyone. The one who goes into the dark singing so the people at the glass will have something pleasant to remember." Her hands had gone still; I kept my eyes on my heron, which is the courtesy of confessionals everywhere. "I watched a theatre's worth of your story before I ever met you, child, and I recognized the manual. I wrote it. You executed it more faithfully than I did, and faster. It seemed efficient that one of us should be spared the full term."

She was quiet for a long time. Then she leaned sideways, without announcement, and put her head on my shoulder — I am given to understand she does this to the professor as well; I have joined a small guild — and said: "It's a stupid manual."

"Yes."

"I'm writing a better one."

"I know," I said. "I have read the early chapters. The prose is undisciplined and the pacing is chaotic and there is far too much toast."

She laughed — the bright, helpless laugh she brought back out of the void, the one that scatters — and picked her penguin back up, and we carved, and I thought that the reels had shown me many kinds of rescue but had somehow omitted this one: the counterfactual, alive, leaning on your shoulder, complaining about your prose.

It was as she was leaving, penguin under her arm like a rescued cat, that she stopped at the gate and asked the other question.

"You spent one in there. On the puppet. A wish." She was not looking at me; she was looking at the crystals in the lattice, all the wishes that were never hers. "I felt it go past. Everything in there felt like nothing, and then for a second something went by that felt like — summer. It smelled like—" she frowned, retrieving it, "—like a festival. What did you wish for?"

The terrace was very quiet. The bell hung low for children and did not ring.

"Popcorn," I said.

She blinked. She waited, in case the sentence had relatives. It did — it has a century of relatives — but they were not in attendance that afternoon, and after a moment she nodded, gravely, the way she accepts boundaries she has decided to honor rather than appeal.

"Okay," she said. And then, because she is her mother's daughter and cannot leave a silence unfurnished, she hummed as she went down the ridge path — three notes, the three notes, *da-da-da* — and the crystals in the lattice took the frequency and held it, faintly, the way frost holds everything it is given, and I sat on the step of my keeperless shrine with my badly proportioned heron and let the summer she had smelled come the rest of the way in.

## V. What the Wish Was Made Of

I was sixteen the summer of the long drought, and not yet the office's.

I must be careful here. I have noticed, in the accounts of others — I am a great reader of accounts — that memory told late in life acquires a varnish, and I do not wish to varnish this; it has survived ninety years without assistance. So: the facts, as the frost kept them.

The Shrine of Flaming Sakura took in orphans in those days, as it always had; the mountain's charity was practical and unsentimental, and so was I. I was the pale one, the one the pilgrim children stared at, red-eyed, white-lashed, born looking like a small winter, and I had concluded early — I remember concluding it; I remember the actual afternoon, which is the kind of thing one should not be able to remember and I do — that having was the antechamber of losing, and that a person of sense would decline to enter the building. Gyokuro was Miko then, twenty-six and incandescent, and she used to look at me across the sweeping with an expression I did not decode for another eighty years.

The summer festival came regardless of the drought. Festivals do. The pilgrim road wore its lanterns; the bay wore its boats; the east gate wore its stalls, and one of the stalls sold kettle popcorn, and the stall was minded, that year, by the vendor's son, because the vendor's knee had given out in the spring.

His name — I have written this far, and I see no reason to grow careful at the last fence — was Sōta.

He was not remarkable. I want that recorded, because everything else about the memory has been asked to carry so much that the boy himself is owed a plain description: seventeen, brown as the drought, quick with the ladle, laughed at his own jokes a half-second before the punchline in a way that ruined and improved them. He gave the shrine children misshapen batches for free and claimed it was inventory control. He called me *yuki-jō*, the snow miss, which from other mouths that year was a wall and from his was, somehow, a door.

On the last night there were fireworks over the bay. The whole city went down to the water, and the shrine children were herded along, and in the crush at the sea wall I was pressed — by the crowd, I have always maintained; the crowd was very sincere — against the side of the popcorn vendor's son, and the first shell went up.

I had heard fireworks all my life from the hill, where they are a picture. From inside the crowd they are a weather, and the report of the first one went through the sea wall and the soles of my feet and I flinched — I, who at sixteen had already stopped flinching at most things, on principle — and his hand came around my shoulder, easy, unceremonious, the way you steady a stack of anything, and my ear was against his chest.

And under the fireworks, which were the loudest thing I had ever stood inside, I could hear his heart.

I had spent sixteen years learning to hear everything. It was already the whole of what I would become; the mountain had noticed; the bell would notice within the year. And I had never once stood close enough to a living person to hear that particular sound — that small, ridiculous, unguarded machine, thudding away under a summer shirt, entirely unaware of being audible, entirely unaware of being the loudest thing on a night full of fire. The sky broke itself open in gold and chrysanthemum and I stood in the smell of gunpowder and popcorn with the breeze off the bay in my hair and a boy's heartbeat in my ear, and I made a wish. I did not ring anything or offer anything or address anyone. I simply thought, with the whole force of a sixteen-year-old winter:

*I wish this could last forever.*

The finale faded. The crowd exhaled. His arm returned to his own business, with an apology I waved away, and we walked back up the hill in a crowd of children, and within the year the Suzu rang in my hands of its own accord — it does that once a generation; it is how the mountain hires — and Gyokuro's face when she heard it was the proudest and sorriest face I have ever been looked at with, and I took the white, and the vows, and the bell.

I want to be precise about the next part, because I have lied about it in the records for ninety years and the records deserve a correction.

The office did not forbid him. The office forbids very little; it merely fills the hands. It would flatter me to say the mantle demanded the amputation. The mantle demanded nothing. I volunteered it. I was sixteen and I had a theory about the antechamber of losing, and here was the mountain offering me a building with no such room in it, and I moved in, and I entered the decision in the records as devotion. It has sat in the records as devotion for ninety years. Let this account serve as the amended filing: it was fear, wearing devotion's coat, and the coat fit so well that even the wearer was deceived.

He waited a while. I know this the way one knows things from a hilltop. Then he did the sensible thing, which was everything I did not do: he lived. The stall became a shop; the shop acquired an awning, and later, I am told, an awning fire, nothing serious; there was a girl from the fish market who laughed easily, and paper cranes at the wedding, and in time children who came up the pilgrim road on festival days and rang the bell too hard, the way children should. He came himself, once a year, every year, and made his wish at the offering box like any townsman, and the Miko of Flaming Sakura stood at her station in the courtyard with her hands folded and her face composed and was, the instruments would have confirmed, perfectly stable.

I never asked him his wish. The living make their wishes to the box, not the woman; that is the protocol, and I have never in my life been so grateful for a protocol.

He died at eighty-one, in the winter, an ordinary death of ordinary weight, years before the rain that would not cease — safely dead, I have sometimes thought, spared the taking of the city by the small mercy of a bad heart, and what a sentence that is to have thought about anyone. I performed his rites myself. It was my office and my parish and his family's request, and I stood over the man whose heartbeat was the founding document of my interior life and I spoke the passages in a voice that, I am told, did not waver. His granddaughter thanked me afterward for my composure. She said it was a comfort.

I have carried the wish ever since. Not as I carry the city's wishes — those arrived as freight, one spoonful at a time, other people's reaches across other people's distances. This one was the private stock. The one bead in the reliquary that was mine before the reliquary existed. I did not spend it. There was never anything worth the spending; my own tomorrows I burned by the fistful, crisis after crisis, but the summer evening I kept, the way a ruined bank keeps one coin in a drawer, not as currency, as proof that there was once an economy.

And then, in the place where stories go to die, a puppet with a stolen girl inside her raised a hand against a family that had crossed the end of the world to stay one, and I had a quarter-second and one coin.

*I wish this could last forever.*

I spent it. The moment did what wishes do when they are finally, literally granted: it lasted forever, for four seconds, for someone else — a summer breeze in a place with no air, popcorn in a place with no festivals, fireworks drowned out by a boy's heartbeat in a place that had never permitted a heart — and the frozen forever bought the scissors their opening, and the strings were cut, and the girl was saved, and the coin was gone.

I had expected grief. I had budgeted for it, in fact, on the walk back; I am a careful accountant of my own winters. What arrived instead I am still learning the name of. The memory is intact — I have just spent five pages proving it — but it has changed state. A wish, once spent, becomes simply a thing that happened. The fireworks are still there when I look. Only now they end, as fireworks should, as they were always going to, as everything does; it was the wish that had kept them burning for ninety years, and the burning that had kept me at the window. The sky over that summer has finally been allowed its dark.

I can report that the dark is gentle. I can report that his heart, having spent a century as a shrine, has been permitted to go back to being a boy's heart on a sea wall, seventeen years old, half a second ahead of its own joke. And I can report — this is the finding; this is why the account exists — that of all the ten thousand wishes I have ever carried, the only one I was ever glad to grant was my own, and I granted it by giving it away, and if that is not the whole instruction manual of my office delivered in a single transaction, then I have wasted a hundred years of reading.

He would have found the mathematics hilarious. He laughed early, as I have said. It ruined and improved everything.

## VI. The Reader's Debt

There remained a debt, and I had been carrying it since a night in Honami when a frozen theatre showed me its uncut archive: somewhere in the world was a woman who had no idea that her story had spent a night keeping a stranger from saying yes to the rain. *Every light I'm giving all comes back to you.* Somebody, I had resolved on a hilltop with two bowls of pudding, ought to bring her hers.

I invited her to the shrine on the evening it was finished. Alone — this required negotiation; her household attaches like ivy — and she came up the ridge path at the hour the manufactured sky performs its amber dusk, and stopped at the gate, and looked at the lattice for a long time.

"They're warm," she said, which no one else had noticed.

"They were spent on a road. Roads hold the heat of their traffic."

I had laid the step with a cloth, and the flask, and two bowls of red bean pudding from the campus vendor, who has learned to stop asking. She sat the way she does everything — immediately, entirely, with her whole weight — and picked up the bowl, and then caught the arithmetic of the table and looked at me sideways.

"Two bowls," she said. "Is this a ritual? Am I doing a ritual right now? Iuno says I have to ask before I agree to rituals. There's a list of incidents."

"It is a debt," I said. "Eat, and I will pay it."

I told her all of it. I had rehearsed nothing, which the office would regard as malpractice, and so it came out in the order the frost kept it rather than the order a report would prefer: the schoolgirl in the ruined street who watched me carve. The theatre with no walls and one chair. Seven reels, cut with a curator's love, each of them her — the woman the sea gave back, the murderer who kept a light on, the priestess at the window, the birthday, the grief that was helped up and walked home. The proposition. The rain that was going to need a shape, and the vessel I had spent a century sanding myself into, and my hand rising in the dark without my asking it to, frost going beautiful and wrong at the fingertips.

She had stopped eating. She has a face like a bay; weather crosses it visibly. I kept my eyes on the glacier, which is the courtesy of confessionals everywhere, and told her about the song.

"Two voices," I said. "Behind the last screen. Off-key in one and sure in the other, braided so closely I could not say which was carrying which. She could counterfeit any face in the world, your Architect. She could not counterfeit *between.* And I froze her whole theatre to make it honest, and the ice played what she had cut — the glass, the shell, the spoon, the bench, the graves; forgive me, the graves; you have never performed for me and I have seen your worst rooms — and somewhere in the middle of your uncut story, standing in the wreck of a trap built from your life, I put my hand to my own face and found it wet, and understood what the catastrophe had been all along. It was never the rain." My voice held. I permitted it not to, and it held anyway, out of habit, and I let the habit have this one. "It was the absence of hands. And your story — yours, and your daughter's, and the priestess's, and the wolf's — was the document that proved hands existed. I refused a god's job offer on the strength of your bad mornings, Mei. I have come to return the light. It is yours. It came back."

The dusk cycle finished while she found her voice. The crystals held their road-warmth. Below us the god knelt, and did not intrude, having excellent manners for his size.

"I wasn't even there," she said finally. Wetly. Half a laugh in it, the way she builds all her load-bearing sentences.

"Readers are accustomed to that," I said. "The author is never in the room. The book works regardless. It is the entire scandal of the technology."

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, exactly as her daughter does, or her daughter exactly as she does; the citation order is disputed. And then she was quiet in a different key, turning her bowl, and I watched her decide something, and waited, because waiting is my instrument and I have finally begun playing it for pleasure.

"Someone told me something like that once," she said. "Recently. About a story." She looked up. "I met my author, Hiyuki. In there. Behind the last door."

"I know," I said. "I stood at the threshold while you rebuilt her stage."

"She was—" Mei stopped, and chose, and I watched her choose the true version over the easy one, which is the only heroism I have ever fully believed in. "She was a girl who couldn't figure out how to grow up. She watched everyone else get instructions she never got. So she wrote someone braver and jumped inside. And I spent a whole year thinking that made me fake — the mask a coward wore — and then I sat down next to her and read her journal, and you know what was actually in it?" The bay-weather crossed her face again, and cleared. "A reader. That's all an author is. A reader who got so lonely she started writing letters to the person she hoped would answer. I was the answer. I got to be somebody's answer." She laughed, cracked in the middle, hers. "She told me to keep leaping. I told her she was enough. We were both right, which the High Priestess says is statistically improbable for this family."

"The three of you," I said, "have been passing the same lantern around a circle and calling it three lanterns."

"Yeah." She looked at the crystals. "Author, hero, reader. Turns out it's one chair and we take shifts." Then, with the terrible unhurried aim she disguises as artlessness: "So whose story do you read next?"

I had known the question was coming up the ridge path with her. I had not known my answer would be ready.

"I have spent a century in a single library," I said. "A great and grieving library, and I have read it to the end, and the end, it transpires, was not an ending; the last page says *continued elsewhere.* Meanwhile I am reliably informed that the world prints new material daily. Roads. Queues. Awning fires, nothing serious. I have read a great deal, Mei, and seen comparatively little, and the distinction has begun to keep me up at night, which at my age is expensive."

"Field research," she said, and her grin arrived like the fireworks' first shell — sudden, gold, a weather rather than a picture.

"An undignified term for pilgrimage."

"A pilgrimage to what?"

"Whatever is standing nearest," I said, and she laughed until the crystals rang with it, faintly, keeping it, as frost keeps everything.

We finished the pudding. At the gate she paused, and I saw the request assemble itself behind her face, and braced for something enormous, because with her it usually is.

"Postcards," she said. "Amy collects— well, she doesn't yet, but she's about to, I can feel it. One per stop. Bad ones. The badder the better."

"I will select for poor proportions. It is my area."

"And—" the enormous thing, after all, but quiet: "when you're out there. If it gets heavy. You know where the room is now. It's the one with too much toast in it." She held my eyes the way she held the glass, once, in footage I was never meant to see. "Same time next spring?"

The bay, the breeze, the boy half a second ahead of his own joke; a sister on a veranda; a president with her mirror face-down; a girl with a penguin under her arm. All the appointments I had ever declined to make, on the theory that appointments are hostages given to loss. I have resigned from the theory. Its returns were terrible.

"Same time next spring," I said, and she went down the path humming, three notes, off-key on purpose, and the lattice held the frequency all night; I checked.

## VII. The Stone in Her Throat

I went to Lucilla's office on an evening of low lamps and high paperwork, intending to discuss the atlas she had not misfiled. She did not give me the chance.

I had barely cleared the door when she set the funding-committee mirror face-down on the desk — which is, from Lucilla, the sound of a battlefield being cleared — and folded her hands, and said, with no seasoning whatsoever:

"I asked you to die."

The lamp hummed. Somewhere below us the Academy went on carrying its campfire.

"You asked me to save your students," I said. "The phrasing was economical."

"Don't." One syllable, and thirty years in it. "Don't file it. I have watched you file things across this desk for three decades, Hiyuki; I know the face. The eyes go a half-degree kinder and something behind the sternum audibly shelves itself. You will not shelve this one. It's mine." She took a breath, and I watched the most fluent liar of her generation choose, laboriously, hand over hand, the truth: "The barriers were failing and the children were inside and you walked in smelling of iris with a bell full of futures, and I looked at the only person I have ever— at my oldest friend, and I did the arithmetic, and I said *I am asking you to die,* because you were the best weapon I had left and I have spent thirty years being the woman who spends her weapons. I knew what the words were. I said them anyway. I would say them again tomorrow if the sky came down, and I have hated myself for both halves of that sentence every night since, and I swallowed the apology because you weren't here to receive it, and it has been sitting behind my sternum like a stone, which — " her mouth twisted; even now she could not fully disarm — "I understand is your registered trademark, and I'll thank you not to litigate."

The old way stood ready. I felt it rise, smooth as a century: *What is your wish?* — take the stone, swallow it, add it to the reliquary, let her pass lighter. I have eaten forty-one of these in a day. Hers would have weighed nothing at all and everything there is.

"Then it is heard," I said instead.

She blinked.

"Not carried," I said. "Heard. I am given to understand there is a difference; a reliable authority explained it to me on a hilltop, over pudding. You set it down where I can see you. I witness it. It stays yours, and it stays down, and neither of us swallows anything. That is the whole of the new liturgy. It is embarrassingly simple. I cannot imagine why it took my lineage nine hundred years to fail to invent it."

Lucilla looked at me for a long moment with her lamp-lit eyes too bright, and then she laughed — the real one, the one from before the door, which I have now heard twice in one year, a statistical anomaly the instruments should investigate — and said, "You're going to make me cry in front of the funding mirror again. I'm instituting a surcharge."

"Bill the shrine. It has no treasury. The collections process will build character."

She wiped her eyes with the base of her thumb, unhurried, the way she does everything false and this one true thing, and then she said, lightly, the way one tests ice: "I believe I'm owed a turn. There's a standing offer on the books. Thirty years old, renewed annually. *Let me hold some of them.* You said to ask every so often, until your yes caught up." She tilted her head. "Is it caught up?"

The office was warm. The mirror was face-down. Somewhere east, under real weather, a black tree was deciding what it contained.

"Sit back," I said. "This will take some time. There was a boy."

I told her. All of it — the drought summer, the stall, the sea wall, the heartbeat under the fireworks, the wish, the white, the annual visitor whose wish I never asked, the rites I performed with a voice that did not waver, the coin spent in the void on someone else's daughter, the gentle dark where the fireworks used to burn. She did not reach for the candy drawer once, which for Lucilla is a state of religious attention. She did not joke. She listened the way I have spent a century listening, and I understood, from the receiving end for the first time, why the dead always talk to me: it is the finest sensation the universe manufactures, being heard all the way to the bottom by someone who is not waiting for her turn.

When I finished, she was quiet a moment. Then she said, "Thank you for his name."

Of everything she might have said. I record it because it was correct, and correctness of that caliber should be preserved for training purposes.

Then, because she is Lucilla and the universe requires balance: "I'm docking your pay. That's twice in one fiscal year. There are *procedures*, Hiyuki."

"Understood."

"Also—" and here she reached, at last, sideways, and retrieved the atlas from the low table, and set it on the desk between us, open to the eastern coast, and looked at me with the mild, terrible attention she usually reserves for budget shortfalls and dying stars — "you've been staring at this for two weeks. I have no idea how it got there."

"You misfiled it."

"I have never misfiled anything in my life."

"No," I agreed. "You haven't."

We regarded the map. The coast road ran east off the edge of the local inset, through the resettlement corridor, toward a bay with a city on it again.

"I want a leave of absence," I said. "Not for Honami, or not only. No mission profile. No survey cover, no field-assignment fiction — I have already been offered the paperwork by my own reflexes and I have declined it. I want to go as a woman with a bell and bad handicrafts, by roads I choose, at a pace the office would call negligent. I want to stand in queues. I have it on good authority that the wait is the flavor. I want—" and here the sentence arrived at the frontier of ninety years of records, and crossed it — "I want to see things I am not responsible for."

Lucilla opened the desk drawer.

The form was already stamped. Of course it was already stamped. The date of approval, I noted, was eleven days after my return from the void — the same day, by my private calendar, that the atlas had first appeared on the table. She had been holding it the way she holds everything, oiled and ajar and unmentioned, waiting for me to knock on my own door.

"Duration," she said, pen poised over the field, and then did not fill it in. She crossed the field out entirely, and wrote in the margin, in her small immaculate bureaucrat's hand, four words:

*Until the weather turns.*

"There's a condition," she said, sliding it across. "Non-negotiable. Board-level."

"Name it."

"When it turns cold," she said, "come back."

The lamp hummed. Thirty years of oiled hinges, and there it was, the door's whole purpose, stated as policy: not *stay*, which is a wall, and not *go*, which is an exile, but the third thing, the thing my lineage of departing women never once had waiting behind them — *come back*, spoken by someone with the standing to expect it. I have walked into the dark many times. I have never before walked into anything with a return address.

"Understood," I said, and my voice did the thing I have permitted it to do this year, and she pretended, with great professionalism, not to notice.

She loaded me down after that, ceremonially, as heads of state do: a full bag of sour candy ("For emergencies." — "What emergency does candy address?" — "Sourness. It's everywhere out there, you have no idea"); a demand for souvenirs at the rate of one badly proportioned bird per nation ("I'm building an aviary of your incompetence. It will be the finest collection on Solaris"); and, last, from the desk itself, the small mirror that is not a mirror, its twin remaining beside the heron with the hairpin.

"It's a makeup mirror," she said.

"I don't wear makeup."

"Then you'll simply have to call."

At the door — her door, the one I closed thirty years ago and she has kept oiled ever since, the door I have made her office's whole architecture out of — I paused, and she said, to my back, in the voice she keeps for things she has decided to say only once:

"The office smells of iris for an hour after you leave. It always has. I've never told you. I'm telling you now so you'll know what I'll be checking for, every evening, until the weather turns."

I did not turn around. Some sentences are received best by the back; the front would only have interfered.

I left her door open behind me. She will have noticed. She notices everything.

## VIII. Matters of the Heart

The pack saw me off at the Riseway on a morning the false sky had painted its best imitation of April.

They came in the manner of that pack, which is to say entirely, loudly, and with logistics: the wolf inspecting my boots and pronouncing them "acceptable, barely"; the Blessed Maiden presenting a hand-drawn map of Ragunna annotated *GOAT (avoid)(or don't)*; the bard, with studied carelessness, a small tuning fork — "In case you meet anyone worth being in tune with"; Chisa, a paper packet of tea, unlabeled, that smelled of a magnolia I will now be checking on personally; the professor, by courier, a single-page frequency chart of the shrine's ambient readings with one line highlighted and the annotation *stable. — M.*, which from Mornye is a sonnet.

Amy pressed a cassette into my hand. Home-dubbed, the label in marker: *for the road — extended cut.* "It's the whole thing," she said. "Not just the three notes. There's words and everything." And then, quieter, tariff-free: "Play it somewhere with a real sky, okay? It's never been under one."

Mei hugged me the way she does everything, entirely, and said only, into my shoulder: "Order for two."

"I intend to."

"Same time next spring."

"Same time next spring."

Iuno, who understands departures from the architectural side, offered no gift and no embrace — only, seer to seer, a slight inclination of the head, and one sentence, pitched for me alone: "The dark room. Tell me someday what you decided it contains." I inclined my head in return. Between women who have read their own futures and declined the reading, that is a signed contract.

Lucilla did not come to the Riseway. This was correct. She had made her farewell in the currency she deals in — a stamped form, an open door, a standing scent-check — and to have repeated it on a platform would have been inflation. But when the lift doors closed and the underground city fell away below me, lights and canals and a kneeling god and a keeperless shrine and eleven people who know my tea temperature, the mirror in my sleeve warmed, once, briefly, like a hand on a shoulder, and went quiet. I did not open it. There was nothing to say that the warmth had not said. I am learning the abbreviations of this language late, but I am, they tell me, a quick study.

Then the surface.

I had been living under a manufactured sky for a season, and under a vocation for a century, and I stepped out of the Riseway terminal into the first unadministered weather I have stood in since the rain over Honami ceased — real April, with real fault lines in it, a wind that no committee had approved, clouds proceeding on private business, and light, actual light, the kind that has crossed an actual distance to arrive. It is the distance, I find, that makes it light. Nothing crosses nothing. The gap is not the wound in the design; the gap is the design; it is the width required for anything at all to be *given* — a wish, a bowl, a name, a heartbeat heard once through a summer shirt — and I stood in the wind at the top of the world with a century of other people's reaches behind my sternum, legible at last, every one of them proof of the same theorem: that we are separate, and that the separateness is not the tragedy. The tragedy was only ever the reaches not made. The separateness is the sea the reaching sails on.

I rang the Suzu once at the first road marker. Not as an oracle — I drew nothing out of myself and paid nothing; the bell has spent enough of me — but in the oldest way, the pilgrim's way, the way ten thousand hands rang it at a shrine gate for nine hundred years: to call what is sacred near, and send what is false away.

Nothing parted. Nothing needed to. A bird I could not identify said something rude in a hedge; the wind continued its private business; the road ran east, unimpressed, entirely available. It occurred to me that I have spent my whole life ringing that bell at things — barriers, monsters, architectures of despair — and that this was the first time I had ever rung it at a morning, and that the morning, having nothing false in it, had simply kept being a morning.

That is the report, then, from the woman the instruments call perfectly stable: the bell works on mornings. It always did. There had simply never been anyone standing in one.

## IX. The Long Way Home

Honami lies eleven days east by the coast road, if one does not hurry, and I do not intend to hurry. *You always did take the long way home,* my sister told me, on a swept hilltop, with the whole of death behind her and a wink still in working order, and I have decided to stop treating the observation as a criticism. It was an itinerary.

The surveyors' bulletins reach even the roadside inns now. The east gate district resettles first; the fish market has its awning; and the pudding stand — I have read the notice three times and will enclose it with this account — reopens with the summer festival, and the first hundred customers will receive a commemorative spoon, as was always done after repairs. I intend to stand in the queue from the hour it forms. I am told the wait is the flavor. I will order for two. One of the two will go, as is now the custom of my house, to whoever is standing nearest — a stranger, a surveyor, a girl with her mother, a schoolgirl with a shadow that pools a half-second wrong; the stand, like the shrine, like the office as I have finally come to practice it, takes walk-ins.

And the bulletins say one thing more, which I have saved for last, as the frost saves everything. The great sakura in the shrine courtyard — the black calligraphy of itself, the tree I stood under while my sister passed into the light, the tree I wrote of once that it would have to decide, some spring or other, all over again what it contained — has put out one green branch. Low, on the northern side. As if testing the premise. As if some rooms, left dark long enough, get tired of being told they are empty, and begin, without permission, without a keeper, without anything at all but time and weather and the stubbornness of sap, to furnish themselves.

I walked east all that first day and did not count anything.

In the evening I came to an inn above a river, and took the small room, and played the cassette by the open window, under a real sky, as instructed. Two voices, one steady, one wandering happily off the pitch and back, braided so closely that it is impossible to say which is carrying which; and then the words giving out, not because the song was over, but because the singers had run out of need for them. *Da-da-da.* Any three notes will carry it — hummed badly, by anyone, anywhere there are two.

I hummed the second voice. Behind my sternum, ten thousand wishes kept the time, a library reading itself aloud; east of me a queue was already forming in a city that came back; behind me a door stood open, oiled, checking the evening air for iris.

Someone will remember us, I said — to the river, to the road, to no one, to everyone. I have made rather a career of it. Even in another time.

Then I closed the window against the real and unadministered cold, which is coming, as it always does, and which — for the first time in a hundred years — I am expected home ahead of.

*Even in Another Time — fin.*

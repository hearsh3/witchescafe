# Youngest Gate 12 — The Sea Above

The washing began to rise from the school rail.

Cartethyia had hung the cloths there herself, wringing each one over a bucket before spreading it between the examination screens. Now the wet corners lifted. Beads gathered along the hems and climbed through the weave, taking the cloth with them until the pegs creaked.

She caught a sheet against her chest.

“Cia, would you come and hold this? The children can see through to the examination table.”

Ciaccona set her lute in its case and took the other end. Between their hands the sheet swelled toward the ceiling. Water emerged from it in separate, trembling spheres.

The little boy whose bridge occupied the head teacher's shelf reached for one.

“Keep your hands on the pegs,” Cartethyia told him. “You can help me move these inside. Take the basket too; we're going to need all of them.”

He brought it, watching the water over his shoulder.

Beyond the window, rain travelled up the side of the school. It threaded past the eaves, past the branches of the courtyard tree, into the dark over the roofs. The sound of rainfall thinned to the scrape of gutters emptying themselves. Farther down the terrace, somebody's awning sprang upright with a report that brought three patients out of their chairs.

Baizhi stepped between the screens with a towel over one arm.

“Bring the chairs toward the inner wall. The windows are giving people too much to follow.” She took the boy's free hand and put it against the basket handle. “You can sit beside your bridge when you've finished. The Ranger upstairs has asked for another demonstration.”

Cartethyia and Ciaccona carried the sheet into the passage. Lupa met them there, buttoning a borrowed work coat over her damp clothes. The coat pulled across her shoulders. She had folded the sleeves back twice.

“Panhua wants these returned with all the buttons,” she said, taking the pegs from Cartethyia. “Help me remember that when she sends me through another wall.”

“She's sending you to the lamp-oil store,” Ciaccona said.

“I've seen the door.”

Chisa stood at the outer entrance, one hand resting on its frame. The sound above the school had deepened. Water moved somewhere over the roof in a long, slow surge, with the weight and spacing of waves against a harbour wall.

She stepped outside far enough to look up.

The drops joined high above the city. Each arrival spread a small circle through a surface suspended across the dark. Where the circles met, ridges formed. They ran toward the northern mountains and broke into pale folds, gathering again farther out.

A sea was assembling over Jinzhou.

Chisa watched a ridge pass above the ninth tower. Through it, briefly, she saw the tower's roof from underneath and from above at once.

Chixia came uphill with a patrol lantern held at her knee. Four people followed her hand along a guide rope. The last was a woman carrying a cage beneath her coat; something inside it kept complaining in a thin, furious chirp.

“Room for a tailor and her whole household,” Chixia called. “One member needs a saucer of grain. I've promised him the captain's personal attention.”

She helped the woman over the step, handed the lantern to Chisa, and turned to count the others through. A drop lifted past the red brim of her cap.

Above the northern ridge, light opened in the suspended water.

It curved along one edge of a vast, submerged disc. A wave crossed it and drew the brightness out into a long white wound. When the water settled, more of the disc showed.

Chixia stood with her hand still extended toward the doorway.

The tailor's husband followed her gaze.

“My father used to say the Sentinel comes through the lake when the city needs him.”

From the passage, a wounded Ranger shifted his crutch against the wall.

“We saw that light at Norfall.” His wife put a hand around his wrist. He looked at her and then back at the water. “Three years ago. Get everyone away from the windows, Captain.”

Chixia lowered her arm. The rope had pressed a red groove into her palm.

“I'll take the next group through the covered stair. Chisa, keep this entrance clear for stretchers. Cartethyia, somebody at the landing needs help with a trunk, and he's packed it as though we're moving the whole mountain.”

Cartethyia came down the steps beside her. At the turn, she slipped her small hand beneath the trunk's handle and lifted. Its owner hurried after them, explaining which side contained the crockery.

Over their heads, the white curve widened.

---

Doctor Tang made Jiyan sit while she watched him swallow the antidote.

He had authorised its issue at the evening watch. Along the medical shelter, soldiers waited with their sleeves rolled back and their treatment cards in their hands. Tang's assistants checked the cards against Baizhi's instructions, poured the bitter amber liquid, and marked the time. The men assigned to covered duties took their places beside those waiting for examination.

Jiyan returned the empty vial.

“The western gun crew have started seeing their old emplacement again. Send the next tray there with an escort.”

“Xiulan has taken it.” Tang held two fingers against his wrist. “Tell me how the shelter looks.”

He looked past her shoulder. A soldier in an obsolete breastplate sat on the end of the medicine chest, rubbing his hands above a brazier. The brazier occupied the space where Tang kept her clean dressings.

“There's a man beside your left elbow. I can see the lid through his knee.”

“And the ache behind your eyes?”

“Easier. I can keep the chest in view.”

Tang released his wrist and wrote on his card.

“That is the effect we're looking for. Tell the officers to keep the pairs together after treatment. The rain will continue supplying images.”

Outside, a rack of phantom spears passed through the ammunition wagon. Their red pennants streamed sideways above its loosely hanging canvas. The driver watched them, blinked hard, and asked the woman beside him to read the current gate marker aloud.

Sergeant Du sat near the shelter entrance with his hands wrapped around a warm bowl. Jiyan paused beside him.

“Tang wants you here through the next examination. The receiver crew need somebody to check their message copies. Bao says you know the old route names better than anyone on this shelf.”

Du's thumbs moved over the enamel.

“Rui used to bring me the ones he thought I'd enjoy complaining about. He'd circle the spelling first.”

Jiyan drew a folded dispatch from his coat and laid it beside the bowl.

“There's a driver on his way from the lower depot. This gives him the quarry road under the name it had before the bridge fell. Mark the turn he needs.”

Du put down the bowl. He read the dispatch twice before reaching for a pencil.

Jiyan waited until its point touched the paper, then went out toward the command shelter.

The new positions occupied a shelf of hard ground above the service road. Drainage trenches cut between the gun pits. Beyond them, the old army moved through the rising water: stretcher parties climbing a slope that had since collapsed, messengers running toward vanished tents, a line of archers drawing together under a black banner.

One of the archers turned his head. Jiyan remembered the man asking for a tooth to be pulled before a march. The memory included the little cloth he had brought to bite on and his embarrassment at owning something embroidered.

Jiyan touched the current command flag as he passed beneath it. Wet green fabric dragged across his knuckles.

Inside, Xiulan had weighted the map with four artillery fuses. She moved one as he came in.

“The reserve ammunition has reached the lower turn. Bao is bringing it up. The nineteen at the north culvert are still clearing the gun carriage.”

“Give them the time of the next relief. They should hear it from us before the runner leaves.”

Xiulan bent to the receiver. Jiyan steadied the map against a draught.

A dark sleeve rested on its opposite edge.

“Your hands used to shake after the difficult cases.”

Geshu Lin stood across the table, rain running from his white hair into the collar of his armour. A split in the leather beside his throat exposed the lining. Jiyan remembered stitching that split while the general dictated an order over his head.

The smell came with him: wet leather, burnt pitch, the sharp medicinal wine from a field bottle.

“You would hold them over the kettle,” Geshu Lin said. “Then you went back to work.”

Jiyan's fingers tightened on the map.

Behind the phantom, Xiulan spoke into the receiver. Her voice carried the numbers of the present watch. Jiyan listened until she had finished.

“I remember you bringing that kettle into the treatment tent,” he said.

Geshu Lin looked down at the positions.

“The stretcher bearers were freezing. You had given them your gloves.” A finger followed the northern ridge. “You have moved the line a long way.”

“The convoy came through the lower road. The wounded are in the city.”

“And the enemy has the ridge.”

Outside, a gun fired. Its recoil reached the table through the ground. The phantom's sleeve lay steadily across the jumping paper.

Geshu Lin studied the withdrawal marks with the same concentration Jiyan remembered from nights when they had shared a lantern. Once, after a twelve-hour march, that man had sent his own horse back for a feverish courier and walked the remaining distance beside the baggage. By morning he had ordered the courier's company into a breach.

“I saw what waited beyond those hills,” Geshu Lin said. “I believed we could reach it while it was wounded. Another season would bring another levy, another row of boys waiting for your hands. I wanted to finish it.”

Jiyan could hear the advance drums beneath the current guns.

He remembered carrying a man with a shattered jaw between two ranks of soldiers preparing to charge. The man's blood had warmed his shoulder. All the way to the treatment tent, a hand had beaten against his back, trying to make him understand something.

“They kept coming through the tent,” he said. “The bearers used doors when the stretchers ran out.”

Geshu Lin's eyes rose to his.

“I walked past that tent on my way forward.”

“You stopped beside the entrance. I was washing my hands, and by the time I reached you, the column had begun to move.”

The receiver gave a sharp burst of static. Xiulan put a hand over one ear.

“North culvert reports movement in the gully. They're taking fire from the old embankment.” She looked up. “General, the carriage is still across the exit.”

Geshu Lin's hand moved to the crest above the culvert.

“Put your reserves here. Break the enemy's gathering before it reaches the road. Every man beneath that slope bought you the ground to do it.”

Jiyan looked at the ink marking the culvert. Nineteen soldiers, a damaged gun, one narrow cut through the shelf.

“Xiulan, have the western battery range the mouth of the gully. Send Bao to me with the reserve platoon.”

She repeated the order into the receiver. Geshu Lin remained beside the map.

“The crest is within your reach. Use it. If you have to climb over their bodies, then climb. I would have them climb over mine.”

Jiyan picked up his weapon. For a moment, its weight called up the medical satchel that had once hung from the same shoulder.

“They're holding because we told them relief was coming,” he said. “I'm going to reach them while they can still hear us.”

He stepped through the shelter flap. The old general came with him into the rain.

---

In Mingting, Wen found the night porter standing on the wrong side of the Secretariat gate.

He held his keys out toward the empty street. Rain ran down the iron bars behind him.

“My father used to meet me here after late duty,” he explained when she took his elbow. “He'd bring the little stove on his cart. I could smell the chestnuts.”

Wen brought him inside and put his keys on the table. She asked the second porter to stay with him, then took Jinzhou's dispatch upstairs.

Minister Shen Qiu was clearing a space beneath the leak over his orchid. He caught the last drips in a saucer while she read the first page.

At the account of the upward rain, he set the saucer down.

“Wake the Marshal's liaison. Tell the eastern relay to reserve a speaking circuit. How far west have the reports come?”

“Xuanfang's lower road. And I have a local observation to add.”

He looked at her. She described the porter and gave the time.

Shen moved the orchid to the interior shelf. Then he pulled a chair beside the relay desk for her.

“Enter his account in his own words. Ask the ward clinics to send what they have. They'll need the warning while we are still assembling the picture.”

By the time Jinzhou came through, Grand Marshal Li Zhaoying had arrived carrying Falling Rain in its open travelling cradle. An attendant set the sword beside the desk. She leaned close to the speaking horn while Shen adjusted the lamp.

Across the country, Jinhsi sat at the council table with Changli beside her. The older woman's sleeve had been turned back for a fresh dressing. A shallow burn ran beside the old feather scars where the silkworm tray had rubbed her skin raw.

Sanhua finished tying the cloth and moved the inkstone out of reach of the bandage.

“The accumulation extends beyond the northern observation posts,” Jinhsi told Mingting. “Our observers can see a luminous disc forming within it. General Jiyan has confirmed the same sequence in his report from the battle three years ago. His latest dispatch names Ovathrax, the Threnodian of War.”

Li Zhaoying asked for the camp's current position. Changli gave the shelf, the road, the ammunition rate, and the arrival time of the latest convoy. Wen wrote beside the horn, leaving room for corrections.

“The reserve guns at the western depot can move tonight,” Li said. “The main column from here reaches you tomorrow evening if the gorge road stays open. I can bring medical wagons ahead of it.”

“Mengzhou's units are already nearer,” Changli said. “We have asked Dou Lian for their readiness. The repair crews have reported changes in their cores.”

Li drew the old Norfall campaign map toward her. Her thumb rested over a familiar seal at the foot of the sheet.

“My office sent Geshu Lin forward along that road. I want Jiyan's current ground observations attached to every movement order. Give him the western guns at the depot; he can choose where to use them.”

Shen passed her the release sheet. While she signed, he spoke into the horn.

“Magistrate, the ministries will send questions with the aid. Direct them to this desk. The gate officers will have enough to do receiving the wagons.”

Changli's mouth curved a little.

“I'll put your name on the covers, Minister. They remember your handwriting.”

“So do you. Use whichever resemblance proves useful.”

The line faded under a rush of distant water. Wen leaned closer, hearing breathing at the far end, then Jinhsi again.

“Please send the first medical wagons with lifting equipment. Some of the people coming toward us are very heavy.”

Shen looked at the Mengzhou request beneath his hand. He drew a line under the provision for automata recovery and passed it to the Marshal.

In Jinzhou, Changli reached across the table and adjusted the speaking horn as the connection cleared. The cup beside Jinhsi's hand had gone cold. She replaced it from the pot.

Through the council window, a long white wave rolled above the city.

---

The Mengzhou circuit opened with the sound of somebody hammering metal.

Dou Lian gave her position at the eastern Xuanfang works. She had come forward with the Bulwark's repair crews. Behind her, the hammer struck twice more and someone called for a smaller drift.

“The Sentinel is examining the seven units assigned to the Jinzhou approach. Our transport teams can cross as soon as your receiving yard is ready.”

Jinhsi unfolded the freight-apron plan.

“We can give them the stone apron below the school. The lifting tackle is there already, and the covered workshop opens onto it.”

“We'll need access from both ends. The largest recovery cradle takes the width of the street.”

Changli marked the second entrance for clearance. “Chixia can move the market carts. What is delaying the fighting units?”

The hammering ceased.

Hsin's voice came through the circuit, close enough to make the room seem smaller.

“One of them saluted a captain who died before you were born. Then it put its shield through the workshop wall. The captain used to order it forward at that hour.”

Jinhsi looked down at the seven marks on the route map.

“Can the present crews keep them oriented?”

“They've been trying. The rain remembers those crews too.” A chair scraped at Hsin's end. “I have sent the pumps, the spare joints, and the engineers. The seven will wait here while we establish a way of bringing them through. Three of them have spent forty years learning to recognise a voice. Tonight every voice they have ever loved is standing on the road.”

Changli rested her bandaged forearm against the table.

“Jiyan's soldiers are experiencing the same interference. Their antidote gives them enough separation to work in pairs. Baizhi can send the observations to your artificers.”

“Please have her do that. Zhou Min is beside me with a pencil.”

“And if the soldiers hold long enough for the units to arrive?” Jinhsi asked.

For several breaths the circuit carried the workshop: a chain running through a pulley, footsteps on boards, the soft hiss of a core being cooled.

“I taught these bodies how to close a gate,” Hsin said. “I remember every place where a frightened mind can catch in the work. Jinzhou has people on both sides of its gates tonight. Give me a little time with what I made.”

Jinhsi put her hand flat beside the map.

“The receiving yard will be ready. I'll send our plans with the next courier, including the arrangements for bringing damaged units home.”

When the circuit closed, Changli sat back. The edge of her dressing had darkened where her arm pressed the table.

Jinhsi reached for the clean cloth Sanhua had left.

“You're leaning on it.”

Changli lifted her arm and let Jinhsi slide the folded cloth beneath it.

“The tray caught exactly where the sleeve rests. I should have borrowed a larger coat from Wen.”

“You could have told me it hurt.”

Changli let her adjust the cloth, then flexed her fingers against its clean edge.

“I'll have Sanhua look at it again before the next call. Will you come outside for a moment? We should see what the observers are reporting.”

They went as far as the covered terrace.

The sea stretched across the city from mountain to mountain. Its underside rose and fell above the towers. Rain streamed toward it from every roof, thousands of silver lines pulling through the lantern light. Boats moored in the harbour rocked beneath it; their crews had turned them against the current by touch, eyes fixed on the gunwales.

The moon filled the northern water.

Along its right edge, a thin seam of darkness remained. Jinhsi watched the seam shorten. The light reached it from both ends, slowly enough that she could follow the joining.

Then the disc was whole.

White light entered the terrace. Changli's shadow stretched across the flagstones toward the council room. Jinhsi could see the weave of the cloth beneath her own sleeve.

“When the moon turns full, they shall return.”

She had copied Jué's prophecy in her own hand. She remembered waiting at the lake for the next words, her brush held above the page until ink collected at its tip.

Changli stood beside her, watching the water.

Down in the streets, a man called the Sentinel's name. Others took it up. At the foot of the terrace stair, a messenger stopped to catch his breath and asked the guard whether the Rover had come to the wall.

The northern drum answered through the stone.

In the suspended sea, something passed between the moon and the city. Its shadow crossed six terraces before the brightness returned.

Jinhsi gripped the wet rail.

“I keep listening for him,” she said. “Every time the water moves, I think he'll speak.”

Changli set her hand beside Jinhsi's.

“The lake-watch has a receiver. Sanhua has someone with them. You can hear from that post here.”

Jinhsi nodded. Her breath caught once, high in her chest, and she let it out slowly.

“Stay beside me for the next call.”

“Of course. Bring your cup this time. We may be there a while.”

They returned to the room together.

At the far end of the terrace, Yangyang waited with the courier case. The curtain behind her lifted toward the west, settled, then lifted again.

She put a hand beneath it.

“This afternoon you needed help across the causeway. Tell me how you intend to make this journey.”

Warmth moved along her palm. A corner of the route sheet lifted from the open case. It folded at the first covered relay, then at the second.

Yangyang held the sheet steady while the folds travelled toward Xuanfang.

“The supply carts are taking that road. You can shelter with them as far as the upper station.” She drew out a thinner copy of the dispatch. “Hsin will be at the forward works. Give Dou this page; she has the rest through the circuit.”

The paper rose against her fingers.

Yangyang kept hold of one corner.

“Come back with the escort. I'll meet you at the east arch. If you need to wait under a roof, wait there until somebody can bring you.”

The warmth touched the skin just below her ear. Her eyes closed briefly.

Then she released the paper.

It slipped beneath the terrace roof, passed through the opening beside a shutter, and went west along the line of lamps.

---

Under the full moon, Jiyan could see every buckle on the soldiers ahead of him.

They were three years dead.

Their armour carried the dents he remembered treating through. A drummer walked with the lower half of his face wrapped in cloth. Beside him, a standard-bearer planted his pole, braced one boot against a stone, and leaned forward as the old advance call sounded.

The conditions had assembled in the same order as they had during the Battle Beneath the Crescent: the rain reversing, the water gathering overhead, the moon filling inside it. Jiyan remembered this light on the treatment tent. It had made the blood on his cuffs look black.

Bao touched his elbow.

“The western battery is ready. The gun crew can hear our whistle from here, if the wind holds.”

He turned toward her. Mud had dried white along one side of her braid.

“Give them the signal now. Take the reserve down the cut and get a rope around the carriage. I'll cover the gully with the first squad.”

“The cut is breaking up. Chao says the northern lip will go if we put the whole platoon through.”

Jiyan crouched beside her map case. She held it against her knee and showed him where the ground had split.

“Then take six to the carriage. Keep the others above the cut to haul. Tell Chao to call the movement from where he can see the lip.”

Bao passed the instructions along the waiting line. Men repeated them in complete sentences, close to one another's ears. Each pair checked the rope between their belts before moving.

Geshu Lin stood where the old standard cast its shadow.

“You have enough strength to take the crest,” he said. “Your men can see that. They'll follow you if you turn toward it.”

Jiyan watched Bao's first six descend. At the edge, one soldier paused and shifted the rope higher on his companion's belt. Below them, the trapped gun crew answered the whistle.

“They'll follow because Bao came back for them at Shuanghe,” he said. “Because Chao tells them when the ground is failing, and they know I'll listen. Those are things they've learned while serving with us.”

“Trust can carry them uphill. The enemy will still be waiting when you have brought them down.”

“Then we'll fight from the shelf, with the western guns and the soldiers we bring back tonight.” Jiyan looked at the man across from him. “I sent nineteen people into that culvert. I owe them a purpose they can finish, and a way to reach the next position. Each time they survive an order, they learn what my next one is worth.”

Geshu Lin's expression tightened. In the white light Jiyan could see how young exhaustion had once made him look.

“I stood at the front of every charge I ordered.”

“I remember. Your men loved you. They watched you walk into the fire and tried to reach you.”

The phantom looked past him, toward the black banner.

Jiyan's throat worked. The old drummer lifted his sticks.

“I wanted you to turn around,” Jiyan said. “I was carrying a man who kept trying to speak. I wanted you to come back far enough to hear him.”

The western guns opened.

Earth leapt at the mouth of the gully. Jiyan went down the slope with his squad, green light spreading along his blade. A Tacet Discord came over the broken parapet on all fours, its head carrying the outline of a soldier's helmet. His first stroke drove it sideways into the bank. The second opened a path for the Ranger behind him to pull a wounded gunner clear.

At the carriage, Bao's six hauled the rope beneath the axle. The wheel had sunk between two stone blocks. Every pull lifted it a little, then let it drop.

Above them, Chao called for the haulers to move back from the edge.

The old advance call sounded through his voice.

Three men stepped forward together. Bao caught the nearest by his harness and drove her shoulder into him. Both went down. The rope jerked across the stones, taking skin from a gunner's palm.

Jiyan heard Geshu Lin beside his ear.

“There is your crest. Take it while they are gathering.”

Across the gully, shapes climbed over one another toward the gunfire. Their limbs caught the moonlight, broke into edges, joined again. Through them, the old army kept advancing.

The crest lay clear above their backs. Jiyan followed the approach with his eyes: broken parapet, shallow slope, the last few paces he could cross in a single rush. From there his blade could reach the whole gathering. Behind him, the carriage struck stone again, and Bao called for another pull.

Jiyan raised his weapon, then turned it across the narrow approach to Bao's crew.

“Chao, watch the bank. Bao, take your time with the axle; we have your side covered.”

He struck the next Discord at the parapet. Green light ran along the cut, lifting spray and loose grit into the white air. Behind him, the haulers shifted to the sound of Chao's current count.

The carriage rose. A broken spoke snapped beneath it. Bao got her foot under the wheel and guided it over the stone while the gun crew dragged their wounded toward the opening.

One young soldier tried to lift both ends of a stretcher. Jiyan caught the forward handles and went with him. Halfway up, the bank gave under their boots. The rope took their weight. Hands reached from above and drew them onto the shelf, stretcher first.

Jiyan slid back down for the last pair.

When he climbed out again, a familiar black sleeve hung beside the cut. For a heartbeat he thought Geshu Lin had reached for the rope.

Rain passed upward through the open hand.

Bao sat on the ground with blood running into her boot. Tang's assistant cut her trouser leg while she gave Xiulan the gun crew's names.

Two stretchers lay beneath covers. Seventeen people from the culvert had reached the shelter alive. One kept asking where they had put his gun, and another was trying to answer while an orderly examined the wound across his mouth.

Jiyan knelt beside the covered stretchers. He checked the tags, straightened a bent corner, and pressed it flat against the cloth.

The moon shone through the shelter's seams.

Xiulan crouched beside him with the receiver.

“Mengzhou's transport teams have crossed the lower boundary. The fighting units are still at the works.”

Jiyan put his hand on the earth to help himself stand. It trembled under his palm.

“Tell them we have the south shelf. They can reach the service road under our guns.”

He looked north. The black banner streamed among the rising drops. Around it the dead advanced, feet finding the ground of their own last night.

Jiyan went to help Tang turn a wounded man onto his side.

---

At the first relay, the wind rested beneath a wagon's canvas while the driver changed his mule.

At the second, she stayed through the whole exchange of harness. The dispatch lay against a sack of flour. A woman sitting on the sack held its corner whenever the wagon lurched, having accepted the driver's explanation that the Magistrate's paper had company.

They climbed toward Xuanfang under a sky crowded with water. The moon appeared between the pillars of rock, vanished behind one, and emerged larger in the gap beyond. Along the suspension bridges, keepers tied strips of fresh cloth around the present handrails. Travellers crossed with both hands on the rope.

Once, the wind left the wagon.

Below the bridge, black water opened between houses she knew. A red cap turned beneath a lamp. She moved toward it so quickly that the dispatch slid halfway out from under the woman's hand.

The woman caught the page.

“You'll have to mind this corner,” she said, looking up into the draught. “It's taking all the strain. Come round beside me and we'll keep it flat.”

The air moved back under the canvas.

She sat on the sack for the rest of the climb, holding the paper across her knees.

The forward works occupied a quarry behind the last ridge. There, the upward rain arrived in fits. A patch would lift from the yard, drawing filings and sawdust into a shining column, while water still ran downhill through the drains beside it.

Seven great bodies stood between the sheds. Their shields carried the repairs of several generations: rivet heads of different sizes, plates browned by old heat, a shoulder painted over so often that the garrison emblem had softened at its edges. Crews worked from ladders and platforms around their knees.

Old Seven stood nearest the road.

The vent high on its shield remained open. The wind crossed the yard toward it and slipped through with the dispatch gathered close.

Warm iron surrounded her. She knew the creak in the upper plate, the little shiver of the core at idle, the shelter made by a rule its garrison had kept for her.

Then the shield swung north.

A voice called from the rain. A sergeant's voice, brisk with the confidence of a man whose order had been obeyed hundreds of times.

Old Seven lifted its right foot.

Below it, an apprentice scrambled clear of the service platform. Hsin put her palm against the ankle housing. The great foot came down slowly, crushing the edge of a discarded packing case. Two of her tails spread along the ground behind her, white fur darkening in the mud.

“Bring the travel brace,” she told the apprentice. “Take it by the handles. You've already given that hinge enough of your fingers.”

He hurried toward the shed, tucking a bandaged thumb into his palm.

Hsin looked up at the open vent.

“You've chosen a busy evening for a visit. Come down far enough for me to see what you're carrying.”

The paper unfolded against the shield. Hsin read Jinhsi's instructions while the apprentice and Zhou Min fitted the brace.

Zhou Min secured the final pin one-handed, holding the bracket against the housing with her knee. She tested it, sat back on her heel, and looked at the recovery plan.

“They've allowed for the weight. Their workshop entrance is low for a standing unit.”

The wind turned the page. Changli had drawn the open apron on the reverse, with the covered workbenches facing it. Beside the loading ramp, a second route led back to the city's western road.

Hsin traced that route with a dirty finger.

“A place to mend them. I asked for that before the first detachment moved.” She raised her eyes to the vent. “You have come all this way for something else.”

The paper moved upward. It caught for a moment on an old rivet, and Hsin eased it free.

The wind laid it across Old Seven's reading grille.

Inside the shield, a small lens turned. The core hummed. The phantom sergeant called again from the yard, using a name the present crew had learned from the retirement register.

Old Seven's foot pressed against the travel brace.

Hsin kept her hand on its ankle.

“He can hear both,” she said softly. “That's what we have been working with all evening.”

The wind stayed beneath the plate. She lifted the page at the edge of the grille, letting the lens follow it to the drawing of the workshop. Her warmth thinned as a fresh gust of upward rain entered the vent.

Old Seven's shield trembled.

Then a small lower shutter opened against Hsin's shoulder.

Air passed through the new gap, sheltered beneath the sloping plate. The wind moved into it. Above her, the upper vent closed to a narrow slit.

Zhou Min put down her spanner.

“That shutter was fitted last winter. We use it to reach the oil line.”

Hsin looked at the new opening. Her hand eased against the ankle housing.

From inside the shield, Old Seven spoke in the voice of a woman who had once served its post.

“You'll be warmer here. Stay until the weather passes.”

The words had lived inside the core for years. The lower shutter remained open over the wind.

For a while Hsin watched the gap she could fit her hand through. Then she rose, with mud on both knees, and took the dispatch from the grille.

“Dou, bring the crews over. I'd like them to hear Jinzhou's offer with their units.”

Dou looked from the Sentinel to the open shutter, then went to gather them.

Hsin read the page aloud. She gave the width of the service road, the prepared ground below the school, the recovery cradles, the engineers who would meet them. At the provision for damaged cores, she slowed enough for Zhou Min to examine the wording over her shoulder.

When she finished, she stood before Old Seven.

“The rain along that road is stronger than it is here. You will hear people you remember giving orders. Your present crew will walk beside you, and I will travel with the column. We can keep your shield raised and fit a brake they can reach from the ground. If you lose the road, they will stop you while we find it again.”

The lens turned toward the drawing in her hand.

“Jinzhou is asking you to shelter its soldiers,” Hsin continued. “Its people have offered to shelter you afterward. I would like your answer before we loosen this brace.”

Old Seven lowered its shield until the edge rested on the yard. The wind remained in the sheltered gap. Beneath her, a small platform unfolded toward the crew.

“Bring the long spanner,” the unit said, in the garrison woman's voice. “The left foot has been sticking since winter.”

Zhou Min let out a breath through her nose.

“I've put it on the cart, along with the bearing you said would last another season. We can have that argument on the road.”

She stood, brushing grit from her knee, and went to examine the brake fittings.

Hsin stayed beside the lowered shield. The wind lifted the dispatch to her hand again. Its edge brushed the mud at her wrist.

“I promised you a marching line,” Hsin said. “I have spent the evening remembering their funerals in advance.”

A small current moved through the fur at her shoulder.

She looked back toward the other units. Their crews were reading the page together, one man holding it, another pointing to the route. At the far end of the yard, a unit settled its broad palm over the door of the garrison shelter and kept it there.

Hsin walked over to it.

“You can keep this post. The repair crews will need somebody watching the quarry road.”

Its palm lifted from the doorway. The crewman beneath it touched two fingers to the plate, then went to change the march list.

Another unit's core rose in pitch during the first movement test. Zhou Min stopped it with a raised hand. Hsin listened beside her, then called for a fresh cooling pan and a night crew to remain.

Five prepared to march.

They practised in the yard while the rain rose around them. The ground crews tried the brakes, released them, and tried again at unexpected points. A shield turned toward an old voice; the line beneath it tightened, the brace caught, and its crew waited beside the locked ankle until the lens returned to their lamp.

Hsin watched every trial. At the third, she went under the plate herself and moved a fitting a hand's breadth lower.

“They'll be working this in mud,” she told Zhou Min. “Give them room for a closed fist.”

By the time Dou called Jinzhou, five sets of present-day lamps hung beneath five shields. Each crew carried spare brake pins. The weapons remained secured for the journey, with their releases in the crews' keeping.

Hsin took the speaking horn.

“Magistrate, five units are coming with me. We'll use them to cover the road and your infantry positions. Have the recovery teams follow our markers as soon as they arrive; their first work may be well ahead of your apron.”

Jinhsi's reply came through beneath the hiss of the line.

“They have their orders. We'll meet your people on the road.”

Hsin handed the horn back to Dou and returned to Old Seven.

“Your junior gave you a return route,” she told the warmth beneath the shield. “Keep the paper. I intend to deliver everyone I have just put on this list.”

She climbed onto the service platform beside the crew. Old Seven lifted its foot, carefully cleared the broken packing case, and stepped toward Jinzhou.

---

By the time Mengzhou's first transport reached the freight apron, Lupa had helped dismantle a market stall and developed a firm opinion about its nails.

“Whoever built this meant it to survive the owner.” She held up a bent length of iron. “Chisa, put this with the others. He says he's keeping them.”

The owner, sheltering beside his stacked boards, reached for it.

“Good iron costs money. There's a way to straighten that.”

Lupa put it in his hand.

“Come and supervise the rebuilding when we're finished. I want to see how you get it back through wood.”

Chisa stood near the cleared entrance, the flat of her blade against the paving. She could feel the approaching wheels through it: two heavy axles, then a long vibration from something suspended between them.

“The cradle is reaching the turn,” she said. “You'll have to move the last stack another pace.”

Lupa and the stallholder shifted it together.

The transport came around the corner with eight people on its guide ropes. Its driver looked up at the sea, caught himself, and brought his gaze down to Chisa's lantern.

Cartethyia emerged from the school carrying the boy's model bridge. She set it on the clerk's desk under the awning, beyond reach of the crowd, and gave the boy the lifting string.

“You can keep watch over it here. I need to be taller for a while.”

Light opened around her. The awning lifted in a warm gust, and Fleurdelys stood beside the desk, ducking beneath its edge. The boy's hand halted on the string.

She leaned down to him, her deeper voice carrying through the yard.

“Please guard my bag as well. The little glass garden goes on top.”

Then she straightened and took the transport's forward rope. The driver stared at her for half a breath before pointing to the space between the stone posts. Together they guided the cradle into place.

Ciaccona brought the receiver out from the school. A dock worker took over the announcements while she listened to the northern channel. Tollhide stood inside its small terminal with all four feet braced. The grey marks on her card had crowded together.

She laid the card facedown beside the set.

On the road beyond the arch, water rose around a shape as tall as the houses.

For a moment, the people at the apron could see its shadow. A broad shield, a human crew walking beneath it, a line of lamps moving north along the outer road.

Someone began to cheer.

The driver beside Fleurdelys put two fingers in his mouth and whistled for his team.

“That's our column. We need the empty cradles back on the road.”

Lupa looked at Chisa. Chisa lifted her blade from the stones.

“I can find the damaged paving for him. The wheel spacing is close to the edge.”

Lupa picked up the rope coil. She went to Ciaccona first and leaned close enough to be heard over the yard.

“We're going out with the recovery crew. Tell the others which road when they ask, and keep something hot for us.”

Ciaccona caught the front of the borrowed coat and fastened the button Lupa had missed.

“Panhua will be pleased to learn her coat has joined the army. Come back through this gate so I can see what you've done to it.”

Lupa put a hand briefly against the back of her neck, then joined Chisa at the front of the cradle.

The wheels began to turn.

---

The first shield reached Jiyan's western position before dawn.

Its lower edge came down behind the guns with a force that shook water out of the trench walls. Beneath it, the gun crews found room to stand upright. One man tipped his face toward the iron overhead and stayed that way until his loader pulled him back to the breech.

Four more shields moved along the shelf. Human voices travelled under them, calling clearances, checking the ground, telling the units where the wounded lay.

Hsin came down from Old Seven's platform with a spanner in her sleeve.

Jiyan met her at the trench mouth. He had a strip of bandage caught on one wrist and blood drying above his collar.

“We can hold the service road from here,” he said. “The eastern end of the shelf has started to split. I need your crews to see it before they move farther.”

“Show Zhou Min. She'll decide where eleven tons can put its feet.” Hsin looked at the stretchers passing beneath the first shield. “How long has that medical team been standing?”

“Since the withdrawal. The relief is coming with the city wagons.”

She took the spare lamp from her belt and passed it to a bearer struggling with a broken handle.

The next impact came through the earth while he was fastening it.

All five shields rang. Far to the north, the sea bulged downward around a dark mass. The moon's light slid along it, catching ranks of shapes moving within the water.

At the eastern position, Old Seven's left foot dropped through the crust of the shelf.

The crew pulled the brake line. Its leg locked with a crack that carried above the guns. The shield tilted toward the trench, and the soldiers beneath it scrambled onto the bank.

Hsin was already running.

Old Seven braced its right arm against the ground. Its open palm took the weight, fingers burying themselves in the mud. The left ankle housing came apart around the old bearing.

The crew chief looked down into the split, then raised his lamp toward Jiyan.

“We need the road held while we get the cradle under him.”

Jiyan turned to Xiulan.

“Move the eastern gun crew beneath the next shield. Their first task is covering this recovery.”

The battery began to shift. Hsin knelt beside the broken ankle while Zhou Min laid out the braces. Between their hands, small joints of light appeared around the cracked housing, holding it together long enough for the crew to fit the iron.

The recovery lamps came up the service road.

Chisa walked before them with her blade touching the verge. Lupa and two engineers guided the leading wheel around a sinkhole. They brought the cradle under the shield a little at a time, pausing whenever Zhou Min called from beside the leg.

Inside the lower shutter, the wind held close to the warm plate. A strip of paper trembled against the grille.

The dead sergeant stood beyond the trench, calling his unit onward. His voice was clear and familiar. Old Seven's raised lens turned toward him.

Then Hsin put her hand against the grille.

“Your crew is taking your weight now. Lift your right hand when Zhou tells you, and let us bring you down.”

Zhou Min checked the final brace. She called to the haulers, waited for their answer, and gave the signal.

The great hand rose from the earth.

Chains tightened. The cradle settled on its springs, carrying the shield, the broken leg, and the warmth inside it. Lupa leaned her shoulder into the guide bar while the drivers turned its wheels toward the service road.

Under the next shield, the gun crew opened fire to cover them.

Jiyan walked beside the cradle as far as the road marker. A black standard flickered beyond it. Geshu Lin stood beneath the cloth with one hand resting on his weapon, watching the damaged machine go past.

For a moment Jiyan saw the man as he had looked beside the treatment tent, holding a kettle by its wrapped handle.

Then the rising rain drew his outline into the light.

Jiyan turned back toward the four shields on the shelf. Xiulan was waiting with the revised positions. He took the map from her and held it where both could read.

---

Yangyang met the returning cradle at the east arch.

She had been helping the keeper replace a guide lamp. When the wheels sounded beyond the turn, she put the lamp down and went out with the receiving crew.

Old Seven lay at an angle on the padded supports. Mud filled the broken ankle housing. A crewman sat beside it with both hands on a brace, watching each movement of the chains.

Yangyang walked alongside until she found the lower shutter.

The dispatch emerged first, damp at the edges, folded along its return road.

She took it. Warm air brushed the inside of her sleeve and stayed there.

For several paces she looked down at the paper.

“I've kept your place by the stove,” she said. “You can stay with me while they get him settled. The school has enough people for the next watch.”

The warmth moved toward her wrist. She cupped her other hand beneath it as she walked.

At the apron, Cartethyia had returned to her smaller shape and was helping the clerk make space beside the repair platform. Ciaccona brought a pot from the school. Lupa sat on an upturned box while Chisa pulled a splinter from the heel of her boot.

The transport crew lowered Old Seven onto the supports. Its shield faced the road; beneath it, the little service platform stood level with the school step.

The boy came down carrying his model bridge by the base.

“I can show you how this middle part lifts,” he told the lens turning toward him. “You pull here, but you have to keep the other side steady or the whole road goes crooked.”

He set it on the platform and worked the string. The lens followed the rising span.

Above the awning, the moon remained full. Its light lay on the road, on the iron shield, on the white bandage the driver was winding around his hand. North of the city, four great shapes held their places beneath the suspended sea.

The receiver called for fresh bearers.

Chixia took the names of the next crew and read them back slowly. Beside her, the boy lowered his bridge for a second crossing, and Old Seven adjusted the platform to keep the little road level.

# The Last Liberty

The burning village folded into the hourglass.

First the roofs, then the road, then the young soldier with his mouth still open around a word he would never finish. The sand received them all. Hsin turned the glass upon its side, and the last red grain lay still.

Suisui’s hand remained against her breast.

There was no wound. Her body had not yet accepted this.

Between them stood a low table. Upon it, a weiqi board waited beneath a lamp, its pale intersections running toward the darkness. Hsin settled behind it. Her tails passed over one another with the quiet of silk being put away.

“One round,” she said. “Since you have such confidence in an ending I have never dared imagine, let us discover what you will pay to keep imagining it.”

Suisui lowered her hand.

At the edge of the chamber, Dumpling perched upon Yangyang’s folded coat. The bird had tucked one foot beneath itself. A small tear marked the coat’s cuff, where Suisui had meant to put in three stitches before they left Jinzhou.

Above the table hung a moon.

It shone upon no sky.

“What are we playing?” she asked.

Hsin set a black stone at the centre of the board.

“Your sister.”

Suisui looked at the stone for a long moment.

“Her name is Yangyang. If you mean the one who went through your gates, say so. There are two women you might mean, and I have already found that you prefer a word with more than one door.”

The Sentinel’s eyes narrowed pleasantly.

“The Yangyang born to your mother in this telling, who entered Xuanfang after sending her wounded companion home. Shall I also give you her registry?”

“You may leave her employment out of it.”

White stones appeared around the black. Their arrangement enclosed it almost completely. One bare crossing remained.

“The people of Xuanfang,” Hsin said. “Some you passed on the road. Most you will never meet. There is a way by which I can make your sister’s return certain. Before this incense has burned away, I wager that you will ask me to take it.”

A slender stick stood beside the lamp. Its tip was dark.

“And what does that way cost?”

“One person’s continued existence.”

Suisui’s gaze rose.

Hsin rested her cheek against the back of her hand.

“You need not decide before I name the person and explain the price. I have no taste for a victory obtained from someone who mistook the account.”

“You have just spent a considerable amount of effort making me mistake the account.”

“Consider it an education purchased at a fortunate rate.”

Suisui sat.

The cushion beneath her was warm, as though someone had only just risen. She shifted to its unheated edge.

“If I knowingly agree to that person’s destruction in exchange for Yangyang’s safe return, you win. If I do not, before the incense ends, I win.”

“You will put the black stone into my hand when you agree.”

“And you will not mistake a dropped stone for consent, or speak through my mouth and call the answer mine.”

“Your will remains yours. I may furnish it with things to consider.”

That was the first moment Suisui felt the room’s full size.

There were no walls she could find. Beyond the lamp, a door stood open upon a courtyard; beyond that courtyard stood another door. The moon shone through all of them.

She had heard such stories at home. A traveller accepted a fox’s invitation; the wine was warm, the host attentive, the night without an end anyone thought to ask after. In the morning, the traveller’s staff had rotted where it leaned.

“The time must be named as well,” she said.

Hsin smiled.

“One stick of incense, burning at its own pace. I will neither lengthen it nor renew it. Whatever hours I show you, no more than a falling drop shall pass outside this place.”

“And my stake?”

“Your remaining freedom. Should I win, you surrender the copied authority and enter my service for the rest of your natural life. You will hear petitions beside me. You will learn the names beneath the stones. I shall find ample employment for a woman who objects so eloquently to the manner in which I perform my duty.”

Suisui looked toward the coat.

All the visits she had put off. All the journeys for which there had seemed to be another season.

“And should I win?”

“The Prism.”

“With the authority to use it.”

“With the authority to use its actual powers in seeking and protecting Yangyang.”

“And your instruction. A vessel I cannot open would be a very elegant way of keeping it.”

Hsin’s smile showed a little tooth.

“The Prism, the permission, and the instruction.”

Suisui laid both hands upon her knees. The left had begun to tremble. She held it there without hiding it beneath the right.

“One more thing. Nobody who comes looking for me becomes part of this bargain merely by entering your room.”

“Your companions retain their lives and their choices. Only you and I are bound.”

Hsin drew one finger through the air.

A narrow line of vermilion appeared above the board. The terms gathered along it, each character holding its place without paper. Suisui read them twice.

Then she touched the final character.

The incense caught.

\---

At once, water ran beneath the table.

Suisui drew her feet back. The water was black with soot. Something red turned in it, struck the table leg, and caught there by its torn fastening.

A cap.

She knew it before she looked closely enough to know.

Beyond Hsin, the library burned.

“Norfall ended differently here,” Suisui said. Her voice came more quietly than she had intended. “The Wind showed me where she came from.”

“Yes. She knows what an altered ending costs.”

Hsin lifted the cap from the water. River mud came away between her fingers.

“She crossed the dark with a shell and an errand. She reached the far shore, and the errand went unanswered. She spent years following the life she had come to save. When at last she touched it, she came apart.”

“She found Mei.”

“She did.”

The answer was almost gentle.

The library’s windows opened into the flooded streets of another Jinzhou. A woman stood waist-deep in the water, holding a bundle above her head. Farther away, someone was trying to lift a door that had swollen shut.

“And before she found her,” Hsin continued, “how many people called?”

Suisui could hear them.

They called from houses, from boats, from rooms whose stairs had gone under. Some called names. Others had been calling so long that only the effort remained.

The sound entered beneath her ribs.

She knew this grief. The shell had carried it into Yangyang’s room, and Ciaccona’s strings had given it somewhere to stand. Yet knowing what was coming did nothing to soften the next cry.

Hsin placed the cap upon the board.

The water vanished.

Yangyang stood where the library had been.

Her sword was raised. Blood had darkened the cloth beneath her ribs. Behind her, a line of people pressed toward a gate while metal feet came down the road.

Suisui rose before she meant to.

Yangyang turned toward her voice.

Then the scene changed.

In the next, Mei reached her. Suisui saw the gold of Mei’s eyes, the marked hand extended, the relief breaking across Yangyang’s face. Beyond them, a guardian’s arm swept through a crowded shelter.

In another, the shelter stood. A girl with pink hair knelt beside a broken device and tried to hold two severed ends together. Someone was calling her name.

In another, the gates opened upon a clear road. Nobody remained to walk it.

Hsin moved a white stone.

Each scene settled into the next with the ease of a sleeve falling across a wrist.

“Every road I have laid before you ends in a death,” she said. “You may examine them as closely as you wish.”

Suisui remained standing.

The girl beside the broken device was still trying to make the ends meet.

“Will you stop that one while I speak to you?”

Hsin looked toward it. The image stilled.

For a moment Suisui could not remember the question. She could only look at the girl’s bent head.

Then she sat again.

“You said every road you have laid before me.”

“I did.”

“Are these things that will happen?”

“They are outcomes I have reason to fear.”

“Have you shown me everything that can happen?”

The Sentinel’s tail-tip moved once beside the lamp.

“I have shown you enough to understand the danger.”

Suisui let out the breath she had been holding.

There it was: one word left unspoken, and a whole country hidden behind it.

“The danger is real,” she said. “I have seen enough of it without your assistance. But you have arranged these roads so that I arrive at the same grave whichever way I turn, and you would like me to believe the grave was there before you built them.”

Hsin regarded her over the board.

“Be careful. Discovering the painter does not make a painted blade harmless to the person who must eventually meet the real one.”

“No. It tells me whose hand to watch while I ask where the real blade is.”

The lamp-flame leaned toward the Sentinel.

Hsin laughed softly.

“Jué’s young Magistrate has become a troublesome correspondent.”

“She answered my questions.”

“She survived to acquire that luxury.”

Suisui’s hands tightened upon her knees.

Hsin saw.

“At Mt. Firmament, they had old machinery, records, a damaged supply that could be restored. They had people who understood the work. A third course was found because something remained from which to build it. You speak of that mountain as though its generosity travels in Mei’s coat pocket.”

“I spoke carelessly.”

That checked the Sentinel more effectively than an argument.

Suisui looked at the motionless figure beyond her.

“I said they could carry the weight of my greed. I had no right to promise their strength before asking how much they had left.”

“Then we are making progress.”

“But Jinhsi told me what happened when Jué said his life was the price. They asked him to explain the machinery. They asked where the transfer could be stopped. She asked what he needed to keep for himself.”

The incense gave a small, dry sound.

“She told me he had to measure it.”

Hsin’s amusement thinned.

“I was not suggesting the dragon failed to keep accounts.”

“I am suggesting that someone asked a question he had stopped asking himself.”

For the first time, Hsin looked away from her.

Only toward the stone beneath her hand. Only for a moment.

Then the frozen scene moved again.

The girl called for her mother.

Suisui closed her eyes.

She did not ask Hsin to stop it a second time.

\---

The next sound was a comb falling under a chair.

When Suisui opened her eyes, she was at home.

Morning rain touched the courtyard. A practice sword lay across a child’s knees. Their mother was calling from the kitchen, with the impatience that meant breakfast had already been kept warm once.

Yangyang turned her head.

“You always pull hardest when you’re thinking about something else,” she complained. “If you tell me what it is, perhaps I can help.”

Suisui’s fingers were full of hair.

She knew the weight, the place where it divided naturally, the strand that always escaped near the left ear. The child before her smelled of sleep and the wooden sword she had insisted upon bringing to the chair.

Suisui did not move.

The comb lay beneath her foot.

Hsin stood in the courtyard with a paper umbrella over one shoulder. Rain reached the stones all around her and left her hem dry.

“This evening can also be arranged,” she said.

The child became a woman.

Yangyang sat at the same table, laughing into a cup. There was grey near Suisui’s temple. Someone beyond the kitchen door objected to the amount of firewood being used. Their mother’s flute lay across Yangyang’s lap.

Outside, the practice post had weathered to silver.

Suisui felt an old ache in her finger where it had never quite straightened. Her eyes had weakened. She leaned closer to see the tiny repair she was making in Yangyang’s cuff.

It was the coat she had carried into the Pagoda.

Three stitches would have been enough once. Now the cloth was thin all around them.

“Your sister at home,” Hsin said. “Years in which to ask about the birds. Years in which to answer the letters properly. I can give you the evening for as long as you have breath to sit within it.”

Yangyang lifted the cup.

Suisui waited for the little turn of her wrist.

It came.

So did the laugh.

The same laugh, arriving fresh.

Suisui put the needle down.

“Can she leave?”

“Why would you send her away from something you have wanted so badly?”

“That was not my question.”

Hsin tipped the umbrella back.

“The room would preserve her as you see her.”

“And when she wants something I have not imagined for her?”

The rain continued to fall.

The Sentinel watched her steadily.

“You are difficult to please.”

Suisui rose from the table.

Yangyang looked up.

The face was so dear that Suisui had to grip the chair before she could let go of it.

At the door, she turned back once. Her sister’s cup stood beside the mended cuff. The room had everything she had meant to visit for.

“I could become very happy here,” she said.

Hsin’s expression altered.

“Yes. You could.”

“I think that is the worst thing you have shown me.”

The courtyard darkened. The woman at the table became lamplight, then the pale grain of a board.

Suisui was seated before the black stone.

Her face was wet.

Hsin let her wipe it before speaking.

“You would refuse even the happiness you came to demand.”

“I came to find her. She will have her own ideas about what happens afterwards.”

“And if one of those ideas is leaving you again?”

Suisui drew the damp edge of her sleeve through her fingers.

Beyond the table, the coat lay where she had put it. Dumpling had woken and was looking toward the dark.

“Then I should like to be there when she tells me where she is going.”

\---

A faint scrape came from beyond the chamber.

Dumpling answered it.

The sound came again: cloth drawn over stone, then a light, repeated tapping at no door Suisui could see.

Hsin’s ears turned.

“Our other traveller has found the threshold.”

Suisui stood so quickly that the cushion slid behind her.

“Is that the Wind?”

“The woman who crossed between tellings is outside this room. She has followed your bird’s calls as far as my door.”

“You will let her in.”

“Our terms permit it.”

“I am asking whether you are going to make me watch her try.”

Hsin held her gaze for a breath.

Then she lifted one finger.

The room opened.

Cold crossed the floor and reached Suisui’s ankles. It climbed too quickly, lost its shape at her waist, gathered again beneath her scarf.

Suisui put both hands around the loose fold.

The familiar pressure found the hollow beneath her chin.

For several breaths she could do nothing except bend over it.

“I kept looking for you in the fog,” she said. “I couldn’t tell which way the road had turned. I thought if I went back, I might pass you without knowing.”

The cold moved against her throat.

Her loose hair rose from her cheek and settled behind her ear.

Across the table, Hsin said, “That touch is hers. Since you are about to ask.”

Suisui looked up.

“And from now on?”

“From now on, you would be wise to go on asking.”

The Wind drew out of the scarf.

She crossed to the board and stopped above the black stone. The lamp-flame flattened. Hsin’s nearest tail lifted clear of the floor.

“I remember you as well,” the Sentinel said. “You need not rearrange the furniture to establish the acquaintance.”

A white stone rolled against its neighbour.

Hsin caught it.

For an instant, something almost ordinary passed between them: an old host, an unwelcome subject, the guest who had never learned to leave it alone.

Then Hsin set the stone back.

“You are just in time to hear the price.”

Suisui’s arms went still around the empty scarf.

The Prism rose from beside Hsin’s sleeve.

Within it shone a narrow brightness, neither flame nor moonlight. The two shells stirred beneath the folds of Yangyang’s coat.

“The fog tangles a living frequency among old ones,” Hsin said. “A search can wander. A call can go unanswered. But two forms of the same beginning can be made to recognize one another without error.”

The Wind settled.

Hsin looked directly at her.

“You retained enough of that beginning to find this girl across a year of ordinary weather. Your shell retains more. If I draw out what remains of your coherent self, I can make a continuous passage to the Yangyang inside the Hold. It will hold until she reaches safety.”

Suisui heard the sentence finish.

She waited for some part of it to become another sentence.

The Sentinel continued.

“I can accomplish this with the power presently at my disposal. I can preserve Yangyang’s body through the passage. The Wind will be wholly expended in making it. There will be no hidden remnant in the shell, no voice waiting in the eaves. That is the price.”

The cold at Suisui’s hands withdrew.

She turned.

The Wind had gone to the coat.

One fold lifted. The shared cloth appeared beneath it, opening carefully at the knot. The older shell turned until its mouth faced the Prism.

“No,” Suisui said, and then drew breath because the word had come out as though she were commanding a child. “Please wait. I want you beside me while we understand this.”

The shell remained where it was.

Hsin watched without smiling.

“She understands it.”

“She has heard what you said.”

“She brought Chixia home because Yangyang asked it of her. She could bring Yangyang home as well. Shall I ask her whether she is willing?”

Suisui looked toward the shell.

The Wind had become very quiet.

She remembered the bag slipping from a shoulder that was ceasing to be a shoulder. The stockings falling at the edge of the world. A heel darned in another colour.

She remembered the flour jar.

Her other self had been making bread.

There were people who would give themselves away so readily that you might mistake their willingness for evidence that nothing precious was being taken.

Suisui laid a hand beside the shell.

“Before you answer her,” she said to the air, “will you hear me?”

The cold touched her wrist.

“I know you want her safe. I know what it was like to put Chixia into other people’s hands and come back without her. I am not going to tell you that you ought to want something less.”

Her breath caught. She waited until she could continue.

“But you came to Mengzhou for words. She wanted to hear your voice. At the mountain, she told Jué you could answer him when you returned.”

The shell trembled.

“You are allowed to want that still.”

Hsin’s voice came softly across the board.

“And if wanting it costs the younger one her life?”

Suisui turned toward her.

There was no painted fire now. No false courtyard.

Only the question.

This was where the fox had been leading her: past every scene she could expose, every word she could take apart, to a fear that needed no assistance to become convincing.

Hsin held out her empty hand.

“Give me the stone. I will ask the Wind for her free assent, and if she gives it, Yangyang comes home. You need never wonder whether you might have saved her.”

Suisui looked at the black stone.

Her fingers could remember the shape already. It would fit easily in her hand. She could lift it, cross the little width of the board, and end the uncertainty before the next breath.

Yangyang would live.

There would be dressings to change. Food to coax into her. The argument about how much she had written in her letters and how little she had said.

One sister beside her.

The other nowhere.

Hsin did not hurry her.

Suisui put her hand upon the board.

The Wind crossed her knuckles. A small pressure, gentle and deliberate, tried to ease her fingers toward the stone.

Suisui bowed her head.

For a moment she let herself feel how easy it would be.

Then she turned her hand over beneath the cold.

“Stay here a little longer,” she said. “I haven’t finished asking.”

\---

The incense had bent beneath its own ash.

Suisui looked at Hsin.

“You have told us the way by which you can guarantee her return. What can the Prism do if nobody is spent?”

“Less.”

“Please tell me how much less.”

“It can distinguish frequencies. It can carry a call through places ordinary sound cannot reach. With a living answer to guide it, it can reveal a passage through the Shroud.”

The cold above Suisui’s palm moved.

“And without an answer?”

“It may follow an echo. The way may fail. The person you seek may be too weak to respond.”

“Can it protect her while we try to reach her?”

“Within limits. Every portion used for that purpose is power that cannot be carried onward to my true self.”

There it was at last: a measure of power, an unfinished road, something difficult which had not yet been tried.

Suisui looked down at her open hand.

“The sure road takes the Wind,” she said. “The uncertain one asks Yangyang to answer.”

“It asks much more than that of all of you.”

“Then tell us those things as well.”

Hsin’s gaze sharpened.

“You believe I have concealed an easy kindness.”

“I believe you have described a possibility as though its uncertainty were the same thing as its absence.”

The room became very still.

Suisui could hear the faint mechanism of Dumpling’s movement upon the coat. Beyond it, nothing.

Hsin lowered her hand.

“You may attempt the uncertain road and lose both of them. The Wind will try to hold it when it begins to fail. You have seen enough of her to know that. You may stand with the Prism in your hands and discover that the life you refused to spend is being spent anyway, too late to purchase anything.”

Suisui swallowed.

“I know.”

“Do you? You have had time to love the idea of a third course. You have not yet heard your sister calling from the far end of one while it closes.”

The black stone warmed beneath the lamp.

Suisui’s hand had begun to move toward it again. She brought the hand back to her knee.

“No,” she said. “I have not.”

Hsin’s eyes rested upon her.

Suisui could not look away.

“If that happens, I will have to live through what happens. I cannot make myself equal to it beforehand. But you are asking me to kill someone who is here because you can make me afraid enough of someone who is not.”

The Wind pressed against the inside of her wrist.

“I am very afraid,” Suisui continued. “You have not failed to make me understand that part.”

The Sentinel’s face changed by a degree too small to name.

Behind her, the board’s white lines widened into roads.

Along one of them, Yangyang stood upon the broken checkpoint landing.

Chixia was bleeding. The Wind strained beneath her weight. Yangyang’s fingers held the cuff in which she had so often sheltered her older self.

Her voice reached the room exactly as it had reached the gorge.

“One of us has to make it back.”

Suisui’s head turned.

She saw the bag placed over Chixia’s shoulder. The careful lifting of the buckle clear of the wound. Their foreheads touching.

She had heard the account.

She had not seen her sister’s hands.

“This occurred,” Hsin said. “I have altered none of her words.”

Yangyang gave the rising air to the Wind.

Chixia’s fingers slipped.

Suisui made a sound she would have been ashamed to hear from anyone else.

Hsin let the image go.

“She made the choice you condemn.”

For a while Suisui could only breathe.

Then she lifted her head.

“She sent for help.”

“She remained where death could reach her.”

“Yes. And she wanted the people who left to bring the others back.”

Hsin said nothing.

Suisui’s voice steadied as the distinction became clear enough to hold.

“She said one of them had to make it home. She did not say only one of them was allowed to. You opened your gates, and now you would like to close that sentence behind her.”

The Wind rose sharply from her wrist.

Hsin’s ears went still.

Suisui stood.

“Did you open them for her?”

“Yes.”

“Did you know we would come after her?”

“I judged that you would.”

“Mei and the pack had already agreed to visit you. They were bringing the Wind to the founders. Why was that not enough?”

“Because a visit may be postponed.”

“And a wound cannot.”

Hsin looked at her without answering.

Suisui felt the room sway. She put a hand against the table.

The Sentinel could have given her another burning city. Instead, she left the silence bare.

At last she said, “My guardians are striking the people they were made to shelter. My reach has narrowed while the things reaching into my country have multiplied. There are reasons I cannot put into an open summons. There are powers I needed here before the roads were wholly lost.”

“So you chose someone they would not leave behind.”

“I chose a bond I believed would hold.”

Suisui looked toward the cold beside her.

The Wind had crossed worlds to answer an absence. She had found the shore, the sleeper, the table. She had spent her last word on a day she wanted to be present for.

Hsin had found that healed place and put a blade against it.

“You knew what bringing Chixia back would cost her,” Suisui said.

“I knew what failing to bring anyone here might cost Xuanfang.”

“And Yangyang?”

The Sentinel’s hand closed upon her sleeve.

“I knew she might die.”

The words settled between them.

No illusion had been as terrible.

Suisui stood with the truth for a long time. The anger came so strongly that for a moment she wanted to sweep the whole board into the dark.

Then the Wind touched her elbow.

The old correction.

Suisui looked down at the place where a sword might have been.

She let her arm loosen.

“You brought Mei here because you believe she may do something you cannot,” she said.

“Yes.”

“And you brought the others because she does not do those things alone.”

Hsin watched her.

“You know what happened at Norfall. You know how many voices it took. You know what they did on the mountain. You did not go to all this trouble to obtain another player who would make the move already waiting beneath your hand.”

The Sentinel’s expression closed.

“Choose your next words carefully.”

Suisui sat again.

She was tired enough now that sitting hurt.

“You have been asking me to accept that this game has only one ending,” she said. “But every dangerous thing you have done to bring them here depends upon your hope that it does not.”

Hsin’s tails ceased moving.

“You cannot frighten me out of believing in the very thing you wagered my sister’s life to obtain.”

\---

The room went dark beyond the lamp.

When Hsin spoke, the teasing had left her voice.

“There was a winter when I refused to abandon an outer settlement. I could hold the crossing another night. The scouts believed relief would reach it by morning.”

A white stone turned beneath her finger.

“I gave the order. The relief column arrived after the crossing fell. We lost the settlement and the people I had sent to save it.”

She moved her hand away.

“There are names from that night which I have never entrusted to a clerk. I still know where each man stood when I last saw him. When I say that an uncertain road may take more than a certain loss, I am not decorating an argument.”

Suisui listened.

The Wind settled at the edge of her scarf.

Hsin looked at the bowed incense.

“You ask why I count. Because the dead do not become fewer when the person who ordered them forward had an admirable reason.”

“No,” Suisui said. “They do not.”

“And if your road fails?”

“I will not ask you to call the failure a victory.”

The Sentinel raised her eyes.

Suisui’s throat tightened. She thought of the coat. Of Baizhi asking her to bring it because the road would be cold.

“I cannot promise that no one will die. I wanted to, when I came here. I wanted a power large enough that I would never have to hear what happened on that landing again.”

She placed her hand beside the black stone.

“But I have listened to you. I have listened to her.”

The cold shifted against her neck.

“Courage did not save everyone in the Wind’s world. Love did not make the crossing kind to her. I know there are people she can never bring home.”

Hsin’s gaze remained steady.

Suisui continued.

“She still came back to this one. She found a girl at a window and helped her hold her elbow correctly. When her breath ran out, the girl played the rest of the note.”

The lamp-flame moved.

“That is the future you did not put on your board. Not because nobody bleeds in it. Because someone may yet arrive with something the person standing here does not have.”

“You are waiting for a kindness.”

“I am preparing to ask for help. I should have done so before I called their lives a trump card.”

A little ash broke from the incense and fell beside its holder.

Hsin watched it land.

Then she offered her hand one last time.

“The bargain is still open. I can bring Yangyang home. You may refuse me, take the Prism, attempt your passage, and lose her within the hour. Nothing you have said prevents it.”

Suisui looked at the waiting palm.

She imagined the letter she would have to write.

She imagined going home with the coat folded over her arm. Her mother would see it before Suisui found the words. There would be a sound from the kitchen, something set down too quickly.

Her fingers closed against her knees.

Hsin waited.

Suisui bent her head until her forehead nearly touched the edge of the board.

For a little while she could not speak.

The Wind moved out of the scarf. She passed beneath Suisui’s chin, trying to raise it, then stopped trying and remained there.

Suisui took one breath. Another.

When she looked up, her face was unguarded.

“If I give you the stone, I will know exactly where one of my sisters has gone,” she said. “You have made that very tempting.”

Hsin’s hand did not move.

“But I want to find out where both of them can still go.”

The black stone remained upon the board.

The last red point of the incense dimmed.

Hsin looked from it to Suisui.

Then, without a sound, the ash fell.

\---

Nothing opened.

No music rose from the dark. The moon continued to hang where Hsin had put it, and Suisui remained seated with her hands cramped upon her knees.

She looked at the incense until she was certain it had gone out.

Only then did she breathe deeply enough to hurt.

Hsin withdrew her hand.

“You are a very troublesome guest.”

“I did try to make an appointment first.”

A small sound escaped the Sentinel.

It held little amusement. Enough to be recognizable.

She lifted the Prism.

The vermilion terms unwound above the board. Their characters passed into its facets one by one, leaving a thin red brightness at the centre. Hsin touched that brightness with her thumb.

“This names you as bearer. The authority you borrowed brought you into my room. My word will let you use what you came for.”

Suisui held out both hands.

The Prism settled into them.

It was warmer than she expected.

For the first time she felt how little of Hsin it contained, and how much that little still had to do.

The Sentinel’s outline had thinned around one shoulder. The darkness showed through a strand of her hair.

Suisui looked up.

“You are included in what I said.”

Hsin’s ears tilted.

“You have won a fragment of my tail and are already advising me how to live.”

“I am asking what you need to keep. Jinhsi said it was a useful question.”

For a moment Hsin looked very old.

Then the expression passed.

“Enough to guide you out. Enough to answer when the Prism is called. The rest is a matter for a less agreeable conversation with my true self.”

“I will expect that conversation.”

“I have no doubt you will arrive with notes.”

Suisui closed her fingers more securely around the Prism.

“And Yangyang will need to hear what you told me.”

“Yes.”

“I cannot give you her forgiveness.”

“I did not place it among the stakes.”

They regarded one another across the board.

Hsin reached for the black stone.

Suisui’s hand moved before she could stop it.

The Sentinel paused.

Then she turned her own hand palm-up, empty, and left the stone where it was.

“Our wager is concluded,” she said. “No further move is owed.”

Suisui let her hand fall.

For several breaths neither woman spoke.

At last Hsin said, “Do not be so charitable as to decide I intended to lose. I thought you would take the certain road.”

“I nearly did.”

“I know. That is why I have not found the last part amusing.”

She rose.

The table’s pale lines began to loosen. They flowed across the wood like water released from channels, gathered at one edge, and descended into the darkness.

Beyond them appeared a road.

It was narrow. Nothing about it suggested certainty.

“My ally,” Hsin said, then let the old address rest.

“Suisui. For now, I place my hope in your hands.”

Suisui looked down at what she held.

It shone through the gaps between her fingers.

“Then please tell me how to carry it without dropping anyone.”

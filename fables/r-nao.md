Chapter 1: Rè Nao

The lower course of the eastern breach no longer pointed at the third company’s barracks like an iron arrow.

It did not point at anything in the known world. Over the course of three mornings, forty feet of dressed grey granite had been levered, wedged, shimmed with split roof-slates, and turned three degrees off the mason’s cord at every fourth joint, so that the new footing wound across the ditch in a slow, deliberate wave like an arthritic snake.

Halfway up the middle tier, a three-foot circle of lime mortar had been baked into a bubbled, glassy black crust. Yesterday afternoon, tired of waiting for the damp mountain air to stiffen the bedding paste, Buling had pasted a Five-Thunder quick-drying slip onto the buttress and snapped her fingers. The blast had vitrified the lime, blown Bai Tieshan’s wooden plumb-level into the turnip patch, and left a soot fan the height of a man up the old stonework.

Qingxiao stood on the wet deal plank above the trench, her hands tucked inside her moon-white cuffs, looking down the length of the serpentine masonry.

Six paces to her left, perched high on the unbroken spur of the old parapet with her white ankle socks crossed neatly at the shin and a ledger open on her knee, Ruoyun didn’t even bother to look down.

“Go on,” Ruoyun told the morning mist. “Look at the culvert corner. Ask her why there’s an earthenware pickle crock mortared into the drain hole.”

Down in the shadow behind the stacked lime kegs, where a wide pine wheelbarrow had been tipped onto its side to block the gorge wind, the round black crown of an official’s hat poked up over the wheel-rim.

Buling was sitting on an upturned mortar-bucket with her knees drawn up under her royal-blue cheongsam, a chipped enamel mug of thick, condensed-milk coffee held in both hands to warm her palms. She lifted the yellow paper talisman off her forehead with her chin so she could see up the bank.

“That crock is an acoustic resonator,” Buling called up cheerfully. “The gorge wind comes up the ditch like a flute. Before, every time it blew from the south, the stones were vibrating at a bad pitch—making the whole wall tired. Now the crock catches the draft and turns it round. You listen.”

A gust of cold river air swept up the ramp. Passing the lip of the buried pickle jar, it let out a low, mournful *whooo-uh* like a cow in a well.

On the parapet, Ruoyun closed her eyes behind her round brass spectacles. “I have to listen to that until the walls fall down. Every time the wind shifts east, the breach moos at me.”

“It is grounding the sha qi,” Buling said with dignity, letting the paper strip drop back over her nose as she took a sip of coffee. “And don’t scold the curve. Straight line in a mountain gap is asking for a landslide. You put a straight rigid bone against a rushing dragon vein, one big knock and *pak*, broken again. You bend it a little bit, the force slides off into the cabbages. Even the head mason agreed with me.”

“You bribed the head mason off to the west gatehouse!” Ruoyun barked, pointing her bamboo brush straight down at the wheelbarrow. “And you slept behind those kegs till noon!”

“My ankles were cold! This ditch has zero sun before lunch!”

Qingxiao stepped off the deal plank onto the middle course of the crooked wall.

She set the sole of her white boot against the projecting corner of the granite block nearest the blackened mortar patch and gave it a single, solid push.

The stone did not shift by the breadth of a hair. Wedged against its neighbors at three competing angles and fused at the seam by the thunder-baked lime, the ugly grey rib sat locked into the shale like an iron tooth.

Qingxiao looked from the glassy black scorch mark to the mooing pickle crock, and then up at Buling’s hat behind the wheelbarrow.

“You are worse at masonry than you were at the stick,” Qingxiao said.

Buling lowered her enamel mug an inch. “Shifu, the wall is standing—”

“It is an eyesore,” Qingxiao said evenly. “And if you fire another thunder slip into wet lime, Tieshan will throw his trowel at you.”

Up on the parapet, Ruoyun let out a sharp, triumphant breath and dipped her brush into the portable ink-pot hooked to her thumb. “Good. Put her back on the south sluice. The grate’s choked with willow roots as thick as my wrist, and she can’t blow up a mud-bank.”

“No,” Qingxiao said.

Ruoyun’s brush stopped dead.

Qingxiao turned on the crooked granite block, the pale ribbon of frequency drifting lightly around her elbows in the river draft. “The quiet we have now will not hold long. When the barrier fails, the Fox will still be missing, and a mason who sleeps until noon is no use in the ditch.”

She looked down at Buling.

“Pack your canvas bag,” Qingxiao said. “Ruoyun will take you up the hill to the third storehouse. Yangyang is staying there with the foreigners from across the sea.”

For two beats the only sound in the breach was the buried pickle jar going *whooo* in the breeze.

Then Buling scrambled up off the mortar-bucket so fast she nearly spilled her coffee, her red eyes shining behind the yellow paper strip.

“Foreigners!” Buling said. “Wah, Shifu, very forward-thinking. International delegation, they definitely need a local consultant to explain the terrain. Do they have a stove inside? What’s the roof like?” She paused, her eyes narrowing under the brim of her hat. “Wait first. Third storehouse is the one above the cooper’s lane? That’s two hundred steps up the cliff.”

“Two hundred and forty,” Ruoyun said flatly.

She had closed the leather logbook on her knee with a snap. She slid off the high stone parapet, landing lightly on the gravel in her dark strapped shoes without a sound, and marched three paces across the cut to plant herself in front of Qingxiao.

“Have you lost your mind, A-Qing?” Ruoyun demanded. “You gave her to me until the second-month thaw! Putting her in a heated storehouse with a pack of tourists who fry meat three times a day isn’t a sentence, it’s a boarding-house holiday!”

“When it comes, they will be standing in the middle of it,” Qingxiao said. “They always are. She knows the old seams in the gorges. She can earn her rice with them.”

“Then let her walk up the stairs by herself!” Ruoyun’s hand drifted halfway up toward her left horn before she caught it and jammed her fist onto her hip instead. “I am not marching into that building in broad daylight. Have you walked past that door at breakfast? There are eight of them in there and it sounds like forty! That gigantic girl with the wolf tail threw an iron skillet at a rat on Tuesday and dented my rain-barrel!”

“She missed the rat,” Buling noted with interest, tucking her enamel mug into her canvas shoulder-bag. “Bad feng shui by the rain-barrel, I can fix for her.”

“You can shut your mouth!” Ruoyun wheeled back on Qingxiao, her voice dropping half a register. “They’ve got the whole lower lane staring up the hill as it is. If I walk through that door, I’ll have six different foreigners gawking at my head and asking me where the latrine trenches are.”

“Yangyang is my youngest disciple,” Qingxiao said mildly. “You are delivering her senior to her door. You do not need to stay for tea.”

Buling froze with her canvas strap halfway over her shoulder. Slowly, she pushed the yellow talisman up onto her hat brim with one knuckle.

“Wait,” Buling said. “The Mingting girl with the blue feathers on her collar? She’s your disciple now?”

“Since the rain,” Qingxiao said.

Buling’s chest expanded by two full inches under her royal-blue silk. She smoothed her lapels with both palms and gave a solemn, elder-stateswoman nod. “I see. New batch. Very green. No wonder you need me upstairs, Shifu—somebody has to teach my little shimei how to survive your morning stick-drill without getting lumps on the head.”

“She does not get lumps,” Qingxiao said. “She keeps her left heel down.”

With that, the Sword Transcendent stepped off the crooked granite course onto the timber ramp and walked down toward the inner courtyard, her moon-white hem clearing the lime dust without a smudge.

Buling stood beaming in the gravel for three seconds, already reaching into her side slit to check her Terminal, until Ruoyun’s fingers clamped onto the back of her royal-blue cuff like an iron bench-vise.

“Put that glass away and pick up your crowbar,” Ruoyun said grimly. “If I have to walk into that madhouse before lunch, you’re carrying your own tools up the steps.”

***

Inside the converted Ministry storehouse at the top of the hill, the third bell of the morning was underway at full volume.

At the brick hearth, Lupa was working two cast-iron skillets at once over an open blaze that had turned the room into a bakery oven. She had a mountain of fried dough-twists draining on brown paper to her left, a pot of ginger-and-mutton broth bubbling at the back, and a long iron fork in her fist as she turned a batch of scallions in spitting lard.

“Don’t you touch that paper, bard!” Lupa barked without turning her head as Ciaccona’s green sleeve drifted lazily down from the window-seat toward the top twist on the pile. “They’re for the Wardens on the gate! Eat the broken ones on the saucer!”

“The broken ones are cold,” Ciaccona complained from the sill, where she lay flat on her back with her boots crossed on top of her lute case and her feathered hat balanced on her stomach. “A cold dough-twist is just a candle you can chew. Cartethyia, tell the wolf I’m wasting away.”

Over by the sunny corner of the east window, Cartethyia had the glass lid off her small wooden terrarium and was carefully wiping a bead of condensation from the inside of the pane with the corner of her handkerchief so the camellia cutting could catch the morning light.

“Lupa,” Cartethyia said earnestly, “if Cia faints on the stairs, I’ll have to carry her and her lute, and the pegs always poke me in the collarbone.”

“She’s got three ribs of mutton in her from last night, she’s not fainting before Thursday,” Lupa snorted.

At the long pine table in the middle of the room, Chisa sat in her crisp black uniform blouse and pale blue silk tie, methodically peeling a bowl of boiled chestnuts with a small paring knife and setting every second nut onto a clean saucer beside Lupa’s elbow. Across the scarred wood from her—right past the lopsided four-legged linen turtle that still held the place of honor by the salt—Amy was sitting sideways on the bench with Lupa’s spare sheepskin vest hanging off one shoulder and her chin hooked over the table edge.

She was squinting fiercely at a folded sheet of foolscap sitting three inches from Iuno’s left knee, reading it upside down while Iuno wrote with a sharpened cedar pencil.

“What’s item seven?” Amy asked, tapping her fingernail on the pine. “You wrote *two yards of waxed linen* twice, and your *g*s look like little ducks when you write upside down.”

Iuno did not cover the page; she simply shifted her elbow half an inch so the crystal at her left shoulder caught the sun and threw a bright prism of blue light straight across Amy’s nose.

“The first two yards are for the medicine tins,” Iuno said mildly, making a neat tick on her pad. “The second two are for wrapping your mother’s spare boots, because the next time the sky comes down on this mountain she will inevitably be standing in the cistern. And stop breathing on my ink.”

“It’s pencil!”

Down on the floorboards beside the warmth of the ash-box, Mei was sitting cross-legged with a wooden darning egg in her lap and a thick steel needle between her fingers, attempting to mend a hole in the heel of her wool sock. Every time she drew the yarn tight, the heel puckered a little rounder, until the wool ball had grown four distinct corner-lumps and a snout.

On the side-trivet two feet above her head, the heavy two-gallon iron kettle began to rattle its lid, spitting boiling drops onto the stove-plate with a sharp *hiss-clatter*.

Mei didn’t even think about it. She stuck the needle into the wool, braced one palm on the floor to push herself up, and reached her right hand toward the scorching iron handle—

“Mei!”

“Ma!”

“Drop it!”

Iuno’s pencil didn’t even stop moving; her booted foot simply slid out under the bench and hooked lightly over Mei’s shin, pinning her ankle to the floorboard, while Amy leaned thirty degrees off the bench to snatch the quilted pot-holder away and Lupa swung the butt of her iron fork round like a semaphore flag.

Mei froze with her hand four inches from the steam, then pulled her fingers back into her lap and grinned sheepishly up at the three of them.

“I was just going to slide it onto the cold brick!” Mei protested, holding up the lumpy sock. “Sliding isn’t lifting! Ask Yangyang, sliding uses zero stomach muscles!”

Over by the open doorway, where Yangyang sat on the low three-legged stool pulling a dry rag through the guard of her sword while Dumpling dozed on top of the grain chest, Yangyang looked up with a completely level face.

“The kettle weighs fourteen pounds with the water in it,” Yangyang said gently. “Chisa can slide it.”

Chisa was already rising from the bench, wiping chestnut crumbs from her fingers—

And then she stopped with one hand on the table edge, her head tilting half an inch toward the timber door before a sound had reached anyone else’s ears.

“Two people on the landing,” Chisa said. “One of them is dragging her feet.”

Half a second later, someone rapped twice on the heavy pine door—not asking if anyone was home, just two brisk, peremptory knocks of an iron latch-bar—and shoved the door open without waiting for an answer.

Ruoyun stepped over the threshold first, her left hand locked around the loose silk of Buling’s right elbow as she hauled the jiangshi inside and gave her a firm shove toward the center of the floorboards.

“Don’t scuff the sill,” Ruoyun snapped over her shoulder. “I planed that oak strip myself in the autumn and I’m not doing it again because you can’t pick your heels up.”

“My shoes have flat soles, very slippery on frost!” Buling complained, immediately craning her neck around the yellow paper talisman on her nose to inspect the room. “Wah. Big stove. Door facing the east window, kettle on the south fire-mouth—okay, not bad, who put the water-butt behind the door? That’s blocking the wealth corner—”

In the storehouse, three things happened at once.

By the grain chest, Yangyang stood up from the three-legged stool so fast her scabbard tapped the wall, her spine going straight and completely still as she stared at the small golden horns and the round brass spectacles in the doorway.

At the pine table, Iuno stopped her pencil in the middle of a word and set it down flat on the pad.

*Well, darling,* the High Priestess murmured inside her head, sounding distinctly entertained. *She has considerably more color in her cheeks upstairs, and much better sleeves.*

And from the window-seat, Ciaccona didn’t even lift her head off her lute case. She simply pushed the brim of her feathered hat up half an inch with one thumb, looked sideways across the room at the silver hair, the bright red eyes, the old-dynasty official’s hat, and the royal-blue silk cheongsam, and let out a single, delighted snort.

“Madonna,” Ciaccona said to the rafters. “Look what the mountain coughed up. Did you lose a bet with an opera troupe, Buling, or are you haunting magistrates for a living now?”

Buling’s head whipped toward the window-seat. The yellow talisman swung left across her cheek, and her whole face lit up—though she immediately crossed her arms and tucked her chin down as if she’d known Ciaccona was lying on that sill all along.

“Eh, redhead,” Buling said briskly. “Still lying down in the daytime ah? Very consistent. And don’t anyhow insult the hat, this is authentic winter court style, very warm on the ears.”

“Wait—Buling?” Mei scrambled halfway up before Iuno’s hand settled firmly on her shoulder and pressed her back down onto the floorboards. Mei craned her neck around the table leg anyway, her golden eyes wide as she looked from the yellow talisman bands at Buling’s ankles up to her red eyes. “From the café in Septimont! With the little braid and the headphones! You changed your hair!”

“And your eyes,” Lupa grunted from the stove, squinting through the scallion steam with her iron fork held at guard. “Last time I saw you, you were sitting under an umbrella drinking iced coffee while the street shook.”

“That was business travel mode,” Buling explained, waving a hand airily as she stepped toward the stove to see what was in the skillet. “Out there you walk around like this, people scream, call the temple, very troublesome for invoicing. Here is home ground, no need to waste energy.”

By the east window, Cartethyia carefully set the glass lid back on her terrarium and took two quick, polite steps forward, her crystal laurel glinting in the sun as she bowed toward Ruoyun.

“Good morning, my lady!” Cartethyia said brightly. “I am Cartethyia of Egla Town. Are you a friend of Buling’s?”

At the words *my lady*, and at *friend of Buling’s*, Ruoyun’s spine went so rigid she seemed to grow an inch taller in her strapped shoes.

Every trace of the barking sergeant vanished from her face. She drew her heels together on the threshold, folded her white ruffled cuffs neatly over her wrists, and inclined her head to Cartethyia at the exact, glacial angle a viceroy’s widow uses for a tax-collector she intends to have drowned in the canal.

“You are too courteous, Miss Knight,” Ruoyun said, her voice dropping into freezing, crystalline formality. “I am merely the woman who clears the leaves from the Hold’s gutters. I would not presume to claim acquaintance with an international consultant.”

On the bench, Amy’s mouth fell slightly open. She looked from Ruoyun’s icy bow to Yangyang, who still hadn’t moved from the grain chest, and then down at Iuno’s motionless hand on the writing pad.

“Miss Ruoyun,” Yangyang said quietly, inclining her head from the chest.

Ruoyun shot Yangyang a single, sharp glance through her spectacles—warning her off with half an inch of eyebrow—and turned her chin back toward the center of the room.

“The Paragon inspected the eastern wall this morning,” Ruoyun announced to the rafters, clip-clapping every syllable off her teeth. “She has concluded that this person is a danger to the masonry, the quartermaster’s lime supply, and the hearing of the third company. As you are evidently staying, and as she allegedly knows what to do when the ground goes wrong, the Paragon has reassigned her to your quarters until the second-month thaw. She is your problem now.”

***

For two seconds nobody spoke except the mutton broth bubbling at the back of the stove.

Then Buling stepped neatly out from in front of Ruoyun, hooked two fingers into her side slit, and flicked a slightly crumpled business card onto the pine table beside Mei’s linen turtle.

“Don’t listen to her, she’s just sour because she doesn’t understand modern geomancy,” Buling told the room at large. “Allow me to re-introduce. Buling, senior artifact maker, feng shui consultant, top 1 percent contributor on the Huangboard—handle *BigThunder88*. For friends of the redhead, I give twenty percent discount on site readings. Room layout, compass calibration, cursed kitchenware, all can do. If you got a ghost in the luggage, thirty percent extra, payable before lunch.”

Amy leaned across the table and snatched up the card, turning it over in her fingers with immediate professional respect. “Wait, you’re *BigThunder88*? You’re the one who got into a ninety-post flame war with the Huaxu Academy guy over copper conductivity on Tuesday!”

“That guy is an idiot,” Buling said flatly. “He only tests in a dry lab. You bring his resonator out in the mountain fog, two minutes and it pops like a chestnut. Look at your bard here—when she was stuck in Septimont, who gave her the Sacred Bell of the Nine Heavens’ Thunder to split the illusion open? Me. High-grade craft.”

On the window-seat, Ciaccona finally swung her boots off her lute case and sat up, resting her elbows on her knees.

“Sacred Bell of the Nine Heavens’ Thunder,” Ciaccona repeated dryly. “Buling, it was a three-inch copper cowbell with a piece of yellow paper stuck to the handle, and you threw it at me across a café table because you didn’t want to walk up the hill yourself.”

“Efficiency is part of the Dao,” Buling said.

Ciaccona’s mouth twisted at the corner; then she looked across the room at Ruoyun and Iuno, and the teasing went out of her voice, leaving it plain and steady.

“It worked, though,” Ciaccona said. “Whatever she did to the metal, when we rang it in the dark, it pulled twenty years of buried noise straight out of the stone. If she hadn’t handed me that bell at the café, we’d still be walking in circles under the basilica.”

“Oh!” Cartethyia clapped her hands together in front of her chest. “The little bell from the square! That was yours? It had such a clear voice—like a chapel bell under water!”

Down on the floorboards beside Iuno’s shin, Mei’s smile faded.

She set the wool sock and the darning egg down beside the ash-box and looked up at Buling.

“Buling,” Mei said quietly. “I’m the one who had your bell last. At the bottom of the sea, when the sky came down.”

She rested her palm flat on her knee, her golden eyes direct and sorry.

“I rang it as hard as I could to break the floor open,” Mei said. “The copper cracked across the lip, and when the water came in, I couldn’t hold onto it. I’m sorry. I didn’t bring it back.”

The noisy room went quiet around the stove.

Buling looked down at Mei sitting on the floorboards. Her right hand went to her own waist, where a small brass bell no bigger than a walnut hung from a red cord at her hip. Her thumb touched the rim once without ringing it.

For a second Buling didn’t make a joke, and she didn’t quote a price.

“The copper one was made to ring once in a bad place,” Buling said simply. “If the floor broke and you came out, means the bell finished its work. No need to invoice.”

She lifted her chin, sniffed once at the air drifting from the hearth, and pointed a finger at Lupa’s skillet.

“Anyway, if you feel bad, give me two of those hot dough-twists from the middle,” Buling added briskly. “Not the broken ones on the saucer. The long ones with the sesame.”

“Ha!” Mei’s face broke back into a grin; she reached up to tap the edge of the table. “Lupa, give her the biggest one on the paper!”

“Wait just a minute,” Ruoyun cut in from the doorway.

She hadn’t moved a foot inside the threshold, and she wasn’t smiling at all. Her bright blue eyes fixed on the back of Buling’s neck through her brass lenses.

“A bell,” Ruoyun said. “How very grand. She gives away a three-copper cowbell in a coffee shop five hundred miles away and you’re ready to feed her the fat of the pan. Did she mention on her little glass board that she walked out of this Hold on an errand for red ink and left the post empty until the walls fell in?”

Buling’s shoulders stiffened by a fraction of an inch under the blue silk. She didn’t turn around; she reached out and nudged the stoneware water-crock by the table leg two inches to the left with the toe of her shoe so it cleared the draft-line from the door.

“I came back when I heard the drum,” Buling said to the water-crock.

“You came back two days after the bleeding stopped,” Ruoyun said, her voice clean and hard and entirely conversational. “And the only reason you’re standing in this kitchen instead of halfway down the gorge in a basket is that the Paragon has grown soft in her old age and doesn’t like the mess on the steps.”

Buling didn’t answer that one. She took her foot off the base of the crock, walked over to the upturned flour crate by the pantry curtain, and sat down on it with her hands tucked into her sleeves.

Over by the stove, Lupa slowly lowered her iron fork. A wide, feral Septimontian grin spread across her face, showing her canines as she looked Ruoyun up and down—from the small golden horns to the pressed white ruffles and the Mary Jane shoes.

“I like her,” Lupa announced to Chisa. “She talks like my first spear-master in the pits. Hey, glasses—you want a dough-twist? You can have the big one.”

“I do not eat between meals,” Ruoyun said stiffly. “And if you don’t take that second skillet off the front ring, you’re going to scorch the lard and stink up my ceiling beams for a month.”

Lupa barked a laugh and yanked the skillet two inches back onto the trivet anyway.

At the pine table, Iuno rested her right forearm on the wood and looked across the room at Ruoyun. Ruoyun looked back at her over the rim of her brass spectacles.

Between them, both women took in the exact hang of the other’s sleeves, the set of the chin, and the utter refusal to apologize for existing in a room.

“You are the one who keeps the roof-slates straight on this hill,” Iuno said evenly.

“Someone has to,” Ruoyun replied. “Since the tenants treat the rafters like a gymnasium.”

“Then I will refrain from thanking you for the dry floorboards,” Iuno said, the corner of her mouth curving by a hair, “as you look as though you would bite my head off if I tried.”

Ruoyun’s chin came up half an inch. “A sensible woman,” she said, and looked away toward the grain chest where Yangyang was standing.

On the upturned flour crate, Buling had already recovered her bounce. Seeing Ruoyun look at Yangyang, Buling leaned sideways on the crate, hooked her elbow over the back of the pantry chair, and wagged two fingers at Yangyang’s sword.

“Eh, little bird,” Buling said cheerfully. “Shifu told me you’re the new disciple. Don’t be nervous. When she tells you to do five hundred swings in the snow, you just listen to your shijie—I know all the shortcuts.”

The word dropped into the room like a coin hitting a stone floor.

Yangyang didn’t flinch, and she didn’t look angry. Her hands simply went still on the oiled rag around her scabbard, and at the side of her throat, the small cerulean feathers of her collar lifted once and settled flat against her skin.

Down by the ash-box, Mei’s head turned half an inch toward Yangyang’s boots; at the table, Iuno’s fingers closed lightly around her pencil.

Buling’s red eyes flicked from Yangyang’s collar to the quiet around her mouth, and then to the way Mei had looked over. It took Buling less than a second.

She lifted one hand and tapped the brim of her black official’s hat with a loud *pap*.

“No, wait,” Buling corrected herself without missing half a beat. “Cannot call shijie. Wrong hat. Look at this brim—this is third-rank magistrate headwear. Shijie is for small girls in matching robes. You call me Senior Consultant. Or Grand-Auntie of the Stick. Much more auspicious for the seniority.”

Yangyang blinked once. The tightness around her shoulders eased out on her next breath, and the corner of her mouth tipped up just enough to show.

“Senior Consultant,” Yangyang repeated in her plain, gentle voice. “Does that mean you take the first turn with the heavy practice staff?”

“Cannot!” Buling said immediately, waving both hands in front of her talisman. “Consultant is purely advisory! I stand under the tree with a cup of tea and tell you when your elbow is crooked!”

***

“You will stand wherever you’re told to stand,” Ruoyun said, stepping fully over the threshold at last to close the ledger she’d been carrying against her hip.

While she’d been talking, Buling had already slid six inches to the left on the flour crate, hooked her boot heel behind the leg of Mei’s low wooden footstool, and dragged it half a foot away from the ash-box so it sat out of the straight line between the stove and the door. Then, without rising an inch from the crate, Buling leaned thirty degrees sideways across the end of the pine table, lifted her forehead talisman with her left pinky, and snatched a hot fried dough-twist straight off the brown paper beside Chisa’s elbow, stuffing half of it into her mouth before Lupa’s fork could come down.

“Hey!” Lupa snapped, though her tail gave a single amused thump against the bricks.

Ruoyun ignored the theft entirely and marched up to the end of the pine table, stopping squarely in front of Iuno.

She looked down at Iuno’s immaculate posture, the silver-blue hair pinned with court precision, and the pad of foolscap with its numbered columns.

“You’re the one running this camp,” Ruoyun said to Iuno, tapping the spine of her closed ledger with one finger. “So I’ll give the terms to you.”

On the bench, Amy clapped a hand over her mouth, her eyes crinkling above her fingers as she looked down at the floor.

Iuno did not correct her for half a second. She simply sat with her hand resting near her teacup while Ruoyun laid it out.

“Don’t pay her in advance for anything,” Ruoyun said briskly. “If she moves your bed and hands you a bill, put it in the stove.”

Around a mouthful of fried dough, Buling made an indignant noise through her nose.

“Keep her away from the lime,” Ruoyun continued without looking at her. “And if she slips off down the mule road because she met a client at the crossing, shout. I’ll hear. I’ll fetch her back by the other ear myself.”

Ruoyun stopped and looked expectantly down through her spectacles at Iuno. “Understood?”

Iuno inclined her head with grave courtesy, and then looked down at the floorboards beside her left boot.

“I only keep the lists,” Iuno said mildly. “You want the one with the sock.”

Ruoyun’s gaze dropped from the bench to the floor.

Sitting cross-legged against Iuno’s shin, with grey hearth-ash on her knee and the four-legged wool sock-turtle balanced on her palm, Mei beamed up at Ruoyun and gave a cheerful salute with the darning needle.

“We’ll keep her busy!” Mei promised. “Nobody’s getting fetched by the ear, though. Do you want to sit down for a minute? The floor by the bricks is really warm.”

Ruoyun stared down at the Arbiter of the realm sitting in the dust with a mangled sock, then up at the nine-foot saint currently compressed into a five-foot girl offering Buling a saucer of pickled radish, and then at Amy, who had already leaned across the table to ask Buling if she had the offline archive of the Jinzhou noodle-stall rankings on her Terminal.

Ruoyun’s hand twitched toward her right horn, caught itself halfway, and smoothed her pleated skirt instead.

“No,” Ruoyun said flatly. “I have a roof to tar before the noon mist comes up.”

She turned on her heel toward the open door.

Before she had taken two steps, Mei stuck her needle into the wool ball, caught hold of the bench edge with one hand—very slowly and carefully, while Iuno watched her ribs like a hawk—and got to her feet without pulling a single stitch.

Mei reached across the corner of the stove, scooped the two largest, hottest pork-and-cabbage buns out of the steamer crock onto a square of clean dock-leaf, and stepped into the doorway ahead of Ruoyun.

She didn’t ask if Ruoyun was hungry, and she didn’t wait for permission. She simply took Ruoyun’s free right hand and set the hot, leaf-wrapped bundle squarely into her palm.

“For the roof,” Mei said warmly, and stepped back to lean against the table leg before Ruoyun could hand it back.

Ruoyun stopped dead on the threshold.

For two full seconds she looked down at the steaming dock-leaf sitting in her hand. The heat of the fresh dough came straight through the green leaf into her fingers, smelling of ginger and fat pork.

Her jaw set hard; her shoulders drew back inside her pressed white sleeves as if she were about to set the buns down on the grain chest and deliver a three-line lecture on garrison rations. Then she caught Mei looking at her—not teasing, not waiting for a thank-you, just already reaching down to pick up her darning egg again—and Ruoyun’s mouth shut with a small click.

She closed her fingers around the dock-leaf.

“The crust on your dough-twists is too dark,” Ruoyun told Lupa across the room, and stepped out onto the stone landing.

Yangyang was standing two paces from the doorjamb, holding the latch-bar so the draft wouldn’t slam it against the wooden turbine on the sill.

At the step, Ruoyun paused. She looked once at Yangyang’s bound ribs under her sash, and across the room at Iuno, whose chipped crystal shoulder gleamed faintly by the table.

Ruoyun adjusted the round brass spectacles on her nose with her left knuckle.

“You sang the fourth bar on pitch in the dark,” Ruoyun said to Yangyang, low enough that it didn’t carry past the sizzling skillets inside. “Don’t let that fraud in the hat tell you how to hold your wrists.”

Yangyang’s eyes warmed; she bowed her head an inch, the way she bowed to the old sergeants at the Jinzhou barracks. “Thank you, Miss Ruoyun. For the Rafter Kites, too.”

“Don’t start,” Ruoyun snapped, and marched down the stone steps into the lane without looking back.

Behind her, before the heavy timber door had even latched shut, the noise inside the storehouse went up by half an octave—Lupa roaring at Buling to get her sleeve out of the chili saucer, Ciaccona striking a bright, insolent G-chord from the window-seat, and Buling’s voice cutting cheerfully through the din to announce that if they moved the grain chest three fingers north, everybody in the room would digest their mutton twice as fast.

***

Twenty minutes later, Ruoyun climbed the narrow timber stair to the watch gallery above the inner gate.

The morning mist had burned off the slate roofs, leaving the cold mountain sun clear and pale across the cedar planks. Ten feet back from the parapet, sitting on the dry pine bench with the indigo oilcloth folded neatly under her knees, Qingxiao had her seven-stringed paulownia qin across her lap.

She wasn’t playing a march; she was plucking the open third string with her thumb while she turned the wooden tuning peg a hair’s breadth at a time, listening to the wood settle in the sun.

Ruoyun came up the top tread without a breath out of place, walked over to the corner embrasure, and leaned her hip against the stone cap, remaining on her feet.

“Well?” Qingxiao asked without looking up from the peg-box.

“It’s a poultry market in there,” Ruoyun said. “Half of them are sitting on the furniture backwards, the bard’s got her boots on the windowsill, and Buling had her hand in the frying pan before I got my foot out the door. They deserve each other.”

Qingxiao’s thumb plucked the third string once. It rang clear and warm under the cedar eaves.

“And Yangyang?” Qingxiao asked.

“Her ribs are healing fine,” Ruoyun said gruffly. “She’s got more sense than the rest of the room put together, though that’s a low bar.”

She looked down at the green dock-leaf bundle she was still carrying in her right hand.

After a beat, Ruoyun unfolded the top corner of the leaf, broke one of the hot pork-and-cabbage buns cleanly in half so the white steam puffed up into the cold air, and set the larger half down on the end of the pine bench beside Qingxiao’s elbow.

She didn’t sit down. She stayed standing by the stone embrasure, looking out over the crooked grey snake of the eastern wall and the sunny lanes of the Hold below, and ate her half of the bun before the grease could touch her cuffs.

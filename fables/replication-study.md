# Replication Study

*A Rinascita Saga story — after Null Result, alongside Messenger of Spring*

---

*A finding means little until it happens twice.*

— NANALLY, *Foundations of Frequency Metrology*, marginalia (hostile), Archive copy

## I. Supervision

The weekly meetings had a shape by midwinter, and the shape was a duel with tea.

Mornye held them in her office, which had a window onto the Solistree avenue and a kettle she operated herself, slowly, as a point of doctrine. Nanally arrived each time at the last permissible second, dropped a stack of documentation on the desk formatted to Institute standard down to the *margins,* and then defended it like a thesis under siege, pacing, jabbing, ears semaphoring, while Mornye read at her own speed with a stylus and an expression of judicial calm that had been, over eleven weeks, refined into a weapon.

"Page six," Mornye said. "Your radius projection."

"Flawless."

"Your error bars have shrunk again."

"Because my *instruments* have improved, because I recalibrated the—"

"Your error bars," Mornye said, turning the page around, "are smaller than the instrument's stated precision. You've started believing your own hopes, intern. Widen them."

Nanally snatched the page, glared at it, and saw it. Her ears went flat with the specific fury of the correctly caught. "...Noted," she said, through enamel.

Ally watched from the corner. She attended every meeting, officially as subject, actually as audience, sitting cross-legged on Mornye's spare chair with a bag of the good marshmallows the commissary had started stocking since a certain famous household began buying them in bulk. She had learned to read the duel the way she read everything, by census. Nanally interrupted Mornye eleven times per meeting, average. Mornye let nine through and stopped two, and the two she stopped were always the two where Nanally was wrong, and Nanally knew it, and came back next week with the wrongness fixed and unmentioned. Neither of them had ever once said *good point.* The census had a column for it anyway. The column stayed empty and load-bearing.

The bubble stood at one point nine meters that week. It had shrunk through autumn on a clean curve, want by logged want — the orange sodas, the canal-light blues, a growing and fiercely defended opinion about which bridge was the correct bridge — and Nanally had presented the curve to the board in a session that ran four hours, three of them Nanally, and left with her funding doubled and a standing request from two departments that all further presentations be submitted in writing.

Mornye poured. Three cups. She had started pouring three in week five, without comment, and Ally had entered it in the census under a heading she showed to nobody.

"There's an agenda item," Mornye said, when the documentation was stamped. "Mine."

Nanally, mid-marshmallow theft from Ally's bag, paused. Mornye's agenda items were rare and structural. The last one had been the guardianship clause.

"In spring I'm going east. Overland. A field season — the Heliogyre drift, the boundary corridor, the surface. The President has approved it. The shrine keeper is leading it. I return when the weather turns."

The office went quiet. Down on the avenue, the Solistrees ran their slow amber. Buddy, on the Starstack, pulsed once, already packed.

"You're my *supervisor,*" Nanally said.

"I've arranged coverage. Your weekly reports route to me by courier. Annotated replies within the fortnight. The protection stands; the paperwork travels."

"You're going to walk across the Frostlands with your legs in a chair and a hovering filing cabinet, in *actual* weather, to look at a drift you could model from here in an afternoon—"

"I could model it from here in an afternoon." Mornye set the kettle down. "I've modeled everything from here for thirteen years. The room where I do my best work has been the same room since I was nineteen, and I have recently been informed, by a demon, at length, that I am at my most interesting outdoors." She said it level, the way she said everything now, each word placed like a component. "I'm going to look at the sky the sun crosses without a schedule. I'd like to know what my equations look like under it."

Nanally opened her mouth. Ally watched the objections queue up behind her teeth — six of them, minimum, she could count them by ear now — and watched something else overrule the queue, something that had been accreting in the office for eleven weeks of poured thirds.

"Your napkin math is going to be *illegible* by autumn," Nanally said. "Field conditions. Wind. I'm putting it in my report."

"Widen your error bars," said Mornye, and the meeting adjourned.

## II. Send-Off

On departure morning Nanally declared the entire event sentimental, unscientific, and beneath attendance, and spent it in the lab with the door locked, recalibrating a sensor array that had been calibrated on Thursday.

Ally went.

She stood at the edge of the small crowd on the plains road with her hood up, hands in her pockets, bubble at one point nine, and watched the flock assemble: the shrine keeper with her flask and her antique unhurry; the professor's chair humming at travel pitch; a girl in a driftwood-buttoned coat supervising the baggage by lounging near it; and a candidate with a journal, checking the requisition list against reality at full effort, at dawn, out loud.

"Five bedrolls," Sigrika counted. "Four travelers. The rounding—"

"Has been explained," said Denia. "At length. To the President. I'm told she filed it."

Ally drifted closer. Her field did what it did — a well-wisher forgot her twice — and then it met the edge of the group and stopped working, all at once, on all four of them, because one of them had spent a decade in the deep part, and one of them read anomalies for a living, and one of them was an office that had received the wishes of things far stranger than a girl, and one of them was Mornye.

"Ally," Mornye said, and the chair turned, and three strangers looked directly at her with zero seconds of latency, and Ally stood in the sensation like standing in rain.

Sigrika had the journal out before the introduction finished. Ally watched the pen move — swift, furious, merciless of proportion — and craned, shamelessly, because record recognizes record.

*Grey bird,* the entry read. *Juvenile. Resident. Field-effect: attention slides off (does not work on flock; investigate). Eyes: the color of the sky before the broadcast.*

"That's accurate," Ally said.

"I know," said Sigrika, and kept writing, and the two of them stood in a silence of profound mutual approval while their elders made travel noise around them.

Denia came over last but one, in her own time, the way she did everything. She looked at Ally the way an auditor looks at a very short ledger written in a hand she recognizes, and Ally looked back at the girl who read, under the coat and the buttons, faint as a watermark, of home. The old home. The deep part.

"So you're the flake," Denia said.

"You're the residue."

"*Retired* residue." The strand went behind her ear. "There's a pension. It's custard-based."

She held out a sour candy, label out. Ally took it, considered the exchange rate, and produced from her pocket an orange one, which Denia received with the gravity of a treaty. They ate them simultaneously, watching each other, two small economies establishing relations, and nothing further was said, because nothing further was owed.

Hiyuki came to her last, and the frost came with her, and stopped, politely, at the bubble's edge, and then crossed it as if the bubble had held a door open.

"You keep a census," Hiyuki said. "I'm told."

"Three noticings and a want. Daily."

"Mm." The shrine keeper studied her — antique, unhurried, and underneath the unhurry an attention total enough that Ally felt it on her skin like the pressure of the kneeling god, scaled down to person-size, warmed. "Then you know the question by trade. What is your wish?"

Ally stood very still. The road ran east. The flock stood around its luggage. Somewhere behind her in the city, a locked lab recalibrated a calibrated sensor.

"Ask me again," she said, "when you're back."

Something crossed Hiyuki's face — an amusement a century deep, the recognition of a sentence arriving in its second life — and she inclined her head, exactly, the way appointments are made.

"Autumn, then. The office takes walk-ins."

The flock took the road. Ally stood on the apron until they were points of color on the corridor, and then walked back through the waking city, and let herself into the lab, where Nanally was aggressively studying a sensor readout upside down.

"Well?" Nanally said, to the readout.

"They're gone. Sigrika put me in the journal. Denia gave me a candy. Hiyuki asked my wish."

"And?"

"I deferred it. Pending data."

Nanally turned the readout right side up. Her ears did a small complicated thing, pride adjacent, that the census had a column for.

"Correct methodology," she said.

## III. Correspondence

The first courier packet went east in the second week of the thaw, and it was eleven pages, and it was perfect. Institute standard. Margins immaculate. Radius: one point eight two meters and falling on-curve. Appendices A through C.

The reply came back in nine days, in Mornye's small exact hand, annotated throughout, and the annotation on the final page read: *Format exemplary. Content sound. Appendix C is padding and you know it. — M.*

Nanally read the reply four times, pinned it to the corkboard next to the framed board finding (*NANALLY WAS CORRECT*, official, stamped), and told Ally the whole thing was supervisory harassment, and started the next report that evening.

The reports grew. This was measurable — Ally measured it — from eleven pages, to fifteen, to twenty-two, and the growth was all appendix, and the appendices stopped being padding. Appendix D: an unsolicited stability analysis of the Heliogyre drift figures Mornye had mentioned in passing, torn apart and reassembled better, with commentary. Appendix E: a redesign of the chair's cold-weather power curve, submitted "for the group's benefit," in a phrase Nanally had stolen without noticing from a woman carrying a black cube. Appendix F: Doctor Whiskers had arrived by freight, was adjusting to the Deep Wing poorly, had bitten a board member, and was thriving.

The replies grew back. Mornye wrote at night, by firelight, on whatever the road provided — the fortnight's annotations arrived on requisition forms, on the backs of Sigrika's discarded census drafts, and once, gloriously, on a paper napkin dense with hand-run parallax, at the bottom of which, beneath three hours of argued reference frames, she had written: *The stars here move all night and answer to nobody. Your power curve is good. I'm using it. — M.*

Nanally did not frame the napkin. She built a display case for it, which she insisted was archival practice.

"You *like* her," Ally said, watching the case go up.

"I *respect* her instrumentation."

"You built a case."

"Napkins degrade."

"You put a light in it."

"*Archival,*" said Nanally, adjusting the light, "*practice.*"

And through all of it, in every packet, buried where a casual reader would miss it and Mornye would find it in eight seconds, ran a thread. Report four, Appendix B, footnote nine: *Radius decay projection crosses zero in late summer. Behavior at the boundary condition: unknown. Literature: none. n = 1.* Report six, same appendix, same footnote, one line longer. Report eight, the footnote had become a section, and the section had error bars widened to honesty, and the honesty said: *the field may be scaffolding, or the field may be substrate. If scaffolding, zero is graduation. If substrate, zero is —* and there the section ended, mid-sentence, formatted perfectly, because there are sentences even Nanally declines to finish.

The reply to report eight came back in six days, faster than the courier route allowed, which meant a witch had been involved. The annotation on that section was long, technical, and ended:

*Your instinct is scaffolding. Mine also. Here is the argument —* and there followed a full page of Mornye at her cleanest, the moving-geology logic of a mind that had once recalculated a sun's ascent by hand — *but instinct is what we have and n = 1 is what we have, and I know what you're doing with the footnotes, intern. You're carrying it alone in an appendix. Stop. Route it through me. That's what the supervision is for.*

And beneath that, in smaller writing, placed like a component:

*She calls you the loud one, in her census. It's the largest entry.*

Nanally read the reply once, put it in the case with the napkin, locked the case, and worked until four in the morning, and if the lab's night log recorded that she stopped twice, for six minutes each time, and stood at the window doing nothing measurable, the log had the decency to file it under *calibration.*

## IV. Boundary Condition

The zero crossing arrived in the eighth week of summer, three days ahead of projection, at eleven in the morning, in the middle of a preference battery, between one question and the next.

"Ranking task," Nanally said, cards up. "Bridge four at dusk versus bridge seven at—"

"Four," said Ally, and the instruments sang.

Every readout in the lab lit at once — the flatline that had spent a year decaying in nine-second sighs snapped to a full continuous signal, resonance, mass, presence, *person,* sustained, and Nanally was out of her chair with her ears vertical and a sound starting in her throat that might have become a whoop —

— and Ally folded.

She went down the way a broadcast goes down, all at once, signal to snow, and the snow was visible: her edges lost commitment, her grey went greyer, the bubble that had guarded a four-meter round of the world for a year collapsed inward through her skin and took her outline with it. The instruments held their beautiful new signal for nine seconds.

Then the sigh.

Nanally caught her before the floor did. The girl weighed what she had always weighed, which was almost enough, and her eyes were open and finding nothing, and her lips were moving, and Nanally put her ear down to them and heard, in a voice from under a grate, from the far end of eleven days:

"It's quiet. It's — I can't hear the wanting. Where did the wanting go—"

Nanally got her to the table. Strapped nothing. Attached everything. Her hands were fast and her voice was faster, running the whole diagnostic register out loud, *ambient decay inverted, field collapse, substrate withdrawal,* and underneath the register, in the place Nanally kept the things she never routed through anybody, the unfinished sentence from report eight finished itself: *if substrate, zero is dissolution,* and the loudest researcher in the hemisphere stood in her shielded lab, alone, with her whole methodology in her hands and her methodology insufficient, at n equals one.

She looked at the console.

There is a message Nanally had never sent in her professional life. She had been expelled sooner than send it. She had lived in a basement thirteen years sooner than send it. She sent it now, priority channel, all recipients, four words, and the four words were the entire content, unformatted, no appendix:

*I NEED HELP. — N.*

Luuk Herssen arrived in nine minutes with his white gloves and his black bag. Amy arrived in eleven, hair unbrushed, still in the jacket she'd grabbed, and behind her Mei, who stopped in the doorway and looked at the girl on the table flickering at the edges and went, for one second, to a cabin in the snow with a blue door, and came back, and stayed.

"Talk," said Luuk, hands already moving.

Nanally talked. She gave it to them in ninety seconds, clean, the year of it — the curve, the fills, the scaffolding hypothesis, the boundary condition, the collapse — and it was the best presentation of her career and she would never put it on a citation index, and at the end of it Amy, who had been standing at the table's edge with her hand an inch above Ally's, said, quietly:

"She's not dissolving."

"The signal—"

"I *know* the signal. I lived in the signal." Amy's voice had the flat certainty of the one qualified reviewer. "The field wasn't holding her together. The field was the eggshell. She's been building a self inside it for a year — every want, every log, every stupid orange soda — and the shell just ran out of egg. What's happening now is she's *hatching,* and hatching is loud, and she's never once in her existence been loud, and it's terrifying her." She looked up. "She doesn't need instruments. She needs the thing that was always on the other side of the shell."

"Which is *what*—"

"Us, you idiot," Amy said, with enormous affection, and took Ally's hand.

"Ally." Nothing. Flicker. "Ally. It's Amy. The quiet you're hearing — I know that quiet. It's the sound of the nothing leaving. It leaves all at once and it takes the walls with it and for a minute you're standing in yourself with no walls and it feels like dying. It's the opposite. Listen to me. It's the *opposite.*"

The flicker slowed. The lips moved: "...how do you know."

"Because I hatched last year. Bigger egg." A wet laugh. "Ma was there. Now I'm here. That's how it works. That's the whole system."

The signal climbed. Held eleven seconds. Sighed.

"More," said Luuk, watching his readouts. "Whatever that is. More of it."

And Nanally — who had explained to this girl, on the first day, in a basement, that her attention went where she told it, and had spent a year proving it — leaned into Ally's eyeline, took her other hand in a grip with no bedside manner whatsoever, and deployed the only instrument she had left.

"Void kid. Census. *Now.* Three noticings and a want."

"Nanally, I can't—"

"Wrong answer, try again. Three noticings. Go. What do you notice."

Silence. Flicker. Then, from very far under:

"...Your ears are flat. All the way. You only do that when—" a breath — "when you're scared."

"*One.* Logged. Two."

"Amy's hand is warm. Warmer than — people are warm. I keep being surprised."

"Two. Logged. Three, and make it good, I'm publishing this."

The grey eyes found her. Actually found her — snagged, held, the way a needle holds a frequency — and something at the girl's edges chose a line and committed to it.

"You bought your own marshmallows," Ally said, "and pretended they were mine. Sixteen weeks. I have a heading."

The lab was silent. Amy looked at Nanally. Luuk looked at his readouts. Nanally's ears performed a maneuver with no prior classification.

"...Three," she said, roughly. "Logged. The want. Say the want."

And Ally, hatching, edges committing, signal climbing past eleven seconds and rising, said the want the way you say a thing you have decided, and it came out with error bars widened all the way to true:

"I want to still be here when Mornye gets back. I want to hear you two argue. I want the *meetings.* I want—" the signal crested — "I want the heading to keep filling up."

The instruments sang, and this time they held the note.

Sustained.

Continuous.

Luuk watched the line run flat and full across the screen for one full minute, then two, a person-shaped signal with no decay anywhere in it, and pulled his gloves off, and said, to the room, in the voice of twenty years of watching this city refuse its own endings:

"Congratulations. It's a girl."

Ally slept eleven hours. Amy stayed, cross-legged on the next table, standing the watch with the professionalism of family precedent. Mei made everyone eat. And Nanally sat in the corner writing, all night, and what she wrote went into no appendix and carried no footnotes, and went east by witch-assisted courier before dawn, and read, in full:

*Report, unscheduled. Boundary crossed. Scaffolding confirmed. She's fine. She's better than fine, she's CONTINUOUS, come look at the readouts, they're the most beautiful thing I've ever measured. Also she wants the meetings. So do I. Hurry up and finish your weather. — your intern.*

The reply came back in four days. One line, small hand, placed like a component:

*Autumn. Pour three. — M.*

## V. When the Weather Turns

The flock came home on the first cold morning of the posted autumn, down the Riseway with the real season a day behind them, as arranged, and the city took them back with the smell of a whole civilization's dinner.

Mornye's chair came off the terminal apron, wind-worn, humming a quarter-tone higher than it had left, Buddy riding the headrest with the veteran calm of a chick that had crossed weather. Protocol said debrief. The President's office said report. Thirteen years of habit said the lab, the room, the same room since nineteen.

She went to the Deep Wing.

The doors opened on an argument already in progress — "the beak is *wrong*" — "the beak is *stylized*" — and inside, at the whiteboard, under a year-old decay curve annotated to death, Ally and Nanally stood shoulder to shoulder disputing a sketch. Ally saw her first. The girl's edges, Mornye noted, at instrument speed, out of habit, held: committed, continuous, a person all the way to her outline, wearing — this registered second — a lab coat, tailored small, with an intern's badge on which someone had crossed out INTERN and written, in a furious pink hand, JUNIOR AUTHOR.

"You're early," Ally said.

"I'm punctual. It's going around." Mornye rolled in. The kettle, she observed, was already on. Three cups stood already out, and had, from the dust pattern, stood out for some days. "The sketch?"

"Sigrika sent census pages ahead by courier," Nanally said, aggressively casual, ears betraying everything. "For *cross-referencing.* Her grey bird entry needed corrections. We're issuing errata."

Mornye read the whiteboard. Under the dead decay curve, in two hands, ran a fresh column of entries — *white bird (elder), migratory, confirmed. Quiet bird with the amber chick: farthest-traveling by intent. Returns when cold. CONFIRMED* — and beside the last word, underlined twice in pink, a date. Today's.

"You tracked my route."

"We tracked the *couriers,*" said Nanally. "Standard logistics."

"There's a map," said Ally.

"There is *not* a—"

"It has pins."

Mornye looked from one to the other — the loudest mind in the hemisphere and the quietest, a year into orbiting each other, both of them pretending the kettle had turned itself on — and did the thing she had learned east, under the unfixed stars, which was to let her face be seen doing what it did.

"Pour," she said. "I have a season to report. It runs long. The stars move all night up there, and I've got the napkins to prove it, and there's a boundary corridor full of stones that are switching sides, and a lake with a form under a rock, and I have been instructed —" she unpacked the words with care, like instruments — "by a candidate, in writing, to inform you both that the census recurs annually. It's a field census. The field is east. The form has been set to recur."

The lab held still.

"The next requisition list," Mornye said, "hasn't been drafted yet. The rounding, I'm told, is flexible."

Nanally poured, loudly, to have something to do with her hands. "Fieldwork," she said, in the tone of a woman opening negotiations she had already lost. "With a *shrine keeper.* And a *bureaucrat with a journal.* And whatever Denia is."

"Retired," said Ally.

"My subject is barely six months continuous. There are protocols. There are stability minimums. There would have to be a full portable instrument suite, which would have to be *designed,* which would take all winter—"

"Then you'd best start," said Mornye, and took her cup.

Ally took hers. She held it in both hands, against her sternum, where the heat could work, and looked out the lab's high window toward the ridge where the shrine sat over the city, and thought about a deferral, pending data, and about data, now, in hand.

"I have an appointment to keep first," she said. "Up the ridge. There's a question waiting for me. From spring."

"What'll you tell her?" Nanally asked.

Ally drank her tea. Continuous. Committed all the way to the outline. Somewhere under the world a god knelt on his glacier saying one word patiently down the roots, and somewhere in a shrine of ten thousand kept reaches a bell hung low enough for children, and somewhere in this room a heading was, at that exact moment, filling up.

"The truth," she said. "I'll tell her the wish already recurs. I set it to."

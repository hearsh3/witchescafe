## i. Wednesday

Agrat bat Mahlat kept Wednesdays the way other beings kept faith.

The board waited where she had left it, on the flat stone in the fields east of the city, forty-eight pits worn smooth by a patience older than agriculture. Above her, the artificial dusk came down in bands of Solistree gold, and beyond the ridge the Exostrider knelt against the glacier the way a mountain kneels: geologically, and on its own schedule. Awake now. She could feel it listening to the city it carried, the way a mother sleeps with one ear.

She was sowing the first pit when the light changed quality, and Nuwa was standing at the edge of the stone.

"You still play with seeds," Nuwa said.

"You still play with people."

"Mine grow back."

"Mine too," said Agrat, "if you plant them properly. Sit."

Nuwa sat. She regarded the board with the expression she brought to everything — the serenity of a being for whom the universe was a bench, and every object on it either a tool, a result, or a question that had been rude enough to stay unanswered. She lifted one seed and weighed it against some interior standard.

"Explain the rules."

"You sow. You reap. Whoever holds the most at the end has fed her house best." Agrat turned a hand over. "It is the oldest game because it is the only one. Everything else is Mancala with costumes."

Nuwa played. Nuwa lost. She examined the final position for a long moment, the way she had once examined a shattered child on a laboratory floor.

"Again," she said.

She lost again. Something moved through her face that in a mortal would have been delight and in Nuwa was the acquisition of data, which for her had always been the same organ.

"You withhold seeds early," she said, "to starve my side of the board late. You feed me small captures so I overextend."

"Yes."

"The witches never see the second layer, when you play them."

"The witches see it," Agrat said. "They pretend they don't, so I'll keep coming. It's the coven's oldest recruitment tool. I've been *nearly a member* for six hundred years." She swept the seeds home and began resetting. "You went to the professor's lecture."

"Third row. A borrowed face. She lectured for two hours on distributed synchronization architecture and credited forty-one names." Nuwa laid one seed precisely in the center of an empty pit. "Two hundred students. No augments in the room worth the term. Chalk, and error, and correction in public. They laughed at her jokes. The jokes were bad."

"You're smiling."

"I am cataloguing." A pause with centuries in it. "She refused My miracle in the medbay. I administered one anyway, without her knowledge. I have been asking Myself since whether that was mercy."

"It was appetite," said Agrat. "You wanted act two."

Nuwa considered this with total equanimity. "Yes," she said, and moved.

They played. Somewhere below the ridge the city made its evening noise — trams, and someone's terrible practice trumpet, and the deep patient hum of a sun that human hands had hung. Between moves, Agrat spoke to the board.

"The old thing under the gate told the pilots a number, before the end. Fourteen. Fourteen stories, fourteen girls who leapt and burned. It had been keeping score." She dropped a seed. "It counted fourteen and folded."

"Fifteen came home," Nuwa said.

"Fifteen is a different number." Agrat captured three seeds and did not gloat, which was itself a form of gloating. "You can build doctrine on fourteen. Fifteen is just a girl with her mother's smile eating festival food. Doctrine has no answer to that. It never has."

"The tally-keeper will revise and return."

"Everything returns. That's Wednesday's whole thesis." Agrat glanced up. "A hobbyist of our acquaintance was in the crowd at the gate. Volunteer's vest. She hummed."

"We have no acquaintances."

"A colleague, then. A collector." The red eyes were mild. "Fourteen times she pulled that little song out of the dark and it never once opened for her, and now she stands in crowds and hums it, off-key, on purpose, with all the time in the world. She's begun *waiting to be surprised.* For her kind, that's a conversion experience."

"You watch her too closely."

"I watch everyone at exactly the correct distance," said Agrat. "It's why I'm invited to things."

The game ended. Nuwa had won, by two seeds, and sat looking at her own victory with faint suspicion, as though it might be an instrument reading that required recalibration.

"You allowed that."

"I invested it," Agrat said, rising, furling nothing, gathering everything. "Now you'll come back."

Nuwa stood. At the edge of the stone she paused, and the dusk moved around her the way rooms had once rearranged themselves around her convenience.

"The wager you set on the Rover," she said. "The bead. Her room has light in it now."

"So I hear."

"Then collect."

"Sabbath is soon enough." Agrat set the umbrella against her shoulder, a queen closing a modest court. "I like an appointment. Same time next Wednesday."

"I will be watching," said Nuwa.

"You say that to everyone."

"It is true of everyone."

## ii. Thursday

On Thursday, Atropos sat in a barber's chair in the Third Ward and let a mortal raise scissors to her head.

The shop smelled of cedar oil and warm towels. The barber was a broad woman with forearms like dock rope and the conviction, universal to her profession, that silence in the chair was a wound requiring treatment.

"New in the city?"

"Old everywhere," said Atropos.

"Ha. Aren't we all. What do you do, love?"

"I ended things."

"Retired, then?"

"Restructured."

The scissors began. Atropos watched in the mirror with the whole of her attention: the blades opening, the blades closing, the grey lengths falling to the floor. Each cut exact. Each end final. And the woman in the chair went on breathing, went on being, diminished by centimeters and injured by nothing. On the tiles the cut hair lay like clippings of thread from a garment still being worn.

*So this is the trade,* she thought. *Severance in the service of shape.* Her own shears sat in her lap, wrapped in grey silk, and did not stir. They had gone gold once — one stroke, one honest cut through a dishonest weave, a lie shorn off a frightened girl — and the gold had stayed in the metal like sunlight in stone. She had spent an age learning what her sisters never asked: that the cut was never the point. The cut was punctuation. The sentence belonged to the one who chose the thread.

"Family in town?" the barber asked, combing.

"Two sisters."

"Close by?"

"One spins. She took a stall in the yarn market and undercharges, because she loves the work past all economics. One measures. She's at a tailor's on Meridian Row. She says the customers lie about their waists." A pause. "She permits it. She says the lie is load-bearing and the trousers can absorb it."

The barber laughed the laugh of a woman filing away a story for tonight's table. "And you? What brings you in? Special occasion?"

Atropos looked at the falling grey.

"I want to see it grow back," she said.

It came out plainly, and the barber, who had heard every reason a person takes to a chair, nodded as though this were the most ordinary sentence in the city, which — in this city, this year, under that patient kneeling god — it very nearly was.

Through the window, the street carried its evening crowd. Two girls passed the glass: one with paint on her knuckles and a mercenary's stride gone loose at the shoulders, laughing at her own joke; one dark-haired, quiet-eyed, a pair of floral scissors riding at her hip the way other people carry keys. The dark-haired one turned her head — she read the world by gait and voice and habit, faces being closed books to her — and in the mirror-glass her attention crossed the reflection of the woman in the chair, and stopped.

A held moment. Two pairs of shears in one frame of glass.

Atropos inclined her head, one professional to another. *You chose the thread,* she had told her once, in a place built of lies. *Eventually,* she had told her, and left. The appointment stood. It stood the way good appointments stand: unhurried, certain, far down the calendar, in no one's handwriting yet.

The girl outside smiled — puzzled, polite, the smile you give a stranger who seems briefly familiar — and was towed away by her laughing friend.

The barber whisked the cloth away. "There. Six weeks, come back, we'll keep the shape."

"Six weeks," said Atropos, tasting it. An appointment that ended in growth. She paid in exact coin and tipped in a single thread of gold, which the barber would find at closing and keep in a drawer for the rest of her life without ever once spending it, and stepped out under the bell.

Behind her the door chimed, bright and brief. Ahead of her, for the first time in the history of endings, her own hair was scheduled to return.

## iii. Friday

The lighthouse stood on the last teeth of the Rinascitan coast, past Porto Veno, past the camellia country, where the charts went honest and the water stopped pretending. Its keeper answered to Lucio.

The visitor came up the cliff stairs on Friday evening with a bottle in one hand and a document case in the other, and let himself in without knocking, because knocking was a courtesy of the mortal world and between the two of them courtesy had died screaming several cosmologies ago.

"You're late," said Lucio, from the lamp room.

"I'm eternal. You're impatient."

"I'm a keeper. Everything on this coast runs on my schedule now, including you." Lucio came down the spiral with his sleeves rolled, wiping lamp oil from his hands with a rag. He had chosen a middle-aged face and kept it, weathered and amused, the morning star gone comfortably to seed. "You brought the good bottle."

"I brought a bottle. Your standards do the rest."

They sat where they always sat, at the scarred table by the east window, and the visitor unpacked the case: a docket, three fingers thick, ribboned in the fussy style of Ragunnan clerks.

"They're still calling you Michele down there?" Lucio asked, pouring.

"They are."

"You took your brother's name."

"He'd hate it."

"That's the tithe," Lucio agreed, and they drank.

The docket was the winter's work: restorations. The exiled Fools of Ragunna were being returned to the record name by name, and the living had been restored years ago — the bard's father first, songs written about it. The living were easy. The living could file. The visitor's clients were the other kind: three, four generations deep, exiles who had died with the word *Fool* nailed over their names and no descendant left to pull it down. He worked their cases through the Council of Stewards under his borrowed name, an unremarkable advocate with an airtight brief and no fee, and one by one the dead were acquitted into history.

"Seventeen this quarter," he said. "A fishmonger's grandmother. A cartographer. A boy who sang the wrong verse at the wrong funeral in the wrong century." He turned a page with the tenderness of a man handling evidence. "I read the transcripts. Do you know what I keep finding? Nothing. No crime. Not once. Sixty generations of prosecution and there was never a case."

"You'd know," said Lucio.

"I would." He set down the glass. "I was the case. Ten thousand years in ten thousand ears — *you could have saved her, you should have been faster, it was you, it was always you.* Airtight, every brief. The bereaved convict so easily; they arrive at the bar pre-confessed." He looked at the wine the way old men look at rivers. "I never once entered evidence. That's the question I walked out with, you know. Standing in that cathedral, watching a wolf-girl and a bard and a one-armed prophet refuse the verdict. *Where was the evidence?* I've been doing discovery ever since."

"And?"

"Grief has no defendant." He said it like a man laying down a stone he had carried up a long stair. "So now I prosecute the record instead. The record can take it. The record, unlike the widow, is actually guilty."

Lucio refilled him. Outside, the lamp turned its great slow wheel, and the beam went out over the dark water asking nothing of it.

"And you," the visitor said. "Still refusing to refuse things."

"Still keeping the light." Lucio leaned back. "You want the joke? I spent an age teaching the drowning to fold their arms. *Reach for no one. Need is a debt. Pride will keep you warm.* And they folded their arms, and they sank, and I called each one a victory for the principle." He nodded at the lamp stairs. "Now I run a machine whose entire doctrine is *here I am, if you want me.* It commands nothing. It pursues nothing. Ships steer by it or they don't. It burns either way." He smiled, and the old light was in it, banked down to a hearth. "First honest work I've ever kept."

"The throne's empty," the visitor said. "You could have it now. Nobody's even guarding it."

"That's the discovery on my side of the docket." Lucio turned his glass. "The throne was always empty. He was never on it. He was down in the water the whole time with the rest of us, in shorts, waiting for someone to govern themselves." He shook his head slowly, an old rebel savoring the punchline of his own war. "You can't overthrow a lifeguard, Michele. I checked. There's no procedure."

They laughed, the way their kind laughs — brief, dry, seismic somewhere far below.

"Any word from the old weather?" the visitor asked.

Lucio rose and took a postcard from where it was pinned beside the lamp stairs, at eye height, where he would pass it climbing to his work. Unstamped. Delivered anyway. He handed it over and let it speak for itself.

The visitor read it twice, and set it face-down with unusual care, and raised his glass.

"To the flood," he said.

"To the flood," said Lucio. "May she stay retired."

The visitor left near midnight, docket under his arm, seventeen dead names lighter. On the cliff stairs the beam swept over him — once, twice — a light with no throne behind it, asking for nothing, and it passed on over the water to whoever might be out there, aiming for home.

## iv. Saturday

The visitor came down the Solisylum stairs on Saturday morning with a rifle slung across her back and a paper bag of pastries in one hand, and the coven let her pass, because Agrat had vouched for her in writing — Agrat vouched for everything in writing; the letter had a reference number — and because Herta had met her eyes at the door and found, filed behind them, something she recognized from her own margins: a scholar of a single subject, tenured in it, retired from it.

Mina was at the pool's edge, learning coffee.

It was her fourth morning of coffee. Tea she had down; tea had been the first word of her new alphabet, porcelain and warm, and coffee was proving to be tea's difficult cousin — bitter on purpose, the witches insisted, bitter *as a feature* — and she was on her third sip and her third private decision to persevere when the stranger's boots stopped beside her and a scarred hand held out the paper bag.

"They're better dunked," the stranger said. "Trust me. I've had twenty years of practice at breakfast and you've had a week."

Mina looked up. The woman was lean and weathered, hair the color of deep water going grey at the temple, a long scar seaming one cheek, eyes the color of the hour before dawn. Something in Mina's oldest tide turned over and knew her — the way water knows water across any distance, through any pipe, under any name.

"You're—" Mina began.

"I was the whole weather," the woman said. "Now I'm a watcher with a rifle. Leva." She sat down on the stone lip, uninvited, at ease, a person who had learned sitting from scratch and gotten good at it. "Kept one syllable of the old name and filed the rest. Paperwork's easier."

"Mina," said Mina.

"I heard. It's on your cup." Leva glanced at the painted letters, flared at the base like hulls, and something crossed her face that had learned, at great cost, how to be gentle with itself. "They wrote it *down.* Five of them. You know what I got, my first day out of the deep?" She held up fingers. "A hand up. A car door held open. And a colleague's tired little prophet giving me instructions like a pharmacist. *Stay in the room. Let someone see you.*" She took a pastry herself and bit it. "I've been following the dosage ever since. It works. Slowly. Like everything that works."

They sat with the amber veins pulsing slow in the water below, two retired oceans at a shared shore.

"They said I ate children," Mina offered, in the tone of one comparing service records.

"They said I *was* the reason the reaching fails." Leva chewed, unbothered. "They were right, in my case. Ten thousand years of case files. You were slandered; I was documented. Between us we cover the whole genre."

"And the rifle?"

Leva unslung it and laid it across her knees, and her hands on it were the hands of a craftsman, and her voice, when it came, had the flat kindness of deep water.

"I collected the falling, before. Every grief on the ledge, I was underneath it, mouth open. Now I mind the ledge." She thumbed a scratch in the stock, some old campaign. "When somebody jumps into the dark for the right reasons — and they keep doing it, this crowd, it's a *pathology* — I keep watch on the rim. And if they don't come back, I make sure someone remembers why they went. That's the whole job. Witness, with optics."

"Does it pay?"

"Terribly."

"What did grief pay?"

"Everything," said Leva. "It was a terrible wage too."

Mina laughed — small, waterlogged, catastrophic, the muscles coming back to it a little more each day — and dunked the pastry, and it was, in fact, better dunked.

From somewhere far above, down the cavern shaft, music arrived: thin, sun-warm, a radio doing its best across a great depth. *All the leaves are brown.* Mina tilted her head.

"The cavern is singing."

"That's my ride." Leva jerked her chin upward. "He waits. He's good at it — he waited out an entire age tied up by a colleague with strong opinions about unity. Now he waits in parking spots. He calls it a lateral move." She stood, brushed crumbs into the pool with the sober courtesy of one feeding an old god, and shouldered the rifle. "Walk me up. Doctor's orders say you're due for weather. The real kind."

The stairs took a while. Mina's legs were a hundred years out of practice and Leva matched her pace without comment, which Mina understood, by the end of the climb, to be the entire message.

At the cavern mouth the day was enormous. Wind off the woods, Helios high and human-made and shining anyway, and on the track below, a low green machine with its engine ticking, and a middle-aged man in a t-shirt leaning on the door with his face turned up to the sun he had waited an age for people to build themselves.

He saw them. He raised two fingers from the door frame — easy, neighborly, a wave the width of a whole cosmology — and aimed it at Mina.

No one, in the entire history of water, had ever waved at the tide.

Mina lifted her hand and waved back.

"Same water," Leva said, going down the track, "different weather," and got in the car, and the radio carried them off between the trees, dreaming of somewhere warm all winter long.

## v. Sabbath Eve

The fireworks began at full dark, which in Lahai-Roi was a civic decision: Helios dimmed by decree to a coal-glow, the artificial sky handed over, for one night, to gunpowder and delight.

Agrat bat Mahlat watched from the roof of the Startorch bell tower, because queens take the high seat out of habit and keep it out of view. The umbrella rested open against her shoulder — old instincts; the sky used to drop worse — and the first shells went up gold and broke into slow willows of light, and beneath them the city roared its soft civilian roar, thirty thousand people practicing wonder in a place built under a stone because the surface had once been ended.

She found them in the crowd without looking. She always found them without looking; looking was for beings who could lose things.

There: a silver-blue coat and a wolf's tail keeping time against its owner's discipline. There: a bard's hands conducting the explosions on principle. A red string catching shell-light on a thin wrist; an orange head three steps ahead of its own datapad, for once looking up. A one-armed woman whose crystal caught every color the sky produced and paid it back with interest, her hand held fast by —

— there. The Fool. And on the Fool's shoulders, above the whole crowd, a girl of gold eyes and borrowed years and ferocious, unpracticed presence, both fists raised at the sky as though the fireworks were her personal correspondence, laughing with the total commitment of someone thirteen years behind on laughing and making up the arrears.

*We'll see the fireworks together. I promise.*

Mortals and their promises. They made them the way sparrows made nests — out of anything, in defiance of everything, in places guaranteed to flood. And then, every so often, insufferably, they kept one, and the whole ledger of the world had to be recopied to make room.

Agrat took the bead from her sleeve.

Glass, small, perfect. She held it up to a bursting shell and the light went through it whole and came out the far side organized — a room's worth of light in a bead's worth of glass. The wager's terms had been exact: it waited for the day there was light in the room to be seen by. She looked down at the Fool, who had gone into her own locked dark and decided, at last, what it contained, and had come out carrying a daughter and a sentence — *you're enough* — delivered forward for once instead of backward.

Settled. Paid in full.

She pocketed the bead. Then she took, from the pouch at her belt, a single Mancala seed, dry and patient, and set it on the parapet stone, facing the crowd, terms undisclosed.

Every settled wager was a vacancy. The House stayed open. That was Sabbath's whole thesis.

Far below, at the crowd's hem, a girl in a volunteer's vest stood with her face tipped up, humming three notes very slightly off-key, on purpose, with all the time in the world. She did not look at the tower. Agrat did not look at her. Between their kinds, noticing was the entire transaction, and both parties had excellent credit.

The finale went up — a whole treasury of it, gold over gold over gold — and the city made a sound like a held breath ending, and somewhere in the fields to the east a game board sat under the falling light with its pits swept clean, waiting for Wednesday.

Agrat furled the umbrella and stayed for the smoke.

Immortality, she had found, was mostly this: keeping the small appointments until they added up to a taste for the world. The frightened had written ten thousand books about her kind — the hungers, the bindings, the leash of the guarded name — and in all those trembling volumes, sixty generations deep, they had missed the one thing worth fearing about demons, the single finding no bestiary ever dared to enter in the record:

They keep coming back.

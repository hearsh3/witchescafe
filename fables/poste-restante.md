# Poste Restante

*a oneshot of the Banat al-Rih*

---

*]a lit window is a kind of address[*
*]the road does not ask who you meant it for[*
*]it delivers what was sent[*

---

The cloud arrived on a Thursday, shortly after four, and sat above the bell-gable of the schoolhouse without permission.

Phrolova stood in the middle of the lane and looked at it the way she had once looked at a second horn who came in during a general pause.

It was not a large cloud. It was the size of alaundry basket and the color of washed mutton-wool, drifting south-southeast across a sky that had been, for three weeks now, a single unblemished sheet of soprano blue. For centuries the sky over Stellavento had been painted in the permanent crimson-and-ash of a late movement that never resolved; then a guest had walked through the score, and the ceiling had cleared to blue, which Phrolova had tolerated as an acoustic adjustment. She had not authorized weather.

Down by the well, two of the children—Milo and the cooper's little girl, Sera—were squatting over a puddle that had no business being there either, poking it with sticks.

"It's moving," Sera said.

"Everything moves, stupid," Milo said, and shoved her shoulder, and when he stood up to run past the bakery doorpost, the crown of his dark head cleared last spring's chalk-mark by a quarter of an inch.

Phrolova's eyes went to the chalk-mark, stayed there for the length of a dotted rest, and moved on.

The whole village was slipping its key. Not all at once, which she could have corrected with three bars and a downbeat, but by commas and quarter-tones. Up the hill, Master Corrado's parlor window stood propped on a stick, and through it came the sound of an exercise in C minor—reluctant, sticky in the shift to third position—fourteen minutes past the hour when the lesson had ended every afternoon for three hundred years. From Triss's kitchen came the smell of the Thursday stew, and there was tarragon in it. There had never been tarragon in the Thursday stew. Tarragon did not grow in the crater.

"You're standing in the draft," Triss said, leaning out over her half-door with a wooden spoon in her fist to shake it at the children. "And you two—out of the dirt before I fetch the broom. Supper's when the shadow hits the trough, not when you feel like it."

"You put green stuff in it," Milo said accusingly.

"I put what I had in it, and you'll eat what you're given." Triss's gaze traveled up the lane and found Phrolova. She did not lower the spoon. "And you can tell Master Corrado if he keeps the boy til dark again, his share goes to the dogs."

"We have no dogs," Phrolova said.

"Well, to the birds, then," Triss said, and went back to her pot.

Phrolova walked on toward the square. Her heels clicked on the cobbles with their usual metrical exactness, sixty-six to the minute, an andante she had kept since the day she crawled out of the ash. If she kept the tempo, the street would have to keep it too.

In the square under the fuchsia trees, Granny Leah's black carriage stood in the traces. The two bay mares had stood harnessed since the morning the world ended, their ears twitching at flies that never bit, waiting to take Phrolova down the mountain to the first concert hall of the season.

Today there was a wicker hamper strapped to the box beside Granny Leah's horsehair cushion, with the corner of a white linen cloth tucked under the lid.

Granny Leah sat on the bench by the smithy, greasing a trace-buckle with a rag of tallow. She squinted up past the fuchsia boughs at the wool-colored cloud.

"Wind's coming round from the water," she said.

"There is no water within forty leagues," Phrolova said.

"Tell the cloud," said Granny Leah, and spat on the brass to rub it bright.

Phrolova did not ask about the hamper. To ask was to concede that there was an answer she did not already own.

She went up the three stone steps to her house, shut the door behind her, and stood in the parlor until the light outside dropped from gold to the long, cooling slate of a real dusk. The curtains on the front window were drawn back, tied with black cord; she had tied them back the day she returned from the northern rift.

On the sill stood a brass oil-lamp with a tall glass chimney.

She lifted the chimney off and set it on its felt coaster. With a pair of small silver embroidery scissors she trimmed the black char from the cotton wick—less than a sixteenth of an inch, cut square so the flame would not horn at the corners. She struck a wooden match, touched it to the oil-soaked braid, replaced the glass into its four brass prongs, and turned the thumb-screw down until the yellow tongue sat steady and clear, neither smoking the flue nor guttering in the draft from the sash.

It cost nothing. It was half a gill of colza oil and thirty seconds of wrist-work.

She stood with her fingertips resting on the warm brass screw until the square outside had gone dark enough to give her back her own face in the glass, pale and straight-backed above the flame, and then she turned and went out to supper.

---

The long table under the fuchsia trees was laid for twenty-two, which was one too many.

Phrolova stopped two paces from the head of the board.

Halfway down the left-hand bench, between Milo and the smith's apprentice, sat a young man she had not written into the village. The children had shoved down to make room for his elbows; Sera was passing him the salt-cellar with both hands.

He wore the dark, high-collared coat of a Fractsidus initiate, and he was wet through. Not the damp of a man caught in a shower, but the black, heavy saturation of wool that has been at the bottom of cold water for a month. A dark ring had spread across the scrubbed pine around his forearm. Beneath the bench, the flagstones were puddled, and the hem of his skirted coat ticked steadily—*drop, drop, rest, drop*—onto the stone.

"Come on, then," Triss said, coming out of the kitchen yard with the great earthenware tureen in her mittens and setting it down in the middle of the board. "It'll skin over if you stand there admiring it. This young man came all the way up the crater road, and nobody's cut him the brown loaf."

They could see him.

When the living had walked down this lane, the villagers had looked through them as if they were window-glass. Milo was watching the stranger butter his bread with the solemn, unblinking scrutiny of seven years old.

At the sound of Phrolova's step, the young man looked up.

His hair was plastered dark to his forehead; his skin had the tallow-gray cast of whalebone. His spoon stopped halfway to his mouth. By reflex he shoved the bench back—the wood scraped loudly on the wet stone—and stood, his right hand rising toward his breastbone in the lower-terrace salute.

"Overseer—" he said.

There was sea-water in the back of his throat; the word came out with a small, loose rattle, like shingle drawing back down a beach. He blinked at the fuchsia blossoms overhead, at Triss's floury apron, at the children's bowls, and lowered his hand slowly to his seam.

"Miss," he corrected himself. "Pardon."

Phrolova did not move. Her hands remained folded at her waist, the left wrist lightly over the right.

She knew him. Not by his face—the lower ranks wore masks in the corridors, and she had never found a reason to look at them when the masks were off—but by the frequency humming raggedly under his wet ribs. A thin baritone, slightly sharp in the upper register because he breathed too shallowly, always half a beat ahead of her stick out of sheer, puppyish eagerness to be of use.

The third landing of the stair, during the descent. He had stepped out of the guard-alcove with his short-blade raised when the intruder broke the perimeter, believing with his whole chest that the rehearsal was the play, and Phrolova had opened him from the left collarbone to the fourth rib without breaking her stride because he was cluttering the tempo.

The cloth of his coat was whole, but the water knew where the stroke had gone: a diagonal ribbon of blacker wet ran from the hollow of his throat to his waist, refusing to drain.

"Sit down, boy," Triss said, tapping his shoulder with the flat of the bread-knife as she went past. "Nobody stands at my table unless they're carving."

He sat, looking apologetic about the puddle under his boots.

"How did you find the road?" Phrolova asked. Her voice was level, pitched to carry the length of the board and no further.

"There wasn't much of one, Miss," he said, his fingers closing around his pewter spoon for something to hold. "Only it was dark all the way down, and we knocked a good while at the bottom and nobody answered the door. And then when I came up the rise I saw the window." He nodded toward the house behind her, where the brass lamp burned square in the pane. "It's a good clear light. You can see it a long way off."

"Eat your soup," Triss told him. "She'll keep you talking till it's tallow."

"Yes, ma'am. Thank you." He took a mouthful, swallowed carefully, and looked down at the crust of rye in his other hand with a shy, bewildered relief, as if he had been afraid he might have forgotten what bread was for. "It's very good. My mother used to put that bitter leaf in on Thursdays—what do you call it—"

"Tarragon," Milo said darkly. "It's poison."

"Is it?" The young man smiled a little, ducking his head. "I can't hardly taste it for the salt."

Phrolova stood behind her chair at the head of the table. Her fingers rested on the carved wooden rail. She knew the exact vibration of his ribcage when the string took it; she knew the key he had died in; she had no idea what he had been called when he had a mother who used tarragon.

"Your name," she said.

He looked up, surprised to be asked. "Luc, Miss. Third Choir, under—" He caught himself again, glanced at Sera's ribbons, and amended it. "From down the coast. Near the salt-pans."

"Luc," Phrolova said. She tried the syllable against the thin baritone. It fit without a burr.

Triss set a full bowl at the head of the table and nudged the pewter spoon against Phrolova's knuckles. Phrolova did not pull the chair out. She took the bowl in her left hand, lifted it from the wood, and stood straight-backed at the head of the board while the village ate.

She did not touch the spoon. Nobody at the table asked why she was standing. Halfway through the second helping of bread, when the dark water under the bench crept toward Sera's Sunday shoe, Luc drew his heels back under the plank with a murmured pardon, trying to take up less of the stone.

Ten days later, while the lamp-glass was still warming, a woman came through the lych-gate in a dress of watered green silk.

It was a Rinascitan festival gown, cut low for summer weather, with gilt-paper ribbons tied at the elbows. From the waist down, the silk was black and heavy as lead with sea-water, slapping wetly against her ankles at every step, and one of her satin slippers was gone. She stopped in the middle of the lane, looked up at the fuchsia lanterns, and began to hum.

Four bars of an allegro in D minor. The overture from the Piazza del Mare. Every time she came to the turn in the third bar, she flattened the sixth.

Phrolova, standing on her front step with the match-box still in her palm, opened her mouth to say *B-natural*—and closed her teeth on her tongue.

The woman turned her head. She had a smudge of gold stage-paint left on her cheekbone that the tide hadn't washed off. She had been in the third row by the canal rail when Phrolova raised her baton on the painted barge and brought the Discords over the balustrade for the finale.

She looked straight into Phrolova's face. Nothing in her eyes sharpened into terror or accusation; she merely shivered, wrapped her bare, wet arms around her middle, and looked past Phrolova toward the kitchen smoke.

"God," she said, and her voice had the same small shingle-rattle at the bottom of it. "Is that onion? I walked out of my shoe a mile back. The stones on that hill are wicked."

"The cobbler's got felt slippers on the last," Granny Leah said from the carriage box, where she had taken to sitting with a wool lap-rug for the evening air. "Won't match your ribbons, girl, but they're dry. What do they call you?"

"Cesca," the woman said, and went toward the benches, still humming the flat sixth under her breath.

Four nights after Cesca, Granny Leah did not wait in the square. Half an hour before dusk she clicked her tongue at the bays, turned the carriage round the well, and drove half a mile down the gray ash of the crater track to fetch a man who had sat down against a milestone because his left knee had given out.

He wore the boiled-leather cuirass of Septimont's perimeter guard, buckled one hole off-plumb as if he had dressed in the dark when the horns blew, and there was black rift-silt crusted in the creases of his gorget—from the north trench above the camp, during the twenty minutes after Phrolova introduced the dissonance into the maiden's frequency and the Dark Tide came through the seam like a burst dam.

Luc went down the steps to help him off the footboard. The soldier—Varro, he told Triss when she brought him a mug of hot broth—looked once at Phrolova standing beneath her front window. He knew her; soldiers on perimeter watch know the shape of what stood on the ridge above them. He looked at her for two seconds, then down at his sodden greaves, and let out a long, tired breath.

"My sister had a burner like that on the sideboard," he said to Luc, nodding at the window. "Kerosene, though. Smoked like a wet hayrick if the draft caught it."

"Colza oil," Luc said, taking the man's wet helmet to hang on the post. "She keeps the wick square."

On the Tuesday after Varro came an old man in yellow oilskins who did not belong to Phrolova at all.

He was a crabber off the outer shoals whose dory had turned over in a white squall forty leagues west of the archipelago; he had never heard of the Fractsidus, or the rift, or the conductor of the dead. He had simply met two of the others at the crossroads in the dark and followed Varro up the hill because Varro looked like a man who knew where the barracks mess was.

His arrival disturbed Phrolova more than all the rest. When they were hers, there was at least the architecture of a reckoning in it, a score returning its own notes. An old man with a lobster-gaff who asked her politely if there was salt-fish for breakfast meant there was no score at all. It meant the door was simply open, and anything wet could walk in.

By the third week there were nine of them boarding in the empty cottages behind the smithy.

They did not rattle chains or stand weeping in the orchard. They sat on the low stone wall by the trough after supper and argued amiably about whether Ragunnan bakers watered their yeast; Cesca dried her gilt ribbons on the smithy hearth—they never quite dried, remaining cold and limp as kelp to the touch, though she braided them into little Sera's hair anyway—and Varro spent his mornings with a tin of neat's-foot oil, working three hundred years of stiffness out of Granny Leah's harness leather while the old crabber told lies about dogfish. Triss doubled the stew, then took down the wash-house copper to triple it, drafting Luc to peel turnips on the back flagstone with a wet sack across his knees.

None of them ever spoke to Phrolova unless she spoke first. When they met her in the lane, they touched their foreheads or stepped aside into the grass with the quiet, unemphatic courtesy tenants show a landlady who keeps to her rooms and collects no rent.

In her parlor behind the lit window, Phrolova unlocked the bottom of her traveling trunk and took out a third notebook.

It was bound in black buckram, unruled, stiff at the spine because it had never been opened beside the red and the grey. She laid it flat on the table beside the silver scissors, dipped a steel nib, and wrote in a small, upright, copperplate hand:

*Luc. Third landing of the stair. C-sharp minor. Left clavicle.*
*Cesca. Piazza del Mare, bar 44 of the overture (sixth flat). Drowned in the surge by the bridge.*
*Varro. Septimont perimeter, third watch. The breach.*
*Nereo. Outer shoals. Not mine.*

She did not sleep. She had not required sleep in lifetimes—only the stillness that looked like it—and she dispensed with the stillness.

At two in the morning, when the village was silent save for the small, perpetual sound of nine wet coats dripping from the wash-line behind the bakery, she stood before the front window.

She raised her right hand and held her palm flat, half an inch above the top of the glass chimney.

A fraction lower, and the draft would choke. The little yellow leaf of flame would stretch, turn blue at the root, and go out. The crater road would return to the dark it had kept since the meteor fell. Whatever else was walking up from the sea would miss the turn and walk on into the ash.

The rising heat bit the center of her palm, dry and sharp. Beyond the glass, the square was empty under the high stars, the cobblestones dark in patches where Varro and the crabber had stood talking before turning in.

Phrolova lowered her hand. She picked up the silver scissors from the tray, lifted the hot glass by its brass collar with her handkerchief, and snipped a microscopic bead of carbon from the left corner of the wick.

She could turn them out. A single chord on the strings—she would not even need the baton—and the frequencies of the village would unfasten like a loosed corset; the cottages would shut their doors, the children would forget how to see them, and the nine drowned strangers would be swept back down the ash like leaves.

And the instant she played it, the cloud would vanish from the bell-gable, the tarragon would die in the pot, Milo's head would drop a quarter-inch below the chalk-mark on the bakery wall, and she would be the warden of a box of wooden dolls until the sun went cold.

The next evening at supper, when Triss set nine extra trenchers down the middle of the board, Phrolova took her place at the head, standing straight, her hands folded behind her back while the turnips went round.

---

On the twenty-eighth evening it rained in the Lost Beyond.

It was not a theatrical storm—no thunder, no wind to tear the fuchsia blossoms—only a soft, gray, slantwise rain that smelled of wet slate and turnip-fields.

The village came out of doors to stand in it. Master Corrado stood on his porch steps with his spectacles in his hand, letting it wet his bald crown; the children tipped their heads back with their mouths open; even the lodgers came out under the wide eaves of the smithy, watching water fall from above with the dry, professional skepticism of people who had had quite enough of it from underneath.

Up the crater road came a young woman.

She wore a grey wool shawl pinned neat at the collar, no proper coat, and carried a deep wicker basket over her left forearm. She walked at an easy, traveler's pace, looking about her at the cinder-banks and the garden palings with bright, unhurried interest, as if she had come to view a well-kept parish. Though the rain was drumming on every shingle and running in threads from the eaves, the hem of her skirt did not cling, and her hair—pale as wood-ash beneath the fold of her shawl—was entirely dry.

"Evening, Triss," she said pleasantly as she came through the lych-gate. "That smells like tarragon. Very sensible in the wet."

"Does well enough," Triss said from the kitchen porch, wiping her hands on her apron as though this girl had been calling with the eggs every Friday since the village was built. "You're late."

"The road's long when you come round by the point," the young woman said, and paused by the carriage wheel to hand Granny Leah a small parcel wrapped in broad green leaves. "For the morning. They're the dark ones off the south wall."

Granny Leah took the parcel into her lap. Even from across the lane, Phrolova saw the old woman's gnarled fingers settle over the leaves the way people hold a warmed brick in January.

The young woman glanced toward the smithy eaves. "Luc's got some color back, hasn't he? How's the knee, Varro?"

"Stiff when the glass drops, Miss," Varro called back from the dry spot by the water-butt, touching his forehead before he paused, frowning slightly at his own boots as if wondering where he'd met her. "Can't complain."

"Oh, you can always complain, it's half the liniment," she said cheerfully, and turned up the flagged walk toward Phrolova's door.

She stopped outside the parlor window first. She leaned in a fraction to look at the brass lamp on the sill—not with solemnity, or pity, or anything grand at all, but with the brisk, domestic approval of a woman checking that the bread-oven has kept its heat—and gave a small nod. Then she came to the foot of the three steps.

Phrolova stood in the open doorway under the lintel.

The rain made a bead-curtain between them. Behind Phrolova's ribs, the air had gone motionless; twenty thousand dead frequencies drew together like violin strings wound to the breaking peg, tuned to cut at the lift of a finger.

The young woman shifted her basket to her other arm and smiled up at her. "You do keep a good wick," she said. "Some people let them char at the edge, and then you spend all Tuesday on a stool with vinegar and newspaper."

"Who are you," Phrolova said.

"From up north. In the woods." She gestured vaguely over her shoulder with her chin, as if the northern frostlands were two fields past the cemetery. "You've got a very nice sky here now. A little thin over the ridge, maybe, if you squint at it, but two days of rain will thicken the plaster."

"You brought them here."

"Pointed them, really," the young woman said, altogether amiable. "You can't carry the drowned—not unless you're our mother, and she's been asleep since before the snows moved. They've been coming down to her pool in droves since the water changed hands last month. Knocking and knocking at all hours, poor things, with their boots full of sea, and nobody awake to open the door. Alice was losing her place in her notebooks with the draft."

Phrolova's eyes did not move from the girl's face. "And so you sent them to my house."

"Well, you had a lamp lit," the girl said, as though explaining a household economy so obvious it scarcely needed words. "Only one for miles. You'd be surprised how dark the middle is when you've just gone under and haven't had your supper. So we walked them up as far as the milestone and told them to keep the window over their left shoulder." She glanced back at the square, where Cesca was helping Triss carry the benches under the awning. "We started with yours. It seemed polite."

The hum behind Phrolova's breastbone dropped an octave, flat and cold as a cellar flag. "Is this a sentence."

The young woman blinked at her. For a moment she looked genuinely puzzled, her head tilting a fraction under the grey shawl. "Like a magistrate? Goodness, no. We don't do that. Other people do a great deal of that—bishops and Ephors and heroes with long swords, they're terribly fond of it—and it leaves the roads in such a state. No, they were just yours, and they were standing in the wet."

"Take them back."

"Oh, I couldn't possibly. Once they've had hot soup under a roof, they're resident until the tide comes round again. Besides, Triss would have my skin; she's only just got the salt right for thirty." She reached into her wicker basket, lifted out a single dark purple fig resting on its own broad leaf, and set it on the stone windowsill outside the glass, six inches from the brass lamp. Even across the threshold, Phrolova could feel the faint, late-August sun coming off its skin.

"Then what is it you want," Phrolova said.

The girl brushed a dry crumb of leaf from her cuff. "Supper's at seven," she said. "Usually half-past when Herta's in the middle of a chapter, which is every evening, so call it quarter to eight if you don't care to wait on the stairs. It's the house past the third Solistree, where the white roots come over the limestone. Knock on the door that isn't there—Viviane oiled the hinges on Tuesday, so it swings in now."

Phrolova looked at the fig on the stone, and back at the clear, pleasant eyes under the shawl. "You believe I will walk out of my own house and leave it to them."

"I think nine's a crowd when you're used to sitting alone," the young woman said cheerfully, "and there are four more on the road behind me who walk slow. Mind the crater lip if you come out after dark; the clay's greasy in the rain."

She gave a small, neighborly nod—neither a bow nor a challenge, simply the nod one gives over a garden gate after returning a borrowed basin—and turned down the walk toward the rim of the crater.

Phrolova stood motionless while she counted five slow beats. Then she stepped down into the rain and walked to the lych-gate.

The gray ash of the crater rim curved away half a mile to the left and half a mile to the right under the steady drizzle. There was no one on it. By the gatepost, where the young woman had stood to speak to Triss, three dry flagstones led toward the ditch and ended in the middle of a puddle.

In the morning the rain had stopped, leaving the air washed thin and sharp, with three clouds now instead of one, moving east toward mountains that had never previously possessed a far side.

Phrolova opened her front door and stopped on the threshold.

The whole of Stellavento was in the square. Not gathered in a ring, not staring at her porch—busy, with the brisk, elbowing industry of a country town twenty minutes before the mail-coach leaves.

Granny Leah sat up on the box of the black carriage with her best plaid shawl pinned at the throat and the reins looped through her fingers. Strapped to the rear boot was Phrolova's brass-bound traveling trunk. On the buttoned leather seat inside lay her wooden violin case, the brass catches rubbed bright with brick-dust. Triss stood by the near hind wheel, wedging an earthenware crock of stew into the wicker hamper beside a round loaf wrapped in clean linen; a thin feather of steam leaked through the bladder tied over the crock's mouth.

Unlooped from its single day, the village had remembered what the carriage was standing there for. It was finishing the morning it had been interrupted in the middle of: the morning she went away on tour.

Phrolova's fingers closed on the doorpost until the painted wood creaked under her glove.

"Take that down," she said.

Nobody stopped what they were doing. Master Corrado was kneeling by the trace-bar, testing the leather with a critical thumb.

"The near billet-strap's dry," he told Granny Leah. "Have the harness-maker at the junction put a rivet in it before you try the pass."

"It'll hold to the valley," Granny Leah said.

Phrolova walked down the steps into the square. The nine lodgers were there too, standing a little back by the well-curb so as not to be in the way of the family—Cesca with an old wool coat of Triss's over her wet green silk, Varro leaning on an ash stick, the old crabber chewing on an unlit pipe.

"I am not going anywhere," Phrolova said. She let the levelness out of her voice, letting the iron show through—the Overseer's pitch that had brought three halls of initiates to their knees in the dark. "Take the trunk off the rack. Unhitch the horses. You are dead, all of you, and that road ends at the ditch."

By the horses' heads, Milo and Sera stopped plating clover into the mane and blinked at her—not at the words, which slid off them like rain off a slate roof, but at the volume.

Triss did not turn round. She tucked the corner of the linen cloth tighter round the loaf so the crust wouldn't take the wheel-dust. "You'll want to eat the stew before noon," she said briskly, "or the grease settles on the top. I put a horn spoon in the side pocket. Don't leave it at the inn like you did last spring."

"Triss—"

At the back of the carriage, the heavy leather luggage-straps groaned.

Luc stood behind the boot, his sodden black cuffs turned back twice from his wrists. The heavy trunk had slipped an inch off the iron rest; he put his shoulder under the brass corner, heaved it square onto the rack, and drew the tongue of the buckle home through the third hole.

Phrolova walked around the wheel and stopped three feet from him. The cold, salt smell of the bottom of the sea came off his coat in the morning sun. The dark diagonal line across his collarbone was level with her chin.

"You will put the lamp out," she said to him, very low, because cruelty was the last coin she had in her pocket, "before the wheels clear the gate."

Luc tested the strap with his thumb, then stepped back from the axle and wiped his wet palms on his thighs.

"No, Miss," he said plainly. "We'll light it at dusk. Varro knows the screw on that sort of burner, and I can trim it well enough if you leave the little scissors on the tray."

She stared at him. "Why."

"There's four more on the road," he said. He met her eyes once—not warm, not forgiving, with the clear, flat steadiness of a clerk reading out the bottom line of a ledger that would never balance in this world or the next. "Don't mistake me, Miss. It isn't for you. You cut me down on the stair when I had my blade up for you, and I expect I'll mind that as long as I'm here. Only it's dark at the bottom, and somebody ought to be home when they come up the hill."

He held his right hand out between them, palm up. The skin was pale and creased from the salt water.

For a long moment Phrolova stood still in the wheel-rut. Behind her, Granny Leah clicked her tongue to the off-mare to settle her in the shafts. The last time she had put her foot on this iron step, she had looked back from the crest of the road at twenty roofs in the sun, and when she came back up the mountain there had been only red glass and cinders and a silence she had spent lifetimes filling with noise.

Slowly, Phrolova reached into the pocket of her skirt, took out the small wooden box of matches, and laid it on his wet palm. She was careful not to touch his skin.

She turned, set her boot on the iron step, and climbed into the carriage. The leather seat was cool and smelled of dust. She sat with her spine straight, one gloved hand resting on the lid of her violin case, looking straight ahead through the front glass at Granny Leah's shawl.

"Keep your right elbow up in the bariolage!" Master Corrado shouted from the well-curb, cupping one hand to his mouth as the whip cracked and the traces took the strain. "You always drop it when the hall is damp!"

The iron tires took the cobbles with a lurch. Milo and Sera and four of the smaller ones ran alongside the doors, laughing and slapping the lacquered panels with their open palms the way they had done three hundred years ago, keeping pace past the bakery, past the smithy, past the lych-gate at the edge of the village where the track had always ended in a wall of grey nothing.

The children dropped back at the gatepost, waving their caps. The mares' hooves struck hard, unfamiliar stone where the nothing had been, and the carriage kept going.

---

It took no time at all, or a season; the light outside the carriage window simply thinned from morning gold to pewter to the deep, clear blue of snow under trees.

The wheels stopped without a jolt.

Phrolova opened the door and stepped down into four inches of dry powder snow.

Above her rose the Solistrees—trunks wider than cathedral towers, their bark pale as bleached bone, their high boughs braided with a cold, slow luminescence that gave light without heat. Beyond the canopy, the northern sky was immense, black-violet, and crowded with real, indifferent stars that had never heard her name or kept her tempo.

She turned to speak to Granny Leah.

The forest track behind her was unbroken snow. There was no carriage, no horses, no hoof-prints, no wheel-ruts between the drifts. Her brass-bound trunk sat neatly at the edge of the track beside Triss's hamper, the earthenware crock inside stone-cold now, and the violin case was in her hand.

Ahead of her stood three great trees in a row along the rise. Past the third, where the pale roots heaved up over an outcrop of black limestone, there was a flat face of rock between two buttresses of bark. It had neither lintel nor latch, only a faint square of thawed stone in the rime, as if a hearth were burning on the other side of the mountain.

Phrolova set her jaw, walked through the snow to the rock, and knocked twice with a gloved knuckle.

The stone swung inward without a sound—well-oiled, as promised—and let out a wave of warm air that smelled of beeswax, dried rosemary, old vellum, and mutton broth.

It was a long, low-beamed hall built into the root-vaults of the tree, lit by tallow dips and a stone fireplace wide enough to roast an ox.

Nobody rose to meet her. Nobody dropped a spoon.

At the near corner of the hearth-rug sat a girl in a black dress that was slightly too severe for her, an enormous pointed hat pushed back on her head, three open folios balanced across her knees, and a ginger tomcat asleep on the hem of her skirt. She looked up at the draft, quill poised, her eyes bright with scholarly greed.

"You're the Fractsidus—" she began.

A tall woman drifting two feet above the floorboards on the bell of a translucent, spectral jellyfish reached down with one booted heel and trod, calmly and firmly, on the scholar's shoe.

"Ow," the scholar said, glaring upward, and shut her mouth, though she immediately dipped her quill and wrote something in the margin of her book instead.

"Mind the threshold," said the woman on the jellyfish, touching two fingers to the brim of a battered pirate hat. She had a thick white braid over one shoulder and red eyes that took Phrolova's measure in half a second—the way a shipwright looks at a salvaged mast to see what canvas it can still carry—before she swam lazily through the air toward the chimney-breast, where a wooden staff with an oiled circular saw-blade mounted at its head leaned beside the brass poker like an ordinary hearth-broom. "The flagstone tips on the left. I haven't got round to the mortar."

High up in the rafters, where the pale root-arches crossed in the smoke, a woman in dark purple veils hung upside down by one knee from a rigged rope, her hair trailing toward the chandelier. She did not come down. She had a pack of tarot cards in her hands; she slipped one card from the bottom of the deck, glanced at its face upside down, smiled to herself as if someone had told a mildly improper joke, and tucked it into her bodice without a word.

At the head of a stone cellar-stair in the back corner, a young woman with ink on her cuffs and a Guild scribe's badge at her collar looked up from a thick sheaf of paper, gave a shy, busy nod, and went back to her copying.

And at the long oak table in the middle of the room stood the woman from the crater road, her grey shawl hung on a peg by the door, setting out trenchers.

"You're in good time," she said warmly, as if Phrolova had merely stepped out to the shed for kindling. "The broth's just off the boil. Leave your trunk by the settle; Viviane's jellyfish will bring it up when it's done sulking."

There were four places laid at the table. As Phrolova watched, the young woman set down a fifth—not the heavy everyday earthenware the others had, but a plate and a cup of thin, sea-pale porcelain that looked centuries older than the table, taken down from the high cupboard where a sleeping householder's own china was kept.

Nobody asked where she had come from. Nobody asked what she had done in Rinascita, or at the rift, or who was eating turnips under her trees.

Phrolova stood on the threshold with her violin case in her hand, her spine iron-straight, braced for the tribunal, the bargain, the terms—and the silence where the terms should have been pressed against her ribs until she could scarcely draw breath. She had been hated by experts and forgiven by fools; she had no defense prepared for a house that simply set a plate.

"I do not eat mutton broth," she said coldly, because if she did not say something sharp she would do something unforgivable, such as sit down.

"There's hard cheese," said the girl on the hearth-rug, still rubbing her instep while the ginger cat climbed into her lap, "and cold tea, if you're going to be tedious. Shut the door, you're letting the snow in on the bindings."

After supper—at which Phrolova sat on the extreme edge of her chair, broke one crust of bread in half for manners, and spoke only when passed the salt—Viviane took a tallow dip from the mantel and jerked her chin toward the corner stair.

"Best say goodnight to the landlady," she said. "Mind your head on the middle arch."

They went down the winding stone steps into the earth, past the young scribe at her desk, into a cavern that smelled of wet limestone and very old salt.

The pool filled the floor. The water was black and still as cut obsidian, threaded through with slow, pulsing veins of amber light from the roots of the Solistrees above. Over the surface, invisible to the eye but loud as a symphony to Phrolova's ear, great rings of frequency turned in a vast, patient gyre.

And beneath the water, resting on a shelf of dark stone as though in a bed with the counterpane drawn up to her chin, lay a woman with dark hair fanned across the rock, fast asleep.

Viviane waited on the bottom step with the candle. Phrolova walked alone to the lip of the pool.

She had spent centuries listening to the dead—every ragged, frantic, looping bar of them, conducted by force or stitched together out of echoes. She looked down through the black water and listened to the sleeper's frequency.

It was deep, and unhurried, and completely whole, and somewhere in the undertone of it—the way a cello holds a low pedal point beneath a shifting chord—there was a warmth she knew by heart: the exact, terrible, uncalculating kindness of someone who had once sat on a bench beside her and promised to come back for the second movement.

Phrolova's gloved fingers tightened on her cuffs. Her face did not alter by a hair. She stood at the water's edge for a full minute, looking down at a stranger who had never heard her name, and then turned and walked back up the stairs.

On the upper landing, under the high eaves where the tree-roots made small, dry gables, the young woman from the road opened the door to a narrow room with a bed, a washstand, a wooden music-stand someone had brought down from an attic and dusted off, and a casement window looking south into the snow.

"Blankets are in the cedar chest," she said, setting the candle on the washstand. "The wind comes round the root here after midnight, so drop the iron latch if it rattles." She paused with her hand on the door-ring, looking back—the only question anyone in the house had asked all night. "We keep an hour each, when the house doesn't knock. Which one would you like?"

Phrolova looked past the candle at the dark square of the window, where the sky went south over the frost, over the mountains, toward a crater where nine wet coats were hanging behind the bakery.

"The one after dusk," Phrolova said.

"After dusk," the young woman repeated, nodding as if that were the most convenient hour on the clock.

From the stairwell below came the scholar's voice, brisk and aggrieved: "Elaina! If you used my good gall-ink for the butcher's list again I'm hexing your boots to the floor!"

"That's the third bottle from the left, Herta, the cheap one!" Elaina called back cheerfully over the banister, and gave Phrolova a pleasant nod. "Sleep well."

The door clicked shut.

Phrolova stood in the middle of the narrow floorboards. After a moment she walked to the window, set her violin case down on the sill, and looked out into the northern dark, where the snow was coming down between the pale trees.

Four hundred leagues south, the shadow of the bell-gable reached the horse-trough. In the parlor with the drawn-back curtains, Luc wiped his wet hands on the lining of his coat, struck one of her matches on the side of the box, and touched the flame to the square-trimmed wick. He set the glass chimney back into its four brass prongs, turned the screw until the light sat clear in the pane, and went out to the long table under the fuchsia trees, where Triss was taking the lid off the pot and four new people were coming up the hill in the dark.

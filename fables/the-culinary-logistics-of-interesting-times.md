# The Culinary Logistics of Interesting Times

*Being a partial archive of the Long Table Mutual Provision Society, recovered from six kitchens, two customs offices, and one lacquered pudding box. Assembled without permission by persons who feed people for a living.*

---

## I. EGLA — THE STANDING ORDER

The bakery in Egla Town kept a book of standing orders. Most of them were alive. One of them had been dead for twenty years.

Rosa wrote it out fresh every January anyway, in the front of the book, where her grandmother had written it before her: *Lemon cakes, sugar crystals, extra crystals, no fork. C.*

Her husband thought it was superstition. Rosa let him think that. It was cheaper than explaining that some recipes are graves you keep swept, and that Gram had made her promise on a bag of pastry flour — *the girl liked them crunchy on top, you keep the crunch, whatever the Order says about her* — and a promise made on flour binds harder than one made on blood, because flour is what everyone actually lives on.

So when a black-haired stranger with golden eyes walked in at closing, ordered lemon cakes with sugar crystals in a voice that was clearly relaying someone else's small, careful words, Rosa boxed them, tied the string, and said three sentences.

Gram kept the recipe. I got married. The door was always unlocked.

The stranger's hands shook on the box. Rosa pretended to count the till.

That evening a dead saint sang in the square and the whole town wept into its aprons, and Rosa stood at the back doing arithmetic, because somebody had to. Two hundred people crying in a square at supper hour. The tavern would flood. Cosimo would need bread by dawn, twice the usual. If pilgrims started coming — and pilgrims *would* start coming, pilgrims follow miracles the way gulls follow trawlers — she'd need flour beyond her quota, and the Order's requisition office charged double for "unforecast demand," which was their word for joy.

She went home, kissed her husband, and wrote her first letter to the woman in Ragunna who could get flour past the Order's tariff men in coffins marked SALT.

Miracles are lovely, she wrote. Miracles also eat.

## II. FROM THE FOUNDING DOCUMENTS OF THE LONG TABLE

*(single sheet, thirty years old, handwriting precise to the point of hostility, unsigned)*

Principles of the Provision Ring, drafted for the alley kitchens of Septimont in the Year of the Grain Fraud:

1. An army marches on its stomach. A city riots on an empty one. Whoever fills the stomach steers the city. Remember this and tell no senator.
2. Evacuation orders are written by people who have never proofed dough. Assume every order arrives four hours later than your bread needs. Bake accordingly.
3. Feed the heroes last. They are the only ones guaranteed a meal — someone always feeds a hero. Feed the stretcher-bearers, the sandbag lines, the woman who counts the boats. Heroes run on glory for a day or two. Everyone else runs on soup immediately.
4. Keep the ledger honest and the manifests creative.
5. When the sky is on fire, the correct response is a bigger pot.

*(Beneath, in Rosa's grandmother's hand, added later:)* Whoever wrote this reorganized half the grain contracts in Septimont at seventeen and then vanished into the temple. If she ever presents this page, feed her, and charge her thirty years of interest.

## III. RAGUNNA — MARGHERITA, RE: HERO APPETITES

Letter, Margherita of the Vecchio Forno pizzeria, Bank Street, Ragunna, to the Long Table circular:

Sisters, brothers, assorted grease-witches,

Three items of business.

ITEM ONE. The Carnevale. You will have heard the Order's miracle turned out to be a whale in a harness. What you will want to know is the catering angle. When a city discovers its god's punishments were machinery, it does two things in this order: it stands very still, and then it gets extremely hungry. We sold eleven days of stock in one night. Old men who hadn't left their parishes in a decade came to Bank Street to eat something honest with their own hands. I recommend all establishments keep a Heresy Reserve of flour from now on. Faith is volatile. Appetite is forever.

ITEM TWO. Hero appetites, a field report. There is a tiefling who orders our largest pie — the one we built as a joke, the one with its own table — and finishes it, and then apologizes to the crust. She brings a small acolyte in white who eats one slice over the course of an hour and glows the entire time. They think they are being discreet. The tiefling once left a tip that covered a window some other hero broke fighting something on our roof. This is the whole economy of heroism, from where I stand: they break the window, they pay for the window, everyone calls it salvation. Fine. The pizza was good. Even the roof-thing looked like it wanted a slice.

ITEM THREE. Evacuation drills. The Order's new protocol gives Bank Street a nine-minute clearance window. I have explained to three separate officials that a wood-fired oven at temperature takes forty minutes to bank safely and that an abandoned one takes out the block more reliably than any Tacet Discord. The third official finally understood when I put his hand near the vent. We now have a Baker's Clause: ovens get a fire-warden and a proper cool-down, and in exchange we feed the wardens. I am sharing the clause text with this letter. Take it to your own officials. Bring a hot loaf to the meeting. Negotiation is 40% leverage and 60% smell.

Yours in crust,
Margherita

P.S. To whoever in Septimont is hoarding the good bergamot — the High Priestess's tea comes through my cousin's dock, and I know things about your olive invoices. Play nice.

## IV. SEPTIMONT — MUKA MUKA, THE CORRECTION

Faustino had owned the Muka Muka café for eleven years and had learned to read the city through its orders the way sailors read weather through gulls.

Senators ordered wine at noon when the vote was going badly. Gladiators ordered milk and honey the night before a bout and pretended it was for a nephew. And for years, longer than Faustino had held the lease, there had been a standing arrangement handed down from the previous owner like a haunted heirloom: a pot of Earl Grey, brewed and delivered to a certain balcony, and left. You did leave it. You did leave without waiting. The tea went cold. That was, apparently, the point. Payment arrived monthly, exact, in an envelope smelling faintly of temple incense, with occasional notes in that surgical handwriting: *Steeped 40 seconds long on the 12th. I could taste the impatience.*

Faustino had theories about the balcony arrangement. All of them were wrong, and he knew they were wrong, which is the most a small businessman can hope to know about the doings of the High Priestess of Septimont.

Then one afternoon two women took the corner table. One had silver-blue hair, one arm, and the posture of someone who had recently been discharged from either a hospital or a mythology. The other had golden eyes and the fidgety happiness of a first date. Faustino brought menus. The silver-haired one looked at him the way an auditor looks at a decade of receipts.

"Earl Grey," she said. "Hot."

"Of course."

"I want to be clear." A pause. The whole table's weight went into it. "Hot when it arrives. Hot when she drinks it."

"Iuno," the golden-eyed one said, delighted, scandalized.

"I'm placing an order."

"You're apologizing to a teapot."

"I'm placing," she said, "an order."

Faustino, who had carried cold tea up a temple stair for eleven years without asking why, brought the pot out at a temperature that could have sterilized instruments, and stood at the counter watching the one-armed woman watch the other woman drink, and understood, with the specific vertigo of a caterer glimpsing history, that he had spent a decade as the instrument of somebody's worst habit and was now present for the correction.

He comped the biscotti. It felt liturgical.

The standing balcony order was cancelled the next week, by envelope. Enclosed was payment through the end of the year and one line: *Serve it hot from now on. To everyone. Consider it doctrine.*

Faustino framed it. Customers assume it's a health inspection certificate. In a way, he tells them, it is.

## V. THE EARL GREY CRISIS — A CORRESPONDENCE

**1. Dockmaster's cousin (Ragunna) to Margherita:**

Cousin. Bad news travels in crates. The bergamot shipment out of the southern groves is sitting in the Order's customs yard, seal broken, "pending doctrinal review." Doctrinal review of TEA. The clerk says citrus oils can carry "frequencies." The clerk is angling for a bribe he is too pious to name. Meanwhile the whole eastern run is snarled because half the inspectors got reassigned after the Carnevale scandal and the other half are pretending they never signed anything ever. Earliest release: five weeks.

**2. Margherita to Faustino (Septimont), urgent, by privateer post:**

Faustino. Your tea is in prison. Five weeks. How bad is this on your end, honestly.

**3. Faustino to Margherita:**

Margherita. Let me describe the situation with appropriate calm.

The Earl Grey standing order now serves a household that includes: the former High Priestess of Septimont, who drinks it the way other people breathe; the Champion of Septimont, who has apparently spent years drinking it cold out of devotion and now gets it hot out of justice; and a wolf gladiator who once told my counter boy, and I quote him quoting her, "Hot tea. Every morning. Or I'm spearing you again." My counter boy believes this was said to the priestess. My counter boy also believes it applies transitively to suppliers.

Above all of them sits the Ephor. Augusta came in personally last month. Ordered Earl Grey. Recited the brewing spec from memory — four minutes, served hot — like a woman reading a will. Drank it looking at the empty chair across from her. Tipped like a funeral. Whatever that cup is to her, I am not going to be the man who tells the Ephor of Septimont that her cup is pending doctrinal review.

I have nineteen days of stock. I am cutting the public menu today and telling customers it's a seasonal blend rotation. Get me tea, Margherita. The stability of two governments may genuinely — I want you to sit with this — depend on a citrus.

**4. Margherita to Rosa (Egla):**

Rosa. Your coffin man. The one who moves flour past tariffs in boxes marked SALT. Does he do leaves?

**5. Rosa to Margherita:**

He does anything that fits in a coffin, which, he wishes me to note, is everything, eventually. But he won't touch the customs yard itself — the Order's yard has salt-line wards and a very awake dog. What he CAN do is meet a boat outside the harbor mouth if somebody with papers and no shame gets the crates over the wall to the water.

Do we know anyone with papers and no shame?

**6. Margherita to Rosa:**

We know privateers. The Troupe of Fools ran relief boats during the Carnevale mess and their captain owes me for six months of dockside lunches he charmed his way out of paying for. His exact words at the time were "put it on the ledger of destiny." I am calling in destiny.

**7. Brant, of the Troupe of Fools, to the Long Table, written on the back of a betting slip:**

Ladies. The crates are on the water. You should know it went beautifully, by which I mean the dog liked me, the clerk got a case of "sacramental grappa" that is regular grappa with a ribbon, and at no point did anyone have to invoke the word "frequencies." Roccia carried four crates at once and refused to make it look difficult, which ruined my dramatic timing.

One crate got wet. Consider it pre-steeped.

The ledger of destiny is settled. Feed us when we dock.

**8. Faustino to the Long Table circular, three weeks later:**

Resupplied. Crisis averted. For the record and for posterity:

The Ephor came in the morning after the first fresh brew. She drank it, set the cup down, and said, "It's right again." I said we'd had a supply hiccup. She looked at me for a long moment and said, "Septimont thanks its quartermasters," and left a tip that paid off my espresso machine.

I want the record to reflect that she said quartermasters. Not shopkeepers. Somewhere in that armored head we have been reclassified as strategic.

I have complicated feelings about this and will be processing them via inventory.

**9. Appended by Rosa, undated:**

For the archive. The clerk who impounded the tea was reassigned to a rural parish two months later. The reassignment order came down through channels nobody can trace, in handwriting the coffin man swears he's seen before on thirty-year-old grain contracts.

We fed a priestess her tea. Somebody, somewhere, filed the paperwork of gratitude.

I keep saying we're a supper club. I'm starting to suspect we're a government.

## VI. MINUTES — LONG TABLE QUARTERLY, HELD IN COSIMO'S BACK ROOM, EGLA

Present: Rosa (chair, under protest), Margherita (via letter, read aloud with dramatic emphasis by Cosimo), Faustino (in person, on "vacation"), the coffin man (present; declined to be minuted by name; hereafter "SALT"), two soup-sisters from the Septimont shelter kitchens, one Fisalia household cook who says she's just here for the recipes and takes more notes than anyone.

AGENDA ITEM: The Sovereign campaign, post-mortem.

The soup-sisters reported. When the army marched on Murmurstown, official provisioning covered the gladiators. It did NOT cover: the stretcher lines, the anchor-stone crews, the families who walked to the city walls to wait, or the two thousand refugees the official plan assumed would "self-provision." The Long Table moved four tons of bread, root vegetables, and salt fish in six days. Attached: costs, three commandeered mule carts (returned, one bitten), and a formal complaint from a quartermaster we ignored so thoroughly he eventually started helping.

Rosa raised the structural point, and asked that it be minuted in full:

"Every ballad from that campaign is about the six of them at the Wedge. Fine. They earned their ballads. But I talked to a woman who was there, on the anchor lines, and you know what she told me? The heroes fought for nine hours. Nobody fights for nine hours on courage. Courage is the first hour. The other eight are breakfast. Some cook in that camp fed them at dawn, and that cook is in the ballad the way flour is in the cake — invisible and load-bearing. I want us to stop being surprised by this. Heroes are a delivery mechanism. The payload is calories and somebody's grandmother's stubbornness. We ARE the miracle's supply chain, and I move that we start planning like it."

Motion carried. Faustino abstained on the grounds that being load-bearing gives him heartburn.

AGENDA ITEM: The insulation problem.

SALT raised it, and SALT talks so rarely that everyone shut up.

"That pack. The Champion's people. You ever notice they're never hungry? Cities drown, gods fall, and that lot always has a hot meal and a tavern table waiting. You think that's luck?" He let it sit. "It's us. Has been for years. Standing orders, warm rooms held on quiet instruction, tabs that get settled by envelope before anyone asks. Somebody has been paying, all along, to make sure that when those six stumble home from the end of the world, there's soup."

Discussion followed as to who. Faustino said the envelopes smell like temple incense. The Fisalia cook said nothing and smiled like a woman whose employer waters camellias and knows everything. Rosa ruled the question tabled indefinitely, on the grounds that some patrons tip best in the dark.

Minuted for the philosophers among us: the difference between a hero and everyone else may simply be a well-run tab.

AGENDA ITEM: Expansion. Correspondence received from the north — a chicken establishment in a city under a mountain, and, stranger, a letter from a city that until recently did not exist. Both applications attached. Both approved. The Table gets longer.

Meeting closed. Cosimo fed everyone. This is also minuted, because it always should be.

## VII. HONAMI — THE PUDDING STAND AT THE EAST GATE

The rain over Honami fell for thirty years, and then one afternoon it stopped, and the city came back the way a held breath comes back, and among the ten thousand impossible administrative problems this created was a small one at the east gate: a pudding stand, intact, its recipe card still pinned above the burner in a dead man's handwriting.

Kanta was the grandson. He'd grown up outside, on the story — grandfather ran a stand by the shrine road, red bean, the miko sisters used to stop there, then the rain came and the whole city folded into the sky. He reopened it because the recipe card existed and because unemployment makes historians of us all. He soaked the beans grandfather's way. He got the wobble right on the fourth batch.

His first regular came on a grey morning, white-haired, iris-scented, wearing the kind of stillness that makes queues form somewhere else.

"Two servings," she said.

He looked past her. The road was empty.

"Two," she said.

She ate one at the little counter, slowly, like someone keeping an appointment. The second serving sat across from her, steam rising, spoon set out. She paid for both, thanked him for both, and left the second untouched.

She came back a week later. Two servings. Again the week after.

Kanta wrote to the Long Table because the coffin man's routes had reached even here, and because he was twenty-four and needed adults:

*Question for the network. I have a customer who always orders for two and always eats alone. The second bowl goes cold. I've started making the second one slightly less sweet, on instinct — grandfather's card has a note in the margin, "G. — less sugar, she'll never admit it." Same instinct? Different customer? Do I say something? What is the etiquette for feeding someone's ghost?*

Rosa's reply came back in ten days, and Kanta pinned it under the recipe card:

*Kid. I've kept a dead girl's cake order in my book for twenty years, and last spring she walked in and ordered it. You keep making the second bowl. You keep the sugar low. You say nothing. Our whole trade is this: we feed the living, and we hold the table for everyone else. Sometimes the table pays off. Bill both servings and keep the spoon polished.*

So he does. And once — only once — he came back from the storeroom to find the second bowl scraped clean, the spoon licked, and his white-haired regular laughing into her sleeve at something the empty air had said, her breath fogging like it was cold, in summer, in sunlight.

He charged for two. She tipped for three. Neither of them explained anything, which Kanta has come to understand is the highest form of customer service.

## VIII. LAHAI-ROI — THE LUCKY BIRD, THE LONGEST TEN MINUTES

Grethe had run the Lucky Bird for nineteen years under an artificial sun, and her position on evacuation orders was on record with the district office in writing:

*The fryers stay hot until the last shuttle. People do stupid things on empty stomachs and evacuations are made entirely of people. You want an orderly line to the Riseway? It smells like my chicken. Signed, Grethe. Again.*

So when the sirens went and the announcements started — barriers, Stridergate, the god is leaving, everyone climb — Grethe did what she'd drilled for and never told anyone she'd drilled for. She cranked every fryer. She turned the counter into a pass-through. Her two counter kids ran baskets to the barrier crews and the medics and the transit marshals, because Principle Three is Principle Three: someone always feeds a hero, so you feed everyone else.

An academy volunteer in an oversized vest stopped mid-run, staring at her like she was the strangest thing in an evacuating city. "You're cooking? NOW?"

"You eaten today?"

"I — no?"

"Then shut up and hold this." Basket. "Barrier crew, section nine. Tell them there's more."

The girl ran. Grethe dropped another load in the oil.

The ten minutes became eleven. Grethe knew the number the whole city knew — ten minutes, that's the barrier's whole promise — and she watched it break on the departure board and kept cooking, because the math of her entire life was simple: as long as the oil is hot, we are still a city. Twelve minutes. Impossible things on the feeds — a woman conducting mathematics, an umbrella, whole waves of monsters freezing mid-lunge. Thirteen. Her counter kid was crying and portioning slaw at the same time, which Grethe minuted, internally, as the human condition.

Then the mountain shook, and someone screamed THEY'RE BACK, and the whole street sound changed key at once.

Grethe took the next basket out of the oil, salted it, and started the family-size order she'd been holding shell-eggs for since the sirens started. Regulars are regulars. They'd be hungry.

## IX. LAHAI-ROI — THE LUCKY BIRD, THE REGULARS

*(From Grethe's letter of application to the Long Table, excerpted, later read aloud at the quarterly to sustained cackling:)*

You asked me to describe our relationship to "hero logistics." I will describe my Tuesday.

They come in around six. Seven of them now. I will use my counter names, which are the only names I need.

WOLF orders the family bucket. For herself. She has done this since her first visit, when my counter kid asked "for how many?" and she looked at him until he stopped asking. She eats with total focus, ears tracking the room, and leaves the wing tips arranged in a neat line, which I believe is either a compliment or a threat and have decided is a compliment.

LAUREL orders "what Wolf's having, but small," then eats a third of Wolf's anyway. Wolf allows this and no one else. Laurel once tried to pay with a doll's button, apologized for a full minute, and now overpays and hides it under the napkin.

LUTE orders the same thing every time and describes it differently every time. Last week my chicken was "a brass section." I've stopped charging her for drinks because she tunes the room. Fights stop when she's in. That is worth a lemonade.

SCISSORS knows my counter kids by their footsteps. Orders by pointing at the menu with terrifying precision. Once, when a drunk got loud near the till, there was a sound like the air being edited, and the drunk's belt was suddenly on the floor and his mood entirely reformed. She tipped extra that night for "the mess." There was no mess. There was, if anything, less mess than before.

TEA drinks tea. In my restaurant, which serves fried chicken under a man-made sun a mile underground, she produces her own porcelain, one-handed, with a dignity that makes my whole establishment feel like a temple annex, and asks for hot water like she's granting me a sacrament. Fine. FINE. I stock Earl Grey now. Your Faustino sent me the spec — four minutes, hot when it arrives, hot when she drinks it — with a note reading "trust me, this is doctrine." The first time I served it correctly she looked at the cup, then at me, and said, "You've been talking to Septimont." I said the Table is long. She left a tip and a look I can only describe as auditor's respect.

GOLD is the mother. She orders for whoever forgot to, catches the falling glass before it falls, and always sits facing the door, and, when she thinks nobody's watching, watches the whole table eat with an expression I know from my own face in the fryer's steel. Head count. Everyone here. Everyone fed. Feeds herself last. I've started boxing her a portion "for the road" so the woman gets a meal she doesn't stand guard over.

And the new one. LITTLE BIRD. First visit, she held the menu like a foreign document and ordered by pointing at what Gold had. Ate the first piece slow, eyes closed. Second piece faster. Third like the fryer might be a rumor. She's filling out week by week and Gold watches every bite land like it's being carved into stone somewhere. Last Tuesday, Little Bird finished the drumstick, sat back, and said — loud, amazed, like it was breaking news — "It's BETTER when you're hungry."

Wolf said, "That's the whole secret, pup."

Tea said, "There are others."

"Like what?"

"It is better," Tea said, "when it's hot."

And Gold laughed until she had to put her cup down, and the whole table caught it, and my counter kid asked me why I was smiling at the fryer, and I said grease in my eye, and we are all liars in this business, every single one.

Enclosed: my dues, a jar of my brine, and the Lucky Bird's answer to your standing question — who gets to feed the heroes?

Whoever shows up and lights the oil. Same as feeds everyone.

## X. CLOSING THE LEDGER

*(Final page of the archive. Rosa's hand.)*

Last spring an envelope arrived at the bakery. Temple incense. No name. Inside: settlement, in full and beyond, of thirty years of accounts I hadn't known were open — the balcony pots, the held rooms, the quiet tabs, the grain contracts my Gram used to complain about that turned out to be the pilings under half of Septimont's peace. Also a reassigned customs clerk, since we're itemizing.

At the bottom, one line, in the handwriting from the founding page:

*You fed her before I loved her. The debt was always mine. Consider the interest paid. — a former quartermaster*

And beneath that, added in a second hand, rounder, warmer, gold as an egg yolk:

*She means thank you. Also — the lemon cakes travel well. C. says extra crystals next time. We'll come collect them ourselves.*

So the dead saint's standing order is active again, and the Table stretches from a bakery in Egla to a pudding stand in a resurrected city to a chicken joint under a mountain, and somewhere in the ballads six women are saving the world, and good for them, sincerely.

Here's what the ballads leave out, and what this archive exists to state for the record.

Every miracle you have ever heard of was catered.

The sky opens, the god kneels, the girl comes home — and forty minutes later, every one of those people is hungry, because bodies are honest even when stories are grand. Somebody has the pot on. Somebody held the table. Somebody smuggled the bergamot past the wards so the woman who holds the line could hold her cup.

Miracles run on calories. The calories run on us.

Keep the ledger honest. Keep the manifests creative. Keep the second bowl warm.

The Table is long, and it is set.

— R.

# Seven Grounds
### *a Lyre, Speak to Me cycle — one story for each of the pack, set in the season between the Nihility and the road east*

---

## Preface: The Assignments

Each of these seven has a philosophy she reaches for and a philosophy she is. The reach is usually inherited — a father's sentence, a mother's sentence, a dramatist's lesson, an old story about clay. The other thing is what her hands have been doing all along while her mouth quoted the inheritance. The cycle below sits in the gap.

**Mei** reaches for the catcher — the figure standing at the edge of the field where the ground stops, taking the small bodies out of the air before they arrive at the drop. It is Salinger's fantasy and it is a fantasy of stillness: nobody grows, nobody lands, the catcher holds the post forever. What Mei has done in fourteen recorded instances is go over the edge alongside. She has caught nobody. She has accompanied everybody. The title of the job was misheard, and the misheard version is the one she has been performing.

**Iuno** reaches for the oracle — Cassandra with the arithmetic, the one whose knowledge licenses the act. She is Kierkegaard's Abraham: the beloved on the mountain, the knife raised on the authority of a voice, and above all the silence, because Abraham cannot explain himself. The moment he explains, it becomes murder. Iuno's throat sealed for thirty years around exactly that structure. What redeems Abraham is that Isaac comes home by virtue of the absurd, and what redeems Iuno is that Mei keeps walking back up the mountain uninvited.

**Lupa** reaches for Pindar and the marble — kleos, the victory ode, the name that outlasts the body, the terror of the stamp that reads *withdrawn.* She embodies Arendt: the space of appearance, the plurality without which a deed is unwitnessed and therefore undone, the distinction between *what* a person is (asset, designation, Schedule B) and *who* she is, which shows only in speech and action before others. She wanted marble. She became the chorus. She says other people's names out loud in rooms where saying it is binding, and there is no other mechanism, and she checked.

**Ciaccona** reaches for her father's torch — parrhesia, the truth raised where it lights the road. She is Benjamin's storyteller: the sung memory set against the archive with an owner and the archive with no singer; the tale that has no manuscript and no author and therefore cannot be confiscated; the hole in the verse where the congregation gets in. The torch was inherited from a man composing exit music in four seconds. The low fire is hers.

**Cartethyia** reaches for the knight — Bastiano's Quixote, the madness of seeing life as it should be. At six she asked the question no child had asked in forty years of tellings, and the question was about the giant. She has been the giant ever since: the thing sealed in the tower with buttons for eyes, born in the dark water, mistaken for a monster, turning its enormous arms and declining to flatten the person swinging at it, because arms that long could have finished it on the first day and it wanted the company.

**Chisa** reaches for the cut — the tool she was handed, precision as ethics, the clean severance. She is Levinas: the face-to-face that founds obligation, in a woman for whom faces are a smear of color. She built the whole apparatus out of gait and hem and the third syllable. And the object that made her carries the doctrine on it — the inner face, bare alloy, the surface no inspector has ever examined, where a stranger stood at a bench and put a wave with a hammer for a girl he would never meet. The trace of one who has already left.

**Amy** reaches for the golem — *emeth*, the word in the clay, truth of the kind that requires no witness, erased when the work is done. She ran the proof alone in the dark for ten years: *where there is a song, there is a singer.* It is Descartes' proof, and it works, and it is a sealed room. What she needed at the bottom was a shrine maiden asking the question twice. What she needs now is somebody to hold the other end of the pencil, because the whole difficulty with measuring yourself is that the line comes out crooked.

## I. Somebody Will Meet You There
### *Mei*

---

### i.

The drop above the fourth agri-tier had been a maintenance ledge before somebody in Flight Instruction noticed that it was eleven meters of clean air over standing barley, and that barley, unlike concrete, forgives.

Mei took the Tuesday session because Lucilla asked and because she liked the smell up there. Two hundred meters of engineered spring ran below the ledge in long green shelves, and the sprinklers came on at the eleventh bell in a system-wide sigh, and the whole tier went silver for four minutes while the light came through it.

Nine first-years. Eight of them had gone off the ledge twice already and were arguing at the bottom about who had made the better shape.

The ninth stood at the edge with her boots exactly parallel.

"Sela."

"I'm calculating."

"How's it coming."

"The numbers keep agreeing with me." Sela was sixteen and built like a fence post and had the flattest voice in the cohort. "That's the problem. I'd like one of them to disagree so I'd have something to argue with."

Mei came and stood beside her and looked down at the barley, which was doing what barley does, which is lean.

"I'm at the bottom," she said. "That's the arrangement. You come off, I'm there."

"I know."

"So?"

"So I've watched you catch four people this morning," Sela said. "You do it early. You're already moving before they leave the ledge." A pause. "I've been trying to work out what you'd do if two of us went at once."

Mei opened her mouth.

"You'd get one," said Sela, and stepped back from the edge, and sat down on the ledge with her arms around her knees, which was the fourth Tuesday in a row that this had happened.

---

Down on the tier, the crew was reaping.

They came up from the Roya settlements on rotation, forty of them, and they worked the barley with short scythes because the machines bruised the stalk, and they sang while they did it in a way that had nothing to do with morale. The songs were metronomes. Mei had established this in week one by asking, and had been given a long patient explanation about stroke rate and a demonstration of what happens to your wrist when you lose the count.

The verse came up through the sprinkler-mist at the same place in every pass.

*Come down the row where the barley leans,*
*somebody will meet you there.*
*The ground is soft and the ground is wide*
*and the ground was always there.*

Mei had been hearing it for a year. She sat down on the ledge next to Sela and let it come up, and the eight downstairs went quiet for it the way people do, and somewhere in the third row a woman with a voice like a hinge took the descant.

"Do you know this one?" Mei said.

"Everyone knows that one."

"What's it about?"

"Cutting barley," said Sela.

---

### ii.

VOLITION — *She sat down. Four Tuesdays. Sit down with her.*

SHIVERS — *Eleven meters. Sprinkler-mist at the eleventh bell, which is a curtain, which is why the crew sings loudest through it. Nobody down there can see the ledge for four minutes. That is a designed privacy. Somebody built it and then let everyone forget they had.*

INLAND EMPIRE — *She said you'd get one.*

THE FOOL, from the cheap seats — *She's right.*

---

### iii.

Iuno was in the kitchen with the pot and the good cup and a stack of Guild correspondence she had been reading with the expression of a woman finding errors and declining to fix them for free.

"How does a person learn to fly," Mei said.

"You're teaching it."

"I'm asking how *I* learned it."

Iuno turned a page over. "Cliff. Sea. You were furious with me by the second hour."

"And what did you do."

"I explained the frequency. You had the ear for it inside a morning; the hands took a week." The page went down on the pile. "You know all of this."

"Where did you stand?"

Iuno's real hand stopped on the paper.

"Below," she said. "Or above."

"Which."

"Neither is the answer you want." She looked up. The crystalline stump caught the ceiling light and threw a small stripe of it across the table. "I went first, Mei. Every time. Off the same edge, four seconds ahead of you, so that you were following someone rather than obeying someone." A breath. "It was the only honest thing in the entire arrangement and I did it for tactical reasons and I have thought about that a great deal since."

Mei sat down across from her.

"You never told me that."

"You'd have asked why I was in a hurry to be at the bottom." Iuno picked her cup back up. "Ask your girl what she's afraid of. She'll tell you. That generation says everything out loud; it's remarkable and I find it exhausting."

"She already told me."

"And?"

"She said I'd only get one of them."

Iuno drank her tea. It was hot. It had been hot every day for eight months, and neither of them had made a single remark about it, and this was, in the private accounting of the household, a form of daily observance.

"She's not asking to be caught," Iuno said.

"I know what she's asking."

"Then go and be four seconds ahead of her."

---

### iv.

Amy was on the dormitory roof with the handheld rig and a spool of copper wire and an expression of deep grievance directed at the ionosphere, which Lahai-Roi lacked.

"Ma. The ceiling eats everything above two hundred megahertz."

"Sounds rude."

"It's *structural.*" She wound wire. "Mornye says I can have the west relay for an hour on Thursdays and she said it in the voice that means she already filed the paperwork four days ago and wants me to be surprised."

Mei sat down on the parapet.

"There's a reaping song," she said. "The crews do it on the fourth tier."

"Barley one?"

"Barley one."

"That's the good one." Amy bit through the wire, which she had been told about by three separate adults. "Ma used to do it wrong."

Mei went still.

"Do it wrong how?"

"The second line." Amy set the spool down. She sang it — thin, accurate, in the Royan she had grown up inside. "*Somebody will meet you there.*"

"That's what they sing."

"That's what they sing. Ma sang *somebody will catch you there.*" She grinned. "It's one consonant. City people get it wrong constantly, it's a whole thing, the crews think it's the funniest joke ever constructed. Ma got it wrong for years and I was like nine when I found out and I was *insufferable* about it."

Mei looked out over the terraces.

"What's the difference," she said.

Amy considered this with total seriousness, because she had inherited a great deal.

"Catching's a thing one person does to another person," she said. "Meeting takes two."

"That's the joke?"

"That's not the joke, the joke is that the wrong word scans better." Amy went back to her wire. "The crews think that's hilarious. That the wrong one's *prettier.* They sing it wrong on purpose sometimes when there's a city kid on the tier, to see how long it takes."

"How long does it usually take."

"Depends how sure of themselves they are."

---

### v.

The sprinklers came on at the eleventh bell and the tier went silver and the crew took the verse up through the curtain of it, and eight first-years stood at the bottom looking up into a wall of mist.

On the ledge, Sela had her boots parallel again.

"You're going to tell me the numbers don't matter," she said.

"The numbers are fine."

"That's worse."

"Yeah." Mei came and stood at the edge, on Sela's left, close enough that their sleeves were touching. "Here's the arrangement, and it's a different one. Nobody's at the bottom."

Sela turned her head.

"I'm going off this ledge in about six seconds," Mei said. "Down there is barley and eight people who will get out of the way. If you come, you come. If you sit, you sit, and I'll be back up in four minutes and we'll do Tuesday again."

"You're not going to catch me."

"I've never caught anybody in my life."

Sela's mouth did something. "You catch people constantly. It's your entire — there are *pamphlets.*"

"I go over with them," Mei said. "Every single time. Ask anyone who was there." She looked out at the mist. "I've been telling myself the other word for about a year and I got corrected this morning by a nineteen-year-old with a soldering iron, so I'm passing it on while it's fresh."

She stepped off.

The frequency came up under her the way it had come up on a cliff above a sea in another country, with a woman four seconds ahead of her whose reasons had been terrible and whose method had been correct, and she went down through the silver curtain with her arms out and the barley coming up green and the crew's stroke-count arriving all around her, and at some point in the fall she heard the second set of boots leave the ledge.

She kept her eyes forward the whole way down. That was the whole of it, and it took eleven meters to understand.

Sela's shape was bad. Her landing was worse. She came out of the barley on her back nine meters from where she should have been, with the wind knocked out of her and her arms still spread, and Mei got to her at a walk, which cost something, and sat down in the standing grain beside her while the eight others converged shouting.

"You went first," Sela wheezed.

"Yeah."

"That's cheating."

"It's the only part of the job that works."

The sprinklers ran their four minutes out and shut off. The crew was on the sixth row by then and the verse came round again, and Mei, lying in the wet barley with a first-year swearing beside her and the false sun coming through, sang it under her breath with the right word in it.

It scanned worse. She sang it anyway.

## II. A Priestess Must Never
### *Iuno*

---

### i.

There is a date in her.

It has been there since the Nihility, arriving the way they always arrived, unbidden and complete, in the fourteen seconds after the void gave her back her own voice: a season, a light, an hour of the afternoon, and the exact quality of the air. She has had thirty years of practice with such objects and she knows better than to argue with the ones that come whole.

She has been carrying it for four months and eleven days. She has attempted to say it four times.

---

### ii. *The first draft*

In this one she says it over tea, at the kitchen table, in the morning, plainly, which is the version her old office would have recommended and the version she rehearses most.

The pot is between them. Amy has gone down to the labs. Lupa is splitting wood on the roof for a stove nobody in this city owns. The kettle has been amended.

*Mei. There's a date.*

And Mei, who has taken a god's horn through the abdomen without altering her expression, sets her cup down and says: *Okay.*

That is the whole of the first draft and the reason Iuno cannot use it. *Okay.* The woman would take it the way she takes everything — into the chest, filed under things to be carried, no complaint entered, no invoice raised. She would be kind about it before lunch. She would arrange her entire remaining life around it inside a week, quietly, with the efficiency of a person who has been rearranging her life around other people's arithmetic since she came out of the water.

The first draft ends with Mei being gentle about it, and Iuno being permitted to watch, and that is not a thing she has the stomach to purchase.

---

### iii. *The second draft*

In this one she says it in the dark.

This one has the advantage of being nearly true, in that she has attempted it. Three nights ago, in the narrow bed, with the vents cycling and the false stars in their wrong arrangements above four floors of dormitory, she turned her head on the pillow and found Mei already asleep with one hand shut around a fistful of Iuno's sleeve, which is a habit that started at the precipice and has never been discussed.

And Iuno said it. Out loud. The season, the light, the hour of the afternoon.

It took eleven seconds.

Mei slept through all eleven, and the room absorbed it, and at the grey hour Iuno got up and made tea and stood at the window with the cup going cold in her hand and understood the shape of what she had done. She had said the sentence into a place where it could do no work. She had discharged an obligation to an audience of nobody and gotten the relief of confession without any of its consequences.

Thirty years ago she left a woman a cup of Earl Grey on a balcony and went away before it could be drunk, and called that an apology.

The second draft is the same instrument in a different key. She has retired it.

---

### iv. *The third draft*

In this one she uses the office.

It is the strongest draft. In it she stands, and her spine does the thing it does, and the High Priestess of Septimont delivers a prophecy in the register the position requires — level, unhurried, admitting no argument, the voice that emptied a fifty-thousand-seat arena in a single verdict and put a House off its own gate with a crane and two chains.

*The Temple has spoken.*

She likes this draft. That is what disqualifies it. She likes it because in it she is standing up, and because in it the information arrives with authority attached, which means the recipient's job is to receive, which means Mei has been handed a fact where a conversation should have been.

She has read what that costs. There is a corkboard in her memory with a plot converging on a single point, and a character sheet with a name filed under a word she wrote in her own hand, and a woman in a Writer's Room finishing the safe ending on an impossible typewriter.

Rose Marigold, who read twenty years of true prophecies in the margins of false ones and waited the whole time for the author to stop herself, would recognize the third draft on sight.

---

### v. *The fourth draft*

In this one she writes it down.

She gets further with this one than with any of the others. She gets as far as paper.

The house keeps a ledger. It sits on the shelf in the front room under a lamp, and the second ink has an hour before the kettle, and there is a page in it where a copyist has been forecasting a stranger's spring with the confidence of a woman who has learned weather from an author. Iuno has been writing in the margins of that book for a season. It is the only place she has ever been able to tell the truth on the first attempt, which is a fact about margins and a fact about her.

She sat down with it at the grey hour on a Thursday and put the nib to the margin and got two words in.

*The date*

And stopped, because she had seen this page before. She has stood in a room made of it. A page left where the beloved will find it, so that the beloved may be informed without the author having to be present for the informing. An author, leaving an ending propped where somebody will walk into it.

She blotted the two words out. It took the whole of a bad hour and the mark is still visible if the lamp is at the wrong angle, and Amy asked about it, and Iuno said the pen had misbehaved, and Amy said *sure* in a tone she has learned from her mother.

The fourth draft is the Shadow with better manners. Iuno had thirty years of the Shadow and gave it a birthday and took it home, and she is not opening the door to it over a piece of correspondence.

---

### vi.

Here is the sentence underneath all four drafts, and she has known it since the second one.

Her mother died when she was nine. Temple robes two sizes too large, an eclipse birth, a line of women stretching back before the Republic and before the coastline had opinions about itself. What survives of her mother is one sentence, delivered in a corridor, in a tone Iuno has spent forty years failing to reproduce accurately.

*A Priestess must never fall in love.*

She built a life on it. She built a plan on it, and an office, and thirty years of calculated distance, and an arithmetic that could put a hundred and seventy-three names in a column and a hundred and seventy-fourth in a different one. She built the whole cathedral on that single load-bearing beam and never once, in forty years, went down to the cellar to look at it.

She looked at it this season. It took an afternoon.

Her mother was dying. Iuno was nine and standing in a corridor with a lamp. Her mother said *A Priestess must never* — and then stopped, because a woman with that particular failure of the lungs stops in the middle of things, and the stopping went on long enough that a nine-year-old with an eclipse in her chart and a talent for finishing other people's sentences finished it.

She has no memory of her mother saying the last four words.

She has an extremely clear memory of the corridor, the lamp, the smell of the wick, the exact weight of the robe on her shoulders, and the sentence.

*Must never what.* She has run it forty ways since Thursday. *Must never lie to the Ephor. Must never take the cup before the petitioner. Must never let the tea go cold. Must never be the last to know. Must never look away. Must never* — the woman was a Priestess of the Temple with three children and one surviving daughter and a chest full of water, and there are a hundred sentences she could have been building and Iuno picked the one that would eventually let her put a girl she loved into a column and call it a profession.

Thirty years of prophecy. One unfinished line. The seer supplied the ending herself and then obeyed it like scripture.

She has told nobody this. She intends to tell one person, eventually, and it is going to be a very poor evening.

---

### vii. *What happened instead*

Mei came in from the fourth tier on Tuesday with barley in her hair and her jacket soaked through the shoulders and a first-year's name she wanted to say out loud twice, the way she does when a thing has gone well.

She got as far as the doorway and stopped.

Iuno was at the table with the pot and two cups and a face she had rehearsed at least twice.

"You've got something," Mei said.

"Sit down."

"I'll drip."

"Sit down anyway."

Mei sat down and dripped. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Down the corridor Cartethyia was arguing with a vending machine and losing on points.

"There's a date," Iuno said.

Mei's hands went flat on the table. It happened fast and quietly and Iuno, who has watched this woman receive a god's verdict without blinking, catalogued it and filed it and will be taking it out again later when she is alone.

"You've seen it."

"Yes."

"How long have you had it."

"Four months and eleven days."

Mei looked at her hands.

"Is it today?"

Iuno had prepared for a great many things. Four drafts, a cellar, forty years of a sentence. She had not prepared for the question to arrive from the far side of the table wearing the shape of the answer.

"No."

"Is it this year?"

"No."

Mei let out a breath and did something to her shoulders and picked up the second cup, which was hot, and drank about a third of it.

"Then that's all I need for today," she said.

"Mei."

"I'm serious. I'm not being brave, I'm being *lazy*." She set the cup down. "You've had it four months. You've been doing the thing where you look at the door before you look at me. I've had four months of that and it's been the worst four months since we got back, so if the answer to *is it today* is no, then I'd like the rest of today, please, and you can ask me again tomorrow."

"That's an arrangement."

"It's an appointment." Mei pushed the cup back toward her. "You know how to keep those. You're famously good at them. You're just bad at the part where somebody else sets the hour."

Iuno looked at the cup.

"Every morning," she said.

"Every morning."

"You'll get tired of it."

"Probably." Mei stood, and put a hand on the back of Iuno's neck in passing, the way punctuation finds a sentence, and went to find a dry shirt. From the doorway: "Iuno."

"Mm."

"Was your mother nice?"

Iuno sat very still with a hot cup in her one hand and forty years of an unfinished line standing up in her chest like a person getting out of a chair.

"I have no idea," she said. "I was nine. I'll tell you what I've got."

"Tonight?"

"Tonight."

## III. Blanks Disallowed
### *Lupa*

---

### i.

The nine-week post came in on a Thursday and Lupa was at the passage office before the clerk had the sacks open, which was a thing the passage office had come to expect and had made a small accommodation for, in the form of a stool.

"Guild," she said.

"Guild's the grey sack."

"There's two grey sacks."

"There's two because *somebody* filed a volume complaint in the spring," said the clerk, "and now Septimont sends everything in duplicate to prove a point."

The Guild bundle came out tied with the tape they use for board matter. Minutes of the quarterly, which she would return unmarked as she had returned every set of minutes for two years. A charter amendment concerning appeals, three pages, which she read standing up in the doorway and approved of. A concourse-board maintenance schedule.

And under the tape, held flat by the schedule, eleven envelopes in eleven hands, forwarded from the Olymdos post with a Guild slip on top in a small hard clerk's script:

*Circuit correspondence, fourth quarter. Eleven items. Ref: Sched. of Particulars, col. 3 — recipient of record. Please advise if delivery is to be discontinued.*

Lupa stood in the doorway of the passage office in a city inside a god and read the slip four times.

---

### ii.

The charter has three questions now. She wrote the third one herself, in a corridor in Ragunna at two in the morning, on the back of a customs form, with a bard asleep on a bench behind her.

*Would you go to the Hill.*
*Would you fight.*
*Who will you write to.*

The first two came in already answered, worn smooth from handling at a kitchen table. The third one arrives at a child who has never been asked to hold a silence that size, and the child turns around inside it looking for a name, and if she finds one it goes in the box, and if she does not, the circuit rider fills it in her own hand and countersigns it, because blanks are disallowed and a blank has to cost somebody.

Lupa knew about the eleven. She had known about them for two years, the way you know about weather in a country you have left. Vibia's flags went up to Septimont every year and came down marked *reviewed, no action* from an office that had never put a name on the note.

That was the arrangement. She had built the arrangement. She had built it specifically so that it would run without her.

The clerk's slip said *recipient of record.*

---

### iii.

She took the bundle to the terrace and put it on the crate and sat looking at it for the better part of an hour, and at some point in the hour Ciaccona arrived with two cups of something and sat down on the other crate without being asked, which was the bard's entire technique and had never once failed.

"Board matter?"

"Post."

"Eleven of it."

"Eleven of it."

Ciaccona drank. Below them the Ward changed shift. "Are they addressed to a person or an office?"

Lupa turned the top envelope over. The hand was young. The ink had been pressed hard enough to emboss the back.

*To L. of the Guild circuit, care of the Olymdos post.*

"Both," she said.

"Ah." Ciaccona set her cup down. "That's the worst possible outcome and you know it."

"Say it anyway."

"An office can send a form letter. A person can't." The bard picked a thread off her cuff. "You've got about a day before you start telling yourself these are administrative."

"I've had four hours."

"How's it going."

"I've drafted six," said Lupa. "In my head. They're all logistics." She flattened the envelope against her knee with her thumb. "Stipend rates. Where the medic is. Which touts to walk past fast. Nine words on the hometown column and a recommendation to eat at the common table."

"That's a good letter."

"It's a *provisioning report.*" Lupa's ears went back. "It's what I do for strangers on a road at six hours' acquaintance and it works and it's the only language I've got, and this kid is eight and she wrote to me because a woman with a folding table put my initial in a box because there wasn't anybody else in the whole world to put there."

The vents cycled.

"Write it for me," Lupa said.

"No."

"You write *epics.* You've got a running gag about my tail in *iambs*—"

"I write things people can mend." Ciaccona picked her cup back up. "You want me to hand an eight-year-old a beautifully finished object with my fingerprints all over it, and she'll read it forty times and never once be able to talk back to it, because it'll be too good." She shrugged. "Write it badly. It's the whole technique. I've published on it."

---

### iv.

She read them properly that night, in order, on the floor of the terrace with the drying lines above her and the manufactured stars going through their wrong arrangements.

Eleven children.

A boy of eleven in Vetta Alta who had made a list of every fighter he had ever seen lose and wanted to know whether that was a normal thing to keep.

A girl in Porto-Veno, thirteen, whose whole letter was one paragraph about a knee and one paragraph about her sister and then, at the bottom, in different ink and on a different day, *do you have to be angry to be good at it.*

A boy who wrote four lines about the weather and then apologised for having nothing to say and then wrote nine more lines about the weather.

Two who had drawn her. Both drawings had the tail wrong.

A girl of nine from Sette Strade who reported that she had lost her first three bouts and had decided to write about the third one only, because the third one was the good one.

And the eighth envelope, the one with the ink pressed through to the back.

*Dear L.*

*I am Puela. I am eight. I am from Caldara which is three days above the Sanguis Plateaus in the dry hills. There is a lake and the elders say it boils and a lady came and measured it and wrote down that it boils, locally attested, so it is on the record. My mother says you know where it is.*

*The lady with the folding table asked me the three questions and I said yes and yes and then the third one and I did the thing where you look around inside for a name and there wasn't one. She wrote you in. She said you would probably not write back and that this was allowed.*

*I am not going to ask you about fighting. Everyone here asks me about fighting.*

*What I want to know is:*

*1. Is the sun in your city really a machine.*
*2. Does it get cold there or do they just decide.*
*3. Is there a lake.*

*That is all. You can answer just one.*

*From Puela of Caldara.*

*P.S. The wolf takes two. That is the rule here. I take two and I still win, that is why it is the rule.*

---

### v.

Chisa found her at the fourth hour, still on the floor, with all eleven laid out in a grid and her hands over her face.

"You've been up here since the seventh bell."

"Mm."

"There's a pot on downstairs."

"Mm."

Chisa sat down on the crate. She left her hands empty on her knees, which she did when she was going to stay a while.

"You've read them," she said.

"Four times."

"And the difficulty is."

Lupa took her hands down.

"There's no gap in them," she said. "That's the difficulty. There's nowhere to be *useful.* Not one of these kids has asked me for a thing I know how to give. There's a girl asking whether you have to be angry. There's a boy apologising for the weather. There's an eight-year-old who wants to know if there's a lake." Her tail lay flat on the concrete. "I've got a ring of keys in my head that fits every door in this city and not one of these letters is a locked door. They just want somebody to be there when they open it."

"So be there."

"With *what.*"

"With whatever you'd say if she was sitting on that crate," said Chisa.

"That's not how it works. The whole point of me is the roar. You say a person's name in a room and the room has to hold it, that's the whole apparatus, that's the only mechanism there *is*—" She stopped, because the sentence had turned over in the air and shown her the other side of itself.

Chisa waited.

"They said mine," Lupa said. "Eleven of them. In writing. To my face."

"They did."

"I've been saying other people's for two years and I've never once been on this end of it and it's *horrible.*"

"Yes," said Chisa. "Welcome."

---

### vi.

She wrote it badly. It took four nights and she burned two.

*Puela of Caldara.*

*I am going to answer all three because I am on a board and boards are indulgent.*

*1. The sun here is called Helios and it is a machine and it is two kilometers across and it hangs in the ceiling and it comes up in the morning because engineers agreed that it should. It is warm on your face like a real one. I have run under it for a year and I still look up. The people who live here do not look up. This is the same everywhere.*

*2. They decide. There is a committee. It gets cold at the twentieth hour because a subcommittee voted for it. I find this funnier than the people here do.*

*3. There is a reservoir. It is not a lake. A knight I live with went into it fully dressed at the seventh bell to settle a dispute about a fountain in another country, and we got her out, and she has done it twice since. So I would call it a lake, for our purposes, and I have written to the housing office about the railing in a tone I would describe as unhelpful.*

*Your lake is real and it is on the record. I have read the report. Shallow, warm, saline, in slow retreat, boils, locally attested. That last part is the whole of Caldara in four words and whoever wrote it deserves a drink.*

*Now the part I would rather not write.*

*The lady with the folding table told you I would probably not write back. That is a true thing about most people in that box and she was right to say it. Here is what I would like you to know about the box.*

*I was seven and I was asked the first two questions at a table with a curtain behind it and I said yes both times and I said it twice a year for four years and every time I said it my parents' faces did a thing I did not understand for twenty years. There was no third question then. I sat in a hall with forty pillars afterward and nobody in that building knew where I came from and there was no place on any paper to write it down.*

*So I put the third question in. It cost a year and I would do it again. The point of it is the silence. Somebody has to sit in a room with you while you look for a name, and you have to find out how long that takes, and so does she.*

*You found out. That is the whole exercise. I am sorry it is a rotten one.*

*You have me until you have someone better, and you are allowed to get someone better and stop writing, and I will not ask why. Write when you want. Do not write when you do not want. If you write about the weather for nine lines I will read all nine.*

*Here is a name I want you to have, since you did not ask for it and it is the only thing I have that is worth the postage.*

*Contender Mya. Of Ragunna. Junior circuit through Agon XXXI. She was the weakest Resonator in her bracket and every commentator said so every second sentence and she met every exchange head on and she never once ran. She dropped her shoulder before the lunge, every time, and I have watched the tape enough to fight both sides of it. She won a championship. She was taken out of the record by a word and the record ran out because nobody was paid to keep writing it.*

*Somebody is paid now. There is a file on the third terrace and it is sixty-three pages and growing.*

*Say her name at the table sometime. That is the mechanism. There is not another one; I checked.*

*From Lupa of Caldara.*

*P.S. The wolf takes two because the wolf is a story and stories carry handicaps. In practice you should take one, and take the one on the left, because the one on the left is the one who is watching the other one.*

*P.P.S. Column nine is on the concourse board at Olymdos and Caldara is six letters of light on it and I put it there. Go and stand under it when you get to the Hill. Everybody does. Everybody pretends they are waiting for someone.*

---

She sealed it and put it in the outgoing sack, and then took it out again and sat with it another hour, and then put it back.

Nine weeks each way. It would reach a village three days above the Sanguis Plateaus somewhere around the turn of the year, in the cold, when the lake would be flat and glassy and steaming at dawn and telling everybody it boiled.

She built the fire too big that night. Nobody asked why. Everyone sat close.

## IV. Everyone Knows That One
### *Ciaccona*

---

### i.

The Phosphor District at the twentieth hour is the only honest architecture in Lahai-Roi, because it admits that it is coral and gets on with it. The blue-violet comes up through the walkways from beneath, so everybody standing in it is lit from the wrong direction, and faces go strange, and the whole quarter looks like a room where people have decided to tell the truth for an hour.

Ciaccona went down there on Thursdays because the acoustics under the arcade were criminal and there was a stall that sold a wheat-adjacent liquor and a busker's pitch that turned over four times a night.

The kid on the pitch was maybe seventeen. Bad haircut. A four-course box lute that had been strung by somebody in a hurry, and a technique that suggested three months of enthusiasm and one lesson.

She was playing the Ballad of Ciaccona.

---

### ii.

It had left Lahai-Roi twice by then and come back both times heavier, which is how you can tell.

Cartethyia had started it in a dormitory room with a thermos and a tuning fork, humming something structurally unsound, rhyming *lute* with *truth* on a vowel doing all the work and doing it badly. She had, as threatened, taken it to taverns. She had sung it wrong on purpose, having been given to understand that this was the correct technique. She had lost control of it inside a month, which had been the entire objective, and had reported the loss at breakfast with the smugness of a woman who has successfully released a wasp indoors.

The kid's version had four verses. Ciaccona had heard two of them before.

The third was new.

*And the guards came up the garden path*
*and her father in the door,*
*he gave her the key and he gave her the word*
*and the sea took him for sure —*

*but a lamplighter watched from the headland tower*
*and he wrote what he could see:*
*'one aboard, and the wind from the west,*
*and singing.' That's all he'd be.*

Ciaccona stood at the edge of the light with her hood up and a cup in her hand and did the professional's arithmetic, which is: who told her that.

It had come the long way. It had been told, at some remove, to somebody who told somebody, across two oceans and nine weeks of post and a season of taverns, and it had arrived here in a seventeen-year-old's mouth in a coral district under a machine sun, with the lamplighter in it and the wind in it and the exact phrasing of a sailcloth ledger that four people alive had ever read.

The kid's meter was atrocious. She had put a rest where the grief goes.

Then she went into the fourth verse, and Ciaccona stopped drinking.

---

### iii.

*So the bard went down the coastal road*
*and she found him on the shore,*
*with the salt gone out of his beard at last*
*and the singing done and o'er.*

*And she carried him home and she set him down*
*where the wind runs off the lea,*
*and she plays his song on a summer night*
*and the old man sings the harmony.*

The pitch had about twenty people on it. They liked the fourth verse. Somebody at the back sang the last line along, in the flat two-thirds of a beat behind that means a person learned it in a room.

The kid finished, and grinned, and said *thanks, that's the good one,* and started tuning.

Ciaccona stood in the coral light for a long moment with a cup she had stopped holding properly.

Then she went and put money in the box and asked, casually, in the voice she uses for buying fish, where the song was from.

The kid shrugged the shrug of the entire oral tradition.

"Everyone knows that one."

"Do they."

"I got it off a woman on the freight run. She got it off a sailor." The kid worked a peg. "It's Rinascitan. It's about a woman who tells the truth and everyone thinks she's a liar, and then her dad gets exiled and she goes and finds him."

"And that's how it ends."

"That's how it ends."

"What's her name?" said Ciaccona. "The bard's."

The kid thought about it.

"Doesn't have one," she said. "That's the thing about it. She's just *the bard.*"

---

### iv.

She walked home the long way and stood on the canal bridge for twenty minutes.

The doctrine had never once failed her. Leave the hole in the verse; hand the pen over with the tune; a finished song has an owner and an owner can be arrested and exiled and burned on a boat, and a song with a hole in it belongs to whoever mends it, which is everyone, which is the only congregation that cannot be excommunicated. She had built a life on the mechanism. She had watched a caulker's wife bless innocent wood in it and a drunk oracle give her father the last of his water and a woman in Lastra reach into a hole in a stranger's song and pull out her grandmother's joke whole and living from the far side of a man's death.

In fifteen years of menders, this was the first one to put her father on a shore.

That was the thing. That was the whole thing, standing on a bridge in a foreign country under a manufactured night. Somebody had reached into the hole and taken out a rescue.

She could correct it. Two sentences would do it. She could go back to that pitch on any Thursday and say, in the voice that empties a tavern, *the man died on the water and there was no shore and the ledger is real and I have read it,* and the kid would put it right, because the kid was seventeen and playing a song she loved and would have gone white and grateful and fixed it inside a week, and then it would be fixed everywhere the kid played, and the wrong version would start to die.

The bard on the bridge stood there and looked at the water and worked out, slowly, what she would be doing if she did that.

She would be a woman with a document, confiscating a song.

---

### v.

She said it out loud at the table, four nights later, because the house had a rule about that.

"There's a version going round with a happy ending in it."

Cartethyia's fork stopped. "Of the—"

"Of mine. The one you started, you menace." Ciaccona turned her cup a quarter-turn. "Somebody's given my father a shore and a rescue and a summer night and the old man sings the harmony."

The table did the thing this table does, which is to get very quiet in a way that carries no judgment in it at all.

"That's a lie," said Amy.

"It is."

"Are you going to fix it?"

"I've been asking myself that for four days and I've now asked seven people and every single one of you has said something useful and different, which is the most annoying possible outcome."

"What did Iuno say?" said Mei.

"Iuno said, *a rescue is what people put in when the true version costs them more than they can carry, and you may take that as diagnosis or as permission.* Then she drank her tea and left the room. I have never wanted to throw a pot at anyone so badly in my life."

"Lupa?"

"Lupa said if she ever hears a version where her parents came back she's burning the tavern down." Ciaccona's mouth twitched. "Then she thought about it for eleven seconds and said, *no, leave it, somebody in that room needs it,* and went upstairs and split wood for an hour."

"And Chisa," said Cartethyia.

"Chisa said, *you've been telling people for twenty years that a song belongs to whoever mends it. This is what that costs. You've just never been the one paying.*"

"Ouch."

"She had the courtesy to say it while handing me a cup."

---

### vi.

Cartethyia followed her out into the corridor afterward, which was expected, and leaned on the wall next to her, which was also expected, and said nothing for about a minute, which was the part nobody ever expects from her and which is why the knight is dangerous.

"Ask it," Ciaccona said.

"Would he have minded?"

Ciaccona looked at the ceiling.

"He'd have loved it," she said. "That's the answer and I have been avoiding it for four days. He was a wedding musician. He played what the room wanted and made it better than the room deserved and he went to his death giving a performance to an audience of gulls. If he heard a version where somebody came for him he would have found the best key for it and put a floor under it and taught it to three people by the end of the night."

"So."

"So the version with the rescue is more him than the true one." She pushed off the wall. "And the true one is the only thing I own of him, and it's one line long, and the second I let go of it there is no manuscript, there is no author, and my father becomes a thing that happens in other people's kitchens forever."

"That's what you wanted."

"I wanted it for everyone else, Cartethyia."

"Mm," said the knight, who had spent twenty years naming dolls so the dark could not take her name, and who understood the architecture of a lifeboat better than anyone in the building. "Do you want my three things?"

"There will be *fingers,* won't there."

"There will be fingers." She held up one. "First. You said last year that the sea keeps everything and gives nothing back, and that's a grave. And you said the Order kept what it needed and burned the rest, and that's property. And you said the third thing — the sung one — is partial and biased and warm and *wrong,* because somebody with a heartbeat chose."

"I said *alive.*"

"You said alive and you meant wrong and you knew it when you said it." Second finger. "Second thing. You keep saying *the true version.* There isn't one. There's a lamplighter's line and a wind direction, and every word after that is somebody's guess, including yours. You wrote him a hole. Somebody filled it. You've been calling their filling a lie and yours a memorial and there is no instrument on this continent that can tell them apart."

"That's two."

"Third." Cartethyia straightened up. "There's a seventeen-year-old on a pitch in the Phosphor District who thinks the bard in that song has no name. And she's right, and you built that, and it's working exactly as designed, and you're standing in a corridor at midnight wanting to walk down there and tell her who you are."

Ciaccona was quiet a moment.

"Yes," she said.

"Yeah." Cartethyia bumped her shoulder into hers. "That's the bill."

---

### vii.

She went back on the fourth Thursday.

The kid was on the pitch. Ciaccona put money in the box and then stood in the front row with her hood down, and when the song came around she sang along wrong.

Loudly. With her whole chest. A quarter-tone under, in three places, and an ornament in the fourth verse that belonged to a different mode entirely, and a wandering interval on the word *shore* that would have been outlawed in every tuning on every coast.

The kid stopped playing.

"That's — sorry — that's flat."

"Is it?"

"You're a *quarter-tone* under."

"I learned it off a woman on a boat."

"Well, she was *flat,*" said the kid, with the pure moral outrage of the seventeen-year-old musician, and then, unable to stop herself, "here, look — it goes—" and played the line properly, twice, slowly, and Ciaccona did it wrong again on purpose, and the kid said *no, listen,* and did it a third time, and by then two people in the crowd were arguing about whether the fourth verse was even part of the original.

It took twenty minutes. Somebody's aunt claimed the whole thing was Royan. A dock hand said the lamplighter verse was newer than the rest and got shouted down. The kid, who had never in her life been the authority on anything, defended her text like a canon lawyer and won.

Ciaccona bought her a drink at the end of it, and while the kid was catching her breath, said, in the voice she uses for buying fish:

"Your third verse. It stops."

"How d'you mean?"

"*One aboard, and the wind from the west, and singing.* Then you go to the shore. There's a gap." She turned her cup. "Somewhere in there he's on the water for four days with nobody. That's a whole night of song and nobody's put anything in it."

The kid frowned. "What'd he sing?"

"That's the gap."

"Huh." The kid looked at her lute. "Somebody must know."

"Somebody probably does," said Ciaccona, and paid, and left.

---

### viii.

Down the corridor at home a door was open and a light was on and a nineteen-year-old with a house key on a cord was awake at an hour when nobody in that building had any business being awake.

Ciaccona stopped in the doorway.

"Three notes," she said.

Amy looked up.

"They're finished," Ciaccona said. "You finished them. I've had the ending in this building for four months and I have not once been able to make myself play it."

"I know."

"Do you."

"Cia." Amy pulled her knees up. "I wrote it at fourteen for a woman who was gone, and then I sat in a sun for ten years with the ending done and no one to give it to, and the whole time I was telling myself that as long as I didn't play it, nothing was over." She shrugged. "You held the front half and I held the back half and we were doing the exact same thing in two different rooms. It's honestly a little embarrassing."

Ciaccona sat down on the end of the bed, which she had never done, and unslung the lute.

"Right," she said. "I'm going to play the last four bars, and I'm going to do it badly."

"How badly?"

"Structurally."

"Okay," said Amy, and grinned, and it was her mother's grin down to the last calibrated millimeter, and it worked on Ciaccona exactly the way it was designed to. "Go."

Ciaccona played it. She dealt it off the bottom of the deck — dragged the second bar, fumbled the resolution, put a rest where the joy goes and landed the last note a whole step under, and it was, by any standard she recognised, an atrocity.

"That's flat," said Amy.

"Is it."

"That's — okay, no, that's *deliberately* flat, I'm not a child—"

"Fix it, then."

And a girl who had been a hundredth of a degree of warmth in a machine sun for ten years reached over, and put two fingers on a bard's hand, and moved it, and sang the ending of her own song out loud in a dormitory corridor at the fourth hour, with two Sylphs on the curtain rod and a door open down the hall and a light on.

Ciaccona built a floor under it. That was the trade.

Later, alone, she took out the notebook and found the running page and drew the star beside the entry, small, uneven, her father's star.

*Somebody put a shore in it,* she wrote. *I let them.*

And underneath, in smaller letters:

*He'd have taught it to three people by closing.*

## V. Arms That Long
### *Cartethyia*

---

### i.

He came up the Riseway on the second shuttle of the day and stood in the concourse for two hours reading the direction boards, and then walked to the Startorch gate and told the porter he had come to kill the Blessed Maiden of Rinascita.

The porter, who had worked that gate for nine years and had opinions about all of it, put down his tea and said, "Have you got an appointment?"

"I'll wait."

"You will," agreed the porter, and sent a runner.

Cartethyia came down in a training shirt with her hair wet and Ser Pincushion in her belt because she had been mending him and had forgotten he was there. She stopped about four meters out.

He was twenty-two, maybe twenty-three. Rinascitan the way the coast is Rinascitan, dark and salt-cured and thin in the wrong places from nine weeks of ship food. He had a fisherman's forearms and a sword with a shop's chalk still on the scabbard, and he was shaking, and he was standing his ground anyway, which she noted and filed and thought better of him for.

"I'm Cartethyia."

"I know what you are."

"That's a different question."

"Fleurdelys," he said, and got it out level, which had clearly cost him the whole two hours in the concourse. "The Blessed Maiden. The one they put in the glass. The one who caused it."

The concourse had gone quiet in an eight-meter radius, the way concourses do.

"Yes," said Cartethyia.

He had prepared for a denial. She watched him arrive at the edge of a speech and find the ground gone.

"There's a hall on the second level," she said. "It's got mats. I'll be there at the sixth bell tomorrow and I'll be there at the sixth bell the day after that. Where are you staying?"

He stared at her.

"You'll want the hostel on Ward Three," she said. "The one on the East Terrace has a stove with a bad regulator and a friend of mine has been writing letters about it. Have you eaten?"

---

### ii.

Lupa was waiting at the top of the stairs with her arms folded and her ears flat.

"No."

"Lupa."

"*No.* He's come across two oceans with a bought sword and a grief and he's going to open your throat in a training hall in front of nobody—"

"He's going to try."

"That's what I said." Lupa fell in beside her, which was the tell. "I'll talk to him."

"You'll count him."

"I'll *count him,* yes, that's the job—"

"And he'll be ours by supper and he'll never get to hate me." Cartethyia stopped on the landing. "He needs to hate me for a while, Lupa. He's carried it nine weeks on a ship. If you take it off him at the door he'll spend the rest of his life not knowing where he put it."

Lupa's tail did something complicated.

"Six bells," she said. "I'm in the gallery."

"You're not."

"I'm in the gallery being extremely subtle."

"Lupa."

"There is *no version of this* where I'm not in the gallery," said Lupa, "and I'd like that noted."

"It's noted," said Cartethyia.

---

### iii.

**Day one.** He came at her with everything he had and she took it on the flat of the crystal thorn and put him on the mat in eleven seconds, and he got up, and she put him down again, and he got up, and after the fourth time he stayed down and swore at her for a while in Ragunnan with real fluency.

She let him finish. Then she said, "Six bells tomorrow," and went and got two cups of water and left one on the mat beside him and left.

**Day two.** Nine seconds. He was swinging at her head, which is a beginner's habit and a grieving man's habit and had nothing to do with either. She corrected his guard once, wordlessly, with the flat, the way Cadanto used to turn her wrist two degrees with one enormous finger, and he understood what had happened about half a second later and it made him so angry he lost the next exchange in four.

**Day four.** The hall stayed empty. She sat in it for an hour with Ser Pincushion propped on the bench beside her and mended a seam.

**Day five.** He came, and they went straight to the mat, and the fourth stayed where it was.

**Day nine.** He got a hand on her. Nothing landed; he closed the distance and had a fistful of her shirt for about a second and a half before the mat came up to meet him. He lay there breathing.

"You're letting me get closer."

"I'm not."

"You *are*—"

"You're getting closer," said Cartethyia. "That's different. Six bells."

**Day fourteen.** Lupa was in the gallery. Lupa had been in the gallery every single day and had, by that point, abandoned subtlety as a project, and had additionally begun bringing a thermos.

**Day nineteen.** He landed one. It was a bad one and it was on her shoulder and it stung, and she stepped back and rolled the joint and said, "Left foot."

"What?"

"Your left foot was behind you. That's why it worked. Do it again."

He stood in the middle of the mat with his sword down and looked at her with an expression she recognised from a great distance, because she had worn it herself in a flooded nave with a thorn ground out of her own crown.

"Why are you *teaching* me?"

"Six bells," said Cartethyia.

---

### iv.

**Day twenty-six.** The sword stayed at his side.

He came into the hall at the sixth bell and stood in the doorway with it in his hand and stayed there, and Cartethyia sat down on the bench with her back against the wall and waited, because she had spent twenty years in a building that was going to say nothing back and had gotten extremely good at the intervening period.

It took him nine minutes.

"You could have finished this on the first day."

"Yes."

"You could have finished it in a *second*, I've watched you, you cut a — I saw the recordings from the Plains, you *split* — " He stopped. He started again. "Twenty-six days. Every morning. You've got a whole life up there and you come down here and let a fisherman swing at you."

"I do."

"Why."

And the knight of Egla, who had asked the only question that mattered at six years old sitting cross-legged at the edge of a circle in a cottage full of prop swords, said:

"Because arms that long could have flattened him on the first day. And it just kept turning."

He stared at her.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got, and I've had it since I was six, and I have never once been able to say it out loud without sounding insane." She put her hands on her knees. "There was a play where I'm from. A knight and a giant. Everyone in the world asks why the knight kept fighting. I asked why the giant let him live." A shrug. "Nobody else ever came. That's my answer. It's a bad one. Sit down."

He sat down on the mat, six feet away, with the sword across his knees.

And then, because twenty-six days is a long time and the body gets tired before the story does:

"Her name was Marta Orsalin," he said.

---

### v.

"She was my mother's sister and she raised me after and she was a herring-buyer on the Thessaleo run and she went down in the spring of the year the water stood up. Fifty-one. She had a laugh like a gate. She used to eat the first herring standing up at the stall like a gull and my mother said it was disgraceful and she did it *harder.*"

Cartethyia had gone very still.

"And I've had twenty years of a window with your face in it," he said, "forty feet of martyr, and a moral bolted on the bottom, *give your sorrows to heaven and hold your peace,* and I gave mine, I did the whole rite, I sat in the queue for six hours and I said her name into a grille and a man behind it told me the week's allowance was seven days and I'd had nine. And I held my peace for twenty years. And then last spring a broadsheet came off a Ragunnan boat and it said the Maiden was alive and it said what the Tide *was,* and I read it on a jetty and I couldn't—"

He stopped.

"I couldn't put her anywhere," he said. "That's the thing. That's all of it. For twenty years I had somewhere to put her and it was heaven and heaven was a lie and now she's just — loose. In me. And I don't know what to do with a person who's loose."

The hall hummed. Four floors above, a city breathed inside a kneeling god.

"Say her name again," Cartethyia said.

"Marta Orsalin."

"Whereabouts did she confess?"

He looked up sharply. "What?"

"Whereabouts. Which house." Cartethyia's voice had gone somewhere it rarely went. "Ragunna, the cathedral, the Maiden's hours. Between the fourth and the twelfth year. If she came to me I have her."

"She never—" He stopped. "She went once. She never said what for. She went once, in the eighth year, and she came back and she wouldn't say and she was *lighter,* and my mother was furious with her about the fare."

Cartethyia closed her eyes.

Ten thousand sealed sorrows had gone into a girl in a sealed room, one at a time, by design, filed in her chest by name because that was the only place there had ever been to put them. She had told them all the same sentence in the same warm voice. *I keep them somewhere safe.*

She had believed it. The believing had been the point; the believing was what made the harvest taste right.

And then a god had come apart into light and put her back together with everything restored, and nobody had thought to warn her that *everything* included the comb.

"Orsalin," she said. "Marta. Eighth year, late autumn, morning hours, the third stall. Smelled of brine and rope. Sat down like a dropped net." Her eyes were still closed. "She apologised before she said a word. They all did; there was a fine for it. She said she'd come about her sister's boy."

He made a sound.

"She said he was eleven and he'd stopped talking, and she'd tried everything, and she was frightened she was doing it wrong because she'd never had one of her own and she was going about it like a job." Cartethyia opened her eyes. "She wanted to know whether it was all right to love a child you were only holding for somebody else. That's what she spent the fare on."

The hall was very quiet.

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her the holding *is* the loving and there is no other kind and anyone who says different is selling something." Cartethyia's mouth moved. "I was twenty-four and I made it up on the spot and I have never once had cause to revise it."

He put his face in his hands.

She let him. She sat six feet away on a mat in a training hall with her hands on her knees and let a man cry for a woman with a laugh like a gate, and she stayed exactly where she was for the whole of it, because there had been a day in a corridor in Septimont when a woman had said *I need to not look at you right now, don't leave,* and Cartethyia had understood in that second what her entire profession actually was.

She stayed. That was the profession.

### vi.

He came at the sixth bell for another eleven days, and on most of them he swung, and on some of them he sat.

On the thirty-eighth day Lupa came down from the gallery with the thermos and put a cup on the mat beside him without breaking stride and went back up, and he looked at it for a long time and drank it.

On the forty-fourth day the doorway stayed empty, and Cartethyia sat the hour out anyway, and Ser Pincushion sat on the bench with his permanent expression of alarm, and the seam she was mending was in a shirt that was Amy's and had a tag on it that was staying.

On the forty-fifth day the porter brought up a note. It said:

*Berth on the ore run, four months. They wanted a hand who could cook.*

*I am not forgiving you. I want that written down somewhere by somebody official.*

*But I would like the eighth year, late autumn, morning hours, third stall, written down as well, and I would like it in your hand, because I have a sister and she was four when it happened and she has nothing.*

*— G. Orsalin*

Cartethyia wrote it out that night. It took four attempts. She kept all four and sent all four in the same envelope with a note on top that said *the first one is the truest and the fourth one is the tidiest, take whichever your sister needs.*

### vii.

"You should have let me count him," said Lupa, on the terrace, in the false dark.

"I know."

"He was ours by the ninth day and you both knew it and you let him keep swinging for five more weeks."

"Yes."

"*Why.*"

Cartethyia leaned on the rail and looked out at the reservoir, where the lights broke up in the water.

"Because a giant that catches you on the first day is just a wall," she said. "You can't fight a wall. You can only find out how much of you is left over after you stop."

Lupa's tail moved once on the concrete.

"That's a horrible way to run a life."

"It's the arms I've got," said Cartethyia. "They're very long and they go round and round and they're murder on the shoulders. Are you going to sit down or are you going to be tall at me."

Lupa sat down.

Above them the manufactured stars went through their wrong constellations, each one named by a subcommittee, each one incorrect, and somewhere out past two oceans there was a windmill outside a town in the wheat with a knight's sword painted on it in fading ochre by a traveller nobody could name, that meant, in the specific dialect of that country, that somebody had passed this way and refused to see a broken machine.

### i. Lahai-Roi, the twelfth of the month

The tin came out of the footlocker at the fourth hour with a shirt wrapped round it, and Chisa sat on the terrace crate with it on her knees for eleven minutes before she opened it.

The band lay on the shirt. Four years of a growing neck had polished the inner face to a soft grey shine except at the clasp, where the felt had gone dark and slightly furred, and twenty years in a sealed city had done nothing to it at all, because a sealed city does nothing to anything, which is the entire complaint.

She got a finger under it at the front and ran it round to the clasp.

Three lines, curling. Punched into the alloy where the felt met the catch, on the inside, against the skin, in the one place on the entire object that no inspector has ever looked at in the history of the Bureau.

She woke it.

It took nine seconds, the way she had said it would. The hum came up through the alloy and into the crate and into the concrete, two seconds of it, the note you feel in the back teeth, and the drying lines went on drying and the vents cycled and four floors down eight people slept in a pile of blankets and bad decisions.

Chisa sat on a roof inside a god with a two-second sound in her hands and waited for the rest of it to arrive.

Sumika had said something. Every morning for two months, in a city where every clock had stopped except this one, a girl who collected broken timepieces and wound them and set them and put them up and was delighted when they were wrong by evening had heard this noise at six and said something about it. The same joke, probably. Chisa has a hundred and fifty-one entries in a dead girl's handwriting and the shape of her sitting up at the counter with a physics magazine and the exact place in her head where the sound of her saying it used to be.

The hum stopped. The place in her head stayed the shape it was.

She put the band back in the tin and went downstairs and got a pen.

---

### ii. Kuresaki, the same week, roughly

Ren checked the annex's mail before opening, which took four seconds and had taken four seconds every morning for a year and eleven months.

Then he opened, and worked, and at the eleventh hour a woman of about forty came in with a boy of six and a release form and a face that had done its crying somewhere else. Six is under fifteen. The schedule applies. The fee is unchanged.

The boy's band was on its first ribbon. It came off in eleven seconds and the paperwork took forty minutes and the boy stayed silent the whole way through, which Ren logged, because the silent ones are their own category and he has thirty-one cards for them.

He wrote on the back: *asked nothing. put his hand up to check three times before they reached the door.*

Then he sat with his tea going cold, which was a thing he did now.

The letters lived in the same box as the cards, in a rubber band, and there were nine. Two more had come in the spring. One from a man of thirty-four who had worn his from twelve to fifteen in the last year of the old schedule and had written four pages about a summer. One from a woman who had said only: *I have felt the wave for nineteen years and I have never told anyone and I am telling you.*

He had answered them both in three words.

The eighth address was still in the drawer with the good tools, written on the back of the shipping docket for a band that had left his bench eighteen months ago with the pin turned around and the pattern field filled in with a name he had invented at that bench, in the box where the Bureau's own patterns go.

*Kuresaki, second.*

His niece Hana came by at closing with a bag of the fried things she pretended were for him and sat on the customer's stool and put her feet on the rung.

"You checked the post."

"I open at eight. The post comes at seven-forty."

"Uncle."

"Mm."

Hana ate a fried thing. She was twenty-one now, and had a job at the ferry office, and had spent thirty years getting to nineteen and had opinions about waiting.

"Nine weeks each way," she said. "You know that's eighteen weeks before you find out anything. If you send it in the spring you hear in the autumn."

"I'm aware of the arithmetic."

"You've been aware of the arithmetic for two years." She put the bag on the bench. "She's a person. People get post."

"You've said."

"I'll keep saying it," said Hana, and went home.

Ren sat in the annex with the light going off the harbor road and the shears and the punch and the felt on the bolt, and he took out a sheet and wrote, and got two sentences down, and read them, and did what he had done twice before.

Then he did the other thing.

---

### iii.

She wrote it four times before and burned three. The fourth had lived in the tin next to the band for a year and two months and she had reread it perhaps forty times and had come, slowly, to understand what was wrong with it.

It was a good letter. That was what was wrong with it. It explained her circumstances, and thanked him, and released him from any obligation to reply, which is the sentence people put in when they cannot bear to be waited for.

She burned the fourth one on the terrace with the kettle ring, which took some doing, and wrote a fifth on a page torn out of a Startorch notebook.

*To the smith at the Kuresaki bench.*

*I am the girl from the photograph. My name is Chisa. I have your band. I was fitted at sixteen at the Monitoring Center in a storm and I wore it for twenty years in a city that had nobody on the other end of any wire in the world.*

*I found the wave on the inner face in the third summer, arguing with it, the way you do. I understood what it was immediately and I have never once been able to explain to anyone why. I have tried. I get as far as "somebody stood at a bench with a punch and a hammer and put it there on purpose" and then the sentence runs out, because that is the entire content of it and it should not be enough to build a person on and it was.*

*I am writing to ask you a question that has no good reason attached to it. I have thought about the reasons and there are none, and I have decided to ask anyway, because the alternative is to keep not asking, and I have twenty years of experience with that and it does not improve.*

*The band makes a noise at six. Two seconds. You feel it in the back teeth. You will know the one.*

*There was a girl in that city. Her name was Sumika. She woke up to that noise every morning for two months, which was as long as we had, and every morning she said something about it. The same thing, I think. A joke. The city took the sound of her saying it out of my head a night at a time while I slept, in even amounts, and left me her handwriting and her arithmetic and the exact shape of the hole where the sentence used to be.*

*In eleven years at that bench, and thirteen, did anybody ever say anything to you about the noise? Not about the band. About the noise. Anything at all. A complaint. A joke. Something a child said while you were seating a clasp.*

*I am aware that this is a great deal to ask of a stranger nine weeks away about a sound made by a machine. I am asking anyway. If you have nothing, please write and tell me you have nothing, because I would rather have that in your hand than keep the question open.*

*One more thing, and then I will stop.*

*The regulation on reclamation is clear. I read it once, in the first month, when I still thought reading it would help. Ribbon, felt, and personal effects are not weighed. I have thought about that line for twenty years. There is a great deal of the world you can move if you are willing to move it in units the scale ignores.*

*I move things now. I have a pair of scissors and a job and a house full of people who count each other out loud. I learned all of it late and badly and I am not going to pretend I learned it from a punch mark on the inside of a collar.*

*I am going to say that it was the first one.*

*— Chisa. Of Honami, and of the Third Ward, Lahai-Roi.*

---

### iv.

Lupa found her at the outgoing counter with the envelope in her hand and stood there for about forty seconds, which for Lupa is an eternity of tact.

"Nine weeks," she said finally.

"Nine weeks."

"Eighteen, for an answer."

"Eighteen, if he answers." Chisa turned the envelope over. "He might not. He gets letters and he answers them in three words and he might have run out of three words, or he might have died, or he might have moved, or the annex might have closed, because the trade is ending on a published schedule and he's the one ending it."

"That's a lot of might."

"It's a smaller file than I've been carrying." She put the envelope through the slot. "I thought you'd want to know the size of it."

Lupa laughed. It got about half a breath out of her.

They walked back down through the Ward together in the machine morning, past the bakeries and the trams and the arguing, past the corner where a stand-keeper was setting out six tables and a counter and a manager was climbing onto the till end because the till end was his.

"Thursday," Lupa said. Not a question.

"Thursday."

"You'll hear it from me first," Lupa said, "or you won't."

"I know."

"That's all I wanted."

"I know."

### v. Kuresaki, the same week, roughly

He built the docket first, because the docket is the part he knows how to do.

*One regulator. Kuresaki pattern, second. Felt-lined. Sized to enclosed measurements, taken from published photographs and adjusted for eighteen months of a growing neck. Adjustable damping. Catch on the outer face, thumb-sized, ridged, findable in the dark.*

*Carriage paid. Consignee: the address on the reverse. No return required.*

Then the letter, and the letter took him eleven days, and it came out in the end at four sentences, because he had spent thirteen years learning that a card holds one line and a life holds about six of them.

*To the girl from the photograph.*

*I am the smith at the Kuresaki bench. I have made you a band of my own pattern, which is the Bureau's pattern with one pin turned around, so that the only pair of hands the mechanism defeats is nobody's. It is yours to wear, or to put in a tin, or to open in the dark for the sake of hearing the catch go.*

*I have a box of cards here going back thirteen years with what children said on it. A girl of nine asked me last autumn whether the band says goodbye when it comes off, and I told her it had had a long shift, and she informed me that this was insufficient, and she was correct.*

*I do not know what to send you that answers anything. So I am sending the box.*

*— Ojima Ren.*

---

He packed it in straw. A hundred and thirty-one cards in a lacquer box, in date order, held with a strip of the felt he cuts off the bolt himself and pays for himself.

And on top of the cards, in a fold of tissue, a strip of deep rust ribbon that had come off a band on a Saturday in October and gone into a jacket pocket and, four years later, come back to the bench in the hand of a girl of thirteen who had said she had grown out of the color and thought he should keep it, in case it was useful.

He walked the parcel to the harbor office himself and stood at the counter and filled in the fields.

*CONTENTS:* he wrote, and stopped, and looked at the box for a while.

*Personal effects,* he wrote. *No commercial value.*

The clerk weighed it and gave him the number and Ren paid it, and the parcel went into the eastern sack, and the eastern sack went onto the four o'clock, and the four o'clock went out past the harbor wall on the tide.

---

### vi.

They passed each other somewhere in the middle, in the way that things at sea do, without either vessel knowing.

Each of them was a first letter. Each had been written by a woman or a man who had reached the end of her own postponement, and each was addressed into a silence, and each arrived, eventually, at a bench and a terrace nine weeks apart, and was read four times, and was answered.

But that is the autumn's business.

For now there is a lacquer box going east to west across a dark ocean with a hundred and thirty-one questions in it in a small hard hand, and a torn notebook page going west to east with one question on it that has no good reason attached, and between them, on the water, about four thousand kilometers of open sea.

The scale ignores it. The scale has ignored a great deal.

## VII. Hold the Pencil
### *Amy*

---

### i.

"Checklist," said Amy.

"You did the checklist at the passage."

"That was the *departure* checklist. This is the arrival checklist. They're different documents." She had the harness strap in her teeth while she got the pack onto her shoulder. "Firewood."

"Under the tarp on the north wall."

"Kindling."

"In the boot box."

"Matches."

"Amy. There's one match. Laid by the fire. On the stone."

Amy stopped with the pack half on.

"Yeah," she said. "There is."

The shuttle door cycled and the frostlands came in, and the smell of them went through Mei like a hand going through a pocket that had something in it once.

---

### ii.

The Riseway lets you out four hundred meters from the hollow and the walk is the walk. White to the circumference of the world, the wind combing the drifts into long parallel sentences, and above all of it the high pearled ceiling where the stars used to be before the Etheric Sea came over everything like a lid.

Amy walked it with her hood strings pulled tight and her hands out of her pockets, because she had spent thirteen years without a wind on her and had developed an entirely unreasonable attitude toward weather.

Mei walked half a step behind and let her lead.

"Ma."

"Mm."

"You've been doing the thing."

"What thing."

"Where you walk behind me on the left."

"I always walk on the left."

"You always walk on the left of *Iuno,*" said Amy. "You walk on the *right* of Lupa and you walk *in front* of Cartethyia because she'll go into a ditch, and you've walked exactly one and a half steps behind and to the left of me since the Nihility, which is the distance where you can get an arm out."

Mei considered lying about this and decided against it on the grounds of expense.

"Yeah," she said.

"Okay." Amy kept walking. "Just so we've both got the data."

---

### iii.

The cabin sat in its hollow with snow to the sills. The blue door was a blue door.

Amy stopped in front of it with her hand on the key on its cord and stood there for about fifteen seconds, and then unlocked it, and went in, and left the door open behind her, which was the whole of it and cost her something.

Inside was cold in the patient way.

She laid the fire the way it had been laid for her — small sticks first — over the one already there, and used the match on the stone, and it caught, and the room began the slow business of remembering.

Paper planes on the shelf, gone soft gold at the folds. The record player. Seven seal posters, the largest with its hand-drawn speech bubble in handwriting round and young and trying so hard. A mug on the left side of the shelf, handle out, ready, which had been ready for nineteen years.

The console booted with a noise like something clearing its throat, and the save came up, and on the right side of the map a small avatar stood facing a door with the patience of a thing that has been standing there for twelve years.

"Password's nine oh seven two," said Amy.

"I know."

Amy turned around. "You *don't* know. You've never known. You've been told."

"Same result."

"Not remotely the same result," said Amy, and went to put the kettle on.

---

### iv.

They did the terrible hot chocolate. Three spoons of cocoa, the salt crime, marshmallows until structural. Mei drank a whole cup of it with an expression of a woman honoring a treaty.

"It's awful."

"It's *correct.*"

"Both things are true."

"Both things are true and I refuse to explain further," said Amy, which was, whether she knew it or not, a direct quotation from a letter written to a woman who never read it.

The fire got going properly. Outside, the light went down the way it goes down over the frostlands, which is all at once and then not at all for an hour.

Amy went and stood at the kitchen doorframe with her hands in her sleeves.

Mei watched her do it and put her cup down.

---

### v.

The marks went up the frame like a ladder.

*Amy, 6. Amy, 6 and a half (she insisted). Amy, 7.*

And then, above the last one in that hand, four more in a careful and slightly wrong copy of it, each with a crooked line: *8. 9. 10. 11. 12.*

And above those, all that blank wood.

"There's five of mine," Amy said. "Twelve's the last one. That was the year I put the ruler on my head and got the same number as the year before, and I went back to campus and made it as far as the frost gallery and did the whole disgraceful production in front of a window."

"You were done growing."

"I was done growing." Amy touched the twelve with two fingers. "And then I stopped coming up for a bit. And then I came up but I stopped doing the frame. And then I was a hundredth of a degree of warmth in a machine for ten years and the frame did what wood does, which is stay exactly where it is and wait."

Mei stood beside her and looked at the ladder and the blank above it.

"The first seven are mine," she said.

"They are."

"I don't remember making a single one of them."

"I know." Amy's voice stayed level. "That's fine. That's — Ma, that's completely fine, I've had a year to get used to it and I'm used to it, and the thing I want to say is that the wood doesn't care." She tapped the six-and-a-half. "That's the same mark whether you remember it or not. It's not a memory. It's a *dent.* Somebody stood here with a pencil and pressed."

"Amy."

"I'd like you to hold the pencil," said Amy.

---

### vi.

The pencil lived in the drawer. It was the same pencil. Of course it was the same pencil.

"You've stopped growing," Mei said carefully.

"I stopped growing at nineteen and then I stopped existing and now I'm made of a standing wave and about four hundred people's stubbornness, so the height situation is what you would call *settled.*" Amy put her back against the frame and squared her heels. "Yes. I know. Do it anyway."

"It'll be the same line."

"Yeah."

"Amy—"

"Ma." She looked at her. "For ten years I sat in a sun and ran a proof. Every day. It's a good proof, it's about four words long and it *holds:* where there's a song, there's a singer. I ran it every single day and every single day it came out true, and it kept me alive, and it kept me exactly as alone as I was the day before, because a proof you can run by yourself doesn't need anybody, that's the entire *point* of it—"

Her edges had gone soft. She noticed, and closed her hand on the doorframe, and they came back.

"—and the whole thing about measuring yourself," she said, "is that it comes out crooked. That's not a flaw in the ruler. That's the design. Somebody else is supposed to hold the pencil."

Mei took the pencil out of the drawer.

"Ruler," she said.

"Third drawer, under the placemats, where it's lived since before either of us was who we are."

---

### vii.

She put the ruler flat on her daughter's head and got her thumb on the frame and marked it.

The line came out straight. Nineteen years of crooked lines and then a straight one, at exactly the height of the twelve, four marks up from the last one in the old hand.

"Huh," said Amy, turning around.

"Problem?"

"It's *straight.* That's so annoying. That's — Ma, that's actually the most annoying thing that's happened to me since I came back."

"Should I do a bad one."

"Don't you *dare.*" Amy put her thumb on it. "Write it."

Mei put the pencil to the wood and stopped, because the hand knew what to do and started, and for about four seconds the two of them argued about it in the dark somewhere below the level where anybody gets a vote.

The hand won. It always did.

*Amy, 20,* she wrote, in a hand that matched the six and the seven and the six-and-a-half exactly, letter for letter, slant for slant, because it was the same hand and had never stopped being the same hand and had simply been on a long walk.

Amy looked at it for a while.

"Do the date," she said.

"There aren't dates on the others."

"There aren't dates on the others because the others were about how tall I was getting." Amy's voice had gone somewhere. "This one's about who came up here in the cold to do it. Put the date on."

Mei put the date on.

---

### viii.

They sat on the floor with their backs against the stove and the fire doing its work.

"Next year it'll be the same height," Mei said.

"Yep."

"And the year after."

"Yep. It's going to be the stupidest ladder in the world." Amy pulled her knees up. "It's going to go straight up the frame in a column of identical lines with dates on them, like a very boring receipt, and in about sixty years the wood's going to run out and we'll have to start on the other side of the door, and it'll look like somebody was tallying something."

"Somebody will be tallying something."

"Yeah." Amy put her head back against the stove. "Attendance."

Outside, the wind combed the drifts past the hollow and the lake and the wisterias that had declined to read the terms.

"I've got one more thing," Amy said, "and then I want to do the game."

"Go."

"The proof's still good." She said it to the ceiling. "I want that on record. It held for ten years and it'll hold again if I ever end up somewhere it's the only thing I've got, and I've thought about that a lot, and I've decided I'm keeping it, because it's *mine* and I built it at nineteen in the dark and it works."

"Okay."

"But it only ever proved I existed," Amy said. "It never once proved I was *here.*" She lifted her chin at the doorframe, at the crooked ladder and the straight new rung with a date under it. "That's what the mark's for. You can't run that one alone. It takes two, and one of them has to be standing behind you not looking at the number."

Mei put her arm out. Amy came in under it.

"Same time next year," Amy said.

"Same time tomorrow," said Mei, which was the family's actual liturgy, and which the girl had heard three times already that season and had begun, in the private way she does everything, to believe.

---

### ix.

They played the game.

Amy walked the little avatar back from the secret room, across the whole map, all the way to the mission select screen, and stopped the cursor on the final quest, and looked at it.

"If we finish it, the game ends," said Mei.

"Yeah."

"You made a rule about that."

"I made a rule about that when I was nine and shouting." Amy put her thumb on the button. "I've since had some thoughts about endings and I think the rule's due for review."

"Now?"

"No." She backed out of the menu. "Next time. It's a co-op quest, it says so on the box, and Iuno's never played and Lupa will be unbearable about the driving section and I want to see Cartethyia's face when she finds out you can name the horse."

She saved. The console went quiet.

On the way out Amy locked the blue door and then stood there with the key in her hand and turned around and looked at the cabin, whose windows were doing the thing lit windows do in snow.

"It's beginning to be cold," she said.

"Yeah."

"I had it on very good authority that certain people come back when it's cold," said Amy, and put the key on its cord under her collar, against the skin, where it lives.

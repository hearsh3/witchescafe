# Youngest Gate 11 — 梅雨

*The Plum Rains*

The first drop struck Deng Rui on the back of the hand while he was trying to thread a needle.

He sheltered the needle under his chin. The waterproof cover for the field receiver had split beside its buckle, and every time he pulled the flap shut the tear opened another finger's width. Sergeant Du had given him waxed thread, a needle, and an account of how much better these covers had been in the year he enlisted.

“Hold the thread up against the sky,” Du said. “You'll get more light through the eye that way.”

Rui obeyed. Above the northern ridge, a narrow strip of brightness lay under the cloud.

The drop on his hand rose.

It pulled into a bead, lifted from the small hairs, and travelled upward past the needle. Rui watched it join several others beneath the receiver mast. They hung at different heights. Through one of them he could see the north watch platform, including the roof that had burned down four years earlier.

Du walked past him carrying two bowls.

“Sergeant, the mess shelter's this way.”

“Captain takes his on the platform. He'll come down when somebody brings him something hot.”

Rui looked toward the platform again. Its three surviving posts stood above a slope of loose shale. Du stepped between the posts and put a foot onto the remembered stair.

Rui caught his belt.

Both men went down. One bowl broke against a post; the other rolled beneath the platform and continued ringing against the stones after Rui had lost sight of it. He drove his heels into the mud and hauled. Du fought him with the irritated strength of a man whose hands were full of supper.

“You'll spill it down my sleeve. Give me a moment to put these down.”

The deep northern drum sounded.

Rui felt the impact through his teeth. Beyond Du's shoulder, a black shape unfolded from the ditch beside the mast. It had followed the rising drops across the watch line.

Rui pulled the sergeant under him.

Green light crossed the platform. The Discord struck a post, folded around it, and broke apart under Jiyan's blade. Its claw left Rui through the back of his jacket as it dissolved.

Jiyan caught the young man before his head reached the ground.

For several seconds Rui continued trying to pull Du uphill. Jiyan had to uncurl his fingers from the sergeant's belt.

“Stay with my voice,” he said. “Tang is coming from the lower shelter. You can let me carry your weight.”

Rui looked past him at the suspended water.

“There's a needle somewhere near the receiver. Tang will want it out of the mud.”

Jiyan pressed his hand beneath the torn jacket and called for the medical case.

By the time Doctor Tang reached them, the blood had filled the hollow between Rui's shoulder and the ground.

She knelt beside Jiyan. They worked together while two Rangers stretched a tarpaulin over the posts. Under the cover, Du sat holding a bowl in each hand. One consisted of a handle and a crescent of enamel. He kept his hands level.

Tang finally laid two fingers over Jiyan's wrist.

The receiver crackled behind them. Someone had reached the southern relay. The waxed thread swung from its torn cover, gathering drops.

Jiyan drew his hand out of Rui's jacket. Tang folded a clean cloth over it. He used the cloth, carefully, and then helped her lift the body onto the stretcher.

Du watched them carry it away.

“He was speaking to me,” the sergeant said. “I heard him from the platform. He wanted the pepper out of his bowl. His stomach always gave him trouble before a march.”

Jiyan sat beside him on the mud-dark timber.

Across the camp, a green command banner hung over the medical shelter. Rain passed through it and briefly made another banner: black, torn along the lower edge, snapping above men who had been ordered to advance. His right hand reached for the medical satchel he had carried in those days. His fingers found the guard of his weapon.

He kept looking until he could see the green cloth again.

“I heard the old advance call,” he told Du. “I followed it as far as the mast before I saw Rui holding you. Tell me what you see now.”

Du studied the bowls.

“This one's broken. I think I've been holding it together.”

“Put both pieces beside your boot. Tang will examine you here.”

The sergeant placed them down with care.

Captain Bao ducked beneath the tarpaulin. Water ran from the end of her braid onto the map case at her hip.

“West watch has three men following the old quarry road. Xiulan has sent pairs after them. The new recruits can still read the route flags; the men from the last campaign keep seeing the bridge.”

Jiyan looked beyond her. The quarry bridge had fallen during that campaign. In the rain, sunlight lay along its rail.

“Put a guide from each group together. Move the wounded through the lower passage and cover the assembly point. Every order goes through an officer standing beside the people receiving it.”

Bao crouched and opened her map case.

“The south shelf gives us room to withdraw the outer posts. Holding the current line keeps Shuanghe's carts covered another hour.”

“Bring the posts to the shelf. I'll take the ridge while the carts pass.”

She marked the movement. Jiyan watched her mark the abandoned positions, then made himself look at the road those positions had protected.

The drum reached them again.

Bao checked her watch.

“Nine minutes and forty seconds. Yesterday evening it gave us almost nineteen.”

The spoon inside Du's surviving bowl trembled against the enamel.

Jiyan rose.

“Send the Magistrate our full report. Retroact Rain at the highland camp, spreading south along the Tacet seams. Include the change in the interval.” He took the casualty slip Tang held out. “Deng Rui's family lives above the east ropewalk. His sister should hear from an officer before the ward board goes up. Find somebody who knew him.”

“He taught the night signallers,” Bao said. “I can send one of them.”

Jiyan signed the slip on the map case. The paper took the wet impression of his thumb beneath his name.

Then he went back into the rain.

By the twenty-seventh of the sixth month, Mrs Ren's front door needed a shoulder to open it.

Mei supplied the shoulder. The door gave suddenly, carrying her halfway into the yard with an empty tea tray, and Mrs Ren called something from upstairs that Cartethyia translated as advice about the hinge.

“There was more to it than that,” Mei said, looking up.

“There was a remark about people who possess hands,” Cartethyia admitted. “I thought the hinge was the useful part.”

Rain ticked along the gutter. Three weeks earlier the lane had held quilts, open books, and trays of type. Now the walls smelled of wet plaster. The books in the front room stood on thin wooden slats to keep them clear of the shelf, and Mrs Ren had made everyone carry their boots upstairs.

Mei put the tray down and tested the door with her hands.

Lupa came out carrying a bowl of coarse salt and set it beneath the linen cupboard.

“Lift while you pull. The bottom's catching.”

“I thought that was the point of a door.”

“Keep practising. By supper you may qualify to use the cupboard.”

Mei got the door shut. From inside, Amy applauded with the slow, deliberate rhythm she used for achievements she intended to mention again.

Iuno sat at the table with the river observations and a cup of tea held against her shoulder. Her crystal had acquired a deep ache during the night. She had told Baizhi, taken what Baizhi prescribed, and handed the heaviest of her cases to Mei before anyone could develop a theory about her independence.

Mei sat beside her and leaned close enough to smell the bergamot.

“Does the cup help?”

“It gives me something warm to complain into. Your breakfast is under the other saucer.”

Mei lifted it. Lupa had left her an egg, two pieces of fried dough, and a small dish of plum sauce. She took a piece of dough and dipped it far enough to make Iuno reach for a cloth.

At the window, Cartethyia turned her terrarium a quarter-turn. A pale tip had appeared on the camellia cutting. She had spent the previous evening showing it to people, including the delivery man who had brought the lamp oil.

Chisa sat with Ruan's care roll spread beneath her scissors. She had moved her chair twice to hear the hinge above the gutter.

“The courtyard sounds deeper,” she said. “Water's collecting under the paving by the rear step. Mrs Ren should have someone lift that stone.”

Lupa looked toward the back door.

“We can do it after breakfast. You'll have to show me which end gives.”

Ciaccona, who had been working on the quarry song at the far end of the table, set down her pen.

Inside the little field terminal beside her notebook, Tollhide had drawn its four feet together. Its bronze throat quivered. A low note entered the table through the terminal's base.

She took the ruled card from her sleeve.

Three grey marks occupied the stave. As she watched, the distance between the second and third shortened. The paper beneath her fingers remained dry.

Amy reached across the table and turned down the room receiver.

The same low note continued through the wood.

“That came through the table,” she said. “Cia, put the terminal on the folded cloth. I want to hear the floor by itself.”

They waited through a little more rain. Mei's breakfast cooled beside her hand.

Yangyang knocked with the hilt of her sword because both hands were occupied. She carried two message cases and a roll of fresh notices under her coat.

Lupa lifted the door as she pulled it open.

“Come through. Your hat's emptying itself down your neck.”

Yangyang put the cases on the table before taking off her hat.

“Jiyan has pulled the outer posts back to the south shelf. The highland camp is seeing Retroact Rain. His report reached the relay twenty minutes ago.”

Amy's hand closed around the receiver dial.

“The kind we found under the Savantae lab?”

“Baizhi wants the observations you brought back from there. She has sent for the recordings.” Yangyang looked at Mei. “The Magistrate asks for you and Iuno at City Hall. Chixia is opening the ward shelters. She's on her way here with the admission sheets.”

Mei looked at the courtyard. Rain made a steady, ordinary pattern in the water beside the step.

Her hand remembered cold droplets hanging above a basin. It remembered the curve of a machine's shoulder beneath her palm, cable and metal assembled over forty years.

She turned that hand over on the table.

“How many people are still up there?”

Yangyang opened the first case.

“The camp is moving the medical station. Bao is collecting the Shuanghe convoy. I'll have a count when both groups reach the covered road.”

Mei nodded and ate the egg in three bites. She wrapped the remaining dough in a clean cloth and put it into her jacket.

Amy watched her do it. Then she reached for the street receiver they had dried and cleaned on the sixth.

“I'll be at the freight apron. The ward bells cover the terraces, and the dock speakers reach the people under canvas. Captain Chixia can have my voice until her own gets there.”

“Bring somebody onto the channel with you,” Iuno said, gathering her observations. “Someone who knows the households.”

“Panhua knows them by what they owe her. It's an excellent identification system.”

Lupa put two flasks beside the receiver. One went into Amy's bag; the other she pushed toward Yangyang.

Mei rose and came around the table. She bent over Amy's shoulder, kissed her hair, and got a palm pressed against her cheek for her trouble.

“Ma, you've got sauce on your mouth.”

“I'm leaving you provisions.”

Amy scrubbed her cheek with her sleeve, laughing despite the hand she still held on the receiver. Mei squeezed her shoulder once, then followed Iuno to the door.

---

Changli had laid the first casualty message beside Jiyan's route map.

The map occupied the middle of the council table. Around it sat the latest sluice readings, the transport allocations, a tray of bread someone had remembered to bring, and an inkstone Sanhua kept moving away from Jinhsi's sleeve.

Jinhsi read the casualty message twice. Then she turned it facedown and put a small weight on it.

“Has his sister been told?”

“The signaller is on the ropewalk stair,” Sanhua said. “He asked the patroller there to take him up.”

Jinhsi rested her fingers on the weight for a moment.

Mei and Iuno arrived while Changli was explaining the evacuation road. Yangyang went to the wall map and laid Bao's first message beneath the latest one, leaving both visible.

“Shuanghe's convoy can reach the city through the lower service road,” Changli said. “Jiyan's withdrawal brings the northern approaches under its own guns. Once the convoy passes, he will have to give up that cover to join his troops.”

Iuno examined the route. Her river notes went down beside it.

“The lower road crosses the spill channel. Today's reading puts water at the foot of the crossing by late afternoon.”

“The sluice office expects to lower it before noon.”

“Their reading is from the upper gauge. There is a second flow entering below it.” Iuno found the page with the third-watch measurements. “The man who reads this gauge showed me the old feeder. Its mouth sits behind the reeds. He asked for a second measuring post last spring.”

Changli took the page and read it through.

“I'll send his request with the works order. We can open the eastern spillway and relieve the crossing.”

“That water reaches the drying ground,” Jinhsi said.

“Three households are using it for seed-stock. Wen has their names. We can purchase the stock and move what is already bagged.”

Jinhsi looked toward the open door, where a runner waited.

“Send Wen now. Give him the forecast and the time the sluice needs to open. He can bring their answer directly here.”

Changli folded the page along its existing crease.

“The engineers need forty minutes to clear the spillway. If a household disputes the order, that time continues to pass.”

“Tell them that too.” Jinhsi reached for her pen. “We will open it at the necessary hour. I want their objections while there's still time to act on them.”

Changli watched her write, then passed the order to the runner.

“Ask who uses the ground after the seed is moved,” she added. “The household register covers the storage sheds. The people working between them will have their own arrangements.”

The runner left at a quick walk.

Mei had stayed standing. From the window she could see the lower terraces, roofs already gathering water. Every street on Changli's map had a counterpart down there, squeezed between buildings and crossed by clotheslines.

“What does the rain do to someone who grew up here?” she asked.

Baizhi entered behind her carrying the camp's exposure notes.

“It gives their memories more material to work with.” She set down the folder. “The rain carries condensed Remnant Energy from the Etheric Sea. Past events survive in that energy. Contact allows a person's frequency to resonate with them. Exposure matters, and so does attachment to the place.”

She opened to Bao's report.

“Two recruits saw a damaged platform. Sergeant Du saw his former captain eating there. Jiyan reports hearing an order from the previous campaign. Each of them occupied the same few metres.”

Mei looked toward City Hall's oldest foundation stones, visible below the window.

“And if the person remembers with their hands before they remember anything else?”

Baizhi's gaze followed hers.

“Then I would ask them to keep somebody beside them who can observe their hands.”

Iuno moved closer to the table. She left room beside Mei.

Jinhsi drew a sheet from the bottom of her folder. It carried a small plan of City Hall, with one chamber outlined beneath the oldest part of the building.

“The foundation gauge has begun answering the northern pulses,” she said. “We confirmed the correspondence this morning. The sealed annex may tell us why. You can have access whenever you choose.”

Mei studied the chamber's outline. A warm line opened across her palm.

“I want to read it with someone who can explain the language. I'll ask questions as we go, and I'd like time to write down what I remember.”

Jinhsi nodded.

“Sanhua can arrange the room tonight.”

“I'll come back for it.” Mei looked at the road on Bao's map. “Right now, you've got people trying to cross a place where the rain can put a bridge back. Yangyang knows the road. I'd like to go with her.”

“Jiyan has requested help receiving the convoy at the south end,” Jinhsi said. “He has assigned the ridge to his own troops.”

Mei put her finger on the convoy's destination, inside the city wall.

“Where do they sleep after we get them here?”

Changli pulled the accommodation sheet from beneath the transport orders.

Forty-three people still had wagon places and a covered transit courtyard. Their second night's lodging depended on two buildings whose occupants had been asked to share them.

Iuno read the entries. One building was the west school. The other was the cloth hall above the canal.

“I can go to those meetings,” she said. “I know the families on the school stair. I have measurements for the cloth hall, and Mrs Ren knows what its roof does in this weather.”

Changli took up a fresh sheet.

“What authority will you need?”

“Access to the allocations and a clerk who can enter an agreement when people make one. A term ending tomorrow at noon. I can recommend expenditure up to the amount you put here.” Iuno indicated the margin. “You and the Magistrate can end the appointment earlier.”

Jinhsi reached for the sheet before Changli finished drawing the first line.

“The families can ask for another representative as well.”

Iuno considered that, then inclined her head.

“Please write it in.”

*A career with an exit printed on the front,* the High Priestess observed. *We shall have to live on our charm.*

Iuno signed beneath the clause while Mei waited beside her, damp-haired, eating the last of the fried dough from its cloth.

The cloth hall's caretaker had been told to expect bedding.

She had swept the floor, unlocked the side door, and put a bucket beneath the leak. When Iuno arrived with the proposed allocation, she took one look at the word *vacant* and went upstairs.

Iuno followed her.

Six sleeping places occupied the loft. Shoes stood beneath the beds. A man's work jacket hung from a rafter, with the sleeves turned inward to dry. On a box beside the nearest pillow sat a cup, a comb, and half a peeled orange.

“They unload the night barges,” the caretaker said. “They've slept here since the second month. The warehouse lets them have the loft for keeping the rats out of the stock.”

Iuno stood at the top of the stair with the allocation in her hand.

Below, the clerk who had come with her turned a page.

“The return lists storage above the hall.”

“There's storage too.” The caretaker pointed at the cloth stacked beside the beds. “They put it on the dry side. I've been asking for that roof to be done since winter.”

Iuno folded the allocation so that the total faced inward.

“When will they be awake?”

“Two are at the landing. The others came off shift at seven.”

“Then I'll speak to the two at the landing first. May I leave the bedding downstairs while we work out the room?”

The caretaker looked past her at the porters waiting with their bundles.

“Stack it on the boards. That corner takes water when the north gutter fills.”

By the time Iuno reached the landing, her clerk had added six people to the accommodation sheet.

She sent that sheet back to City Hall with the loft's address on it.

At the freight apron, Amy had the street receiver open on a crate. Chixia knelt opposite her, holding a wire at the angle Amy had requested. Both of them had rain running from their elbows.

“Your speakers are older than the plugs,” Amy said. “Someone adapted these by winding copper around the pins. I respect the confidence.”

“That was my predecessor. He'd want you to know they worked through the last flood.”

“I can see the flood in the socket. Give me another handspan of cable.”

Chixia fed it through. Under the canvas beside them, NYX stood with its lifting clamps fitted. Two works crewmen were arguing over the best way to secure a pump in its hands. Amy looked up long enough to settle the question, then returned to the receiver.

Cartethyia arrived with a basket of clean cloths and a furious little boy.

“He has an objection to the school,” she told Chixia. “I said he could tell the captain while we carried these.”

The boy planted himself beside the crate.

“They've put beds where our projects are. Mine's a bridge. The glue's still drying.”

Chixia kept the wire steady.

“Which classroom are they using?”

“The one above the turn. They picked it up by the roof.”

Cartethyia winced.

“I can carry it by the base,” she offered. “You can show me where you'd like it.”

“I want it in my classroom.”

The rain drummed on the receiver's case. Amy let it go on for a moment, then slid a tool toward Chixia and stood.

“Let's look at it. If the glue's wet, we'll need a flat place. You can stay with me until we find one.”

The boy considered her, visibly measuring this against the people who had already moved his bridge.

“It has a middle that lifts. You have to keep your fingers off the string.”

“Good thing you came down to supervise us.”

Amy gave Chixia the receiver. She followed the boy uphill with Cartethyia and the basket, listening to his account of what the middle was supposed to do.

Chixia watched them go. Then she turned the receiver's handle and waited for the dock speakers to wake.

Her first announcement told people where they could bring bedding. Her second gave the addresses of the open shelters. Before the third, she moved the paper farther from the rain and read Jinhsi's warning again.

“The northern patrols have confirmed Retroact Rain,” she said into the mouthpiece. “If you see someone from your past, or a street begins to look the way it used to, take the arm of the person beside you and get under cover together. A patroller will come through every lane. Tell us what you're seeing. Stay with somebody while it passes.”

At the ropewalk, a woman halfway through closing her shutters stopped with her hands on the wood.

The young signaller stood in her doorway. He had Rui's field cap under his arm.

She listened to the rest of the announcement. Then she turned back to him and asked what Rui had said.

---

The lower service road ran between the old scholar tree and the spill channel.

Water spread beneath the tree's roots in a shallow brown fan. Mei remembered its crown in the sunset, the broad green spread above a split in the earth. Today the leaves hung heavy enough to brush the top of Yangyang's hat.

Lupa and Chisa had caught up with them at the second marker. Lupa carried a rope coil and a sack of meal packets. Chisa carried her scissors beneath a fitted oilskin Ruan had made from three smaller covers and several opinions about people who left good steel in bad weather.

Yangyang stopped where the road met the channel.

The crossing was a low stone causeway, broad enough for one cart. Water covered its northern edge. Fifty paces downstream, a timber footbridge stood on tall posts, with a roof over the middle and a girl in a yellow jacket waiting beneath it.

Mei looked at the footbridge.

“That would keep people clear of the water.”

“The covered bridge was taken down before I came to Jinzhou.” Yangyang took the route case out of her coat. “Today's crossing has six stone markers. You can see the first beside my boot.”

Mei lowered her gaze. A stone stood in the mud, its upper half painted blue. She could feel the footbridge's planks beneath her soles, warm from a sun that had gone behind the rain.

She reached for Iuno before remembering who had come with her.

Lupa offered her forearm.

“Use mine. Chisa's finding the rope anchors.”

Mei took it.

Under the scholar tree, Captain Bao had gathered five carts and the people travelling with them. Canvas sagged over the loads. One driver sat with both feet in a bucket because he had mistaken it for the little brazier his wife used to put beside his chair.

Bao helped him take his feet out. She placed his boots where he could see them.

“We have sixty-one civilians,” she told Yangyang. “Eight wounded Rangers, four fit to walk. Ma has the rear cart. I gave the drivers shelter when the bridge appeared.”

“Jiyan's relay says the ridge is still covered,” Yangyang said. “The eastern spillway is opening. We should see the channel fall before the next watch.”

Bao glanced at the people beneath the canvas.

“Several of them are going home every time they stand up.”

An elderly woman had turned toward the bank. Her lips moved around a name. Beside her, a younger woman held a bundle of household papers to her chest and kept saying that the pears would spoil in this weather.

“Her husband grew pears,” Bao said quietly. “Their old yard is somewhere beyond that stand of reeds.”

Mei crouched beside the elderly woman.

“Would you like company while you sit here?”

The woman looked at the hand Mei had rested on her own knee.

“He's brought the small basket. He always says he'll carry the large one and he always comes back with the small basket.”

Her daughter pressed her mouth against the papers.

Mei sat on the edge of the cart with them. Rain dripped through a pinhole in the canvas between her boots. The woman described the pear tree and a wall her husband had built too close to its roots. She became angry about the wall in the middle of remembering it. Her daughter laughed once, then began crying.

Across the clearing, Chisa knelt beside a stone marker. The rain filled the channel with footsteps. Carts rolled along the covered bridge. Harness bells rang from a road she had already checked with her own hands.

She held one blade against the marker and closed her eyes.

“Lupa, I need you on my right. Keep walking while I listen.”

Lupa walked a slow circuit beside her. Chisa followed the pressure of each footfall through the ground until she could separate it from the traffic on the old bridge.

Then a second set of Lupa's steps came toward her from the water.

Chisa opened her eyes and stood.

“The rain has your walk now. Put your hand against my shoulder while I set the rope.”

Lupa did. Together they secured the first anchor.

The drum sounded while they were tightening the knot. Every droplet hanging beneath the tree shivered. At the rear of the convoy, one of the mules began to pull against its traces.

Bao checked her watch and gave the interval to Yangyang.

Eight minutes and twelve seconds.

Yangyang transmitted it to the city, keeping one hand on the blue marker.

The reply arrived through three layers of static. Amy had returned to the receiver.

“The school is taking the first wounded. Chixia will meet the carts at the east arch. We have a dry room for the people who need somebody to sit with them.” A rustle of paper crossed the channel. “Tell me what else you're bringing. We can still move furniture.”

Bao looked at Mei, sitting between the two women on the cart.

“Several families will want to stay together during examination.”

“I'll tell Baizhi. The tables are long enough; we can put chairs on both sides.”

The transmission ended. Through the receiver, a faint harness bell continued to ring.

Yangyang turned the volume down and kept her hand on the stone.

At the eastern spillway, Wen found a woman carrying trays of silkworms out of a shed omitted from the survey.

The three seed-stock households had signed the purchase. The woman had spent the interval between the warning and his arrival getting the trays as far as the dry bank. Mulberry leaves stuck to her wrists. Her youngest daughter carried the empty frames, one at a time, sideways through the doorway.

Changli reached them with the engineer while Wen was reading the household declaration aloud.

“We work here during the wet season,” the woman told her. “The ground stays warm under the wall. They eat better here.”

Changli looked inside the shed. The lower shelf had already darkened with seepage.

“How much time will moving the remaining trays take?”

“With my daughters, an hour. With somebody who holds them level, less.”

Changli took off her outer coat and handed it to Wen.

“Show me where to put my hands.”

They carried the trays to the raised path. Changli held each frame against the flat of her palms. The silkworms moved slowly beneath their torn leaves. At the fourth trip, the woman looked at her bare arm and the old feather-shaped scars.

“You'll want a cloth there. The edge catches.”

Her daughter brought one. Changli let the girl fold it over her arm.

The engineer called the time from the sluice platform. Water had begun lifting loose earth beneath the gate.

Two trays remained, along with a sack of feed and the low charcoal stove.

The woman counted them from the doorway.

“Take the trays. I can buy another stove.”

Changli and Wen carried them out together. The woman went back for the feed. Her daughter caught the sack at the bank and pulled while Changli steadied them both.

The gate lifted.

Water came through the shed at ankle height, brown and quick. It caught the stove, rolled it onto one side, and filled it. Mulberry leaves spread across the surface.

The woman watched until the roof reflection broke apart.

Wen stood with the declaration open. Rain made the figures shine.

“I can enter the stove and the feed under the same assessment,” he said.

“Put the room down too,” she told him. “My eldest was born in that room. When you ask me what it cost, put the room down.”

He turned to a fresh page.

Changli stayed beside her while he wrote.

---

The channel began to fall at the causeway.

First the blue paint emerged on the second marker. Then a ridge of stone appeared between the running strips of water. Bao brought the drivers together beneath the tree. They walked the visible route with Yangyang, holding the rope, before anyone took a cart onto it.

The elderly woman with the pear tree went in the first cart beside her daughter. Lupa led the mule. Chisa walked at the wheel, one hand on its rim, feeling the turn and the changing weight.

Mei waited beside the third marker to help each load through the deepest water.

The first cart passed. The second carried two wounded men and a woman with a bandaged hand. Mei steadied its axle while a wheel climbed the submerged lip of a stone.

Then the rain opened around her.

Sun warmed the back of her neck. Somebody was frying dough nearby. A child with a shaved head ran between two loads of timber, carrying a plum in each fist, and a workman called after him to mind the lime.

The scholar tree stood at shoulder height.

Mei knew the workman. His name pressed against the back of her teeth. She knew the way he would wipe the lime from his brow with the inside of his wrist. His thumb had healed crooked from an injury he laughed about while his wife was in earshot.

Her palm rested on something broad and smooth.

A fitting. An opening. She had stood here before, with this part waiting in her hands.

Beyond the timber stacks, people were putting up the frame of a house. A woman on a ladder held the upper beam while two men argued about its angle. Smoke from the cookfire reached them. The child offered one of his plums to somebody just outside Mei's sight.

The gate in front of her opened toward those houses.

Her right hand turned.

Gold entered the water around the marker.

Across the channel, every suspended drop swung toward her at once. Bao shouted. The second mule reared between its traces.

Mei could hear the workman's wife laughing. She knew where their house would stand when it was finished. She knew which window would catch the evening.

“Mei, put your weight into my arm.”

Chisa's voice reached her from a great distance. A hand closed around her left forearm. The scissors rang once against stone.

Mei tried to turn toward the sound. The fitting moved under her palm. Far away beneath her feet, something enormous began taking up its slack.

“There's a house behind it,” she said. “They've got the roof beam up.”

Chisa's grip tightened.

“I've got the cart against my hip. The wheel is slipping. I need you here with me.”

Mei could feel the workman's crooked thumb guiding her hand over the fitting. A warm hand, callused along the base of the fingers. He was showing her the direction the gate would travel.

She lifted her right hand.

It rose through the memory slowly. Cold water closed around her wrist. The house frame thinned, and she saw the cart's axle in front of her, tilted toward the edge of the causeway.

Mei caught it with both hands.

The mule found its footing. Chisa shoved the wheel upward. Together they brought the cart onto the next stone, where Bao took the shaft and guided it clear.

Mei remained bent over with her hands on her knees.

The child with the plums crossed the water once more. Then the scholar tree's old roots filled the place where he had been standing.

Yangyang reached Mei from the south end. Air gathered around her hat, warm enough to lift the wet hairs from Mei's forehead. The rain slanted away from them.

For a few breaths Mei could see every stone on the causeway.

Then the wind faltered.

Water flashed high above the road: a city reflected in the air, windows open on black floodwater, a red cap turning slowly beneath a broken lamp. Yangyang dropped to one knee as if somebody had struck her behind it.

Her hand went up toward the air beside her cheek.

“Stay close to my shoulder. I'll take you across.”

The warmth pulled north, toward the carts still waiting beneath the tree. Yangyang turned her hand palm upward and held it steady.

“Bao has the drivers. Lupa has the animals. I need help finding the south marker, and I want you with me when I reach it.”

A small pressure settled into her sleeve.

She stood carefully. Mei took her other arm.

They walked the rope together. Behind them, the rain resumed its full weight over the crossing. Bao called the next driver forward. Chisa answered from the third marker, telling him where to place the left wheel.

At the south bank, Mei sat down beneath the receiving canopy and asked the Ranger there for a cup of water.

He brought it. Her fingers shook enough to tap the rim against her teeth.

Yangyang took out the receiver.

“Mei had a severe episode at the third marker. She is under cover and speaking. Her Tacet Mark answered something beneath the channel.”

Mei held out her left hand for the mouthpiece.

“Iuiu, if you're near the set, I'd like to talk to you.”

The response took a little while. Amy kept the channel open. Mei watched the drops beneath the canopy's edge, counting the ones that fell all the way to the ground.

Iuno's voice finally arrived with street noise behind it.

“I'm here. Tell me where you're sitting.”

“South bank. There's a green canopy and someone has given me water.” Mei looked toward the old tree. “I knew the people I saw. I knew where they were going to live. My hand started opening something while I was looking at them.”

Iuno let her finish.

“What is your hand doing now?”

Mei looked down.

“It's holding the cup. I'm using both hands because it keeps shaking.”

“Keep doing that. Bao can use the other people at the crossing. I'll be at the east arch when you arrive.”

The drum came through the ground beneath the canopy. Water quivered in Mei's cup.

“I wanted to follow it,” she said. “Even after Chisa spoke. I was so close to remembering his name.”

On the far end of the channel, a cart rattled across paving. Iuno waited for it to pass.

“We'll write down what you saw when you're home. You can tell me as much of him as you have.”

Mei pressed her left thumb against the cup's handle. Warmth from the radio case entered her palm.

“Bring my dry coat. The one Mrs Ren finally trusts me to hang up.”

“It's already over my arm.”

Jinzhou received the first cart while the west school was still moving its desks.

The boy's bridge had acquired a shelf in the head teacher's room. He sat beside it working the lifting section for a wounded Ranger, who watched with the solemn attention the demonstration deserved. A bowl cooled on the desk between them.

Downstairs, Baizhi had screens arranged around three examination places. Cartethyia held one screen upright while a carpenter fitted its foot. Every time someone came through the door, she introduced the room in Huanglong, then in the common tongue, and pointed out where the families could sit.

An old man stopped in front of her.

“My wife came in ahead of me. She's carrying the blue quilt.”

Cartethyia looked toward the admission table. Several quilts lay over the rail. She took the old man's arm and brought him to a chair beside the door.

“You can tell me about her while we wait. Chixia is checking everyone who comes through.”

He described a woman who had been buried beside the north road six years earlier. Cartethyia listened, keeping a hand on the chair so that he could feel where she was.

Ciaccona sat at the window with her lute across her knees. The classroom had bad acoustics: a hard ceiling, a bare floor, and too many voices occupying the same small space. She tried a chord, listened to it return, and moved her chair.

The rain beyond the window answered with an older song.

She knew it. Her father had played it with the flat bridge, smiling at a mistake he intended to improve next time. Her fingers went toward his fingering.

Then the boy upstairs dropped the lifting part of his model, and a wounded Ranger laughed, and somebody below asked where the toilets were.

Ciaccona put a different chord under the room.

She played softly enough that Baizhi could continue asking her questions. When Cartethyia started humming, she followed her pace. The old man by the door tapped a finger against his knee.

The song outside continued for a while.

Ciaccona's eyes filled. She kept playing the one inside.

At the cloth hall, two night workers moved their beds against the upper wall. They had agreed to share the loft with the people who could climb the stair. In return, the city had hired a carpenter for the leaking roof and guaranteed them a place to sleep through the morning shift.

One of the men watched Iuno's clerk enter the agreement.

“You'll still need this hall when the rain ends,” he said. “What happens to us then?”

Iuno put a date beside the terms.

“This expires in seven days. You'll be at the table when we discuss the next seven.”

“I'll be unloading a barge.”

She looked at the shift list nailed beside the stair, then changed the hour of the meeting.

Mrs Ren arrived with curtains, two extra blankets, and a complaint about the loft's ladder. While the carpenter listened, Iuno crossed to the east arch with Mei's coat over her living arm.

The sixth cart came through near dusk. Bao had borrowed it from the service depot to carry people whose legs had begun giving way after the crossing. Lupa walked beside it, mud to her thighs. Chisa held the rear rail with one hand and Mei's wrist with the other.

Iuno waited until the cart stopped.

Mei climbed down slowly. Her right hand had begun to glow again on the last approach to the city. Through the rain, the foundation beneath City Hall shone with the same thin gold.

“I saw it twice more,” she told Iuno. “Shorter each time. Chisa kept asking me what I was carrying.”

“She changed the answer halfway here,” Chisa said. “Bao gave her the medical case.”

Mei held up the case. Her knuckles were white around its handle.

Iuno took it, set it on the cart, and opened the dry coat. Mei put her arms into it. For a moment she stayed with her forehead against Iuno's shoulder while Iuno fastened the top clasp.

“The school has a room where you can lie down,” Iuno said. “Amy saved you a bowl.”

“I'd like the bowl first. Then you can tell me how many buildings you've acquired.”

“I've obtained the use of part of a loft. The negotiations have damaged my reputation for speed.”

Mei laughed into the damp collar.

Beside them, Chixia checked Bao's count. She read each household back and waited for somebody from it to answer. At the end she passed the sheet to the school clerk, who carried it uphill beneath his coat.

The elderly woman from the first cart was waiting under the arch with her daughter. She caught Mei's sleeve as they passed.

“He came as far as the turn,” she said. “Then I lost the basket.”

Mei sat beside her until the daughter had fetched their bedding.

By then Iuno's tea had gone cold in its flask. She poured it anyway. Mei took a cup in both hands and drank while the rain moved through the arch in slanting silver lines.

---

At City Hall, the northern reports began arriving on sheets that had been written on both sides.

Jiyan had withdrawn the highland medical station. Xiulan held the new assembly ground. The engineers were cutting drainage trenches between the gun positions, and the armourers had begun bringing damaged weapons in from the abandoned line. The rain had raised a second army between the watchfires: men at old posts, men carrying old banners, men walking through earth that had covered them for years.

The current army worked among them.

Jiyan went from position to position with the revised watch orders. At the western gun he found a young officer waiting for a captain who had died in the previous campaign. He sat on the ammunition box beside her and had her take him through the placement of her own troops, one squad at a time, until her hand steadied on the map.

Afterward he returned to the command shelter and asked Xiulan to read his orders back to him.

She did, including the withdrawal from the ridge where Deng Rui had died.

Beyond the shelter, something struck the earth far to the north. The lamp flame flattened. On the map, a bead of water travelled across the Norfall plains and stopped against the weight holding Jinzhou down.

Xiulan looked at her watch.

“Seven minutes and thirty-six seconds.”

Jiyan marked the time. Beneath it he wrote the name the dispatches had been approaching all day.

Ovathrax, Threnodian of War.

He sent the sheet south with the next relay.

Jinhsi received it standing beside the council table. Changli had just returned from the spillway, with mud on the hem of her clothes and a folded cloth still tucked over her scarred arm.

She read the message over Jinhsi's shoulder.

For a little while they listened to the rain hitting the roof. Sanhua went to shut the outer window and stopped with her hand on the latch.

The deep impact reached the city through the foundation.

Jinhsi felt it in the soles of her feet. Her pen rolled across the table and struck the inkstone.

“Send Jiyan the ammunition reserve,” she said. “Open the second medical store and put the armourers on rotation. The eastern district can draw lamp oil from City Hall until the ward deliveries reach it.”

Changli placed the dispatch beside the latest accommodation sheet.

“The city will ask how long the stores will last.”

“Give them the current figure and the rate we're using them. Read it at the ward meetings. They should be able to ask about their own streets.”

Changli nodded and began writing.

Jinhsi picked up the accommodation sheet. Forty-three had become forty-nine when the loft's occupants were added. New arrangements had brought that number down. Below the revised total, the clerk had started another column for the families expected from the north tomorrow.

She ran a finger down the names, stopping where one had been squeezed against the rule.

“We'll need the larger register,” she said.

Changli looked up.

“I had it brought from the records room this afternoon. It's on your chair.”

Jinhsi turned. The book lay beneath her folded cloak, heavy enough to bow the cushion.

She carried it to the table herself.

On Bookbinders' Row, a compositor was setting the warning for the morning posting.

He had run short of one sort. He checked the drawer, the floor beneath it, and the dish where his daughter put pieces she found while sweeping. Then he ruled a small space in the forme and reached for a brush.

The woman waiting at his counter watched him.

“Will the ward accept a sheet finished by hand?” she asked.

“They'll accept a warning people can read. Bring your receipt over here; I'll see what you're owed.”

She set down the receipt. It covered several purchases of type over the previous month, all paid in advance.

The compositor glanced at it, then toward his cases.

“I can fill part of this after the rain. The notices have taken the rest of the afternoon.”

“Take the time you need. I'll be in the city.”

His daughter came through the back door carrying a kettle with both hands. She stepped around the trays on the floor and poured for her father first. The second cup she put on the counter beside the woman's receipt.

“It's very hot,” she said. “The handle stays cool if you take it here.”

The woman wrapped both hands around the cup.

Outside, the rain had gathered in the lip of a broken paving stone. For an instant the puddle held a mown field. Stubble pressed against bare arms. A small finger hooked around another, warm and certain, and the light above it spread until it filled the place where a face should have been.

The woman's little finger moved against the porcelain.

The girl behind the counter asked whether she wanted sugar.

“Two, please,” Nivora said. “And another cup for yourself, if your father can spare it.”

The compositor's brush went into the space left by the missing type. His daughter fetched a cup with a chipped rim and sat on the stool beside him.

Nivora drank while the hand-lettered warnings dried.

---

At the school, Mei finished her bowl with her back against the warm side of the stove.

Amy sat on the floor beside her. The receiver rested between her boots. She had given the late announcements to two dock workers and the school caretaker, and their voices came through in turn, each with its own speed and its own way of pronouncing the street names.

Lupa arrived from the kitchen with another pot. She put it down, rubbed both hands over her face, and leaned against the doorframe while Cartethyia found bowls.

“Panhua says we have enough for the late watch,” she told them. “She also says somebody has been cutting her scallions with medical scissors.”

Baizhi looked up from the far table.

“They were kitchen scissors when I borrowed them. I sterilised them afterward.”

“You can explain that to her in person. She's saved you supper.”

Ciaccona laughed and shifted her lute so a child could squeeze past her chair. Chisa sat near the doorway, drying Ruan's hinge with the cloth from his care roll. The red thread at her wrist had darkened with water. She eased it clear of the steel and went on working.

Yangyang occupied the bench below the window. Her mother's flute lay inside its open case. The curtain nearest her shoulder lifted occasionally, barely enough to stir the air around her cup.

Mei watched it for a while. Then she leaned against Amy, who accepted the weight and continued listening to the receiver.

“I made an appointment at the archive,” Mei said. “Tonight, after this. I asked for someone to read the annex with me.”

Amy turned the receiver a little lower.

“I'd like to come. I can sit outside while you read, if you'd rather have Iuno in the room.”

“Come in with us. You can keep the questions I forget to ask.”

Amy rested her head briefly against Mei's.

Iuno stood at the far end of the room, helping Mrs Ren divide the dry blankets.

She looked over when Mei stood.

“Are you ready to walk up?”

“Give me a minute. Chisa left her coat in the cart.”

Mei went down to the arch, found the coat beneath the driver's bench, and shook the water from its oilskin cover. Through the open gate, the road ran north between lamps set low enough to show the mud. A keeper was lighting the next one with a long pole. He stopped to let a stretcher pass, then raised the pole again.

Above him, the bell in the ninth tower trembled on its beam.

Mei felt the answering warmth across her palm. She closed her hand around Chisa's coat and waited for it to pass.

Beside the school door, Chixia had pinned the weather notice beneath a square of oiled paper. Rain gathered along its lower edge. The two large characters at the top remained clear.

梅雨

Iuno lifted the curtain from inside.

“Mei, your daughter has found the questions already. You may want to hear them before we walk up.”

“I'm coming. Help me get this coat somewhere dry.”

Iuno took one sleeve. Mei kept the other. Together they lifted it clear of the wet step and carried it into the room.

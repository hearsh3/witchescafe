Chapter 1: Rù Huǒ

“First,” Cartethyia announced, planting her small boots at a neat forty-five-degree angle in the woodchips beside the chopping block, “we must establish the bearing of the blade. When Mei enters the lists, she does not merely hold iron. She greets the field.”

In her right hand Cartethyia held a four-foot strip of rough pine lath she had rescued from the kindling pile. She brought the flat of the wood up to the bridge of her nose, bowed her head until the crystal thorns of her laurel flashed in the mid-morning sun, and swept the stick down to her hip in a wide, whistling figure-eight that clipped a dry dock-weed clean off its stalk.

Ten feet away, standing in the rime beside the sixteen-foot cedar ladder with Lupa’s spare sheepskin vest hanging off her shoulders like a fleece barrel, Amy stared down at her own weapon.

It was the kitchen broom, with the straw head unscrewed and sitting upside down on the rain-barrel.

“She has never done that in her life,” Amy said.

“She does it in spirit,” Cartethyia said earnestly. “In Ragunna, the treatise of Saint Vitus calls it *Opening the Belfry*. It clears the space around the wrist so the elbow remains pure.”

“Last week in the alley she hit a machine with a bench,” Amy pointed out, shifting her grip on the worn ash broomstick and sighting along the wood as if she were checking a turret barrel for warp. “Zero belfry. Look, if I swing my arm out that wide on the first beat, my whole right side’s open from the ribs down, and anybody with a spear just pokes me in the liver before the stick comes back.”

Over by the cordwood stack, Lupa brought a six-pound splitting maul down with a single, effortless *crack* that sent two halves of a seasoned birch log flying to either side of the block. She leaned her wrist on the upright handle, her wolf ears pricked forward in pure, gleeful malice.

“Kid’s right, shorty,” Lupa called out. “You wave a stick at me like a church parade, I’m taking your kneecap off and eating your lunch while you’re still bowing.”

“The salute is prior to engagement!” Cartethyia protested, turning toward the woodpile with her pine lath held high. “When I taught Mei to hold the Sanctified Edge in the ruins, the very first principle was that the hand must not choke the hilt! You hold it like a borrowed morning, not a butcher’s cleaver!”

“I’m not holding a holy sword, Carte, I’m five-foot-two and my robot doesn’t fit down the stairwell!” Amy propped the butt of the broomstick against her boot and held her left hand out like a protractor. “Wait—let me see that lunge again. Not the church part, the step. When Ma cuts across from the right, she doesn’t bring her shoulder over her toe like that, does she? Because if the pivot’s on the front knee, all the momentum dumps into the mud the second you miss.”

On the sunny stone step outside the scullery door, bundled from chin to ankle in two grey wool blankets with a pillow wedged behind her lower back, Mei bit the inside of her cheek and kept both hands wrapped strictly around her mug of hot milk-coffee.

She had been parked on the threshold since breakfast under a joint warrant signed by Iuno, Lupa, and Amy: she was allowed fresh air, she was allowed sunshine, and if her boots crossed the mortar-line into the woodchips before lunch, Lupa was going to tie her to the flour chest with the clothesline.

“Ah, yes, the forward pass!” Cartethyia brightened at once, scrambling two paces back to get a running start around the four-foot butt of iron-elm that still sat in the middle of the yard with Lupa’s rusted iron wedge buried in its crown. “Watch the cadence! One, two, *strike for the banner!*”

Cartethyia took two bounding, high-kneed leaps across the frozen chips, rose onto the ball of her front foot like a ballet dancer clearing a puddle, and thrust the pine lath straight at the knot in the apple-tree trunk.

“See, no, that’s a thirty-degree up-vector!” Amy yelled, trying to copy the bound with her broomstick and immediately skidding three inches sideways on a patch of frost so the sheepskin vest spun halfway around her neck. “Whoa—stupid boots—if I jump into a thrust, the recoil goes straight back into my collarbone! Ma doesn’t hop!”

“I don’t hop,” Mei agreed mildly from the step, taking a sip of coffee. “Hopping’s bad for the shins.”

“It was a gallant bound!” Cartethyia insisted, trotting over to tug Amy’s vest straight before putting two hands on Amy’s elbows to arrange her arms into a high, gothic arch above her head. “Now, raise the guard to the third firmament—chin up, Amy, imagine you are looking over a parapet at a very tall heretic—”

The scullery latch clicked behind Mei’s ear.

Buling stepped out onto the narrow flagstone landing, holding a chipped blue enamel mug of condensed-milk coffee in her right hand and a half-eaten sesame dough-twist clamped between her teeth. She had her royal-blue silk cheongsam buttoned to the chin against the mountain nip, and the yellow paper talisman dangling from the brim of her round official’s hat was stuck slightly crooked to the bridge of her nose with a crumb of sugar-glaze.

She didn’t go down the steps. She looked at Mei’s blanket-pile blockading the left side of the threshold, stepped neatly over Mei’s ankles without spilling a drop of coffee, and walked straight down the yard to the unsplit iron-elm butt.

With a practiced hitch of her silk hem, Buling hopped up onto the four-foot log, planted her black-bound shins on either side of Lupa’s buried iron wedge, and took the dough-twist out of her mouth.

“All wrong,” Buling said around a mouthful of sesame.

Amy lowered her arms from the third firmament. Cartethyia paused with her pine lath at her shoulder. Over by the birch pile, Lupa grinned, resting her chin on the top of her maul handle to watch the show.

“Excuse me,” Amy said, squinting at the official’s hat perched on the log. “Did we hire a second coach?”

“Free observation, no charge,” Buling said, waving the dough-twist toward the back wall of the scullery before pointing at the ground between Amy’s boots. “First thing, your training ground is upside down. Look at this yard. North wall behind you, woodpile on the west, drainage ditch running southeast under the gate. All the wind comes down the cliff and curls around the rain-barrel like a screw. You stand there facing the apple tree, you are swinging straight against the draft. Five minutes and your wrists get cold, ten minutes you pull a tendon.”

“The apple tree is the only target that doesn’t have Lupa’s axe in it,” Amy said.

“Then move the barrel,” Buling said reasonably. She leaned sideways off the iron-elm butt, hooked the toe of her flat black shoe under the empty willow kindling-basket beside the block, and shoved it two feet toward the fence until it sat square over a frost-crack. “There. Better. And second thing, little sister, why you holding that broomstick like you want to pole-vault the ditch?”

“Because *somebody* told me it’s the Guard of the Third Firmament!”

“Very pretty for a temple play,” Buling told Cartethyia, taking a loud slurp of sweet coffee under her dangling talisman. “My shifu sees you lift your elbows up by your ears like that, she hits you in the ribs with a hickory stick before your foot comes down. Twice. Once for the left elbow, once because you made a noise.”

Lupa let out a booming bark of laughter from the woodpile. “Hear that, knight? Even the zombie knows you’re leaving your belly open!”

“I am demonstrating the chivalric line!” Cartethyia said, her cheeks pink under her laurel as she brought the pine lath down to a much more sensible level across her waist. “Naturally, in a narrow lane, one lowers the point by a hand’s breadth—like so—and advances with the weight upon the vanguard toe—”

Amy copied the lower guard, squinted down at her own boots, and lunged forward at the apple tree.

Her front boot hit a slick curl of birch bark; her back heel popped three inches into the air to chase the reach, and the broomstick carried her upper body pitching forward until she had to slap her left palm against the tree trunk to keep from nosing into the frost.

“Ugh!” Amy kicked the birch bark into the fence. “See? Every time I reach out to hit anything farther away than my nose, the stick pulls me over my front knee! How does Ma do that long cut where she steps way out to the side and doesn’t fall on her face?”

On the stone step, Mei wiggled her toes inside her wool blanket.

She looked at the scullery doorjamb to see if Iuno was watching, looked at Lupa’s maul, and gave up.

“You’re pushing off the ground like you’re starting a race, Amymy,” Mei called out from the blanket-cocoon. “Put your left heel down.”

Amy turned her head, her hand still on the apple bark, and glared half-heartedly at the nickname before looking down at her boots. “If I keep my back heel on the dirt, I lose six inches of reach!”

“You get the reach by turning your hip, not by jumping on your toes,” Mei said, leaning an inch forward against her knees and gesturing with two fingers on the rim of her coffee mug. “Pretend someone nailed your left heel to a floorboard. Drop your weight down into your back pocket first, *then* let your front foot slide out as far as it wants. The sword just goes where your belt goes.”

Amy frowned, muttering under her breath as she backed up two steps from the tree.

She planted her left boot in the woodchips, ground the heel down until it bit through the frost into the black dirt underneath, and dropped her hips two inches lower inside the oversized sheepskin vest. When she slid her right foot forward this time, she didn’t lean after the broomstick; she snapped her waist around on the anchored back leg and whipped the ash shaft across the apple trunk.

*THWACK.*

The stick hit the rough bark flat and clean, bounced an inch, and stopped dead in front of her middle while both of her feet stayed planted like iron dogs in the hearth.

Amy blinked at the broomstick. She gave it a tiny test-wiggle with her wrists, looked down at her back heel, and then looked back at the step with her eyes wide.

“Oh,” Amy said. “Wait—that moved the whole fulcrum back to my hip. I didn’t even have to squeeze the stick at the end!”

On top of the iron-elm butt, Buling nodded once into her enamel mug. “Good. Back heel down. Now you only get hit once with the hickory stick instead of twice.”

Cartethyia didn’t miss half a beat. She beamed from ear to ear, struck the butt of her pine lath against the frozen dirt like a drill-sergeant’s staff, and drew herself up to her full five feet.

“Precisely!” Cartethyia declared, waving her left hand as if she had arranged the entire demonstration from the beginning. “Phase two of the Egla guard: the Rooted Heel of the Valley! Once the belfry is open, we anchor the cellar! Again, Amy, from the salute—and this time, do not forget the cellar!”

“I’m skipping the belfry,” Amy said, already dropping her hips back to whack the apple tree a second time.

By the fifth bell of the morning, the sun had climbed high enough over the scullery roof to warm the dark grey flagstones of the step.

Down at the far end of the yard, the sword lesson had migrated toward the fence line, where Buling was still perched on the iron-elm butt calling out commentary around a handful of roasted pumpkin seeds she had discovered in Lupa’s coat pocket, while Cartethyia tried to convince Amy that a proper parry required shouting the name of one’s town on the third beat.

On the doorstep, Mei had shifted six inches to her left so there was room on the warm wool blanket.

Yangyang sat beside her with her knees drawn up slightly to favor her strapped left ribs and her patrol sword resting flat on the stone behind her boots. Across her lap, sitting on a folded square of faded, oil-stained grey blade-cloth, lay *The Swordcraft of the Golden-Eyed Stranger*.

Mei had pulled the flat bundle out from under her jacket ten minutes earlier, when Lupa had gone inside to check the stock-pot and immediately threatened to sit on Mei’s head if Mei so much as stood up to stretch her calves.

“You’re the one who actually has to do stick-drill on this mountain,” Mei had said, nudging the wrapped book onto Yangyang’s knee. “Tell me if the pictures make any sense, because I’m not allowed to move my feet until Thursday.”

Now Yangyang lifted the stiff, slate-blue mulberry cover with two careful fingers, keeping her sleeve back so the silver cloud-work at her cuff wouldn’t brush the brittle edge of the paper.

In the stitched gutter between the first and second leaves, the single dried white plum petal lay untouched where Mei had closed it the day before. Yangyang didn’t move the petal; she held the bottom corner of the right-hand leaf lightly with her thumb so the breeze off the woodyard wouldn’t lift it.

Mei leaned her chin on her own drawn-up knee, her mug of lukewarm coffee wedged between her boots, and looked sideways at the yellowed first page.

The crooked, heavy-handed ink strokes of the forward lunge took up the whole center of the sheet, every line hooking sharply inward at the tail as if the person holding the brush had been in a hurry to get back outside. Down by the stick-figure’s rear ankle, marking the spot where the weight settled into the ground, sat the little round-shelled doodle with four stubby legs and a pointed nose.

Mei grinned, reaching out with one knuckle to point half an inch above the paper without touching the soot-ink.

“Look at that thing by the boot,” Mei said happily. “Whoever wrote this manual was an absolute menace with a brush. That’s supposed to be an ox or a water-buffalo for grounding your stance, right? Look at its little fat neck. It looks like a cow wearing a barrel.”

Beside her, the small cerulean feathers at the left side of Yangyang’s throat lifted by a fraction of an inch against her jaw and settled back down.

Yangyang kept her eyes on the little four-legged creature by the heel.

“The legs are very solid,” Yangyang said in her plain, gentle voice. “You can tell it isn’t going to tip over.”

“Right? Structure over realism, that’s what I always say.” Mei tilted her head a little, her golden eyes moving down to the narrow margin at the bottom of the leaf.

Underneath the big, untidy lunge diagram, the name of the first stance—*Settling the Stone*—had been written out seven times in a row.

The hand there didn’t hook inward at all. Every stroke was small, cramped, and fiercely square, the ink slightly thicker at the corners where a child’s brush had paused to check the model before drawing the next line down. On the fourth copy, a tiny blot of grey wood-ash had been smudged into the paper and carefully written over once it dried.

Yangyang’s thumb stayed very still on the corner of the leaf.

She looked at those seven small, square characters for three full breaths while, down in the woodyard, Amy’s broomstick whacked the apple tree again and Buling called out that her back elbow was flying to Mengzhou.

Then Yangyang’s gaze rose from the child’s margin back to the diagram in the center—to the straight, clean line drawn from the crown of the head down through the back hip to the rear heel, where the little turtle-cow sat flat on the baseline.

“Shifu makes me hold this one every morning before the mist clears,” Yangyang said.

She said it simply, the way she talked about the water level in the canal or which relay station had good tea.

“By the upper cistern,” Yangyang continued, tracing the angle of the rear ankle in the air an inch above the mulberry paper. “Before we do a single cut. If my left heel comes up off the wet stone while I’m turning the point, she taps the back of my boot with her scabbard and makes me start the count over from one.”

Mei stopped smiling at the doodle.

She looked at the ink line on the yellowed paper. Then she raised her eyes and looked out across the sunny yard, where Amy was standing in the woodchips with her left heel ground deep into the dirt, turning her waist on the exact pivot Mei had called out from the blanket five minutes ago.

For two heartbeats the doorstep was very quiet between the blows of Lupa’s axe.

Mei looked sideways at Yangyang’s quiet profile—at the faint blue warmth of the mark on her forehead and the careful, steady way her fingers held the old book open over her knees so the spine wouldn’t crack.

“Is the rest of it any good?” Mei asked softly. “For the cistern?”

Yangyang turned the first leaf over with two fingers, letting it settle gently over the dried plum petal so the second diagram lay open in the sun: a low, short-stepping ward meant for wet shale when a heavier blade was coming down from above.

“Yes,” Yangyang said truthfully. “The third page shows how to turn a downward cut when your boots are in mud and you can’t step back. The Paragon hasn’t shown me that one yet.”

“Well, keep it in your pocket until your ribs come out of the wrap,” Mei said, leaning her head sideways until her temple rested lightly against the soft sky-blue silk of Yangyang’s uninjured right shoulder. “Maybe by the time they let me off this rug, you and Amy can teach it back to me so I don’t embarrass myself.”

Down by the apple tree, Cartethyia tried to demonstrate a leaping riposte off the kindling basket, caught her heel in the wicker, and went sprawling into the woodchips with a loud clatter of pine lath while Lupa roared with laughter and Buling held up three fingers from the log like a tournament judge.

Yangyang didn’t move her shoulder away from Mei’s cheek. She turned the second leaf of the manual with her right hand and kept reading in the sun.

At the second bell after noon, when the storehouse went quiet for dishes and naps, Ciaccona slung her lute case over her back by one strap, walked over to the upturned flour crate where Buling was trying to pry a sesame seed out of her back molar with a fingernail, and hooked one finger into the royal-blue silk of Buling’s sleeve.

“Up, Consultant,” Ciaccona said. “My throat’s full of mutton smoke and Lupa’s about to start boiling clothes. Let’s go find a roof that doesn’t smell like wet wool.”

Buling eyed the door suspiciously. “How many stairs?”

“Downhill the whole way,” Ciaccona promised. “And you can roll back up in a wheelbarrow after dark for all I care.”

Ciaccona’s method for finding a drink in a garrison town had never failed her yet: she ignored the noisy tavern by the dyer’s vat where the off-duty pikemen sang out of tune, bypassed the noodle shed by the gate, and followed her nose down a crooked cobble alley behind the old lime-kilns until she found a bleached wooden sign with half its paint peeled off hanging outside a cellar half-cut into the shale.

There was nobody inside except a deaf seventy-year-old woman knitting a grey wool sock behind three stoneware vats and a single speckled hen asleep under the pickle shelf.

Ciaccona bought a two-pint clay jug of last autumn’s sour plum wine and two chipped earthenware cups, and carried them out to the narrow timber porch that hung out over the dry fern-gully at the back of the shop.

Before she even sat down, Buling gripped the edge of the scarred pine table with both hands, dragged it four inches to the left so the crack between the floorboards didn’t line up with the cellar door, and then dropped onto the bench with a long, boneless sigh, propping both black-bound shins up on the lower rail.

“Aiya,” Buling said, flipping her yellow forehead talisman all the way up onto the flat crown of her *nuanmao* so the afternoon breeze could hit her face. “That storehouse up there very lively, ah. Every five minutes somebody shouting about a sock or a kettle. How you sleep in there without ear-plugs?”

“I grew up with three uncles who played the sackbut before breakfast,” Ciaccona said, pouring the cloudy amber wine into both cups and sliding one across the pine. “This lot’s a convent by comparison. Drink that before you smell it, the bouquet’s like an old boot.”

Buling took a sip, screwed her nose up until her bright red eyes watered, and immediately took a second, larger gulp. “Not bad. Got bite. Better than the herbal tea my shifu used to boil from pine needles. That one you drink, your tongue stays green until Friday.”

Ciaccona leaned her spine back against the shale wall, crossing her boots at the ankle and resting her cup on her belt buckle.

From the porch rail, you couldn’t see the Astrolabe Core unless you leaned out past the eaves; there was only the steep green-and-rust drop of the fern gully, two crows arguing in a persimmon tree, and the distant *clink-clink* of a blacksmith’s hammer three terraces down.

“So,” Ciaccona said lazily, looking sideways at Buling’s silver hair and the yellow paper bands around her ankles. “Last I heard on the relay from Rinascita, you were charging the Ragunnesi harbor-master forty thousand shells to tell him his lighthouse was facing the sea.”

“The door was facing the butcher’s alley!” Buling protested, setting her cup down with a sharp rap. “Every time they butchered a pig, the bad qi went straight up the spiral stairs and broke the lamp-lens! I told them put a bronze mirror over the lintel, they didn’t listen, three days later—*pak*, cracked again. Still owe me for the second visit.”

She reached into her sleeve, pulled out a small paper twist of salted dried plums she had pilfered from Wen’s larder before leaving, and pushed it into the middle of the table between them.

As Ciaccona reached out with her left hand to take one, Buling’s red eyes dropped for the space of a beat to Ciaccona’s wrist—where the pale, healed line from Norfall sat clean under the cuff—and to the strip of fresh white linen still wrapped around the split knuckle of her forefinger.

Buling didn’t point at the hand. She just nudged the paper twist half an inch closer so Ciaccona didn’t have to reach across the wet ring from the jug.

“What about you, redhead?” Buling asked, popping a salted plum into her mouth and chewing around the pit. “You come all the way to Huanglong, still playing in cheap taverns for copper coins?”

“Worse,” Ciaccona said dryly. “I’m playing free marches for a goose on the front stairs at five in the morning. If my father could see my repertoire this month, he’d take my lute away and buy me a turnip cart.”

“That goose got very thick neck,” Buling agreed. “Good guard animal. Better than a dog, dog you can bribe with a bone, goose just bites your trousers for fun.” She spat the plum pit neatly into her palm, tossed it into the fern gully, and cocked her head at Ciaccona. “And the small knight with the thorns on her head? Last time I saw her she was still looking at every bakery window like she just came out of a cave. Now she’s bossing the whole yard around with a stick.”

Ciaccona looked down into her clay cup. A slow, helpless fondness softened the sharp line of her mouth before she could school it into a smirk, so she took a drink of the terrible plum wine to cover it.

“She thinks every third sentence needs an exclamation mark,” Ciaccona said to the persimmon tree. “Yesterday she tried to pay a peddler twelve silver pieces for a wooden comb because he told her it was carved from a sacred peach tree. And she hums a quarter-tone flat whenever she’s wiping that stupid flower-box.”

“Very noisy,” Buling said solemnly.

“Unbearable,” Ciaccona agreed, her thumb tracing the chipped glaze on her cup. “I have to tune the third string down every morning just so my teeth don’t ache while she makes toast.”

Buling watched her for two seconds, grinned behind her cup, and didn’t say a word about it.

Instead, Ciaccona tilted her chin toward the roofs of the Hold rising up the cliff behind them.

“What about your friend with the spectacles?” Ciaccona asked. “The one who hauled you into the kitchen like a sack of wet oats. She looked ready to skin you with a butter knife.”

Buling’s boots stopped swinging against the porch rail.

She reached out with one fingernail and scraped a bead of dried pine resin off the edge of the table.

“Ruoyun,” Buling said. “She’s always like that. Even before, when we were both—when the wall was new. You leave a broom leaning with the bristles down, she lectures you for half an hour. You tell her the roof is fine, she climbs up in the rain at midnight with a lantern to check every peg herself.”

Buling took another salted plum from the paper twist, turned it over between her thumb and forefinger without eating it, and looked down into the fern gully.

“She stayed here the whole time,” Buling said, her voice dropping its quick market bounce until it was just plain and quiet. “Every winter. Same four gates, same leaky ditch, same stupid ledgers. I thought she would get sick of it and go down to the coast at least once, see the big ships, drink something that isn’t boiled river water.” She set the plum back down on the paper. “She didn’t even change her shoes, you know. Still buying the same black leather ones with the strap.”

Ciaccona didn’t joke about the shoes. She poured the rest of the jug evenly between their two cups and set the empty clay pot on the floor by her boot.

At Buling’s hip, a stray gust off the gully caught the walnut-sized brass bell hanging from her red waist-cord, making it give a single, tiny *tink* against the leg of the bench.

Buling’s right hand dropped down to rest over the brass rim, quieting it.

“Eh,” Buling said, looking up at the peeling underside of the porch roof. “Ask you something. Just for research. Before the signal went, the forum was arguing about acoustic bronze ratios again.”

“Fascinating,” Ciaccona said. “Try not to wake the hen.”

“Hypothetically,” Buling said, keeping her eyes on a mud-dauber’s nest in the corner of the rafter. “Suppose somebody had a bit of spare copper sheet left over from a kettle job. And suppose they wanted to hammer out a small piece—nothing fancy, just road-grade, something that doesn’t crack the first time an idiot hits a floor with it. What pitch you think is best? For carrying in the dark.”

Ciaccona stopped slouching against the shale.

She looked across the scarred pine table at Buling—who was staring intently at the mud-dauber’s nest with her arms crossed and her chin tucked into her blue silk collar as if the question had nothing to do with anything in this world.

Ciaccona didn’t smile, and she didn’t thank her. She rested her good forearm on the table, closed her mouth for a beat to hear the room inside her head, and hummed a single, clear, unadorned note that hung warm and steady between them over the smell of sour wine.

“A above middle C,” Ciaccona said. “Tuned a hair sharp, Ragunnesi pitch, not the flat monastery standard they use up north. A-natural cuts straight through wet fog without muddying the thirds, and if my hand’s cold I can still find it on the second fret without looking.”

Buling didn’t pull out her Terminal, and she didn’t reach for a slip of paper. She simply tapped her right forefinger once against the rim of her earthenware cup—*tink*, the exact rhythm of a smith marking a cold chisel—and nodded.

“Ragunnesi pitch,” Buling said briskly. “Very pretentious. Uses more charcoal to temper the lip. If anybody ever makes one like that, definitely costs thirty percent extra.”

“Put it on my tab,” Ciaccona said.

She picked up her cup, drank the last sour swallow, and looked straight at Buling across the wood.

“Why did you come back here, Buling?” Ciaccona asked quietly. “You had five provinces of people willing to pay you twenty shells to move their beds, and you walked straight back into a fort where the roof’s falling in and the warden’s got you shoveling quicklime.”

Buling’s eyes came down from the rafter.

For a breath the red irises were completely still in the shadow under her hat brim. Then she reached over, pinched the last salted plum off the paper twist, and tossed it into her mouth.

“You know how much they charge for a single room in Jinzhou during the winter freight season?” Buling said around the plum. “Eight hundred credits a week, and the landlord doesn’t even give you charcoal for the brazier. Here the stove is free, that wolf girl cooks three kinds of meat every day, and nobody asks for my residence permit. Pure profit.”

Ciaccona looked at her for two beats, and let her keep it.

“I hate mountains,” Ciaccona said instead, leaning her elbows on the table and looking out at the grey shale wall across the gully. “Every time it rains here, the water runs straight down my neck, the chili oil takes the skin off my tongue, and there isn’t a single tavern within three hundred miles that knows how to make a cream sauce without curdling the milk.”

She tapped her bandaged forefinger once against the pine plank.

“And if you put me on a boat back to Ragunna tomorrow morning,” Ciaccona said plainly, “I’d jump off the stern before we cleared the breakwater and swim back up the canal, because all my idiots are sitting in that drafty storehouse up the hill waiting to see if the sky falls down. So here we are.”

Buling chewed slowly, swallowed, and spat the second pit over the rail into the ferns.

“Your idiots eat very loud,” Buling said, swinging her legs down off the bench rail and reaching up to flip her yellow talisman back down over her nose. “Come. Walk slow. If my knees start clicking on the steps, you carry my bag.”

By the time the sixth bell struck from the lower gatehouse, the sun had dropped behind the western ridge, leaving the valley in a clear, sharp, lilac dusk.

Out on the stone landing of the third storehouse, the little wooden turbine on the sill turned with a steady *whuff-creak, whuff-creak* in the evening draft.

Iuno stood with her right hip braced against the timber rail, her heavy wool coat draped over her shoulders and her foolscap pad wedged flat against the top beam under her thumb. Two paces to her left, Chisa sat on the upturned pine crate beside the boot-scraper, her giant iron shears resting upright between her knees and her pale blue tie tucked neatly inside her collar so the wind wouldn’t flap it.

Above them, framed between the black limestone teeth of the gorge, Sentinel Hsin’s Astrolabe Core hung against the darkening sky.

It wasn’t glowing. The three outer bronze caliper-rings sat locked at the exact crooked two-inch offset where they had stalled during the siege, and the great celadon jade sphere inside them looked as cold and opaque as river ice before a snowstorm.

Iuno tapped the cedar pencil once against her pad.

On the page under her thumb, written in her crisp, left-hooked chancery hand, were six short lines:

*1. Deep barrier rotting from the underside (fifty years). Muyu’s cages gone.*
*2. Jade doors bolted. Prism cold.*
*3. Last Sentinel’s Resonator (Xuanqing) gone long before this season. Office empty.*
*4. Celestials under non-disclosure clause now walking the market in daylight.*
*5. Jingran (condenser soot; narrow blade). Suoming (didn’t flinch).*
*6. Buling returned via lower ferry.*

“Read me back number four,” Iuno said quietly, her eyes on the stalled bronze rings in the sky. “Without the titles.”

Chisa didn’t need to look at the paper.

“The dragon and the jiangshi had an agreement with the Fox not to show themselves,” Chisa said in her plain, level voice. “Now neither of them is hiding, and neither of them is looking over her shoulder.”

“Which means,” Iuno said, drawing a single horizontal line under the fourth item, “either they believe the Fox is never coming back to audit the books, or they know she currently has her hands too full to enforce the penalty. And when I asked our new consultant ten minutes ago whether the gorge road would hold through the month, she told me our water-butt was giving the turnips bad luck and charged me three coppers.”

Chisa tilted her head a fraction as the wooden turbine creaked on the sill. “She cannot answer you if the clause holds.”

“I know,” Iuno said dryly. “I have seen what it costs one of them to break it. It does not make it less irritating.”

She let the pencil rest on the paper and looked out across the darkening roofs of the Hold, where the first yellow horn-lanterns were being lit along the bisected eastern wall. The fresh chip at her left crystal shoulder gave a tiny, flat chime as she shifted her weight against the rail.

“For thirty years,” Iuno said to the dusk, her voice losing its council-room edge until it was simply honest, “every time a roof was about to come down on a province, I knew the hour, the gate, and the price of the timber three months before the carpenter bought his nails. I hated it every single day. And now I am standing on a porch with a piece of foolscap, trying to guess why a four-hundred-year-old fox left her front door unlocked while the cellar floods.”

She looked down at the six lines of pencil lead.

“I prefer being surprised at breakfast,” Iuno admitted quietly. “I am less fond of it when Mei still has stitches in her stomach and the sky looks like that.”

*At least when we had visions, darling,* the High Priestess murmured inside her head, resting a phantom elbow on the balustrade beside her, *the prophecies had the decency to arrive in iambic pentameter instead of wearing a magistrate’s hat and stealing the fried dough.*

Iuno didn’t answer her, though the corner of her mouth twitched once in the cold.

On the pine crate, Chisa kept both palms flat on the black iron pivot of her shears. Her eyes were raised toward the dark celadon sphere hanging four hundred feet above the roofs—not squinting at the smudges of color, which looked no clearer to her than anyone’s face across a room, but listening to the long, sub-audible line of frequency she had memorized on the first morning they climbed the pass.

“Do you have anything for the bottom of the page?” Iuno asked her, because Chisa never filled a silence just to hear her own boots. “Or is it only what we have?”

“I don’t know where the Sentinel went,” Chisa said at once. “And I don’t know why the man with the cat had the same soot on him as the body.”

She paused for two breaths while a cart rattled over the planks down by the granary.

“The Core,” Chisa said, and stopped.

Iuno’s head turned toward her. “What about it?”

“I memorised it the first morning,” Chisa said, her eyes still on the dark sphere. “I’ve listened to it every evening since we came down off the wall.”

She was quiet for a moment while the turbine creaked on the sill.

“I can’t tell,” Chisa said. “If it’s the same, or if it’s changed. It’s too quiet now. There isn’t enough of it to hear.”

Iuno looked back up at the silent orb hanging between the karst peaks. Then she turned the sheet over to the blank back, wrote *7. Core: Chisa can’t tell* at the top, and folded the foolscap neatly in half with one hand, sliding it into her inner coat pocket beside her dented steel flask.

“Tomorrow morning after muster,” Iuno said, “we will walk down to the gatehouse and ask Warden Liangyu for the old relay logs from the year Xuanqing’s name came off the roll. Not to find a ghost—just to see which sluice gates they closed that winter.”

“Good,” Chisa said, rising smoothly from the pine crate with her shears in hand. “And we should leave the water-butt where it is. When Buling walked up the steps with Ciaccona, she stepped over the third flagstone without looking down. There is an old drain-vent under it.”

Before Iuno could reply, the heavy timber door behind them was kicked open from the inside with a crash that made the little wooden turbine spin three times in reverse, and a blast of caramelized sugar, star anise, and scorching ginger oil rolled out across the frost.

“OI!” Lupa’s bellow echoed off three warehouse roofs. “Get off the porch before the fat sets on the pork! And tell whoever moved my kindling basket this morning I’m putting their boots in the ash-pit!”

***

Inside the storehouse, every oil-lamp along the rafters was burning high, and the long pine table looked as though a supply wagon had overturned on it.

Nobody had held a committee meeting about where the ninth person was supposed to sit.

When Iuno and Chisa stepped in out of the cold, Buling was already standing at the northeast corner of the room with both palms planted flat against the side of the forty-gallon stoneware water-butt, her knees bent under her royal-blue cheongsam and her tongue wedged at the corner of her mouth as she prepared to shove three hundred pounds of cistern water out of the wealth corner.

“Take your hands off that crock,” Iuno said mildly from the threshold, unbuttoning her coat with one hand, “or you may eat your supper on the roof with your supervisor.”

Buling froze, looked back over her shoulder through her dangling yellow talisman, and tried to haggle by pure reflex. “Just four inches! Right now the water-mouth is pointing straight at the stove door, fire and water fighting in the middle of the floor, everybody’s spleen is going to get damp—”

“You may move one object in this room that weighs less than five pounds,” Iuno said, walking past her to hang her coat on the peg. “After that, if my shin hits a single relocated stool in the dark, I am docking your dough-twist ration.”

Buling narrowed her red eyes, scanned the room like a hawk over a turnip field, and marched straight to the pine table. She ignored the stools entirely, picked up the small, squat earthenware jar of coarse grey salt that sat beside Mei’s lopsided four-legged linen turtle, and set it down three inches to the northeast so the turtle was facing the teapot instead of the door.

“There,” Buling announced, dusting her palms off. “Now the tortoise is guarding the broth instead of running away down the stairs. Worth ten silvers, I don’t charge you.”

At the inner bench, Chisa didn’t wait to be asked; as she sat down beside Lupa’s empty place, she quietly slid her giant iron shears, her teacup, and the wooden bread-board six inches down the plank toward the stove, opening a neat eighteen-inch gap between herself and the corner of the table where the draft from the doorway couldn’t reach.

“Sit down before you trip me, magistrate,” Lupa barked, coming away from the brick hearth with an enormous two-handled clay casserole bubbling with red-braised pork belly, chestnuts, and quartered turnips. She slammed the pot down on the braided straw ring in the center of the board, grabbed an earthenware bowl the size of a wash-basin, and scooped a towering, glistening mountain of rice and three two-inch slabs of pork belly straight into it.

She shoved the bowl across the pine so hard it skidded to a stop right in front of the gap Chisa had cleared.

“Eat that,” Lupa ordered, pointing her iron spoon at Buling’s nose. “Your wrists sound like dry twigs clicking together when you climb stairs. If I have to haul you out of a ditch next week, I want something to grab onto besides paper and silk.”

Buling hopped onto the bench gap without a microsecond of hesitation, her heels hooking over the lower stretcher. She reached up with both hands, took her round official’s *nuanmao* off her head—revealing her silver hair, flattened where the brim had sat all day, with the yellow paper talisman still glued to the front of the hat—and reached back to hang the hat on the fourth wooden peg behind the door, right between Ciaccona’s battered green feathered cap and Yangyang’s patrol hat.

“Wah, very generous,” Buling said, picking up her bamboo chopsticks and squinting into the glaze of the pork. “Wait—this one got chili inside or not? My throat very delicate after the mountain wind.”

From the far end of the board, where Ciaccona was busy fishing the ginger slices out of Cartethyia’s bowl before Cartethyia could accidentally eat one, Ciaccona snorted without looking up.

“Delicate,” Ciaccona repeated. “She drank a pint of vinegar behind the lime-kilns at three o’clock that would’ve stripped the varnish off my lute, and didn’t even blink. Give her the chili saucer, Lupa.”

“That wasn’t vinegar, that was vintage autumn plum!” Buling shot back across the steam, though before Lupa could reach for the chili oil, Mei leaned carefully sideways from her seat beside Iuno, picked up a clean pair of serving chopsticks, and lifted the largest, crispest square of caramelized pork rind out of the center casserole, dropping it squarely on top of Buling’s rice.

“No chili in the brown pot,” Mei said warmly. “Just rock sugar and soy. Try the turnips, they’ve been in there since lunch.”

Beside Mei, Cartethyia had been working intently on a torn corner of brown butcher-paper since the soup was poured, her tongue poking out slightly as she creased the stiff grease-proof sheet against the table edge.

She leaned across Yangyang’s front and set the finished paper creature down on the pine plank two inches to the left of Buling’s elbow, right across from Mei’s linen turtle.

It was a small, slightly lopsided paper person with wide square sleeves and a flat-topped magistrate’s hat folded into the head, and a little strip of paper folded down over its nose.

“So the bench knows where your cup goes,” Cartethyia said brightly. “I wasn’t sure how you write the characters for Buling, so I folded the hat instead!”

Buling’s chopsticks paused halfway to the pork rind.

She looked down at the tiny brown-paper jiangshi standing guard beside her bowl. Her red eyes blinked once, very slow; then she reached out with one finger, tapped the little paper talisman on its forehead so it bobbed politely toward the salt-jar.

“Hat brim very accurate,” Buling said in a mild voice, and picked up the pork rind.

Down at the foot of the table, Amy had stopped eating.

She was sitting cross-legged on the bench with her bowl balanced on her knee, and she had leaned so far sideways to look at the Terminal propped against Buling’s rice bowl that the ends of her pink hair were nearly in the pork. Buling’s right thumb was moving across the glass at a blur while her chopsticks kept working with the other hand.

“There’s no signal on this mountain,” Amy said slowly. “There hasn’t been a single bar since the fog came down. Who are you typing to?”

“Drafts,” Buling said without looking up. “When the relay comes back, I post everything at once. The whole board wakes up.”

Amy tilted her head, read three lines upside down, and let out a sharp, choked noise through her nose that nearly launched a chestnut across the table.

“Oh, you have *got* to be kidding me,” Amy said. “She’s writing about us!”

“Field notes,” Buling said placidly. “Anonymous case study. Standard industry practice.”

“*Anonymous?!*” Amy hooked the Terminal out from against the rice bowl, spun it upside down so the whole table could see, pitched her voice into a theatrical midnight-radio baritone, and read straight off the glass: “*Draft. Thread title: [Urgent] Client site in northern mountains has zero geomantic hygiene, how to bill? BigThunder88 writes: Took a temporary winter sub-contract with a foreign household. Kitchen stove is eighty degrees too hot, the cook has a tail and threatens people with a ladle, the head clerk only has one arm and writes lists like a tax judge, and the boss lady sits on the floor making cloth frogs while her kid hits a tree with a broom. Also the bard still owes me for a broken cowbell. Rate my survival odds.*”

Around the table, Ciaccona choked on her broth and had to be pounded on the back by Cartethyia; Mei laughed hard enough that she had to press both palms flat against her ribs while Iuno’s hand came across automatically to steady her elbow; and even Yangyang’s shoulders shook as she hid her mouth behind her teacup.

“They were *swans*!” Mei protested across the table at Buling. “I haven’t made a frog all week!”

“Looks like a frog from the log,” Buling said, taking a turnip quarter.

“There is one household on this continent with a one-armed clerk and a wolf at the stove,” Iuno observed, setting down her spoon. “Your anonymity is a sheet of rice paper.”

“Correct,” Buling said, pleased. “That’s why no need names. You all already got your own thread on the Huangboard. Since Ragunna. Four hundred pages, last time I had signal at the lower ferry.”

The table went quiet for one beat.

“Four hundred,” Amy repeated.

“Got sub-threads also,” Buling went on, picking a chestnut out of the braise. “One for the wolf’s cooking. One for the bard’s hats. One for the small knight’s goose. One very long one where people argue whether the boss lady and the head clerk already married or not—”

“Don’t,” Amy said.

“—four factions,” Buling said serenely. “Very bitter. Somebody got banned.”

Iuno picked her spoon back up and ate a turnip with tremendous dignity, and a faint warmth crept up the side of her neck.

“Do we have nice names?” Mei asked hopefully. “On the board?”

Buling’s chopsticks stopped over the braise.

“Some,” she said.

“Some,” Iuno repeated.

“Internet way of affection,” Buling said, and started eating noticeably faster. “Very warm community. You don’t want to know.”

“I want to know,” Amy said at once.

“Spit it out, magistrate,” Lupa said, pointing the ladle.

Buling sighed through her nose, looked up at the rafters as if asking them for a witness, and set her chopsticks down across her bowl.

“Okay. Don’t hit me, I didn’t make any of them. Every one came out of a flame war, minimum a hundred pages each. Only the finest.” She nodded at the floor by Iuno’s shin. “Boss lady is Deadbeat Transcendent.”

“*Deadbeat?*” Mei said. “I pay for things! Sometimes!”

Amy let out one bark of laughter, much too loud, then caught her mother’s eye across the table and reached under the board to squeeze Mei’s knee. “It’s the *Transcendent* part I object to,” she said.

Buling looked at Iuno, at the chipped moonwheel crystal at her shoulder, and visibly considered leaving.

“Head clerk,” she said, “is Groomer Mussolini.”

Iuno set down her spoon.

Ciaccona put her forehead on the table. Cartethyia, who did not know who Mussolini was and could tell from Ciaccona’s shoulders that it didn’t matter, clapped both hands over her mouth anyway.

“I see,” Iuno said, in a voice like a closing vault. “Which faction?”

“All four,” Buling said. “Only thing they agree on.”

*Darling,* the High Priestess said faintly, *I have never been so proud.*

“The wolf,” Buling went on quickly, before anyone could reach for anything heavy, “is Char Siew Dog.”

Lupa’s ears went flat. “I’m a *wolf*.”

“Board says dog,” Buling said. “Because of the pork.”

Lupa opened her mouth, looked down at the casserole of red-braised pork she had cooked, and shut it again.

“Small knight, NEET-Maiden.”

“What is a neet?” Cartethyia asked.

Ciaccona lifted her head off the table just far enough to answer. “Someone who stays in her room for twenty years and doesn’t get a job, cara.”

Cartethyia thought about this with great seriousness. “I was *sealed* in there,” she said. “That is completely different.” She paused. “I did name all the dolls, though.”

“Bard is Ringo,” Buling said.

Ciaccona sat up properly. “Why Ringo?”

Buling picked up a chestnut, looked at it, and put it down again.

“Long war,” she said. “Your fans said the bard is the soul of the whole group, without her the story got no music. Other side posted one line.” She cleared her throat. “‘He’s not even the best drummer in The Beatles.’”

Ciaccona stared at her. “I don’t play the drums.”

“That’s not the point.”

“Then what *is* the point?”

“The point is your fans lost,” Buling said. “Six hundred posts. Nobody even knows who the Beatles are any more. The line won anyway.”

“Cia is the *soul*,” Cartethyia said fiercely, to the table at large and the board in particular.

Ciaccona patted her hand without looking at her, picked up her cup, and said into it, “I’m at least the second-best drummer.”

“The quiet one with the scissors,” Buling went on, nodding at Chisa, “is Repeat Student.”

Chisa took a chestnut. “Accurate,” she said.

“And the kid,” Buling said, glancing down the table at Amy, “is Ghost in the Shell. That one got its own photo thread.”

Yangyang had been waiting with her teacup held very still in both hands. When Buling went back to her rice, Yangyang lowered the cup an inch.

“And me?” she asked.

“You, nothing,” Buling said. “You’re new.”

Yangyang considered that, and took a sip of tea, and the corner of her mouth stayed up.

“So who has the most fans?” Amy demanded. “There’s always a ranking. There is *always* a ranking.”

“Everybody got stans,” Buling said. “Even the scissors.”

“Good,” Chisa said.

“That’s not an answer,” Lupa said.

“That one I don’t tell.” Buling picked her chopsticks back up. “Last time somebody posted a ranking, the board went down for two days and a moderator moved to the countryside. Most and least, I take to my grave.”

“You’re already—” Amy began.

“*Second* grave,” Buling said.

Down the table, Amy had put her face in her hands.

“I used to be the one with the fan stations,” she said through her fingers. “I had a *jingle*. It is completely different when there are four hundred pages about my mother’s sleeves.”

“Four hundred and twelve,” Buling said helpfully.

“Gross,” Amy said, and didn’t get up.

Lupa was still frowning at the casserole. “The cooking thread,” she said. “What do they actually say about the cooking?”

“They say you threaten people with ladles,” Buling said. “Photos very popular.”

Lupa considered this, nodded with deep satisfaction, and dropped another ladle of chestnuts into Buling’s bowl.

Before Amy could ask what the photo thread was called, three sharp, rapid knuckles rapped against the upper wooden shutter by the east window—the one five feet off the ground that opened onto the outer stair-landing.

Nobody inside had heard a boot on the stone.

The wooden latch flipped up from the outside, the shutter swung open six inches against the night mist, and Ruoyun’s round brass spectacles caught the orange lamplight from the table. She had a tin tar-bucket hooked over her left wrist and a bundle of oak roof-pegs under her arm, and not a single smudge of pitch on her ruffled white cuffs.

She didn’t look at the pork casserole, and she didn’t step toward the door. Her bright blue eyes locked straight through the window-gap onto the back of Buling’s silver head.

“You left your iron crowbar leaning against the cooper’s rain-barrel,” Ruoyun snapped over the clatter of spoons. “If the frost rusts the bevel before morning, I’m taking it out of your shoes. And chew with your mouth shut, I can hear you from the cistern.”

“The crowbar is grounding the barrel!” Buling yelled back without turning around, her chopsticks still moving.

Ruoyun ignored her completely and shifted her gaze two feet to the left, pointing a tar-free finger at Lupa across the top of the teapot.

“You,” Ruoyun said briskly. “Bank that back flue before the ninth bell. I just put fresh pine-tar on the west valley-gutter, and if you send another shower of fat-sparks up the chimney tonight and blister my slates, I’m pouring the rain-butt down the pipe.”

“The damper’s already half-shut, glasses!” Lupa barked back, lifting a steaming saucer of four hot sesame dough-twists off the corner of the hob with her free hand and holding it out toward the window. “Here! Take these up the ladder before they go chewy!”

Ruoyun looked at the saucer for half a second.

“They’re crooked,” she said, reached through the six-inch gap to pluck the top two twists neatly off the porcelain in a single clean motion of her fingers, and pulled the wooden shutter shut from the outside with a firm *clack*.

Her footsteps didn’t make a sound going back up the lane.

Inside the warm storehouse, Lupa snorted, set the saucer back on the bricks, and dropped onto the bench beside Chisa. Down at the end of the table, Iuno picked up her cedar pencil from beside her plate, uncapped her dented flask to pour a splash of hot Earl Grey into Mei’s cup, and didn’t even blink when Buling reached across the wood with one chopstick to nudge the soy-sauce cruet two inches out of the line of the lamp-wick.

By the door, hanging on the fourth wooden peg between the green bard’s plumes and the blue Outrider’s brim, Buling’s round black official’s hat swayed a fraction of an inch in the warm draft from the stove, its yellow paper talisman fluttering once against the pine wall. At the table, Amy was still muttering *four hundred and twelve* into her rice, and Buling, leaning sideways off the bench to steal the last roasted chestnut off Ciaccona’s saucer, promised the whole table that the first thing she’d post when the relay came back was the sock-frog, and that it would do very well. The small brass bell at her hip gave a single, clear *tink* in the quiet between laughs.

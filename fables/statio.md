# STATIO

*statio*, n. — a post; a picket; a standing-place. Also, of ships: the roads outside a harbour, where you lie at anchor without coming in.

---

**Colours.** Lanze Reiter = `lightsteelblue`. Ronin = `plum`. Merrit (bounty clerk) = `tan`. Ilsa (Common Quarter) = `burlywood`. Doggett (cobbler) = `lightgoldenrodyellow`.

*Continues from* **SPOLIA**.

---

## THE ROAD

They got four miles before Lanze Reiter started talking, and she did not stop for six.

She had opinions about the ground the harpies would be on, which they had read off the board eleven days ago and had been walking past ever since. She had a view on the drainage of the west culling ground and what it did to a shield foot. She revisited the cyclops twice, once for the second knee and once for a detail about the club-arm she had thought of overnight and wanted entered, and she said *wanted entered* out loud, to a road, with nobody to enter it.

Ronin walked on the outside, where the ditch was, and let her.

There had been fourteen years of this road and its cousins, and for thirteen of them the walking had been done in silence, because a route is a route and the log takes it afterward. Ronin had come to think of the silence as a fact about the world, in the way you come to think of a wall as a fact about a room.

Two days ago four people had gone into a field and come out of it, and now the wall had a door in it, and the door had been open since the riftstone, and Ronin had not yet worked out what to do with a door.

<font color=lightsteelblue>"You are carrying it badly."</font>

<font color=plum>"It's a boot."</font>

<font color=lightsteelblue>"It is nine pounds of boot at the top of the pack and it is pulling you left. Put it under the bedroll."</font>

Ronin stopped, took the pack off, and put the boot under the bedroll.

They walked another half mile.

<font color=lightsteelblue>"Why are we carrying a boot."</font>

<font color=plum>"Sparrow said we were carrying the boot."</font>

<font color=lightsteelblue>"…Noted."</font>

## THE BOOT

The cobbler on Bridge Row came out from behind his counter at a speed his knees clearly resented, went round the boot twice, and took his cap off.

<font color=lightgoldenrodyellow>"Oh, that's a *sign*, that is. That's a sign right there. I've been wanting a sign eleven years, I've been *saying* — Marra! Marra, come and look — that's a sign."</font>

<font color=plum>"It's the left."</font>

<font color=lightgoldenrodyellow>"*Better.* Bracket goes over the door, door's on the right, so your toe points *in* — and a toe pointing in says come in, and a toe pointing out says we're shut and try Rennick's. That's trade, that is. My father had a hat."</font>

He gave them eleven hundred for it.

Outside, in the street, Ronin looked at the coin in her hand for a while.

<font color=plum>"The cyclops was four."</font>

<font color=lightsteelblue>"Yes."</font>

<font color=plum>"It took us forty minutes and Sparrow lost an arrow."</font>

<font color=lightsteelblue>"Yes."</font> Lanze Reiter took her share and put it away with the flat professional satisfaction of somebody watching a market price a thing correctly for once. <font color=lightsteelblue>"The market wanted a sign. Nobody wanted a cyclops. Value is what somebody has decided to be short of."</font>

Ronin bought bread on the corner. Two.

She ate one on the walk to the board, and gave the other, without comment or ceremony and without slowing down much, to a man sitting against the wall of the tannery with his legs out in front of him, and he took it without comment either, because they had done this eleven days running and the transaction had long since worn smooth.

She has done that every day for eleven years, in whatever town the road produced. One extra. She has never explained it and nobody has ever asked, which she considers a piece of extraordinary good luck.

## THE BOARD

Merrit ran the culling board out of a shuttered window in the guard yard, with a ledger, a strongbox, a stamp, and a pad that had dried out in the spring and had been on the requisition list ever since.

<font color=tan>"Cyclops, west of the marker. Verified by the outrider at dawn, so you're clear. Four culling points, ten crowns."</font> She wrote without looking up. <font color=tan>"Party?"</font>

<font color=lightsteelblue>"Four."</font>

<font color=tan>"Two hired?"</font>

<font color=lightsteelblue>"Two hired at the stone. Released at the stone."</font>

<font color=tan>"Then it's a party of two with assistance and I write it as two, because the crown declines to pay bounty on assistance."</font> She wrote that. <font color=tan>"Name."</font>

<font color=lightsteelblue>"Lanze Reiter."</font>

Merrit's pen went along without pausing, which was a small kindness Merrit was unaware of performing.

<font color=tan>"Ward?"</font>

<font color=lightsteelblue>"None."</font>

<font color=tan>"House?"</font>

<font color=lightsteelblue>"None."</font>

<font color=tan>"Where are you sleeping?"</font>

<font color=lightsteelblue>"The Stardrop. Eleven nights."</font>

<font color=tan>"Twelve, and don't argue, because the bounty clears at the ninth hour tomorrow and you'll be here for it."</font> She turned the ledger a quarter turn to get the light and wrote three words in the residence column, in a smaller hand, the way you write a thing you have written four hundred times that week.

Ronin read them upside down from three feet away, because that is what she is for.

The first two were *of no*.

Merrit inked the stamp twice, because of the pad, and brought it down hard enough to be legible in forty years.

<font color=tan>"Right. Next."</font>

---

Outside the yard, across the square, a man came out of the Stardrop with his coat over his arm and stood a moment in the door, and saw them, and raised one hand about as high as his belt.

Lanze Reiter raised hers about as high as her belt.

He went back inside.

That was the twelfth day of that.

## THE HOUSE

The woman was waiting for them at the fountain on the way back, and she had been waiting a while, and she did it without any of the small performances of a person who wants you to know they have been waiting.

<font color=burlywood>"You're the two off the culling board."</font>

<font color=lightsteelblue>"Yes."</font>

<font color=burlywood>"I've a house going. Two up, two down, a yard, and a well that works, which is a thing I'd want said early if I were you."</font> She had her hands in her apron. <font color=burlywood>"Ilsa. It's mine and I'm selling it myself, so there's nobody to take a cut and nobody to lie to you about the roof."</font>

<font color=plum>"How's the roof."</font>

<font color=burlywood>"There's a slate off the north side and I've been telling myself about it since the spring."</font>

Ronin looked at Lanze Reiter.

Lanze Reiter looked at the fountain.

<font color=lightsteelblue>"We will look at the house."</font>

---

It was off the second lane past the bakery, in the row where the ground goes up, so the yard was above the street and the street was in shadow by four and the yard was in sun until seven.

Ilsa took them round it the way you show somebody a horse. Here's the fault. Here's the other fault. Here's the thing that will surprise you in the winter.

<font color=burlywood>"The chimney draws wrong when the wind's in the east, and it's in the east about six weeks a year, and for those six weeks you open that window two fingers and it behaves. Two fingers. More and you'll smoke yourself out the other way."</font>

<font color=lightsteelblue>"Noted."</font>

<font color=burlywood>"She takes a while to warm. Three hours from cold. You want the fire lit before you want the room."</font>

The stair was narrow and turned once. Ronin went up it behind them, and the fourth step from the top gave under her weight with a long two-note complaint that went all the way to the roof and came back.

She stopped. She came back down one. She put her weight on it again, deliberately, and it did it again, exactly the same, both notes.

<font color=burlywood>"That's the fourth. She's always done that."</font>

<font color=plum>"…"</font>

<font color=burlywood>"You'll want to know it if there's ever anyone in the house you're trying to get past."</font> Ilsa carried on up. <font color=burlywood>"My son used to take it in one from the third and land on the fifth, and he thought I never worked it out, and I never told him, because it was the only thing he had."</font>

---

Lanze Reiter went through the house like a surveyor.

She got down on one knee at the hearth and put her hand up the flue. She went out into the yard and stood at the corner and sighted up the north wall for settlement, and then went to the opposite corner and did it again to check herself. She looked at the joists from underneath in the back room where the ceiling was open, counted them, and said the spacing out loud. She asked what the wall behind the scullery backed onto and was told, and thought about it. She put both hands flat on the door frame at the top and bottom and stood there a while, and then took them off and said nothing about it.

She went up and looked at the window in the front upper room, and the light coming in was the four o'clock light, thin and orange off the roofs opposite, and she stood in it for a period that Ronin, who counts, made eleven seconds.

<font color=lightsteelblue>"The north wall has moved and stopped. It moved a long time ago and it has been where it is for twenty years or more, because the render over the crack is the old lime and it is unbroken."</font> She came back down the stair. <font color=lightsteelblue>"The joists are at fourteen inches, which is generous, and somebody meant it. The slate off the north side has let water into the batten and the batten is soft for about a foot and a half and it will want doing before the next winter, and it is an afternoon's work for a man with a ladder and a bad temper."</font>

<font color=burlywood>"That's the whole of it, then."</font>

<font color=lightsteelblue>"The well is on the high side of the yard, which is correct, and the privy is on the low side, which is also correct, and whoever laid this plot out knew what they were doing and was probably paid badly for it."</font>

Ilsa laughed once, surprised into it.

<font color=burlywood>"My grandfather."</font>

<font color=lightsteelblue>"He knew what he was doing."</font>

---

She put the paper on the table in the front room and put the ink beside it, and stood back from the table so that nobody would feel her standing near it.

Ronin had gone very still by the window.

Ronin had, by then, done the following arithmetic. There is a paper on a table with a price on it and terms under the price. She can read it. She could read it out loud, here, now, in this room, in about ninety seconds, and it would be an ordinary thing for a companion to do and Ilsa would think nothing of it at all.

And it would put a thing in a room. It would put it in a room with a stranger in it, on a table, next to an ink pot, where it would exist afterward and go on existing.

She has watched a woman decline to ask three people for the alphabet in three cities in eleven days.

It took her about four seconds.

She said nothing.

---

Lanze Reiter looked at the paper for a while.

<font color=lightsteelblue>"The purchase is declined."</font>

<font color=burlywood>"Right."</font> Ilsa put her hands back in her apron. <font color=burlywood>"That's fine. I'd rather it now than in a fortnight."</font>

<font color=lightsteelblue>"The house is sound and the price is under it. You will have four offers by the end of the month and you should take the third one, because the first two will be men who have not looked at the batten and the third will be somebody who asks about the chimney."</font>

<font color=burlywood>"…That's a very odd thing to be told by somebody who's just said no."</font>

<font color=lightsteelblue>"Yes."</font>

Ilsa looked at her for a moment.

<font color=burlywood>"You've done this before."</font>

<font color=lightsteelblue>"Yes."</font>

<font color=burlywood>"Bought, or looked?"</font>

<font color=lightsteelblue>"Bought."</font>

The room did what rooms do at four in the afternoon with the light going orange.

<font color=burlywood>"How'd you find it? The having."</font>

Lanze Reiter picked up her helm off the table where she had set it down to put her hand up the flue.

<font color=lightsteelblue>"It was a good house."</font>

---

At the door Ilsa held them a second, and then took the key off the ring in her apron and put it in Ronin's hand, and closed Ronin's fingers on it with both of hers, the way you do with a child and money.

<font color=plum>"…"</font>

<font color=burlywood>"I'm going to my sister's on the eighth regardless, and the house sells or it stands empty, and either way I'm on the cart."</font> She let go. <font color=burlywood>"Bring it back when you've decided. Or keep it and decide nothing. It's a bit of iron and I've two."</font>

<font color=plum>"There's a way this is meant to go."</font>

<font color=burlywood>"I've been doing it the meant way for four months and I've a cart on the eighth."</font> She was already turning back into her own hall. <font color=burlywood>"Four o'clock's the good hour in the front upper room. Half past three in the winter. That's the part nobody puts in the paper and it's the only part I'd have paid for."</font>

The door went.

Ronin stood in the lane with a key in her hand that opened a house she had no claim on, and put it in her coat, and it went in beside a piece of broken arrow shaft that she had been carrying for two days for no reason she was prepared to write down.

## THE STONE

The parcel was at the riftstone in the morning, tied with string, with the name on it in a hand nobody in the party had ever seen.

The stone does this. You send somebody home through it, and a morning or a week later there is a thing sitting on the flat of it with your designation on the string, sent by a person you will never meet, who had it off the one you sent home. Nobody signs for anything. Nobody has ever satisfactorily explained the carriage, and the trade has stopped asking, the way a trade does.

Inside was a book.

It was small and it was very old and it had been beaten half to death by use — the corners round, the spine gone soft, a water mark across the back third that had dried in ripples. It was a child's primer. The letters were set big, four to a page, with a picture under each. A for the awl. B for the byre. C for the cart, and somebody had coloured the cart in, badly, a long time ago, going outside the line all down one side.

Inside the front board there was a name in ink, and it had been crossed out with two neat lines, and a second name under it in different ink, and that had been crossed out too, and a third name under that which had been left alone.

Folded into the middle was a note.

Lanze Reiter held the book in both hands and stood in the square with it and did not open the note, and the thing she was doing with her face was so entirely without expression that four hundred years of practice had gone into it.

Ronin took the note out of her hands.

She had never done this. In fourteen years she had handed her things, and taken things off her, and put things in her pack and taken things out of it, and she had never once taken a paper out of her hands and opened it.

She unfolded it and read it out loud, in the square, at an ordinary volume, in the middle of the morning, with people going past.

<font color=plum>"*For the grey one. It was my mother's. She learned it at forty-one and there was no hurry in it, and there is none in this either. The archer talks a great deal and about a third of it is worth writing down and this was in the third. — Wilm, of Melve.*"</font>

She folded it again along its creases and put it back in the book.

Lanze Reiter stood in the square holding a child's primer.

<font color=lightsteelblue>"…Noted."</font>

<font color=plum>"Mm."</font>

She put it in the pack, in the bottom, where the rope was.

It is the first thing that has gone into that pack from a person.

## THE SONG

The harpy contract was on the board at six points and they took it because it was on the board, and the west culling ground was a bowl of scrub above the river with a rock stack in the middle of it that the birds used, and the whole business should have taken an hour.

Harpies sing. Everybody knows this and everybody underestimates it, because the way it is written on the board is *soporific effect, see reverse*, and the way it arrives is a woman's voice about forty feet up singing something that is very nearly a lullaby you know.

That is the trick of it. It comes in at the interval where the tune you are thinking of would go and then it goes somewhere else, and the somewhere else is what does the work, because your ear leans out after it and the leaning is where you fall.

Lanze Reiter took the first three off the stack with the shield up and her chin down, in the register she does everything in, calling the count.

<font color=lightsteelblue>"Two on the left. One high. Left side, coming down—"</font>

The fourth one came off the rock and opened up directly over Ronin, close, eleven or twelve feet, and put the note in over the top of her.

Ronin's sword came down.

It went down slowly. It went down the way an arm goes down when it has been told to relax, and her shield came off the guard about six inches, and her head went back, and she stood in the middle of the scrub with her face turned up at a harpy like somebody listening at a door.

<font color=lightsteelblue>"— **Already.**"</font>

Ronin came back so hard she went down on one knee in the gravel.

The harpy was off the rock and away before she had the shield up again, and the sixth one went with it, and the contract closed at four out of six, which is a partial, which pays four points and a note in the ledger.

Neither of them said anything about it on the walk out of the bowl.

---

They stopped at the water at about the second hour of the afternoon, and Ronin sat down on the bank with the shield across her knees and looked at the river.

<font color=plum>"This unit reports an event."</font>

Lanze Reiter went very still, which is how Ronin knew she had also been waiting for this and had decided to let it come on its own.

<font color=lightsteelblue>"Go on."</font>

<font color=plum>"At the fourth engagement this unit experienced a suppression of motor response of approximately four seconds, under acoustic effect, at eleven feet."</font> She said it flat and formal and to the river. <font color=plum>"Recovery on verbal command. There was no injury and no failure of the contract attributable."</font>

<font color=lightsteelblue>"Continue."</font>

<font color=plum>"During the suppression this unit experienced —"</font>

She stopped.

The river went on doing the thing rivers do, which is to have nothing to say and to say it at length.

<font color=plum>"I was in a house."</font> Her hands were flat on the shield. <font color=plum>"Upstairs. There was somebody moving about downstairs and I couldn't see them and it didn't matter, because I knew the walk. And the stair went, the way it does, on the fourth from the top, both notes. And I was going to go down."</font>

<font color=plum>"That's the whole of it. It was about four seconds."</font>

---

Lanze Reiter took the log out.

She sat down on the bank two feet away with the book on her knee and uncapped the ink, and Ronin watched her write, upside down, from two feet, because that is what she is for.

She watched her write the time. She watched her write the number of feet and the number of seconds and the words *acoustic effect* and *recovery on command*, in the small square hand she uses for everything, and then she watched the pen stop at the head of the column, where the column has a name at the top of the page, and the names of the columns on that page are *ground*, *light*, *weather*, and *remarks*.

The pen went to the third one.

Ronin's chest did something with no name in any manual.

<font color=plum>"That's the weather column."</font>

<font color=lightsteelblue>"Yes."</font>

<font color=plum>"That column is for rain."</font>

<font color=lightsteelblue>"It came out of the air at eleven feet and it acted on you and it went away."</font> She blotted it, and closed the book, and put it back inside the plate where she keeps it. <font color=lightsteelblue>"The column is for conditions. It is entered under conditions."</font>

She capped the ink.

<font color=lightsteelblue>"It stays there until I have a reason to move it."</font>

Ronin sat with that.

She had gone into the day expecting the other thing, and she had spent three years quietly building the shape of the other thing so that when it came she would have somewhere to put it, and the shape had a page in it and a number at the top of the page and a stake in a wet field at the end of it.

<font color=plum>"…"</font>

<font color=lightsteelblue>"You called it an event."</font>

<font color=plum>"Yes."</font>

<font color=lightsteelblue>"Events go in the book. That is the whole of the arrangement."</font> Lanze Reiter stood up and put her hand down and Ronin took it and came up off the bank. <font color=lightsteelblue>"We are two hours light. Take the ridge road; the low one floods and the board pays on arrival."</font>

---

Neither of them mentioned the name.

Ronin has decided to think about it later, and Lanze Reiter has decided that it was operationally indicated and requires no entry, and both of these are lies, and the two of them walked up onto the ridge road in the four o'clock light with the river going down on their right.

## THE PICKET

They came off the ridge two miles short of the ferry crossing, where the road bends round the shoulder of the hill, and below the bend there was a hollow with a flat bottom and old grass in it, and in the hollow there was a picket line.

Twelve stakes. Driven in a row, well spaced, on the dry side of the hollow with the fall away from them, which is how you set a line so the ground does not sour under the animals. Somebody had paced them. The spacing was even to within a hand.

Eleven of them were empty.

The cord on the twelfth ran about nine feet from the stake to the left ankle of a woman standing in the grass, and it was tied at both ends in a picket hitch, and the cord was new.

---

She was in grey.

The plate was the same grey. It was the same pattern at the shoulder, the same three-lame pauldron, the same slightly over-engineered joint at the elbow that costs weight and buys you a hand you can still use at the end of a long day. The finish was worn off the pauldron in exactly one place, in a strip about four inches long, where a strap goes.

She had no weapon. Her hands were open at her sides and had been open for a long time.

The grass had grown up through the tops of her sabatons and gone brown and been flattened by weather and grown again.

The cord was new. The tar on it was still black.

---

Lanze Reiter stopped on the road above the hollow.

Ronin went down.

She went down the slope at an angle and came at her from the front and stopped at a distance where a person can see all of you, and put her hands where they could be seen, and crouched.

<font color=plum>"Hello."</font>

The head came round. It took about four seconds. The eyes were doing something with the light that eyes do when the thing behind them has been idling for a long while at a very low rate.

<font color=plum>"How long have you been here."</font>

Nothing.

<font color=plum>"Did somebody put you here?"</font>

Nothing.

<font color=plum>"Did you put yourself here?"</font>

The head came back round to the front, to the empty hollow and the eleven empty stakes and the fall of ground beyond them, which is the direction a picketed animal faces because it is the direction the road is in.

Ronin stayed where she was.

<font color=plum>"There's someone on the road who ties that knot."</font>

And the woman in the grey plate said, in a voice with four hundred years of disuse in it and the vowels of a country that is not on the register:

<font color=lightsteelblue>"No action indicated."</font>

---

On the road above the hollow, Lanze Reiter did not move at all.

Ronin turned round and looked up the slope at her, and got, for about eleven seconds, absolutely nothing.

She has seen her stand like that once before. It was on a beach in another country in a different decade and there had been a woman kneeling in the surf with somebody in her arms, and Ronin had come over the shingle and found her at the top of the strand with her hand on her sword and her weight on the wrong foot, and neither of them had gone down, and they had walked north at 0540, and the page for that morning has three lines on it and one of them is in the margin.

<font color=plum>"Are you coming down."</font>

Nothing.

<font color=plum>"Do you know her."</font>

Nothing.

---

Ronin took her knife out and cut the cord.

She did it in about half a second, one draw, close in at the ankle where the knot had bedded, and the two ends went into the grass and the woman in the grey plate stood exactly where she was standing with nine feet of nothing on her leg.

She stayed exactly where she was standing.

The two cut ends lay in the grass by her foot and went on lying there.

Her face stayed on the empty hollow, in the direction the road is in, and the grass came up through her sabatons, and the light went round the hill the way it does.

---

Lanze Reiter came down off the road.

She came down the slope slowly, in the grey plate, in the four o'clock light, past the eleven empty stakes, and she went past Ronin without stopping and she went past the woman without stopping, and she stopped at the twelfth stake.

She looked at the stake for a while. It was ash, and it had been cut and pointed with a hand axe by somebody who was good with a hand axe, and driven at a slight lean against the pull, which is correct.

Then she sat down in the grass beside it, with her back to it, facing the same way, and put her helm on her knees.

She did not say anything.

Ronin sat down about four feet away, on the other side, because four feet is where you sit.

The three of them stayed like that while the light went off the hollow and the hill put its shadow across the eleven empty stakes one at a time, and somewhere above them on the ridge road a cart went past, and none of them looked up.

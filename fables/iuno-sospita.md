# Iuno Sospita
### a pyrrhic miscellany, from the years of the ascent

## I
*The third-district locks at night. She is seventeen.*

The sealed tablet for Senator Fulvius’s steward was still tucked into Iuno’s rope girdle, warming itself against her ribs, three streets and a hundred and forty steps above where she stood. She had come down the Grease-Stair instead of the Via Lata because her shoes were already wet through and because a fourth-bench novice who goes home by the high street gets sent to scrub the oil-vats before compline.

That was what she would tell Sister Porcia if Sister Porcia caught her by the ear at the wicket.

The truth was sitting on two split herring-barrels beside the lower lock-gate, lit by a pan of mutton-fat that smelled of singed wool. It was a butcher’s ash-wood tray, four spans broad, heaped with salvaged boat-nails—and every single nail in the tray was standing upright on its forged point, trembling like barley in a draught.

Iuno stopped under the dripping beam of the crane-house and pulled her mother’s sleeve back from her right wrist so the cuff wouldn’t drag in the eel-slime. She had tucked the excess cloth up through her girdle twice when she dressed before dawn, and the shoulder-seam still hung halfway to her elbow, making her look like a bolster tied in the middle with string. At the morning skimming, while the three senior readers were arguing over whether the grease-film on the Great Basin meant a blight on the Sabine olive trees or another twin calf at Varia, Iuno had looked down into her own rinse-bucket between her shoes and seen the butcher’s tray. Not the lock, not the lantern, not the tall girl now leaning her hip against the wet iron sluice-rail—only a hundred rusted points standing up in grey wood like bristles on a boar’s back.

A fourth-bench novice was not permitted to have visions in a rinse-bucket. She had emptied the water down the sink-hole, stolen a hot barley cake from the bakehouse trough on her way out, and walked down to the third district as soon as the dusk bell let her off the hill.

"Four asses the bag," said the man behind the herring-barrels. He was a smith’s factor from the shipyard lane—Iuno knew his brass thumb-ring and his greased leather apron—and he had his boot against a dripping sack of canal-dredge so the girl at the rail couldn’t haul it back. "And that’s charity, out of respect for your industry. Half of it’s mud-pitted hoop-iron and the rest is old shoe-tacks."

"Take your foot off the sacking, Varro," the girl at the rail said.

She was nineteen or twenty, half a head taller than the factor, with river-silt dried in grey streaks up to her thighs and a fresh split through her left eyebrow that had been stuck together with cobweb and pitch. Her tunic was pinned at the collarbone with a twisted horseshoe nail. She wasn’t looking at the factor; she was looking down into the black swirl of the lock-chamber, where a length of rusted barge-chain was creeping slowly up the slimy masonry out of the water, link by link, all by itself, trying to reach her bare heel.

"Five asses, then, and a mug of small beer," Varro said, reaching for his belt-pouch. "The points are rotted off the lot."

"Those are four-inch clinchers out of the upper furnaces," Iuno said, before she had quite decided to walk into the torchlight.

Both of them turned their heads. In the tray, forty standing nails leaned three fingers’ breadth toward the canal and quivered.

Iuno stepped out from under the crane-house, hitching her left sleeve into the crook of her elbow. She reached into the tray, plucked a square-shanked spike from the middle—it pulled against her fingers as if it had roots in the wood—and held it close to the mutton-fat lamp.

"Cold-hammered charcoal rod," she said briskly, turning the flat of the head toward the factor’s nose. "Look at the fuller-mark under the crown. That’s the old guild-stamp from the Salt-Gate smithy, three hammers in a lozenge. You can’t rot charcoal iron in twenty winters of canal-mud, and you don’t melt these down for pig, Master Varro. You tumble them in a barrel of dry sand to knock the black scale off and you sell them back to the barge-wrights at twelve asses the score. My father used to buy the bent ones for eight."

Varro squinted at her oversize blue wool, then down at her mud-caked temple sandals. "Who let the stray cat out of the vestry? Run up the steps, little sister, before the night-soil cart splashes your shroud."

"If I run up the steps," Iuno said, setting the nail back on the wood, where it promptly stood up on its toe again and leaned toward the tall girl, "I’ll walk home by the Cordwainers’ Hall and ask the night-warden whether Master Varro has a magistrate’s warrant to buy unmarked iron off the towpath after curfew. They took old Plautus’s bellows away for less at Michaelmas."

Up on the cross-beam of the lock-gate, twelve feet above the black water, a pair of muddy shins swung into the smoky light. Nella had a tallow-greased betting slate tucked under one skinny arm and a pocketful of bullace plums in her lap. She spat a stone down into the factor’s hair.

"Give her two sestertii and the small beer, Fat-Thumb!" Nella piped from the beam. "Or she’ll turn you into a frog and Gus’ll pull your belt-buckle through your belly-button."

The tall girl at the rail hadn’t moved her shoulders. Her eyes—pale, dirty gold under the pitched eyebrow, like winter sun on pond-ice—went from the burn-scars on Iuno’s right wrist up to her face.

"I didn’t ask for a clerk," Augusta said. Her voice was low and grated slightly, as if she had swallowed sand in the pit. "Take your hand off my tray."

"He was cheating you," Iuno said, offended. "And he wasn’t even doing it cleverly. You’ve got three barge-pintles at the bottom of that sack that only need a hot rasp on the gudgeon. Anyone with eyes can see the grain of the metal."

"My iron," Augusta said. "My mouth." She looked back at Varro. Around his ankles, the iron hoops of his own herring-barrels gave a sharp, dry creak and tightened into ovals against the staves. "Six silver sestertii. On the board. Or the sack goes back in the lock and you go in after it to look for your teeth."

Varro looked at the straining barrel-hoops, looked at the two hundred nails all pointing at the tall girl’s knees like compass-needles, and cursed under his breath. He counted four worn silver pieces and eight heavy bronze asses onto the corner of the tray, kicked the sack out of the way, and slung it over his shoulder.

"Bitch-wolf," he muttered, backing toward the boat-yard lane. "Scarus’ll have the hide off your ribs before the moon’s over the tannery."

Augusta swept the coin off the wood into her palm without looking at it. "Go on up your hill, Mouse," she said to Iuno. "Wait any longer and the acolytes’ll lock the pantry."

"I am not a mouse," Iuno said, drawing herself up as tall as her five feet and one inch allowed, though the folded hem of Caecilia’s robe chose that moment to slip out of her rope girdle and drop over her toes with a soft flop. She kicked it back under the wool. "And your eyebrow is bleeding again. Whoever stuck that cobweb on used tallow instead of pine-resin. If you go back into the lime-pit with your head open, all he has to do is throw sawdust in your left eye and take you on the blind turn."

From the lock-beam overhead, Nella whistled between her teeth. "She’s got you there, Gus. Old Bato throws dirt with both hands when he’s losing."

"I fought my bout at sundown," Augusta said, wiping the trickle of blood off her cheek with the back of her wrist. "I’m done for the night."

"Are you, then?" Nella leaned over the beam, pointing down the towpath with her slate-pencil. "Tell that to the aprons."

Three men were coming past the lime-kilns at a heavy trot. The one in front carried an oak cudgel tucked under his ox-hide jerkin and had a brass tally-horn banging against his thigh—Scarus, who kept the pit behind the sheepskin vats. Two link-boys with pitch-torches splashed through the puddles behind him.

Augusta didn’t step back from the rail, but her bare toes curled against the wet timber. On the butcher’s tray, the remaining nails flattened themselves toward the towpath with a sound like dry peas poured into a pan.

"There she is," Scarus puffed, stopping ten paces off with his boots wide. "Get your rags wrapped, girl. The dock-ox from Ostia broke the Samnite’s collarbone in the first turn and the benches have got forty silver pieces laid on the second pair. You’re in before the tallow burns down."

"I fought the fuller’s boy at dusk," Augusta said. "One bout a night. That’s the word."

"The word is what I chalk on the door," Scarus said. "And the fuller’s boy went down so fast the wine-sellers haven’t emptied their first skin. Put the leather on your knuckles or you don’t draw a penny of the gate-share till the Kalends."

"She’s got three ribs strapped under her shirt, Scarus!" Nella yelled down from the gate. "And you laid twenty pieces against her yourself with the one-eyed barber, I got it right here on my slate!"

Scarus didn’t even look up at the beam. "Shut your trap, ditch-cricket, or I’ll have the boys grease the lock-pole. Come on, iron-head. Walk."

Augusta’s jaw set. She reached down to her belt, pulled out two long strips of stiff, sweat-blackened rawhide, and took the end of one between her teeth to wind it round her right fist. Behind her, the barge-chain in the lock-water rose another foot up the slimy wall, shivering like a snake, and the iron cleaver under the herring-barrel slid two inches out of its grease-slot. If she went into the sawdust with her left eye half-shut and her ribs bound, the dock-ox would break her back; if she let the iron fly here on the towpath, the city watch would hang her from the fish-quay crane before breakfast.

Iuno looked at the rawhide between Augusta’s teeth. Then she looked at the cudgel under Scarus’s arm.

She stepped out in front of the herring-barrels, lifted her chin so the blue cowl fell back from her hair, and folded both hands inside the wide mouths of her sleeves.

"This fighter is not walking tonight," she said.

It did not come out of her throat at all. It came up from somewhere under the flagstones—her mother Caecilia’s voice, the one Caecilia had used on the steps of the inner sanctuary when a drunken aedile tried to bring a dog through the bronze doors: unhurried, level, cold as well-water in January, dropping every vowel into the wet air as if there were forty lictors standing behind her in the dark.

Scarus’s hand stopped on his cudgel. The nearer link-boy lowered his torch so fast a gobbet of burning pitch hissed into the puddle at his feet.

"The Tetragon Temple has set her name upon the eastern roll until the new moon," the voice said out of Iuno’s mouth. "If a hand is laid upon her between this lock and the hill, the augurs will inspect the drainage of the third-district tanning-vats at sunrise, and the lime-pits will be bricked up before noon."

Nobody breathed. Even the water slapping the lock-gate seemed to miss a stroke.

Scarus stared at the pale seventeen-year-old face above the great dark folds of the sanctuary wool. He tried to look past her into the shadow of the crane-house to see who was waiting there, saw nothing but wet cobbles, and swallowed.

"Nobody told the syndics she was Temple-marked," he muttered, taking half a step back. "Keep your hands in your sleeves, Mother. There’s plenty of meat in the shed without borrowing yours."

He jerked his chin at the link-boys, spun on his heel, and stumped back toward the red glow of the lime-kilns twice as fast as he had come.

Iuno stood perfectly still until the sound of his hobnails died behind the fuller’s wall. Then both her knees gave way like wet paper, and she caught the greasy corner of the herring-barrel with two hands to keep from sitting down in the mud.

Her heart was hammering against Senator Fulvius’s tablet hard enough to crack the wax. The barge-chain dropped back into the canal with a heavy splash.

Augusta took the strip of rawhide out from between her teeth. She walked over, looked down at the crown of Iuno’s head, and nudged Iuno’s sandal with her bare big toe.

"Do that again," Augusta said.

Iuno straightened up, gulping air that tasted of fish-guts and coal-smoke, and tried to pull her shoulders back. "The—the Tetragon has spoken," she said.

Her voice cracked in the middle like a boy’s and ended in a thin squeak.

Up on the beam, Nella laughed so hard she dropped her slate-pencil into the weeds.

Augusta snorted once through her nose. Before Iuno could tuck her arms back in, Augusta reached out, caught the fullness of Iuno’s left sleeve where the stolen bakehouse cake made a warm lump against the wool, and drew the flat barley loaf out by one corner. She broke it across her thumb—it was tough enough to bend before it tore—kept the heavy half with the crust, and shoved the smaller piece back into Iuno’s fingers.

"What does the Temple want with me?" Augusta asked around a mouthful of barley.

Iuno looked down at the jagged half-cake in her hand. The water at dawn hadn’t shown her the pit, or the Senate, or the bronze chair above the gates; it had only shown a tray of rusty nails standing up in the dark because a girl with a split eyebrow was angry at a cheat.

"Nothing," Iuno said, and bit into her crust before Augusta could take that half too. "The water only showed the nails. I came down to see why they were standing on their points, and I hate watching good charcoal iron go for four asses the sack."

Augusta chewed slowly, her pale eyes fixed on Iuno’s face. Behind her on the butcher’s tray, a single bent clinch-nail rolled across the wet ash-wood, dropped over the lip, and stuck upright with a sharp *clink* against the iron buckle of Augusta’s sandal-strap.

## II
*From the third notebook of M. Valerius Moss, Fellow of the Society of Inscriptions at New Rostra, compiled during his residence in Septimont; preserved in the Society’s muniment room, press-mark VII.c.*

**14.** *On the tarred pine boarding behind the Old Lime-Pit, Third District; incised with a cobbler’s awl, four feet above the kennel.*

> AUGUSTA OF THE LOCKS
> IIII  IIII  IIII  II
> BATO FELL HERE IN THE FIRST SAND
> PAY NELLA AT THE GATE

*Note.*—The earliest epigraphic notice of the Dictatress prior to her elevation. Observe that the primitive score-marks are grouped in fours rather than the customary fives of the metropolitan amphitheatres, a survival, no doubt, of the quaternary timber-reckoning of the ancient Sabellian boat-wrights. "Nella" is evidently the *designator* or marshal of the plebeian games.

**19.** *Colosseum, Lower Arcade, Vomitorium XII; red ochre on stucco, partly defaced by wine-lees.*

> DON’T WEAR YOUR IRON BROOCH TO THE RINGS, SYRUS,
> THE LOADSTONE’S FIGHTING AT TERCE AND SHE’LL PULL YOUR BREECHES OFF.

*Note.*—A jocular allusion to the popular belief that the Champion’s shield was smeared with Magnesian ointment imported from the Levant. The commonalty of Septimont are famously credulous in matters of natural philosophy.

**23.** *Outer cedar doors of the Tetragon Temple, lower left panel; scratched around three iron tack-holes.*

> MAY THE CROWS EAT THE LIVER OF THE NIGHT-CLERK WHO NAILED THE GALLIC BOUT TO THIS DOOR BEFORE FIRST COCK. RUFUS STRABO LOST TWO MULES AND HIS WIFE’S BEDSTEAD ON THE THRACIAN.

*Note.*—This curious malediction refers to the custom of the *Sortes Nocturnae*, whereby the junior priesthood posted agricultural and meteorological warnings for the benefit of the country carters. Why the failure of a Gallic bout—presumably a drought in the cisalpine pastures—should cost a citizen his mules is obscure; perhaps a form of temple tithe.

**27.** *Colosseum, Third Tier above the Water-Gate, back of the stone bench in Row IV; cut very small and neat with a pen-knife.*

> THE MOUSE’S SEAT.
> KEEP OFF YOUR GREASY HAMS OR YOUR POT WILL BOIL DRY TIL MARTIUS.

*Note.*—Here we first encounter the enigmatic figure known to the amphitheatrical factions as *Sorex*, or "The Mouse." From the dimensions of the bench-space marked out (scarcely fourteen inches between two incised lines), I conclude that the Mouse was a celebrated dwarfish *retiarius* or net-fighter of the period, retained by the municipal aediles as a foil to the gigantic stature of the Lady Augusta.

**28.** *Beneath the foregoing, in a ruder hand, accompanied by an indecent cartoon which delicacy forbids my copying into a fair book (though it depicts a tall galeated figure holding a very small veiled figure upon the palm of one hand, while six swords fly outward from the pair).*

> LITTLE BLUE MOUSE WITH THE BIG BLUE SLEEVE,
> WHO WARMS YOUR FEET ON CORNELIUS’ EVE?
> THE LOADSTONE SLEEPS ON THE TEMPLE STAIR
> AND LEAVES HER IRON IN MOUSE’S HAIR.

*Note.*—A corrupt fragment of an archaic Fescennine wedding-catch, adapted by the rabble to mock the rivalry between the two gladiators. "Cornelius’ Eve" was the traditional feast of the fullers.

**34.** *Retaining wall of the Sanguis Road, half a mile above the Sheep-Gate; painted in whitewash with a mason’s brush.*

> THE OLD WHITE SAND WAS HONEST DIRT.
> THE RED SAND BITES THE SENATE’S SHIRT.

*Note.*—In the fourth year before the change of magistracy, the white river-gravel of the Colosseum floor was condemned by the college of augurs following a prodigy of dead crows, and replaced with dark red earth carted down from the Sanguis hills. This ritual renewal of the *harena* with martial red ochre was clearly intended to propitiate Bellona during the border dearth, though the lower orders, ever suspicious of liturgical reform, attributed the novelty to factional malice.

**39.** *Colosseum, Gladiators’ Gate (Porta Sanavivaria), scratched in the soft travertine beside the iron portcullis.*

> VALENS THE SYNDIC’S MAN WENT IN WITH LEAD IN HIS GREAVES
> AND CAME OUT FEET FIRST ON THE HURDLE.
> ASK THE RED SAND WHY HIS SWORD STUCK TO HIS BUCKLER.

*Note.*—Valens of Capua was the last champion put forward by the patrician houses prior to the Great Agon in which the Ephorate was contested. The reference to his sword sticking to his buckler is a poetical trope signifying cowardice or paralysis of the will under the gaze of the goddess.

**42.** *Triumphal Pier of Gate I, facing the Forum; cut three inches deep with a mason’s bolster on the morning after the Great Agon, and below it a second line added by night in another hand.*

> AUGUSTA ALONE BY HER OWN RIGHT HAND
>
> *WHO SAYS?*

## III
*Found wedged behind the lead overflow pipe in the dry cistern under the eastern tower, bound in split pigskin, the lower corners stiff with salt-crust. — R.M.*

*Second night of the waxing horn. Wind off the fish-quay.*

I have brought my box and father’s brass callipers down to the old salt-cistern under the east stair. Sister Porcia came into the upper basin-room twice yesterday pretending to look for the snuffers and trying to read my wax over my left shoulder. Let her come down thirty-eight broken steps in the dark if she wants to see what I am writing. The sea-grate at the north corner lets the tide in over the flags at the full and the change, cold as snow-broth, so I have set two pine planks on bricks between the ladder and the central well and hung my pattens on the wall-peg. Nobody has used this vault since the water-pipe burst in old Tullius’s time. The acoustics are marvellous. If you drop a bronze pin at the grate it rings under the dome like a hand-bell.

Cast the first ring for the outer moon-circle tonight over a charcoal pan. I used father’s wooden flask-pattern from the forge-chest and melted down three cracked sanctuary candlesticks that were due for the tinker anyway. Forgot to prick the sand-mould twice round the gate-lip before pouring, just as father always said I would when I was in a hurry, and blew a steam-blister the size of a walnut right into the third quarter. Filed it flat until both my thumbs cramped. It is ugly as a scarred kettle, but it swings true on the swivel.

*Full moon; rain.*

Augusta slept four hours on the dry limestone shelf above the high-water mark after the bout with the Bithynian. She ate the whole loaf I brought down, six salted sprats, the heel of Sister Lucilla’s goat-cheese, and the four dried figs I was saving for Sunday, and then complained that the stone was hard on her hip. Before she went back to the barracks I made her lie flat on the shelf and hold the harbour-tide ring six feet in the air with her pull while I stood on an oil-jar and drove the bronze wall-pins into the vaulting. We had a furious row because I told her to shift the ring half an inch to the left and she shifted it to *her* left, which was east toward the ladder, and nearly took my nose off with the solstice-lug. She said any smith’s girl who doesn’t know starboard from larboard ought to go back to dipping candles. Her shoulder where the Bithynian caught her with the trident-butt has gone green and yellow, though the swelling is down.

*Third night after the full; dry frost.*

Skimmed the basin four times between midnight and cockcrow. First skimming: nothing. Second skimming: a yellow cur with one ear turned inside out, shaking canal water off its back onto a butcher’s doorstep. Third skimming: two red pantiles sliding off a stable roof into a water-butt. Fourth skimming: the yellow cur again, scratching fleas. If the goddess expects me to govern the winter wheat-freight on the strength of a wet dog, she may come down and skim the cold water herself.

Paid Bona at the gladiators’ bath-house three copper asses and a pot of goose-grease for her chilblains. She is worth ten basins. She washed the linen from the Caelian training-school on Tuesday and says the big Gaul they brought from Ariminum has been spitting blood into his towel after every run, and the syndic’s factor bought two drachms of white poppy-syrup from the Syrian apothecary by the Goat-Market. Which means they mean to drug the Gaul’s posset on Friday if the betting goes toward him, or grease the planks if it goes against. Sent Nella’s youngest brother round to the tavern by the Rope-Walk to see which way Rufus Strabo is laying his silver.

*Last quarter; sleet.*

Rufus Strabo laid everything he could borrow on the Gaul to fall in the third turn. I took the lead matrix I cast from Hortensia’s great signet—mutton-tallow takes a much cleaner impression off warm silver than beeswax, whatever the locksmiths say—and sealed a strip of goat-parchment at two in the morning: *THE TETRAGON Hath Seen The Poppy In The Caelian Cup; The Gaul Stands Three Turns Or The Aediles Sit In Irons.* Nailed it to the middle panel of the front doors with a four-inch boat-spike so the carpenter will have to use pincers to get it out.

Went up to the portico at prime just to watch Rufus Strabo read it. He turned the colour of a boiled turnip and had to sell his bay mule to the sausage-maker before noon to cover his slate.

Practised the voice down here while the tide was out. The dome gives it back three heartbeats after you stop, so you can hear where your throat tightens.
~~*The Temple of the Four Corners looks upon your dealings.*~~ (Too many sibilants; sounds like a goose behind a hedge.)
~~*Beware the wrath of her that watches from the hill.*~~ (Augusta woke up on the shelf when I tried this one and asked if I had a fish-bone stuck in my gullet.)
*The Temple has spoken.*
Four words. Drop the jaw on *spo-* and don’t breathe until you’ve turned your back.

*New moon; thick fog from the marshes.*

Nella came to the cistern grate at dusk with mud up to her elbows. She says the syndics are frightened of the Friday bouts now and want to come to terms—Cotta offered her five silver denarii if the little girl in the Temple would let him know which fighter *Augusta* wants softened before the spring Agon.

I told Nella that if I ever paid a man to lie down in front of Augusta she would take the iron bar off the Temple door and break my neck with it, and she would be right. I do not touch her bouts. I only break the ones they rig before she gets into the ring.

*Fourth night of the horn; clear and cold.*

The old white river-sand in the Colosseum is three feet deep in the middle and rotten with grease, and Bona’s nephew says the carpenters have laid four leaded trap-beams over the beast-cages to catch Augusta’s heel when she circles left against Valens next month. You cannot dig up forty waggon-loads of sand by night without the watch noticing.

So I gave little Sister Tullia and the two kitchen-girls six dead crows I bought off a mole-catcher for two coppers, and had them drop them out of their sleeves from the upper awning-gallery while the augurs were taking the noon auspices in the arena. Old Metellus nearly swallowed his lituus. The whole floor is declared polluted with carrion-omen and has to be carted out to the sea-marsh before the Ides.

Hortensia let me write the warrant for the carters because she hates dirt. I put in the specification for the new gravel myself: deep quarry-grit from the red haematite beds above the Sanguis road. It is half iron-stone and heavy as lead-shot. I did not tell Augusta which quarry I wrote on the warrant.

*The Ides; bright sun, wind north.*

They finished raking the red sand before prime. When the horn blew for the morning exercises Augusta came out of the water-gate alone, walked ten paces toward the middle post, and dropped on one knee as if she were tightening the buckle of her left greave. She took off her gauntlet and laid her bare palm flat down on the red grit. Even from the third tier I could see the fine dark dust jump up between her fingers and cling to the skin like fur.

She stayed on one knee for three breaths with her hand in the dirt. Then she looked straight up into the shadow under the water-gate awning, right at the pillar where I had my feet tucked under my skirt, before she stood up and drew her sword.

*Two nights after the Ides; rain.*

She left a red winter apple on the rim of the brass basin when she went up the ladder before dawn. It has a bruise on one cheek from riding in her belt-bag behind her whetstone.

The smoke from the seven hill-lamps was yellowing the pasteboard card over my writing-desk—the one mother made me write out in big letters the week I was nine and put my hair up in the novice’s fillet: *A Priestess Must Never Fall In Love.* I have hung my greased sheepskin chart of the harbour sluice-gates over the same brass tack in front of it so the soot won’t spoil the ink.

Must send Nella to the coppersmith in the Street of the Tinkers tomorrow for three pounds of sheet-brass and a jar of strong vinegar. The salt-damp is raising green spots all over the solstice-ring, and if I don’t scrape it before the spring tide the pins will bind.

## IV
*Draft of a private epistle in the hand of Hortensia Balba, forty-first High Priestess of the Tetragon, copied into her household letter-book before her departure for the baths at Cutilia; dated the day after the Kalends of May, in the first month of the Ephorate of Augusta.*

To Iuno Caecilia, called Severa, upon her taking the Stole:

You will find the iron key with the notched bow under the salt-cellar in the vestry cupboard. It opens the barley-tithe chest and the lower muniment press. The little silver key on the red cord is for the drawer where the hot-irons and the matrix of the Great Signet are kept, though I noticed when I had the locksmith oil the wards on Tuesday that the keyhole was already well greased with mutton-tallow. If you must continue nailing proclamations to the outer doors—which I suppose you need not, now that your friend sits in the bronze chair and the syndics have all gone to live at their country farms—the carpenter begs that you will use the lower panels. Cedar of Lebanon is ruinously dear since the pirate trouble, and the middle rail is quite perforated with boat-spikes.

I had not known until yesterday at vespers that my lungs were too weak for the hill air in summer. The new Ephor’s physician was good enough to climb the stair and tell me so himself. He is a very civil young man, though he wears a camp-dagger under his gown that clanks against the stools when he sits down, and he had already hired the four-wheeled litter for my journey to Cutilia before he felt my pulse. At sixty-four one should be grateful to be spared the trouble of packing.

Do not let Senator Fabius sit on the bench nearest the brazier when the grain-levy comes up in June. He brings a cambric handkerchief soaked in onion-water specifically so that he may weep over the poverty of his tenants; wait until he has blown his nose twice, and then ask him what he received for his riverside barns from the Ostian salt-merchants. He will pay the full assessment without a third reading. As for the Cornelii, they will offer you a silver patera for the sanctuary roof before the solstice. Take it, weigh it in front of their steward so he knows you know the handles are filled with lead, and put it in the melting-tub.

I have told the porters to leave your boxes down in that dreadful salt-hole under the eastern stair. My knees have not cared for thirty-eight wet steps since the winter of the great frost, and in any case the place smells of vinegar and smith’s filings. I stopped at the iron grating in the pavement on Thursday night to drop a key-ring, and I could have sworn I heard two women’s voices rehearsing a sentence down there in the dark, though the sister at the wicket assured me the Ephor was dining with the tribunes at the barracks and you had gone down alone with one candle. The vault has a trick of throwing the voice back upon itself; old Tullius went deaf in one ear from it. You would do better to move up to the eastern tower where there is a chimney.

There is only one duty of the Stole about which I shall give you a piece of counsel you have not asked for, and that is the autumn muster at the Upper Mews.

You have been very clever with your bath-women and your lock-boys and your red sand, my dear. Do not look sour; an old woman who has skimmed the Great Basin for thirty winters knows the difference between what comes up in the water and what comes up the back stairs for two copper asses and a pot of goose-grease. Both are needful, and you have a neater hand with the grease-pot than I ever had.

Wait until October, when the wild hens are hatching on the Sanguis ledges and you have to go down into the mounting-yard at dawn with the horn of salt-oil. Because the night before the hunt is the one night in the year when the bath-women cannot help you, and the water in the basin will be quite clear. It will show you three bridles, or six, or ten, lying in the shale at the foot of the West Chimney with no heads in them. And then you must put on the clean silk and walk down the hill into the stink of wet feathers and mutton-tallow, and look a boy of nineteen between the eyes while you mark his cheek-strap for a wind you know will break his back before noon.

The first time you stand before such a bridle, you will find that your arm has turned to lead and your hand will not come up out of your sleeve.

Three breaths later, you will find that it comes up quite easily, and that your voice does not even shake when you wish him a clean sky.

It is the second discovery you ought to be afraid of.

I knew your mother, child. When the marsh-fever took her in the November of my twentieth year, I came into the novices’ dormitory after midnight with a taper and found you standing on a three-legged stool in the cold, eleven years old and no thicker than a peeling-wand, with your mouth full of brass pins, trying to hitch her temple-robe up around your ribs so you could wear it to the burning without tripping on the stairs. You would not let Sister Porcia touch the hem. Last week, when I stood behind the curtain in the Senate vestibule and heard you tell the Cornelian tribune to sit down and hold his peace, you dropped your chin on the third word in Caecilia’s exact way—except that when Caecilia used to speak so, she would come into the vestry afterwards and vomit into the wash-basin behind the screen, and you went straight on to the harbor dues.

The orange tom-cat with the chewed ear that comes up through the bakehouse drain is not entered on the Temple victualling-roll. If you give him the heads of the Friday sprats he will sleep on the wool-chests and keep the dock-rats out of the linen. I have ordered the scullions to drown him twice, and nobody in this house ever seems able to find a bucket.

*Hortensia.*

## V
*The mounting yard of the gryphon stable. She is twenty-two.*

Her right hand smelled of cold salt and brass-polish, and the pads of her four fingers were still white and puckered like boiled tripe when the acolyte put the horn cup into them.

She tucked her thumb under the rim so the boy wouldn’t see the skin. Since the third watch she had drained and refilled the cistern well until the skin of her knuckles split, turning the wet brass rings overhead through every quarter of the morning sky—first bell, second bell, the South Cleft, the Red Saddle, the sheep-track above the pines—looking for an arrangement of wind and cloud in which an ash-grey tiercel with a white primary in his left wing came back over the stable wall at dusk.

In the water of the first bell, the white squall hit the ridge while the column was still climbing through the timber, and nine empty saddles came tumbling down the scree into the river, Augusta’s great black hen dragging a broken wing behind them. In the water of the South Cleft, the rock-slide took the vanguard at noon and seven harnesses hung in the juniper bushes. In the water of the second bell, by the West Chimney, the sky stayed blue until mid-afternoon, the wild brood-hens were taken on the nest before they could rise, and three saddles came home empty across the ledgers-mules.

One of the three had a little lead eel from the Lock Three ferry stitched into the cantle-leather for luck.

"Honey-cake, Mother?" whispered little Sister Livia at her elbow, holding up the silver napkin. "You haven’t broken bread since vespers, and the fog’s raw."

Iuno looked down at the sticky square of sesame and boiled honey. Her stomach rose against her ribs so hard she had to swallow salt spit. "Take it back to the pantry, child. And keep your hem out of the mule-droppings."

The yard was forty paces square, walled with high tufa blocks that kept the dawn wind from the lower city out and the ammonia stink of sixty hunting gryphons in. Through the grey hill-fog they loomed along the iron hitching-bars like wet haystacks with beaks—mantling their barred wings, clacking their horn mandibles at the strap-boys who were rubbing hot mutton-tallow into the breast-leathers, striking sparks off the cobbles with their hind talons whenever a girth was drawn a hole too tight. All down the double line, the riders were shouting across the rumps of their mounts in voices pitched half an octave too high, banging their heavy ash lances against their greaves and laughing at nothing because their mouths were dry.

At the head of the column by the open timber gates stood Augusta’s black hen, six hands taller at the shoulder than the rest, her iron beak-guard already buckled on. Augusta stood by the stirrup-block in new hammered bronze that hadn’t taken a single talon-score yet, the Ephor’s crimson cloak pinned at her throat with a plain iron fibula.

She caught sight of the blue silk coming down the steps from the harness-arch. Her face lit all over—broad and shameless as a street-boy’s—and while Brasca had his back turned tightening her crupper-strap, she drew her chin down into her bronze gorget, puffed her cheeks out like a bull-frog, and silently mouthed *The—Tem-ple—has—spo-ken* across the steaming yard.

Iuno’s mouth twitched upward at one corner before she could stop it. The dry crack in her lower lip split and tasted of iron.

"Second bell, Mother!" Brasca called, pulling off his greasy wool cap and wiping his bald crown with a rag. "You sent word up at cockcrow to hold the vanguard for the second bell and take the West Chimney instead of the Saddle. The wind’s dead south-east on the flags."

"It will back into the north before the sun clears the tooth," she said. Her voice sounded thin to her own ears, scraped raw by the damp of the cistern. "Send the heavy lances up the West Chimney in pairs, three lengths between tail and beak. Keep the young birds on the leeward wall where the updraught off the pines will carry them."

Brasca squinted at the grey sky over the tufa parapet, spat into the straw, and nodded. "West Chimney in pairs! Hear that, you puddle-ducks? Stop rattling your spears and stand to your bridles!"

She walked down the wet cobblestones between the two rows of tails. Behind her came the senior augur with the salt-dish and little Livia carrying the spare linen towel.

At the second post stood a broad-shouldered veteran from the Gate-Ward on a piebald hen with a scarred cere. He was thirty, with three children in the weavers’ lane, and he was coming back at dusk with a wild tiercel lashed across his pack-saddle and a scratch on his thumb. She dipped her forefinger in the horn cup, drew the salt-oil down the piebald’s nose-band, and wished him a clean sky.

At the fourth post stood Rufus from the Rope-Walk, seventeen, his helmet-strap twisted under his chin because his fingers were shaking too hard to lie flat. In the basin at four in the morning she had seen his roan bird’s left wing fold back against the cliff-face in the downdraught below the cairn.

She did not look at his eyes. She looked at the brass tongue of his cheek-buckle, made the mark on the leather, said the words to his collarbone, and walked on. One.

At the fifth post, a mason’s son on a yellow-eyed haggard. Safe.

At the sixth post, Cipolla was dancing sideways on his chain, his ash-grey hackles raised like a blown umbrella, nipping furiously at his own breast-strap while Nella hung onto his bit-ring with both hands and cursed him affectionately in third-district barge-talk.

"Stand still, you great feathered cabbage, or I’ll have you stuffed with sage and onions!" Nella caught sight of the blue hem stopping by her boot and beamed, letting go with one hand to knuckle her forehead. She was nineteen this summer; the leather helmet she had inherited from old Macro was padded out with folded wool round the ears so it wouldn’t slip over her nose, and she had polished her bronze breast-plate with brick-dust until you could see the lantern-light in it.

"Look at him, Mother," Nella said proudly, dropping her voice so Brasca wouldn’t hear her calling the High Priestess like a lock-mate. "Picked him out of the third pen myself. He’s got a white feather in his near wing just like the gull that used to sit on the crane-house."

Before the augur with the salt-dish could step up, Nella’s left gauntlet darted out and pressed something small, flat, and warm from her skin into Iuno’s free palm.

"Found fat Varro behind the meal-market last night," Nella whispered, her grin splitting her freckled face. "He was running a dirt-book on how many of the canal-born drop their lances before we clear the tree-line. Took three silver pieces off him and bought this off the ferryman for your cistern."

Iuno opened her fingers an inch inside her sleeve. It was a little oval lead ferry-token from Lock Three, stamped with a curled eel on one side and rubbed bright at the edges by fifty years of wet thumbs.

She looked up from the lead token to the hollow at the base of Nella’s throat, just above the rim of the brick-polished bronze, where the wind coming round the buttress of the West Chimney two hours before noon would turn the grey bird over on its back.

Iuno raised her right hand toward the bridle.

Her arm stopped four inches from the greased leather.

It did not feel heavy; it felt as though the sinews between her shoulder and her wrist had been cut through with a cold chisel. Her fingers hung in the fog over the horn cup, trembling so violently that a grey drop of salt-oil slopped over the horn lip and splashed onto the toe of Nella’s boot. The smell of wet feathers and hot tallow rushed into the back of her mouth. If she opened her teeth to say *The wind go under your wings*, she was going to scream right in front of the augurs and tell Brasca to lock the gates.

Nella’s grin faltered. Her eyebrows drew together under the padded rim of the too-big helmet. "Iuno?" she breathed, so low the gryphon’s beak-clack covered it.

The High Priestess raised her right hand and laid two steady fingers against the cheek-strap of the bridle.

Her skin was quite cool. Not another drop spilled from the horn cup in her left palm. Looking down from a great, clean height—somewhere up near the cornice of the stable-arch, where the ammonia didn’t sting the eyes—she saw a small, grey-faced girl in a cellar-damp hem standing down by the stone mounting-block with a piece of dirty canal-lead crushed in her fist, trying not to be sick on her sandals. The High Priestess did not look at her again.

She drew the oiled thumb-mark straight and clean from the grey tiercel’s brow-band down to the horn of the cere.

"The Tetragon goes before your spear," the High Priestess said. Her voice filled the forty-foot yard from wall to wall, level and silver as a trumpet in frost, so that three stalls away the mason’s son stopped swearing at his stirrup and Brasca took his wool cap off a second time. "May the high air bear you up, and the stone lie flat beneath your talon, and the sun stand at your back until the horn brings you home."

Nella let out a long, shivering breath. The colour came back into her cheeks; she looked up at the blue veil with her eyes shining like wet slate.

"Would you—" Nella swallowed, suddenly shy in front of that voice, and touched the bird’s restless neck. "Would you bless Cipolla too, Mother? By his name? He’s only four, and he’s mortal frightened of the draught in the chimneys."

The High Priestess moved her hand three inches and laid her palm flat between the ash-grey ear-tufts. The tiercel stopped dancing. His yellow nictitating membrane slid once across his eye, and he leaned his heavy skull into the pressure of her fingers.

"Be still, Cipolla," the High Priestess said into the silence of the yard. "The wind in the West Chimney is behind your wings today."

Nella ducked her helmeted head, kissed the embroidered border of the wide blue sleeve, and swung her boot into the stirrup-loop.

"Gates!" Brasca roared, throwing his weight against the timber bar.

The horn blew three short blasts. Augusta raised her iron-shod lance at the head of the line, looked back once through the boiling steam of sixty mantling wings to salute the blue figure by the arch, and drove her heels home. The black hen leaped twice across the cobbles and took the air with a clap like a sail filling in a squall; two by two, in a hurricane of straw, dust, and loosed grey down-feathers that beat the fog into rags, the column rose over the tufa wall toward the red cliffs of the plateau.

The High Priestess stood in the empty yard until the last iron-shod tail cleared the coping-stone. She handed the horn cup to the augur without looking at him, turned on her heel, and walked out through the harness-arch to the foot of the eastern stair.

There were seventy stone steps up to the Temple terrace, cut straight into the rock between two blind walls. She climbed them at an even pace, her spine straight, her chin level, the blue silk falling in clean folds from her shoulders.

Halfway up, under the narrow slit-window where the first yellow bar of sun cut through the hill-mist, her boots stopped on the landing. Down inside her sleeve, her right hand was still shut so hard around the lead eel-token that the edge had cut the skin of her palm.

*Open your fingers, darling,* said a low, unhurried voice behind her breastbone. *You’re getting blood on the lining, and we have the harbor-syndics at tierce. Look at the step in front of you. I have the rest.*

The High Priestess opened her hand. She laid the small grey lead token on the dry stone sill of the slit-window, plucked a drifted down-feather from Cipolla’s breast off the oil on her thumb, and continued up the stair into the sun.

## VIII
*From a stitched quarto pamphlet printed at Nabon without printer’s name or licence, seized by the harbour-searchers out of a cask of dried currants aboard the snow* Halcyon *in the sixth year of Augusta; kept in the Customs Chest.*

### A TRUE RELATION OF THE BLUE HAND;
*Or, The Mirror of Septimontian Tyranny, set forth by a Gentleman of a Consular House lately resident in that City.*

Let no man in the free republics beyond the strait deceive himself that the ancient Constitution of Septimont—whereby the Magistracy of the Sword was contested in honorable arms before the Senate and the Knighthood—yet survives within her walls. I who write these lines sat upon the marble bench of my ancestors in the first podium of the Colosseum on the Nones of November last, between my uncle the former Praetor and my cousins, having come in peaceful robes to witness the afternoon pairs.

There was no herald called into the sand. There was no cedar tablet of indictment laid upon the altar of Victory, nor any freedman brought forward to swear to a single syllable of treason. At the third hour, when forty thousand of the plebs sat gorged with municipal wine under the red awnings, the woman Severa rose from the stool at the usurper’s left hand and stepped alone to the bronze rail of the podium.

She wore neither the white fillet of the ancient priestesses nor the purple border of the state augurs, but a plain gown of dark dyers’ blue, cut narrow in the sleeve, with no jewel upon her save a single crescent pin of silver in her hair. She carried no scroll. She waited until the wine-criers in the upper gallery had fallen silent—for it is a property of this creature that she never raises her voice above the pitch of a drawing-room, so that a whole amphitheatre must strain its ears like whipped hounds to catch her breath—and then she spoke two sentences.

Two sentences only, containing neither statute, nor date, nor the name of an accuser, by which a house that had given four Champions and seven Censores to the Republic before her blacksmith father had a pair of breeches to his backside was cast out into the salt water within three sunsets.

I have heard the roar of the Sanguis cataracts in flood, and I have heard the circus when two four-horse chariots lock wheels at the meta; yet neither is so terrible to the blood of a Christian or a Roman gentleman as the sound forty thousand throats make when not a man among them dares draw breath together. Below the podium, the Senate’s chief notary dropped his reed pen over the coping into the arena sand, where it stuck upright by the feather, and though he sat within an arm’s reach of the steps he did not dare go down to fetch it while she stood at the rail.

Through all this the Butcher of the Locks, whom the rabble style Ephor, sat motionless in her gilt curule chair with her mailed hands resting upon her knees, looking not once toward the bench where Marcus Silva—who had paid for the very roof-tiles of the barracks where she fed—stood white-lipped among his women. And before we had reached the outer archway of the vomitorium, two sanctuary drudges in coarse wool came down the steps, took my little niece Livia (a child of eight, scarce recovered of her winter chin-cough) by the wrists, stripped her scarlet-bordered tunic off over her ears in the public passage, and drew over her head a filthy blue novice’s sack cut for a grown woman and pinned up at the elbows with brass bodkins, leading her away up the hill while her mother was held back by the pikes of the gate-watch.

Because the vulgar in foreign ports are abused with fables of this Severa’s prophetic gifts, I set down here the certain truths concerning her person and her governance, as they are known to every man of quality who has escaped her custom-house:

1. **Of her Birth and Person.** She is no lawful daughter of the priestess Caecilia, who died childless in the marsh-plague, but a changeling bought for three measures of damaged meal out of the third-district barge-huts, where the canal-witches breed blind cats and read the drowned. Though she appears from the benches of the Senate to have the smooth skin and slender figure of four-and-twenty, my wife’s tire-woman had it from a vestry scullion that she is above sixty years of age, and preserves the counterfeit of youth by steeping herself up to the chin three nights a week in a subterranean cistern of rock-alum, vinegar, and the milk of blooded mares.
2. **Of her pretended Divination.** She has never seen so much as tomorrow’s rain in a basin. Her famous "water-sight" consists entirely of three hundred bath-house sluts, night-soil carters, chimney-sweeps, and lock-boys, whom she retains with the confiscated plate of banished Senators to bring her the key-hole sweepings of every bedchamber in the seven hills.
3. **Of her Ubiquity.** Because she has been seen on the same night inspecting the corn-tallies at the Ostian Gate and walking the parapet of the eastern tower, it is well known among the surgeons that there are in truth three of her—three dwarfish sisters from the salt-marshes, indistinguishable in feature and dyed to the same shade of silver-blue in the hair—who relieve one another beneath the veil at six-hour watches so the populace may believe their Oracle never shuts her eyes.
4. **Of the Death of Hortensia Balba.** That venerable matron did not retire to the waters of Cutilia for the catarrh, as the Gazette gave out, but died on the road at the third posting-house after eating a dish of pickled lampreys dressed in the Temple kitchen by Severa’s own hand.
5. **Of the Red Sand and the Usurper’s Sword.** As for the giantess Augusta, she is no true combatant, but a booth-juggler from the canal fairs who wears a five-pound plate of Cappadocian lodestone quilted into the leather of her breastplate. It was for this cause alone that the witch Severa procured the condemnation of the ancient white river-sand of the Colosseum and had the floor laid three feet deep with pounded red iron-stone from the Sanguis mines; by which hidden sympathy of metal the usurper’s boots are held fast against any charge, while the swords of honest challengers are drawn awry in mid-stroke.
   *[Note by the Printer at Nabon.—The Compositor sets this article down verbatim as he received it from the worthy Author’s manuscript, though the Reader is advertised that no Doctor of our Academy credits that common quarry-grit may govern the point of a broadsword at six paces.]*
6. **Of the Engine beneath the Hill.** The locked vault under the eastern stair, into which no Senator has ever been permitted to set his foot, contains no astronomical clock of brass, as the boatmen prattle, but a great siphon-engine of lead pipes connected to the upper sea-locks, whereby the High Priestess may let the harbor tide into the cellars of the third and fourth wards and drown ten thousand plebeians in their beds on the first morning they refuse her corn-laws.
7. **Of the Commerce between the Two.** Let none suppose that the Sword governs the Stole. The creature in the bronze chair is no more than a collared mastiff kept on a short chain by the canal-rat behind her shoulder. With my own eyes, coming late through the rain from the Curia in October before our ruin, I saw the pair of them standing under the windy colonnade of the Treasury when they believed the square empty: the great mailed Champion of Septimont stooping like a nurse-maid to tuck a loose corner of the Priestess’s wet wool shawl under her chin with two scarred fingers, while Severa leaned her forehead for the space of three heartbeats against the bronze boss of the usurper’s wrist-guard before turning her back and walking up the steps alone.

Yet the bitterest poison in our cup is the degeneracy of the commons. I am reliably informed by the master of the Ragusan brig who brought me my trunks that throughout this past winter—while our ancestral roofs stood empty to the owls—the two-pound loaf of bolted wheat-bread was sold at the third-district ovens for a single copper quadrans, a baseness never heard of in my grandfather’s memory, by which vile largesse of stolen corn the Blue Hand has bought the bellies of the rabble against the return of their natural lords.

Let them eat their cheap bread while the dry wind holds. Those of us who walk the foreign moles have not spent our exile among oil-factors. There are gentlemen of science in the maritime cities—poets and naturalists who have sounded the deep water beyond the Headland and know what stirs in the dark tide when the moon is low—who have lent a very courteous ear to the wrongs of House Silva. When the sea comes up the steps of the fish-quay next, three hundred laundresses will not bail it out.

## IX
*Eleven boxwood diptychs filled with blackened beeswax, strung together on a leather boot-lace; together with one broken tablet of unprepared pine. Found in the coal-scuttle beside the cistern stair. — R.M.*

**TABLET I. First Watch. To Brasca at the Upper Mews.**
**The fog on the outer bar will split for the space of twenty breaths when the second watch-horn blows from the mole, and not again until dawn. Have twelve old hens saddled with double panniers of pitch-tow and grease-pots, no lances. When the east tower shows two red lanterns, drop the fire on the seaward piles of the timber breakwater, thirty paces left of the lighthouse, where the grey water is backing up against the tide. Do not let the riders look down after they drop.**

**TABLET II. First Watch. To Mya, Champion of the Agon, at the Outer Mole.**
**Draw your third spear-line back from the boat-slip to the stone steps of the salt-warehouse. The first wave that comes over the parapet at the change of the watch will not be water only. Have the smiths heat the fish-spears in the braziers; cold iron turns on the hide. Hold the steps until the pitch-pots fall from the birds.**

*Scratched across the foot of Tablet II with a dagger-point, returned by the runner:*
Right you are, Mother. The boys had the salt-steps barricaded with herring-casks before your lad got his boots wet. The big ones with the barnacles in their teeth came up the boat-slip right on the horn and we shoved them back with the warehouse benches. Send two kegs of sour wine, a tub of vinegar, and six dry torches. The water’s black as boot-blacking and smells like my uncle’s bait-tub. — M.

**TABLET III. Second Watch. To Augusta at the Sea-Wall.**
**The left flank by the ship-sheds will hold without you. Take the thirty heavy swords from the Gate-Ward and go down to the iron boom at the harbor mouth. What is coming under the chain is coming for the middle pier. Keep your shield-rim down; they strike at the knees from the foam.**

**TABLET IV. Second Watch. To the Lock-Master of the Fourth District.**
**Knock the oak wedges out of the upper windlass and drop the great iron portcullis of the fourth sea-lock to the sill before the sand-glass turns. Drive cold spikes into the ratchet-wheel and break the hammer-shafts off short so the gate cannot be raised until the sun is three hours up.**

*Unsent scrap on a shaved splinter of boxwood, found loose in the scuttle:*
*Bona’s sister has the garret over the net-maker’s shed on the outer side of the fourth lock, and the three curing-houses are full of the night-women salting pilchards. I sent the bakehouse boy running at the first bell to tell them to come inside the gate. The mud on his knees when he came back only goes as far as the fuller’s bridge. He was afraid of the noise by the water and turned back, and the portcullis is already down.*

**TABLET V. Second Watch. To Mya at the Outer Mole.**
**Do not let the men answer what calls to them out of the surf by their mothers’ names. Stop their ears with tallow from the lantern-buckets and keep the drums beating on the warehouse roof.**

*Scratched on the margin of Tablet V, the wax smeared with black salt-slime:*
Plugged their ears with axle-grease off the timber-waggons, Mother, and broke the nose of the Samnite when he tried to climb over the wall to kiss his dead sister. Water’s up to my belt-buckle on the third step and my spear-head’s bent like a pot-hook. Tell the basin if it knows where dry land is I’d like a look at it before breakfast. — M.

**TABLET VI. Third Watch. To the Prefect of the Granaries.**
**Open the lower vaults on the Via Sacra to the women from the third district and broach the six casks of olive oil marked for the winter dole so they may keep the lamps burning. A crowd in the dark will break the gates from the inside.**

**TABLET VII. Third Watch. To Augusta at the Harbor Boom.**
**Do not look over the stone parapet into the white water. What you hear under the chain is what used to sing under the timber piles of Lock Three when you were ten years old and had had no bread since Tuesday; it does not know you unless you answer it. Turn your back on the sea and look up at the eastern tower of the hill. In three breaths there will be a white lamp in the high embrasure. Keep your eyes on the wick until the bowstring sings.**

*On the raw pine back of Tablet VIII (no wax; written in iron-gall ink with a split reed after the Fourth Watch):*

*Ninety-two steps from the cistern grating to the bell-deck of the eastern tower, and the masonry shaking under my soles like a moored barge every time the black swell hits the harbor boom. I have not strung the great horn bow since the summer before Hortensia left. Had to brace the lower tip against the stone gargoyle and throw my whole hip into the curve to get the silver loop over the nock; the cord took the skin off all three fingers of my right hand in one strip, warm and slick on the horn. Down at the harbor mouth you cannot see the mole or the lighthouse—only a half-mile of pitch-black churn and a pale, many-mouthed crest rearing thirty feet above the broken boom where Augusta’s thirty shields are backed against the capstan, and the sound coming off it like ten thousand drowned sheep bleating in one throat.*

*Drew the shaft to my ear until the lunar crest on the bow-limb bit into my cheek. The arrow went down the length of the city like a falling star over the wet roofs—three seconds of white noon across the tanneries, the locks, the warehouse tiles—and took the rearing crest straight through the throat-arch, pinning it flat against the sunken masonry of the old breakwater while the water boiled white into steam. Down on the pier I saw the red cloak leap forward off the capstan and the heavy iron ram go home twice into the pinned neck before the light died.*

*When I laid the bow down on the parapet and brought my hands into the lantern-light to tie my handkerchief round the torn fingers, the nail of my left little finger had no blood under it at all. I held it up to the wick. You can see the flame right through the horn of the nail and half an inch down the knuckle-bone, clear and hard as a chip of green river-ice. I tapped it against the stone parapet and it rang like a wine-glass.*

**TABLET IX. Fourth Watch. To the Master of the Arsenal.**
*(Delivered three minutes after the runner from the salt-warehouse came down the cistern ladder with the Champion’s buckler.)*
**The Outer Mole is clear of the sea. Send four yoke of oxen with timber-hooks and chains at first grey light to haul the jammed drift-baulks out of the boat-slip before the ebb sets hard, and let twenty lime-burners go down with quicklime for what lies on the steps. If the fishermen find the Champion’s body on the bar when the nets go out, it is to be brought to the West Postern under a sailcloth and shown to no one.**

**TABLET X. Dawn Watch. To Augusta at the Harbor Boom.**
**Wash the black salt off your cheek and your sword-arm in the horse-trough behind the customs-shed before the cellars open. Stand on the broken prow of the sea-wall above the boom where the sun strikes the stone first, and leave your helmet off so the roofs can see your hair.**

*TABLET XI. (Unsent; wax scraped clean, only the boxwood beneath scored with the point of a compass):*
*There is room on the inner harbor-ring of the brass clock, two fingers’ breadth behind where NELLA comes round under the third lamp. The metal is thicker there because of the solder-joint; the graver slipped twice before the M bit deep.*

## X
*A Masque for Candle-Light, in One Scene; from a fair copy in the author’s hand on watermarked paper of Ragusa, played once in the vaulted cistern beneath the Tetragon Temple before an audience of one, who did not applaud.*

### PERSONS OF THE MASQUE

* **THE POET**, *a traveler of some taste, newly arrived by sea.*
* **A LADY IN MOURNING**, *lately returned from foreign parts.*
* **THE CHAMPION**, *who lies upon the limestone shelf beneath a tarred sailcloth, and has no lines.*
* **THE SWORD**, *who is never seen upon the floor, being heard only through the pavement-grating overhead as the City cheers her.*
* **THE ORACLE** and **THE GIRL**: *Both to be played by a single actress, who should on no account be told beforehand which speeches belong to whom; let the audience discover it by the posture of her chin and by what she does with her hands.*

*SCENE.—A vaulted Roman cistern beneath a hill. At the centre, a round stone well-head; above it, suspended from the wet masonry by chains, an intricate clockwork of tarnished brass rings that turn very slowly by the weight of the tide. Seven small oil-lamps hang from the vault, representing the seven hills; as the brass rings revolve, the names of dead riders scratched into the metal cast thin moving shadows across the face of the actress. The floor is covered to the ankle with dark salt water. On a dry brick sits a one-eared orange cat, watching the sailcloth on the shelf.*

*At the rise of the curtain, THE LADY IN MOURNING stands by the limestone shelf, one hand resting on the wet tarpaulin. THE POET stands ankle-deep in the tide with an open notebook, admiring the brass-work overhead. The actress stands at the foot of the iron ladder, wearing a robe of deep blue silk; around the little finger of her left hand is wrapped a narrow ribbon of white kidskin.*

**THE POET** *(looking up at the rings)*.
A charming mechanism. Truly, the provinces are full of surprises. Observe, madam, how the inner circle creeps backward against the moon to mark the harbor-silt, and how every scratch in the brass—a calendar of small regrets, I take it?—throws its own little bar of night across our hostess’s forehead as it passes the wick. *(To the actress, with a bow.)* You cast the outer crescent yourself, I think? There is a flaw in the waxing horn where the sand-mould was insufficiently vented. An honest, hurried piece of smith-work. I like it much better than the smoother rings above.

**THE GIRL** *(her right hand on the iron rung of the ladder, her chin low)*.
You have ten minutes before the corporal of the hill-watch comes round to change the lock on the grating. Take your dead and your boatmen out by the sewer-arch you came through, or I will pull the bell-wire and have the pair of you nailed to the sea-wall before noon.

**THE LADY IN MOURNING.**
Pull it, blacksmith’s brat. Pull it hard enough to bring forty augurs and the Senate notaries down the ladder with their lanterns. Let them lift this tarred canvas and see what your fishermen dragged out of the mud at the harbor bar, with two broken grapnel-irons still knotted through her belt to keep her feet on the bottom.

**THE GIRL.**
Mya walked into deep water on her own two feet because the wall was gone and she would not let the surf make a puppet of her throat. That is no shame to the city. We will burn her on four cords of cedar in the Campus Martius tomorrow morning, with her bronze greaves on the pyre and the whole third district carrying torches behind her bier.

**THE POET** *(gently, closing his notebook on one finger)*.
A very pretty funeral. I can already hear the flutes. And when the fuller’s boys from the third district gather round the cedar-ash, who will explain to them why the Champion of the Great Agon had to weight her own belt on the outer steps while the Ephor’s thirty shields stood dry behind the inner boom?

**THE GIRL.**
The inner boom was the gate of the city! If the chain had parted—

**THE POET.**
Oh, the disposition of your forces was beyond praise. I watched from a hill-boat off the point with a perspective-glass; I have seldom seen a third act conducted with cleaner economy. One lock dropped at the second watch; one Champion spent at the third; one shaft of admirable stage-lightning loosed from the high tower at the fourth. You saved the walls. You always save the walls. Only—*(he steps nearer the central well, looking down at the reflection of the lamps)*—there was that awkward space of three breaths on the harbor pier before your lamp lit in the tower.

*(Through the iron grating twenty feet overhead comes a distant, rhythmic roar of forty thousand voices in the Forum: "AU-GUS-TA! AU-GUS-TA!")*

**THE POET** *(smiling upward at the grating)*.
Listen to them. They have put a gilt wreath on her helmet, and the cobblers are kissing her stirrup-leathers. What a pity it would be if some ill-natured pamphlet—printed, let us say, with the admirable types of my friend’s press at Nabon, and dropped by the hundred into the wine-shops along the locks—were to inquire why the great Sword of Septimont lowered her blade on the capstan when the sea called her by the little name her mother used before the fever.

*(The actress’s hand slips down from the iron rung of the ladder. The orange cat on the brick stands up, arches its back, and jumps down onto the dry shelf beside the corpse.)*

**THE GIRL** *(scarcely audible)*.
She did not answer it. She looked at the tower.

**THE POET.**
She looked at the tower because you told her to look at the tower. Before your runner reached the pier, she had one boot on the coping-stone and her ear turned to the foam. She has heard that music in the canal-water since she was nine years old and dredging rusty nails out of the mud to buy barley-crusts. You know it; I know it; and this quiet lady under the sailcloth knew it when the Samnite tried to climb the parapet beside her. How long do you suppose the seven hills will let their idol sit in the bronze chair if they learn that the Tide already has her name in its mouth, and that the next time the sea rises, their Champion may open the water-gate from the inside?

*(Silence in the vault. Overhead, a brass ring clicks on its pin; the shadow of the letters M-Y-A slides across the actress’s cheek and down onto her throat. Slowly, the actress draws her left hand—the one with the white kidskin ribbon round the little finger—into the fold of her silk sash, and lifts her chin half an inch. When she speaks, her voice has lost its hurry; it is level, musical, and three winters older.)*

**THE ORACLE.**
You did not row a dead gladiator through four furlongs of sewer-culvert to lecture me upon municipal acoustics, Master Cristoforo. What do your friends require?

**THE POET** *(delighted, murmuring to himself as he makes a rapid pencil-mark in the margin of his playbook)*.
*Exquisite.* Not a step taken upon the boards, only the left elbow tucked against the rib and the vowel dropped into the chest. Madam, if you ever weary of the priesthood, I have a theatre in Ragusa that would kill for your transitions.

**THE LADY IN MOURNING.**
Give me my child out of the dormitory first.

**THE ORACLE** *(without turning her head toward the Lady)*.
Sister Livia is thirteen this November. She skims the third basin very neatly, has forgotten how to cry when the wind blows from the sea, and tells me she prefers boiled lentils to her grandmother’s orchard. If I sent her down the ladder in her blue wool, she would bite your hand before you reached the boat. *(To THE POET.)* The terms.

**THE POET.**
Modest ones, for a connoisseur. First: this silent person under the canvas does not go to the cedar-pyre in the Campus. A frame of bone and sinew that the Tide desired so ardently—and which pickled itself intact in deep brine before the rot could touch it—is an instrument of great delicacy. My colleagues have a use for her. You will enter her in the Temple rolls as swept out to sea beyond recovery.

**THE ORACLE.**
And the second?

**THE POET.**
Beneath the third arch of the Claudian Aqueduct, where the old fuller’s drain was bricked up after the Silva forfeiture, there is a dry vaulted chamber forty feet square with an iron sluice-gate opening on the subterranean canal. The water-wardens do not go there because the Temple declared the ground cursed four winters ago. You will leave it cursed. You will strike the arch from the surveyor’s map in the muniment chest; you will see that no night-watchman carries a lantern within two hundred paces of the lower grate; and when my barges come up the culvert at the dark of the moon with glass-ware and lime-carboys, the Lock-Master of the Fourth District will be asleep in his chair.

**THE ORACLE.**
In return for which?

**THE POET.**
In return for which, Domina Julia Silva returns to her pleasant villa at Nabon and burns her brother-in-law’s stock of pamphlets. No word of what sings in the Ephor’s ears during a sea-fog ever reaches the barber-shops. The bronze statue in the Forum keeps its head on its shoulders; the cheap bread bakes in the third-district ovens; and the city continues to adore its Sword for another ten years—or twenty—until the water comes up the stair again.

**THE LADY IN MOURNING** *(fiercely, gripping the edge of the sailcloth)*.
You swore to me at Ragusa that the butcher would come down within the year!

**THE POET.**
My dear lady, a tragedy that ends in the first act is a tavern brawl. Let the fruit ripen on the wall; it will fall into our lap of its own weight. *(To the actress, holding out a strip of lead foil and a brass stylus.)* A single line on the surveyor’s warrant to close the third aqueduct walk for repairs to the masonry. In your own admirable hand.

*(The actress does not move for the space of five heartbeats. Her eyes go once—and only once—to the wall above the writing-desk, where a greasy sheepskin chart of the harbor gates hangs over a small square of yellowed pasteboard. Then she steps through the ankle-deep water, takes the stylus in her right hand, lays the lead strip upon the rim of the central basin, and writes without a tremor.)*

**THE ORACLE.**
The walk beneath the third arch is closed from the Kalends. If a single fisherman of the third district disappears into your culvert, or if one drop of what you brew under the bricks leaks into the drinking-conduit of the lower wards, I will bring the aqueduct down on your heads with thirty sledges and tell the tribes the Silvas poisoned the wells. Take the canvas off that shelf before the tide turns. I need the stone dry for the morning skimming.

**THE POET** *(taking the lead strip and drying it with sand from his pocket-box)*.
A pleasure to transact business with an artist who understands the unities. Come, Domina; my boatmen are waiting at the grating with the litter-poles.

*(He whistles twice toward the dark arch of the sewer-culvert. Two mute boatmen in oiled leather enter through the water and take the corners of the tarred sailcloth. As they lift the burden from the limestone shelf, a bare brown ankle, stiff and crusted with dry white salt, slips out from beneath the hem of the canvas and knocks against the stone well-head.)*

**THE GIRL** *(taking half a step forward, her right hand flying out of her sleeve)*.
Mind her feet—! Tuck the cloth under her heel, the stone’s rough—

*(She stops dead. Her left hand, hidden inside the blue sash, presses hard against her ribs. THE ORACLE watches the boatmen carry the sailcloth out into the dark culvert without another word. THE LADY IN MOURNING follows them. THE POET pauses under the arch, looks back at the small figure standing alone beneath the turning brass rings, and removes his hat.)*

*(Above the vault, the distant cheering of the City swells to a thunderous crescendo as THE SWORD passes the portico of the Senate, and three drops of mortar-dust fall from the shaking roof into the basin.)*

**CURTAIN.**

## XII
*The roof of the Tetragon Temple at dusk. She is twenty-nine.*

Getting her left boot over the lead coping of the trapdoor hurt worse than the spear-butt that had done the mischief, and Augusta swore under her breath as she hauled her knee up onto the warm tiles.

"Old horse," she told her own hip, prodding the thick linen bandage under her kilt where the Fabianum chieftain’s ash-shaft had caught her in the mud three days ago. The camp bone-setter had pulled the joint back into the socket with a block and tackle tied to a baggage-wagon and told her cheerfully that she would know when it was going to rain in the south before any augur in Septimont for the rest of her days.

She walked six stiff paces across the gently sloping lead of the western roof and sat down with her back against the warm tufa chimney-stack of the vestry bakehouse, stretching her bad leg out straight in front of her. From inside the breast of her unbuckled cuirass she pulled a twisted cone of coarse brown grocer’s paper, spilled five small, lopsided red orchard apples into her lap, and looked down over the parapet at the Forum three hundred feet below.

They had taken the tarpaulin off the new bronze statue in front of the Rostra that afternoon while she rode in by the North Gate. Even from up here on the hill, with the purple October river-mist rising out of the third district, she could see the torch-bearers gathered round the plinth to admire the twenty-foot monster the Senate’s Greek sculptor had made of her—one colossal bronze greave planted squarely on the neck of a dead sea-serpent, the sword raised toward the harbor, and a square, jutting patrician chin that looked like a milestone with nostrils.

It didn’t even have the crook in the bridge of the nose where old Bato had head-butted her in the lime-pit when she was eighteen.

Behind her, the iron latch of the roof-trap clicked.

Augusta didn’t turn her head; she knew the step—light, even, accompanied by the faint, musical *tink* of something hard brushing the stone jamb that she always assumed was the silver crescent-chain at the High Priestess’s girdle.

"You’re late," Augusta said, picking a dry leaf off the stem of the fattest apple. "The augurs went down to supper half an hour ago, and your sculptor doesn’t know what a woman’s jaw looks like. He’s given me Senator Fabius’s jowls."

Iuno came across the lead tiles without a sound and settled herself on the low stone coping opposite the chimney, drawing her knees up and smoothing the dark blue silk over her ankles with her right hand. Her left arm was tucked lightly inside the broad fold of her sash against her waist, out of the evening wind, as she had worn it since the winter of the big tide. The silk was new this month, heavy Sidonian stuff the colour of deep water under a boat-keel, and it fitted her narrow shoulders without a crease or a brass pin anywhere.

"The sculptor is from Corinth," Iuno said mildly, leaning her temple against the warm brick of the chimney-stack. "In Corinth they believe nobody can govern a city without a chin you could split firewood on. I had to strike three cherubs with trumpets off the plinth before he cast it, or you would have had a bronze baby sitting on your left knee."

Augusta gave a short bark of laughter and winced as her hip twitched. "I rode sixty miles from the Fabianum ford in two days to get back before they pulled the sheet off, and I missed the speeches by an hour."

"Which is why you rode slowly between the seventh milestone and the Sheep-Gate," Iuno said. Her eyes—tired around the lower lids in the red dusk, though her mouth was easy—dropped to the way Augusta’s left foot lay turned outward on the lead. "Did the bone-setter use wine-lees on the poultice or only hot vinegar?"

Augusta frowned, her hand stopping on the apple. "The messenger from the vanguard didn’t reach the North Gate until noon today. I passed him at the tavern in Varia."

"The vanguard messenger rode the high road," Iuno said. "The cattle-drover who sold thirty hornless bullocks to the Fabianum chief’s brother-in-law came in with the salt-carts on Thursday night, and he had a boy with him who saw you take the ford in the rain. You shouldn’t have charged the left bank where the sheep-dip drains into the clay, Augusta. The footing is rotten for fifty yards below the willow-trees; any shepherd’s dog could have told you the horses would slip."

A small, hot prickle of irritation rose under Augusta’s breastbone—the same itch that had made her saddle the cavalry three days ago before the Temple courier could climb the barracks stair. She had ridden sixty miles north into a soaking autumn drizzle specifically to fight a man on four inches of ordinary, unblessed cow-mud that nobody had salted with haematite grit and nobody had skimmed in a brass pan. And she had broken his line in the willow-scrub with her own shield-boss and come up the sixty steps of this roof with her hip on fire just to say she had done it.

She looked across the chimney-stack at the composed, silver-blue head resting against the brick. Where had the skinny little tyrant with the ink-stained knuckles gone—the one who used to hop on one sandal in the cistern when Augusta got the price of charcoal wrong?

"We took forty mountain pikes off their wagon-guard," Augusta said deliberately, reaching for the old bait. "Good northern ash with socketed heads. I told the quartermaster to pay the hill-smiths at Varia ten silver denarii the dozen to replace our broken lances. Dirt cheap for charcoal steel."

Ten years ago, that would have brought the Mouse three inches off her stool with her ears pink, ready to lecture her for ten minutes on the difference between welded bog-iron and cold-hammered rod and call her an ignorant canal-dredger who shouldn’t be trusted with a bronze quadrans.

Iuno didn’t even lift her head from the brick. She smiled—warm, indulgent, impeccably polite.

"Have the quartermaster draw the warrant on the Ostian iron-customs instead of the military chest," she said smoothly. "The guild-charter at Varia was revised in the spring sitting; if you buy socketed heads under the municipal seal, the hill-towns remit the third part of the charcoal duty against their winter road-tax. I’ll have Sister Livia send the clerk down with the tally-sticks after prime."

Augusta stared at her for a second. Then she looked down into her own lap.

In her broad, calloused left palm lay a single gilded-copper laurel leaf she had snapped off the Senate’s triumphal wreath in the vestibule because the wire was scratching her neck. While she had been sitting here talking, without her feeling a thing, the soft copper leaf had curled itself tight around the side of her thumb, winding its gilded point round and round into a hard little metal rosebud.

She pried it off her thumb with her teeth and tossed it into the gutter-lead.

"Do you never get sick of it, Mouse?" Augusta asked, quieter now, shifting her stiff leg an inch toward the warmth of the flue.

"Of the Varia road-tax?"

"Of knowing what every mule-driver between here and the border had in his nose-bag before he left the stable." Augusta reached out with the toe of her sound boot and nudged the hem of the Sidonian silk where it lay over Iuno’s sandal. "Take the silver pins out of your hair for an hour. We could go down the Grease-Stair after the watch changes and buy a twist of fried smelts off the girl at Lock Three. Nobody looks at two women in wool cloaks after the lamps are out."

Iuno looked out over the parapet toward the western hills, where the red line of the sun was going down behind the arches of the Claudian Aqueduct. The smoke from the third-district ovens hung flat and blue over the canals.

"The girl who used to go down the Grease-Stair for fried smelts hasn’t come up the ladder since the winter of the high tide, Augusta," she said. Her voice was quite conversational, light and dry as a dead leaf sliding across slate. "She stays down by the water-grate where the rings go round and keeps the accounts. She sends me up to the roof instead, because my gown fits at the shoulders and I have much better manners when you boast about charging through a sheep-dip."

Augusta snorted, taking it for one of her court drynesses, and shook her head. "Your manners were rotten at seventeen and they’re worse now. At least when you were seventeen you didn’t talk like a tax-collector."

She picked up the smallest, most lopsided apple from the brown paper in her lap and tossed it underhand across the chimney-stack.

Iuno’s right hand flashed out of her lap and caught it smack in the palm before it could touch the silk.

Then—without looking to see if any acolyte was watching from the bell-tower—she rubbed the dusty red skin of the apple once, hard, up and down against her hip on the priceless Sidonian gown, bit straight into the bruised side with a sharp *crack* of her front teeth, and spat a brown pip cleanly over the stone parapet, hitting a fat temple pigeon squarely on the rump as it waddled along the augurs’ cornice ten feet below.

The pigeon exploded into the dusk with an outraged squawk.

Augusta’s mouth fell open; then she threw her head back against the warm bricks and laughed until her wrenched hip stabbed her through the ribs.

She picked up the big apple with the dry leaf on the stem, bit a chunk out of it, and chewed contentedly in the gathering dark while Iuno sat opposite her with her knees drawn up, turning the bitten apple slowly in her right hand and her left tucked deep inside the silk fold at her waist, watching the harbor lamps come on one by one along the water.

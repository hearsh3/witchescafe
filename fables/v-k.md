At twenty minutes to four in the morning the quill at the turn of the cellar stair changed its gait.

For two months Phrolova had used Alice's pen as a nocturnal metronome. Down in the limestone hollow beneath the roots of the third Solistree, where the dark pool lay under its slow-turning gyre of frequencies, the girl's iron-gall nib went *scritch, scritch, rest* across the folio at sixty-four to the minute—the steady, unhurried pace of a clerk copying out three-thousand-year-old tide-marks while the rest of the house slept. When the nib paused on the third beat, Alice was dipping; when the pause ran to eight bars, she was paring down a split point with her penknife.

Tonight the three-beat rest vanished. The steel point tore across the rag-paper in a single, cramped burst of sixteenth-notes, stopped dead with a dry blot against the vellum, and then Alice's boots came pelting up the cut-stone spiral two steps at a time.

Phrolova, lying awake on her cord-strung bed under the eaves with her hands folded over the lavender-dried sheet, did not move her head on the bolster. She listened as the cellar door clicked open below, heard the iron poker stir the banked turf in the kitchen grate to get a light for the tallow dip, and then the rapid scrape of a dry nib as the girl wrote out her fair copy on the oak table before the ink in her head could cool.

When Phrolova came down the ladder at seven, dressed and pinned to the chin, the kitchen smelled of singed toast and birch-smoke, and Alice was already gone back down the stair. Only her fair-copy sheet lay in the middle of the scrubbed deal between the butter-dish and the salt-cellar, weighted flat at the corners with two horn spoons.

Herta was perched on her two thick volumes of maritime law at the head of the board, her enormous black hat pushed back three inches from her forehead and three separate ink-bottles—black, red, and violet—uncorked beside her saucer.

"Look at the labial," Herta was saying to nobody in particular, her steel nib hovering like a wasp over the middle of the sheet. "She's bracketed three variants on the main predicate because the water muffled the dental stop, which is what happens when you employ a Guild copyist who takes dictation by ear instead of hanging a hydrophone in the basin."

Phrolova walked to the foot of the table, rested her bare hands on the top rail of her chair, and read the sheet upside down.

It was written in Alice's upright, slightly harried copperplate, the downstrokes still glittering with black sand from the pounce-box:

*16 Nov., fourth watch (03:38). Low register, through two fathoms; unvoiced dental indistinct against the stone:*

*Something has [risen / woken / come up] in the [deep / sea / nothing].*

*[Note: The verb root is the older intransitive inflection used of winter spring-tides in the coast-songs, not the common waking-verb; the final substantive ends on an open aspirate without a case-vowel. The water turned over once from the bottom shelf and went flat. — A.]*

Across the margins, Herta had already laid down a barrage of violet cross-references and red query-marks, bracketing *nothing* twice and appending a cramped footnote about post-Lament privative nouns.

"If the root is the coastal tide-verb," Herta declared, tapping the first bracket with the butt of her pen, "then *woken* is philologically absurd. A tide doesn't wake; it displaces. And an intransitive displacement verb cannot govern *nothing* as a locative object without an archaic prepositional prefix, which Alice would have heard even through twelve feet of marl."

By the hearth, Viviane had her booted ankle propped on the iron fender and a brass calliper in her lap. She was wiping down the inside of a steam-valve housing with a twist of oiled tow while wearing Phrolova's dark Ragunnan kidskin gloves—both thumbs now permanently shiny from two months of tallow and brass filings.

"It's the tide-verb," Viviane said without looking up from the valve. "From the old shoal-books down the gulf. When the bottom water comes up under the hull in November, the pilots don't put a preposition in front of it. That settles that."

"A pilot's colloquialism is not a citation," Herta snapped.

Above the sideboard, slung by one knee in her crimson silk ropes where the pale roots crossed the rafters, Black Swan reached down with two long fingers, drew a single tarnished-gilt card from the bottom of her pack, and turned it toward her own face in the smoky F-sharp draft near the beams. She studied it for two slow bars, her dark hair trailing an inch above the tallow-dips, and slid it into her sleeve without turning it round.

"The grammar is quite plain, my dear," Black Swan murmured from the ceiling. "The house downstairs has a draught under the floorboards."

Elaina came out of the buttery with her flat wicker basket already hooked over her left forearm and a clean white linen napkin folded across her palm.

"Will it be cold out on the Dimmr?" Elaina asked cheerfully, setting six warm purple figs onto the wooden trencher in the center of the board before tucking a loaf of barley-bread into the basket. "The wind was coming off the glacier when I went to the woodshed at six. If we're staying the week by the new water, we'll want the heavy kettle with the copper bottom—the tin one takes forever when the air's thin."

Phrolova pulled her chair out and sat down.

Her hand paused half an inch from the horn spoon.

*The new water on the Dimmr.*

Alice's sheet said *deep*, or *sea*, or *nothing*. There was no mention of a plain, or a glacier, or any basin within forty leagues of the Solistrees. Yet Elaina was already packing salt-fish and butter-crocks into a travelling basket for the Dimmr Plains as calmly as if a parish clerk had nailed the address to the larder door at Michaelmas, and neither Herta nor Viviane so much as blinked at the name.

In the first fortnight Phrolova had wasted breath asking how a household that never opened a post-bag knew which road had washed out three counties away. Now she simply picked up her spoon and filed the omission away in the cold, quiet room behind her ribs where she kept her score of them.

Nobody looked down the table at her place. Nobody asked whether she was coming, or told her to pack her cloak, or suggested she stay behind to mind the kettle with Alice.

"If we are taking the copper kettle," Herta announced, climbing down from her law volumes and slamming her three ink-bottles into their leather travelling-case with a sharp *clack*, "Viviane carries it. I have four quartos of the *Hydrologia* and the brass goniometer, and I am not throwing my spine out of alignment for kitchenware."

"Hang it off the saw-guard," Viviane said, standing up and slinging her six-foot buzzsaw staff across her shoulder by its tarred leather strap.

Phrolova buttered a corner of barley-bread, ate it, and drank half a cup of tea.

She had no business on the Dimmr Plains. She had left the Fractsidus in the autumn; whatever rifts or sinkholes were opening in the northern rock belonged to the Grand Architect's clerks or the gold-eyed meddler's noisy retinue. She was entirely free to stay here in the warm root-hall for seven days with the oak table to herself, practicing her D-minor variations after ten o'clock without Herta banging on the ceiling-beams with a broomstick.

Yet her eyes went back to the middle line of Alice's sheet.

*Something has come up in the deep.*

Things that came up out of dark water had been Phrolova's trade for four hundred years. And since the night she walked out of Stellavento's square and left the tarnished silver badges of her own dead lying in ranks on the jetty planks, they had been coming up every week—four more since November began, climbing the stone steps from the forty-fathom mirror with the black sea-water dripping from their hems to sit round Triss's brazier and eat barley stew out of blue earthenware bowls.

When water turned over from the bottom shelf, Phrolova preferred to hear what key it was singing in before anyone else laid hands on it.

She stood up from the table, left her napkin by the plate, and went up the attic ladder to the small square room in the fork of the pale roots.

Before she lifted her cloak from the peg or unlatched the Guarneri case from the foot of the bed, she stood still by the narrow casement for ten bars and let her hearing drop southward across three hundred miles of pine and through the unmapped seam into Stellavento.

It was early afternoon in the valley—three hours ahead by the sun she had pitched for it. Down in the square, the washhouse copper was humming at a low, bubbling B-flat; Granny Leah's four steel needles were going *click-click-slip* on the bench by the bakehouse door; and in the front parlor of Phrolova's own house, the oil lamp sat clean and cold on the window-sill with its square-trimmed wick waiting for dusk, when Luc would come across from the woodpile and strike a match with his wet left hand.

The house there was kept.

Phrolova picked up her violin case by the leather handle, threw her dark woollen cloak over her shoulders, and went down the ladder.

---

They did not travel together, because traveling together would have required agreeing on a road, and the Banat al-Rih could not agree on how to cross a threshold without three separate methodologies.

Outside the root-arch in the knee-deep snow, Herta spent five minutes pinning her enormous black hat to her braids with four steel bodkins, slung two bursting leather satchels over her shoulders until she looked like a bad-tempered pack-pony, and planted her staff horizontally in the drift.

"The direct bearing across the upper ridge is thirty-eight kilometres," Herta informed the trees, climbing onto the ash shaft and arranging her black skirts over her ankles with fierce dignity. "If you keep below the cloud-base to avoid the glacier shear, you lose twelve minutes on the tack. Try not to dawdle."

She rose six feet into the freezing air, wobbled once as the brass goniometer in her left bag swung against her shin, corrected her trim with a furious snap of her chin, and shot off between the bone-white trunks like a very small, black-hatted cannonball.

Phrolova looked at the empty snow where the staff had been. She had lived for four centuries among heretics, Threnodian-cultists, and immortal playwrights, and she had never seen anyone actually sit on a stick and fly away on it like a woodcut in a two-penny chapbook.

Behind the woodshed, where a black peat-hole broke the crust of the bog, the water gave a heavy, grease-thick heave. The pale, translucent bell of Viviane's monstrous jellyfish rose three feet clear of the sedge, trailing its mauve ribbons into the dark below.

Viviane stepped onto the crown of the bell with the copper kettle hooked over the teeth of her buzzsaw and touched two gloved fingers to her tricorn hat.

"Room on the lee side if you don't mind the brine," she told Phrolova. "She makes good headway once we hit the deep marl under the glacier."

"I have two feet," Phrolova said coldly.

"Suit yourself. Mind the shale on the south cut." The jellyfish let go of the bog-rim and dropped straight down through the peat-water like a greased caisson, taking the tricorn and the copper kettle out of sight without leaving a bubble on the scum.

Phrolova turned toward the track—and found Black Swan already gone, only a single crimson silk cord drawing itself lazily back up into the high amber canopy overhead like a snake into thatch, while twenty yards down the deer-path between two frosted alder bushes, where there was no road and no gate, Elaina was walking steadily away into a grey fold of air with her wicker basket on her arm, humming a three-four country reel until the alders met behind her shawl and there was nobody between the trunks at all.

Phrolova stood alone in the snow under the third Solistree.

She was not riding a jellyfish, and she was certainly not following a nineteen-year-old through an alder bush. She lifted her bare right hand to the level of her collarbone, hooked two fingers into the freezing air at A-flat below the staff, and tore the seam open the way she had torn it for four hundred years—a six-foot vertical rip of red-black dissonance that smelled of pond-silt and scorched copper.

She stepped through, let the air snap shut behind her heel, and came out on the high southeastern ridge above the Dimmr Plains.

Elaina was already standing ten yards down the slope on a shelf of fractured black slate, smoothing her dove-grey shawl with both hands, while Herta sat on a boulder beside her, furiously knocking frost-crystals out of her hat-brim with a pocket handkerchief.

"You're late," Herta said, squinting up the scree at Phrolova. "And that A-flat is forty cents sharp. You rattled my goniometer from half a mile off."

Phrolova did not dignify that with a glance. She stopped at the crest of the ridge, her violin case in her left hand, and let her ear take the pitch of the plain.

It was an ugly score.

Out in the middle of the vast, sunken bowl, four kilometres north-northwest through the mist, stood the colossal petrified bulk of the Solvein Heartwood. High in its bleached fork burned a single milky eye, dimmed for the winter season, and up through the soles of Phrolova's boots came the tree's fundamental bass: eleven slow beats and a dead bar of rest, *one, two, three... eleven, rest*, repeating across the fractured slate like a church organ with a lead weight jammed on the pedal. Around the shelves, a low lavender mist rose out of the chasms and hung in the minus-ten air, humming a sour, unresolved eleventh that set the back teeth on edge.

And directly below the southeastern spur, sixty metres down the forty-degree talus of greywacke and shale, lay the new water.

It was not a natural tarn. A borehole had collapsed and taken half a boundary dyke with it, leaving a broad, round basin the better part of half a kilometre across, filled to the gravel lip with water as black and flat as poured pitch. Though a bitter draft was coming down off the northern glacier, not a ripple stirred the surface.

Phrolova let her hearing drop down the bank into the black mirror.

The dead were thick in it.

They were not the red, saw-toothed Tacet frequencies she had conducted in Rinascita or at the Septimont rift; this was old drift, thousands of human remnants sanded down by the dark until only a single stubborn bar of their last thought remained, circling round and round the bottom of the bowl like dry leaves in an eddy.

*—sixteen minutes from home—*

*—the readings were good all the way down—*

*—I left the stove—*

Phrolova listened to them for three bars, her face composed and entirely unmoved. It was louder here than in most places because the borehole walls boxed the resonance in, like an orchestra pit dug too deep for the stage, but it was nothing she hadn't walked through a hundred times. Common sediment. Every ruin on the continent had a cellar full of broken gramophones repeating their last groove to the dark.

"Is this what your sleeper woke the house for?" Phrolova asked, looking down the shingle at Viviane, who was standing ankle-deep at the water's edge with the copper kettle in one hand. "A sinkhole full of old static?"

Viviane didn't look round. She reversed her six-foot staff and rapped the iron ferrule twice against the flat surface of the black water. Instead of splashing, the water gave back a dull, heavy *thock*, like a mallet hitting an oak cask full of brine.

"Static's just the weeds on the roof," Viviane said, testing the surface tension with the toe of her boot. "Plenty of head-room under the marl. Come on, the wind's got sleet in it."

Elaina went in first.

She didn't wade, and she didn't hold her skirts up. With the wicker basket hooked neatly over her forearm, she stepped off the frosted shingle onto the black mirror as if she were descending a flight of scrubbed cellar stairs into a buttery. At the third step the dark water took her ankles without wetting the wool of her stockings; at the sixth it closed smoothly over her grey shawl; at the eighth the crown of her braided head went under without leaving a single ring on the glass.

Viviane hitched the buzzsaw higher on her shoulder and walked down after her, vanishing to the tricorn brim in four strides.

Herta hoisted both satchels onto her head with a martyred sigh—"If the damp gets into the third volume of the *Nautica*, I am tearing pages out of the household book to line the press"—and marched straight down into the black until only her enormous hat-brim floated on the surface for half a beat and then went under too.

Twenty yards out toward the middle of the basin, a single crimson silk rope-end flicked once above the mirror like the tail of a trout and drew itself down into the dark. Black Swan hadn't even used the shore.

Phrolova stood alone on the frozen gravel at the water's edge.

The last time she had walked down stone steps into black water, there had been a sword-rent through her bodice and thirty thousand unconducted frequencies tearing at the sky behind her back, and she had gone down forty fathoms with her eyes open expecting the silt to be the end of the score.

Her bare right hand tightened around the leather handle of the Guarneri case. She set her jaw, stepped off the shingle onto the invisible tread beneath the surface, and walked down into the dark water on her own two feet.

Thirty feet down, the water stopped at a bark door that had no tree around it, and inside the door the kettle was already on.

If Phrolova had still been looking for proof that the Banat al-Rih were creatures out of Granny Leah's hearth-tales rather than women with ribs and lungs, that week under the southeastern spur would have settled the question.

The house hung suspended in the middle of the basin's depth, moored to nothing that Phrolova's ear could find, yet the floorboards did not rock by a hair's breadth. It was the exact same long, low-beamed kitchen from under the third Solistree—or else a twin built by a carpenter who only knew one set of measurements—smelling of browned butter, old beeswax, and Herta's damp calfskin bindings. Outside the small square casements, instead of snow-laden boughs, the black water of the lake stood vertical and motionless against the panes like a wall of polished jet.

Nobody conducted a search of the lake. Nobody held a council over Alice's sheet, or lowered an instrument into the dark, or explained what they were waiting for. They treated a drowned sinkhole on the Dimmr Plains as if they had come down to the coast for a week's sea-bathing in August.

On the second morning Herta unpacked a three-foot brass goniometer onto the end of the dinner table and spent forty-eight hours measuring the angle of total internal reflection through the pantry window, covering six sheets of Ragunnan foolscap with violet tangents nobody had asked for. Every three hours, when she thought Phrolova was looking at her score-folios and Viviane was under the sink, Herta would reach surreptitiously into her second leather satchel—the one she kept buckled under her chair with three air-holes punched through the flap—and slip a crust of buttered barley-bread inside.

At supper on Wednesday, the satchel gave a sharp, high-pitched *wheep* at the pitch of D above high C and bounced an inch off the floorboards.

Everyone at the board stopped chewing.

Herta did not move a muscle of her face. She kept her hand flat on her open volume of the *Hydrologia* and stared coldly down her nose at Viviane.

"That was my boot-leather," Herta said. "The humidity in this basin is shrinking the welt."

"Your boot-leather's got grey fur on its ears," Viviane remarked, paring a wedge of salt cheese with her pocket-knife, "and it chewed the corner off my oil-rag this morning. Keep it out of the oakum bucket, those little rock-pikas eat tarred hemp and get the colic."

"It is a specimen of *Ochotona alpina* exhibiting anomalous subterranean wintering behavior," Herta said furiously, going pink to the roots of her hair under the huge hat-brim, "and I am observing its metabolic rate."

Phrolova looked down into her teacup so nobody would see the corner of her mouth move. It was intolerable. She had come down into this basin braced for an ambush or a necromantic working, and she was trapped in a diving-bell with a fourteen-year-old smuggling mountain rodents in a book-bag.

Viviane spent the week flat on her back beneath the buttery hatch with a wrench, a caulking iron, and a tallow dip, taking apart the ancient frequency-baffles that kept forty fathoms of water off the chimney-flue and re-packing the seams with tarred hemp because she disapproved of how the joints had been bevelled. Black Swan slept four hours every afternoon in a web of crimson silk ropes slung from the empty air two feet below the rafters—no hooks, no eyebolts, the knots simply biting into nothing at all—turning slowly on one hip above the sideboard whenever the heat from the stove rose.

And Elaina went up and down the cellar-step threshold to the world above as easily as a housewife stepping out to the pump.

On the first Thursday morning, while Phrolova was scraping a rough spot off her spare bridge by the settle, the bark door swung inward and Elaina stepped down out of the water with her cheeks rosy from the minus-ten air and her wicker basket dragging at her forearm.

"The fish-market on the fifth tier had the fresh figs in from the south terraces," Elaina said happily, setting the basket on the side counter by the door and lifting out a heavy brown double-bag that rattled like gravel down a tin chute. "And I found the boiled sweets."

She fetched the wide cut-glass preserve dish from the dresser shelf, set it on the corner of the counter right beside the threshold latch, and tipped the brown sack up.

Nearly two kilos of pale, sugar-frosted yellow-green ovals poured into the glass bowl in a high, dusty cone that smelled sharply of crushed lemon rind and apothecary acid.

Herta looked up from her goniometer, wrinkling her nose. "Why did you buy four pounds of citric acid?"

"The man at the stall said nobody buys less than the jar if they mean business," Elaina said, dusting the white sugar-powder off her palms with neat, brisk taps. "They're the double-sour ones from the top shelf, by the ginger drops. A very obliging girl at the counter tested one for me before I paid—her left eye squeezed shut three seconds before the right one, so I knew they were the exact sort we wanted. They're for someone at home, and she's going to be very rude about them."

Herta's eyes moved immediately across the oak table and fixed on Phrolova.

It was completely plain what Herta was thinking: nobody in this house had a tongue sour enough to warrant two kilos of unbuffered tartaric acid except the former Fractsidus Overseer sitting by the fire with a knife in her hand.

Phrolova looked back at Herta's ink-smudged thumb and assumed with equal certainty that Elaina had bought them to stop the provost lecturing at breakfast.

After the plates were cleared at noon, Herta wandered casually past the side counter with a volume of maritime law under her arm and reached two fingers into the glass dish—"Simply to verify whether the outer coating is sucrose or maltose"—and Elaina, without turning round from the wash-tub, tapped the back of Herta's wrist lightly with the wooden butter-spade.

"Not yet, Herta," Elaina said pleasantly. "You'll spoil the bowl before she gets here."

Herta snatched her hand back with an indignant sniff. That evening at eight, when Phrolova passed the counter on her way to fetch hot water from the hob, she looked down at the pale green ovals glinting in the lamplight. From the hearth-rug, where she was winding grey yarn into a ball, Elaina looked up and gave her such a bright, expectant, sunny smile that Phrolova kept her hands flat against her skirt and walked past without touching the glass.

The dish stayed by the door, piled to the brim. Nobody ate one.

The next night—the nineteenth—the water above the roof changed its key.

Phrolova had heard it happen while she was sitting on her bunk in the stern-room at midnight. A long, creaking groan had run from shore to shore as the real northern freeze poured down the Riseway vents and locked the surface of the lake in three inches of opaque grey-white ice, dropping the ambient hum of the basin by half a tone into a dull, boxed-in C-minor.

Except directly overhead.

When morning came, the small casements along the walls looked out into a milky, frost-dimmed twilight under the ice-sheet, but through the high skylight-pane cut into the peak of the hall rafters, a ten-metre circle of the surface directly above the chimney remained completely unfrozen—a round well of flat black water open to the mist, so that the house sat under its own ten-metre lens of night while the rest of the lake was roofed in stone.

And there was another thing Phrolova noticed by the fourth day, though nobody in the kitchen ever spoke of it.

None of them went below the floorboards.

The house hung thirty feet down in a borehole shaft that was at least seventy metres deep. Below the oak joists of the kitchen floor lay forty more metres of dark water and whatever drowned rock sat at the bottom of the well. Yet when Viviane dropped a brass three-eighth nut through the open bilge-hatch under the pantry while she was greasing the lower baffle, she did not reach her gloved arm down into the black water between the timbers to fish it off the shelf. She lay on her stomach on the planking, looked down through the square hole into the dark for four slow beats with her red eyes very still, then took a fresh pine board, laid it flat over the hatch, and drove four iron nails into the corners with the butt of her wrench.

They treated the forty metres of water beneath their shoes the way country people treat a neighbour's orchard over a party wall: not locked, not spoken against, simply someone else's ground.

Phrolova watched Viviane's hammer sink the fourth nail flush with the pine and said nothing at all. She kept her hour after dusk here just as she kept it under the Solistree—six to seven, behind the shut oak door of the narrow stern-room, sitting straight-backed on the locker while her ear went south across the seam to Stellavento, listening to the bakehouse bell strike six, the hiss of wet wool drying round the brazier, and the small, left-handed *chink* of Luc's brass scissors trimming her lamp-wick in the parlor window.

On the second Thursday, a week to the day after Elaina brought the sour sweets down from the market, someone came to the rim above them.

At twenty to eleven, three sharp metallic *clinks* rang down through the frozen clay of the northwestern bank like a mason's chisel striking iron, followed ten minutes later by the faint, high induction-hum of a three-axis listening head settling onto the noise floor.

Herta stopped with her teacup halfway to her mouth. Her chin came up; her eyes narrowed to two furious slits under her hat-brim as she listened to the faint electrical tick of the sensor cable anchored in the shore shingle.

"That is a short-baseline geophone," Herta whispered, setting her cup down so hard the saucer rattled. "With a digital inclinometer and a three-channel gain stage. Somebody has planted a tripod on the rim and is taking an acoustic profile of the floor."

She looked from the stove to the larder and down at her own boots on the oak planking, and her face went the colour of a pickled beet.

"The array floor on a three-axis brass head is ninety decibels below ambient," Herta hissed, shoving her chair back and glaring up at the milky ice-ceiling as if she could see the operator through thirty feet of water. "They can resolve lateral displacement inside a rectilinear boundary! Every time Viviane walks to the coal-scuttle in those ridiculous sea-boots, we are printing on some undergraduate's strip-chart as an unperiodic floor anomaly!"

"Let 'em print," Viviane said from the settle, not looking up from a lantern wick she was trimming. "They'll write it off as gravel slipping."

"I am not *gravel slipping*!" Herta sat down on the floor then and there, unbuckled her boots with two vicious jerks of the straps, kicked them under the settle, and spent the entire remainder of Thursday stalking from her bookshelves to the tea-kettle in her thick grey woollen stockings, turning round to glare daggers at the floorboards whenever anyone's heel made a joist creak.

At quarter to five, when the grey light through the ice had gone dim as pewter, the bark door swung open and Elaina came down from the southeastern spur.

She unhooked her wicker basket, shook a non-existent speck of frost from her hem, and lifted the white linen napkin off the leaves. There were four figs left where there had been six at noon.

"The girl from the sweet stall was right up there on the stones!" Elaina said, delighted, as she reached for the tea-caddy. "Crouching by the black cable at the edge of the ice in a pair of woollen mittens. She told me the snow in the city came at seven o'clock last night to the very minute—six centimetres, just like the notice at the Exchange said—so I gave her two figs for her supper. One for her, and one for the tall woman with the white hair who's keeping their fire up on the ridge."

Phrolova, sitting at the far end of the settle with her bone tuning-fork pinched between her teeth and her Guarneri across her knee, did not even lift her eyes from the fingerboard.

Students with tripods and shrine-keepers camping on the frost above had nothing to do with her. She touched the vibrating fork to the bridge, brought the A string up a hair's breadth until the beat vanished between wood and bone, and laid the violin back in its velvet bed.

*Shut the shutter when the wind goes by;*
*Wait—now lift the latch and see*
*Who left cherries under the tree.*

It was past two in the morning when the tune came through the floor.

Phrolova had not been asleep. She had lain on her back on the narrow bunk in the stern-room since eleven, her dark dress still buttoned to the throat and her cloak drawn over her knees against the draft that crept under the bulkhead. In the kitchen beyond the partition the turf-fire had sunk to red ash; overhead, Black Swan's ropes gave no creak; even Herta's stolen rock-pika had stopped scratching in its leather bag under the table.

Up through the two-inch oak planking of the floor rose the basin's nightly drift—the worn, circular murmur of the dead turning over at the bottom of the well.

*—the readings were good—*

*—sixteen minutes—*

*—I left the stove—*

Phrolova listened to it the way one listens to winter rain in a lead gutter: an unpitched background noise in the middle register, going nowhere, asking nothing.

Then, at half past two, threaded through the grey wash of the drift from forty metres straight down under the keel of the house, came four bars of a melody.

It was hummed under the breath, wordless and dry, without vibrato, pitched in G major—though the hummer was taking the F-sharp leading note a quarter-tone flat, the way an untrained voice sags when it has been singing to itself in the dark for a long time.

*Sol, la, si, do—re, si, sol...*

Phrolova's eyes did not move from the dark beam above her bunk, but every muscle along her spine locked against the mattress.

She knew that air.

It was the four-bar nursery round the children in Stellavento used to sing by the horse-trough in June when the early black-heart cherries came down from the high orchard in willow skeps. It had never been printed in a songbook; it had never been scored for conservatory strings; nobody on the face of the living earth knew those four bars except Phrolova and the echoes she had shaped out of her own ear four centuries after the sky fell.

The humming faded under a pass of the stove-voice, then came back at the head of the phrase.

*Sol, la, si, do—re, si, sol...*

At the middle of the third bar—on the *mi* where the tune steps up onto the open E string for *Wait*—the humming stopped dead.

One beat. Two beats. Three beats of empty rest, right in the middle of a bar where no rest existed in the village round, before the voice picked up on *now lift the latch* and carried the cadence down to the tonic.

Two minutes later it hummed the four bars a third time, and stopped in the exact same place, for the exact same three counts—as if the person down in the dark believed that a three-beat pause on the open E was part of how the song had been written.

Before Phrolova's mind had pulled the year or the room up out of the stacks, her bare left hand lying flat on the woollen blanket had already begun the fingering by itself: first finger on the D string, third on F-sharp, and then, at the third bar, her left thumb and index finger lifting automatically off the imaginary neck to reach up toward the peg-box and give a slipping ebony peg a quarter-turn backward against the cold.

*A damp basement corridor with green-painted concrete walls. A travel violin she had bought in a pawnshop in the border towns because she wouldn't bring her Guarneri into a Fractsidus laboratory. An E-peg whose taper had shrunk in the minus-four ventilation draft, so that every single time she reached the third bar she had to stop the bow for three beats and pinch the peg back up to pitch before finishing the line.*

Phrolova sat up on the bunk.

Her feet made no sound on the floorboards. She did not unlatch the Guarneri case, and she did not take the brass candlestick from the washstand.

She walked out into the dark kitchen—past the banked stove, past the oak table where Herta's goniometer glinted dull yellow in the night-light from the open circle overhead, past the side counter where the two kilos of lemon-acid sweets sat in their glass dish by the door—and slipped into the narrow pantry behind the larder.

Kneeling on the cold planking, she slid her bare fingers under the edge of the pine board Viviane had nailed over the bilge-hatch. She did not draw the nails with a tool; she let a single hair-thin thread of her Forte go down into the grain of the oak joist until the iron shanks loosened in their sockets, lifted the board away without a scrape, and looked straight down through the square opening into the vertical black water beneath the house.

No lantern light reached down there. The water in the hatch-well was flat as obsidian.

Phrolova swung her boots through the framing and stepped off the joist into the dark.

For the first twenty metres it was only the cold, mineral-heavy water of the upper basin, pressing her skirts flat against her shins as she sank feet-first through the gloom. Then her boots passed the level of the old borehole rim, forty metres below the ice, and the water ceased to be water.

There was no transition—no thermocline, no current. Simply, between one fathom and the next, the wet disappeared. What stood in the lower shaft had no temperature at all, neither freezing nor warm, and offered no resistance to her woollen hem; it was the Nihility's raw under-water, a thick, lightless absence that did not press against the skin so much as strip the edge off whatever entered it, reporting itself along her shin-bones and wrists as a dull, sourceless ache with no anatomy to hold it.

Phrolova held her right hand up six inches before her eyes as she descended.

From the tips of her nails to the first knuckle-crease, her bare fingers had gone pale grey and translucent as tallow smoke, the outlines blurring softly into the black around them the way her hands had blurred at the bottom of Stellavento's lake when the rift closed.

She drew her hand back to her side and kept going down. She had stood for six hours outside the Septimont gate with thirty thousand dead clawing at her tendons; she knew how much of her own margin she could afford to let the dark chew on before the bone showed.

Ten fathoms above the bottom of the shaft, the hummed G-major air stopped.

Phrolova's boots touched concrete.

---

The facility sat on the bedrock shelf at the bottom of the borehole the way a bad daguerreotype sits on silvered copper after the fixer has turned: whatever had been soft—wall-plaster, rubber door-seals, paper charts, cotton mattress-ticking—had been eaten away by the dark water to a thin grey scum, while everything stamped or ground in steel, glass, and porcelain held its square edges in the gloom.

She stood in a long, slightly tilted service corridor. Overhead, the dead fluorescent tubes hung in their rusted wire cages like peeled bones; ten paces to her left, set into a waiting-alcove wall, a four-foot glass aquarium sat grey and clouded on its angle-iron stand, everything that had ever swum in it long since gone back to nothing except a single small blue plastic castle resting upright on the gravel floor.

At the end of the passage stood a heavy steel fire-door. The grey enamel had dissolved off the metal in long vertical weals, but across the middle plate at eye level the acid-etched letters remained sharp enough to catch a fingernail: `CALIBRATION`.

Phrolova did not slow down to read the wall-plates or peer into the side offices. She knew the floor-plan of this basement before she ever set foot in it; for six years the quarterly outlines from Borehole Four had come up the shaft in sealed leather pouches to sit on the left corner of her desk beside her metronome.

She pushed through the half-open steel door into the primary ward.

Six cylindrical containment pods stood in a curved rank along the rear bulkhead, their overhead feed-pipes disappearing into the corroded concrete ceiling like the ranks of a drowned organ.

The first five tanks were standard height, their heavy curved glass fronts intact but filmed over with black silt. Drifting back and forth between those five cylinders—never rising toward the ceiling, never straying past the rusted manifold at the door—moved five faint, threadbare frequencies.

They had no tempo left, and almost no pitch. One was a frayed B-flat that broke off every three seconds like a wet bow sliding across an unrosined string; another was a hollow alto fifth with no root under it; the other three were barely more than grey scrapes in the lower register. They drifted together from the left wall of the alcove to the right, stopped for four bars by the third tank, and drifted back again.

The five before.

Phrolova stopped three paces inside the door and looked at them.

She had never learned whether the ward clerks gave them numbers or names on the clipboards, because names were for church registers, not bench-trials, and she had known all five of them by ear when the nutrient pumps were running. They were failed drafts—human hulls that had split along the seam when the grafted Tacet tissue rejected the marrow, the exact inverse of the one hundred and eighty-nine mangled Discord-shells Phrolova herself had built and buried behind the Stellavento smithy before she found a harmonic that would hold Triss's face through an afternoon.

She had been able to do nothing for her one hundred and eighty-nine except put them in the ground, and she had been able to do nothing for these five when she walked past their glass with her violin case under her arm, and she could do nothing for them now.

She did not pretend to pity them, and she did not look away from them. She walked straight down the line of the five cylinders—letting the frayed B-flat brush against her sleeve without flinching—and stopped in front of the sixth pod at the end of the row.

It was smaller than the other five, built to the scale of a child's wardrobe. The curved glass front had not dissolved; it had been shattered outward from the inside, the thick silicate shards lying wedged in the floor-grating around her boots, and the heavy iron collar-ring at the base of the tank hung open on its braided steel cable like an unbuckled fetter.

The tank was empty.

Directly beneath the concrete slab under Phrolova's soles—eleven or twelve metres further down in the solid rock where no corridor went, as close and distinct as a tenant turning over in the bedroom below an apartment floor—beat a single, solitary pitch.

Low, steady, unhurried. About fifty-eight beats to the minute.

Phrolova stood with her smoke-faint hands at her sides, looking at the open iron ring in the broken tank, and the room came back at her in two short, jagged flashes like a broken string whipping across the cheek.

*Twelve years ago, kept waiting twenty minutes in this cold alcove while a sub-director hunted for a key: inside this sixth cylinder, before there were hands or ribs or a face, a misshapen knot of raw void suspended in yellow fluid, three Tacet Discord limbs grafted at crooked angles into a dark seam where the light bent wrong, the rack-monitors shrieking a saw-toothed crimson overload—until Phrolova, irritated beyond endurance by the din, snapped open her travel case, tucked the cheap fiddle under her chin, and played the four-bar Stellavento cherry-round, stopping for three beats in the middle of the third bar to twist the slipping E-peg back up against the draft, and every screaming needle on the brass rack dropped flat and silent until her bow left the horsehair.*

*Six years after that: a thin ten-year-old sitting on the steel examination table in the room next door with a paper gown round her shoulders and her bare heels swinging against the cabinet door, looking up as Phrolova walked past in the corridor and saying, in a flat, conversational treble, as if reporting the weather: "F-sharp minor, Miss. The fan in the ceiling. Only the bearings go flat when they turn the heat off at nine." And Phrolova stopping on the threshold, taking one look at the two calibration technicians with their clipboards and their ink-stained cuffs, and saying in her level mezzo, "Clear the room. This unit is under my desk's review until next Thursday," before walking out to catch the noon sled—not because she gave a straw what became of the child, but because the technicians had ugly voices and she disliked watching amateurs fumble a tuning.*

Phrolova shoved both memories back down behind her teeth.

She had not come down here twelve years ago to be a fairy godmother to a vat-specimen. It had been distaste for loud machinery, forty minutes to kill before the Riseway hoist, an audience of one that held still and didn't clap in the middle of a rest. And not long after that she had stopped coming down the shaft altogether.

Then, at eight minutes past three, the slow fifty-eight-count under the concrete floor stopped dead.

Phrolova went rigid beside the broken glass.

In the drowned alcove nothing moved. Even the five worn-thin whispers by the manifold held still against the wall. Out of four hundred years of the stick, Phrolova's mind counted the rest into the dead dark:

*One. Two. Three—*

Just before the fourth beat fell, from straight down under the twelve metres of rock and absence beneath her boots, a girl laughed.

It was not a chuckle, and it was not the neat, installed four-hundred-hour smile from the ward corridors. It came up through the concrete floor like a depth-charge going off in an orchestra pit—huge, waterlogged, catastrophic, the sound of someone with both hands over her mouth losing control of her shoulders because she has just caught the entire house out in a lie and cannot stop herself.

The sound went right through the soles of Phrolova's boots, up her shin-bones into her ribs, and broke upward through the seventy metres of black water over the roof.

And the pitch under the floor came tearing back—racing at sixty-six beats to the minute for ninety seconds while the last reverb of the laugh rattled the dead fluorescent cages overhead, before easing down to settle at a steady, unmistakable sixty-one.

Denia.

Phrolova knew the voice. In six years of occasional corridor passes she had heard the girl speak perhaps forty words, always at the same flat, polite classroom volume, and she knew the timbre of that throat the way she knew the grain of her own Guarneri.

She had no idea what the girl was laughing at in the dark under the bedrock, and she did not send her hearing down through the floor to pry into it.

Standing alone in the ruined ward with her fingertips blurred to grey smoke at the ends, Phrolova tried to locate what she was supposed to feel.

Pity? You do not pity a thing that can laugh like that with seventy metres of black lake on its head; it would be like offering a copper piece to a landslide. Remorse? Phrolova had signed the messenger slips for her Stellavento binders in broad daylight with a steady hand. Relief that the sixth unit had not dissolved with the mattresses?

Every one of them sounded like a counterfeit coin dropped on a stone counter. She threw all three out of her head and walked back out into the corridor.

Ten yards down the side passage, past the calibration room, stood a smaller steel door with its latch sprung. The stencilled white block-letters on the upper panel had peeled away in the water, but lower down, at the height of a ten-year-old's shoulder, someone had scratched five letters into the primer with a nail or a compass-point, and the bare iron had rusted just deeply enough to hold the shape of the name.

Phrolova stepped inside.

The room was six paces by four. The iron cot frame stood bare in the corner where the mattress had rotted away to grey silt; above it on the concrete wall, where eye-level stencilling had once run from corner to corner, only a few disconnected strokes of black paint still clung to the damp aggregate.

In the middle of the floor, where the water had set it down when the turbulence settled, lay a book, face-down.

Phrolova went down on one knee on the concrete.

It was a student's hardbound journal in navy-blue Academy cloth, the buckram weave worn thin as cheesecloth by the dark. On the upward-facing back board, the glue of two stickers was still faintly visible as pale ghosts against the blue: a round badge-shaped circle, and beside it the silver-marker outline of a cat drawn with seven or eight legs.

With two smoke-grey fingers, Phrolova lifted the front board and turned the wet pages from the front.

The book had been full of photographs once; the square gummed corners still sat four to a page on the rag paper, but the emulsion on every print from the first leaf to the fortieth had been licked clean off by the Nihility, leaving forty pages of blank white rectangles framed in little black triangles.

Only the very last leaf, pressed tight against the heavier pasteboard of the back cover, had kept its silver.

It was a colour print, bleached pale at the margins, taken outdoors under the low boughs of an ash tree heavy with spring blossom.

Two girls in matching school uniforms were laughing straight into the lens with their shoulders nearly touching. On the left was Denia—four or five years older than when Phrolova had last seen her swinging her heels in a paper gown, her dark hair grown long and a single strand tucked neatly behind her ear, her mouth open in a quick, unguarded laugh that Phrolova had never once seen on her face in this basement.

On the right stood a girl with a round, earnest face and two thick braids of bright orange hair falling past her collarbones, caught mid-laugh with her chin tilted up.

And between them, where a third girl had stood in the middle under the ash bough, there was a hole in the paper.

It had not been eaten by the water. Someone had taken a small, very sharp blade or a pair of embroidery scissors and cut the third figure cleanly out of the print, following the exact line of a shoulder, an arm, and the fall of the hair, so that not a millimetre of her remained and the two girls on either side stood closer for her absence.

Beneath the photograph, written across the black endpaper in heavy marker that had bled only slightly into the fibre, stood three words:

*Best friend forever.*

Phrolova knelt on the drowned concrete in the minus-nothing dark, her bare knee resting in the grey silt of a rotted mattress, looking down at the two laughing faces and the scissor-shaped hole beside them.

Her notes had come down the shaft into this room.

Every failed harmonic bridge she had logged in the first two centuries after the meteor hit Stellavento—every brutal, exact observation she had written down when Granny Leah's collarbones split in the eighty-sixth trial, or when the cobbler's boy came apart at the fourth bar in the hundred-and-fourth—had gone down the Riseway in calfskin binders and been turned inside out on these benches to keep a child's grafted ribs from flying apart while they grew a lock in her throat.

And hers had not been the first. The shelf this program stood on was older than her desk, older than she was, older than anything in the Fractsidus but the woman who founded it: attempt after attempt, each one reaching into what the Threnodians had left in the world for something strong enough to stand up in a body, each one trying to build by hand a thing that had already been built twice, by other hands, and set down on Solaris-3 without anyone's leave. The girl under the floor was only the latest on that shelf. The five by the manifold had been the ones before her, and there had been others before them that nobody could hear any more.

*I helped make this girl for my own selfish ends,* she thought.

*No,* her mind answered instantly, cold as a tuning fork struck against granite. *For my home. For Triss and the children and the bread-ovens.*

The correction was entirely accurate, and it sat behind her breastbone like a mouthful of cold ash.

She looked at the clean, scissor-cut silhouette in the middle of the photograph.

She did not need Alice's marginalia or Herta's three colours of ink to tell her who had stood in that gap under the flowering ash. She knew only one creature who learned people from their leavings and stood in the middle of their lives wearing a borrowed face, and who would leave exactly that shape behind when she went.

Under the floor, twelve metres down in the rock, the sixty-one-count beat on—steady, unhurried, alone.

Phrolova was four hundred years past playing the rescuer in someone else's third act, and she had never in her life been a fool. She had no rope that reached through twelve metres of granite into whatever stage the girl was standing on; she had no apology that wouldn't be an insult to a girl who had cut the Grand Architect out of her own picture with a pocket-knife; and she had left her violin upstairs in its case.

Carefully, so her blurred fingertips wouldn't tear the wet buckram, Phrolova closed the cover of the journal, turned it over on the concrete so it lay face-up instead of resting on its open leaves, and stood up.

She walked out of the room, down the tilted corridor past the little blue plastic castle in the clouded tank, and went straight up through the black water toward the house.

---

When she came up through the pantry hatch at twenty minutes to four and dropped the pine board back into its seat, every lamp in the kitchen was lit.

The four of them were up and dressed at an hour when even the owls in Bjartr had their heads under their wings, going about the long oak table as briskly as if the clock on the dresser said four in the afternoon.

Nobody looked round at the pantry door. Nobody asked where she had been since half past two, or remarked on the dead, un-wet cold radiating off her woollen skirts.

On the settle by the fire, Viviane was re-threading a brass bolt with a tap-and-die set in her grease-marked kidskin gloves. She glanced sideways once as Phrolova stepped out of the larder passage—her red eyes taking in Phrolova's bare right hand, where the tips of the fingers from the first joint up were still faint and translucent as horn—and jerked her gloved thumb toward the iron stove-pipe.

"Hold 'em two inches off the flue-collar till the nail-beds prickle," Viviane said, turning the tap-wrench a quarter-turn. "Don't put them in the wash-basin first or the skin splits at the quick."

Phrolova walked to the stove and held both bare palms two inches from the hot black iron of the flue. For ten seconds she felt nothing at all; then the heat bit through the grey blur like a row of hot needles driven under the fingernails, and the pale skin flushed dark red as the edges of her fingers came back into the room.

At the oak table, Herta had three folio volumes propped open on book-rests around her pewter inkstand, her steel nib racing across a fresh sheet of foolscap in cramped violet script.

"The floor has been intolerably loud since three," Herta remarked to her blotter without lifting her chin. "If whoever is downstairs intends to drop the furniture in the middle of the fourth watch, they might at least do it on the downbeat."

By the side hob, Elaina was warming the earthenware teapot with a ladle of boiling water. She swirled the steam round the glaze, tipped it into the slop-bowl, and glanced across the kitchen at the side counter by the door, where the heavy cut-glass dish of sugar-frosted lemon-acid drops sat piled to the rim under the wall-lamp.

"Do you think I bought them too sour after all?" Elaina asked, her voice mild and conversational over the hiss of the kettle. "The old man at the stall said nobody buys the top jar except the schoolboys when they're playing dares on the tram, and it would be a dreadful shame if they went sticky in the dish before she gets here."

Phrolova stood by the flue-pipe with her hands stinging in the dry heat.

She looked across the room at the cut-glass dish set squarely on the corner of the counter beside the door-latch—pale yellow-green ovals dusted with white sugar, sitting where anyone coming in over the threshold could reach out two fingers in passing.

She had walked enough corridors in sixty years of institutional life to recognize a dish left on a counter outside an office door.

"They aren't too sour," Phrolova said, her voice flat and even in the warm room. "She'll take two and put one back."

It was the only sentence she gave them.

By the hob, Elaina set the lid on the earthenware pot with a small, neat click, her mouth curving into a quiet, satisfied smile in the steam, and did not look round.

Overhead, Black Swan dropped out of her crimson silk ropes without a sound, landing barefoot on the braided rug by the dresser. She drew her tarnished-gilt pack from her sash, turned up one card toward her own face, looked at it for two beats with a slight lift of one dark eyebrow, and slid it into her sleeve.

Then she walked over to the wide stone jug on the window-sill, where a bunch of large, five-petalled crimson flowers with dark stamped centers stood in cold water—broad, flat blooms of the sort that open at dawn on southern rock-walls and drop their corollas at dusk.

She drew seven of them out of the jug by their wet green stems, shook the drops onto the flagstones, and walked out through the bark door into the water without a word.

The kitchen was stifling.

The smell of toasted cumin, hot stove-iron, damp wool, and four women going about their business at twenty to four in the morning so she wouldn't have to sit in a dark room with her own hands pressed against Phrolova's ribs until the air felt too thick to draw past her larynx.

She took her hands away from the flue-pipe, walked to the door without her cloak, lifted the wooden latch, and stepped out.

---

She came up onto the frosted shingle of the southeastern spur at four.

The air above the lake was twelve degrees below zero, dry and still as an empty church, smelling of frozen slate and the pale lavender mist that hung like a low ceiling between the shelves.

Out in the middle of the grey-white ice sheet, sixty metres from her boots, lay the ten-metre circle of open black water—the unfreezing lens directly above the rafters of the house she had just left.

Riding on that black water, spaced out along the slow, counter-clockwise turn of the surface with their wide crimson faces turned straight up toward the mist, were the seven flowers. Black Swan was nowhere on the ice, and there was not a footprint in the hoarfrost along the shore.

Far across the basin, high up on the northwestern rim by the old culvert stake, a tiny blue-white coal of resin-fire burned behind a slab of rock, and ten feet to the left of it a minute red diode on the tripod box winked once every ten seconds into the dark.

Phrolova turned her back on the water and walked up the thirty-foot bank of frozen greywacke talus to get above the basin's rim.

At the top of the bank, one step above the shingle where the shore gave out, ran the old graded haulage road that skirted the depression toward the Heartwood.

Someone was standing on the roadbed.

Two paces back from the lip of the bank, positioned with the quiet, unhurried patience of a passenger waiting for an early tram, stood a woman in a dark woollen coat with the collar turned up against the frost. She had a canvas book-bag slung over her left shoulder, plain dark woollen gloves on her hands, and sensible low-heeled leather boots whose welts were completely dry despite the two inches of white hoarfrost furring the gravel around her soles. She was holding nothing in her hands at all.

The face she had on belonged to a thirty-year-old clerk from a municipal water board—pleasant, slightly tired under the eyes, the mouth settled in the neutral, courteous line of someone who stamps permits at a counter until five.

Phrolova stopped dead on the top step of the shingle, one foot below the level of the roadbed.

She knew her in the first half-beat.

Not by the pitch—the woman's frequency was ironed to the exact grey, uninflected hum of a third-tier registry office, without even the sixteenth-harmonic seam Phrolova used to listen for—and not by the posture, which had been bought with the coat. She knew her by the eyes. Under fifty borrowed skins and four centuries of tea-tables, the eyes never changed their weight: ancient, unhurried, looking out through the stranger's lids with a quiet, bottomless fondness that made the blood go cold in the wrists, like an archivist who has all of eternity to wait for a page to turn.

And then the draft came down off the scree and brought the rest of her. Under the clerk's damp wool and the cold slate, faint as a flower pressed in a borrowed book, there was blossom, and the bright, bitter oil of orange peel. Phrolova knew that smell as well as she knew the eyes. No skin the woman put on had ever covered it.

Phrolova's first act—automatic, vicious, faster than thought—was to scan the clerk's face from hairline to chin.

She checked the bridge of the nose, the slight asymmetry of the jaw, the chapped place at the corner of the lip, the way the gloved left hand hung by the seam of the woollen skirt.

Nobody she knew. Not Triss, not her mother, not a contralto from the Ragunna choir, not a single soul Phrolova had ever loved or put under the ground.

The relief went through her chest like a needle, and she despised herself for it so violently that her bare hands clenched into fists against her skirt.

"Good morning, Overseer," the Architect said.

She used the office, the way she called every soul on her books by their office, in the pleasant, low-voiced register of two department heads meeting in a basement corridor at the end of a night shift.

Phrolova did not step up onto the roadbed, and the Architect did not step down onto the shingle. Twelve inches of frost-whitened gravel and the dropped edge of the roadbed sat between Phrolova's knee and the hem of the dark coat.

*As fate would have it,* Cristoforo would have written in the margin of his prompt-book, and Phrolova's jaw locked because she did not believe in fate and she could not tell, looking up at the empty gloved hands in the silver dark, whether finding the Grand Architect standing alone in the frost at four in the morning above this particular hole in the earth meant the creature had been drawn out onto the ice without her instruments, or whether the whole basin was still sitting inside her stage-directions. She changed her mind twice before she opened her mouth.

---

Before Phrolova spoke, the woman on the road reached into the pocket of her dark woollen coat, drew out a small, heavy bronze coin—an old Ragunnan conservatory token, the lyre on the obverse worn smooth as butter by centuries of pockets—and laid it neatly on the flat kerb-stone at the very lip of the bank between them.

The fee first. The custom of every street she worked.

Phrolova did not touch the coin. She left it sitting on the hoarfrost.

"You are out without your shawl," the Architect observed mildly. Her gloved hands were folded loosely at her waist; as she looked down the bank, her right thumb and index finger made a small, absent quarter-turn against her left palm, the exact circumference of a porcelain teacup or a steel flask-cap that was no longer there. "The temperature drops another two degrees before the tree relights."

"I was warm indoors," Phrolova said.

"Mm."

The Architect turned her head and looked out past Phrolova's shoulder across the grey-white floor of the basin to the ten-metre circle of open black water in the middle, where the seven crimson flowers rode the slow inward turn of the current. She watched them for a long moment in the silver mist and asked nothing about who had laid them there.

"Is the village well?" she asked after a beat, with the polite, measured courtesy of an old colleague inquiring after a country estate. "The air must be clearer in the square since the sky changed."

"You don't have the address anymore," Phrolova said. Her voice was level, pitched in the flat mezzo that did not carry three yards past the roadbed.

"No," the Architect agreed, entirely unoffended. "The door is shut. That is proper; a house ought to fasten from the inside." She inclined her head a fraction toward the north, where the great dark wall of the mountain rose behind the mist. "I had a pouch from the northern road on Tuesday, if you were wondering where the Arbiter's horses are wintering—"

"I am not," Phrolova said, cutting the bar clean off before a single town-name could cross the step.

The Architect gave a small, tidy nod, conceding the rest. "Very well."

For three bars only the slow eleven-count of the distant Heartwood came up through the frozen shale under their boots, rising and dying away into the mist.

Phrolova looked straight into the clerk's tired, borrowed eyes.

"I went down," Phrolova said.

The Architect went very still on the roadbed. She did not ask how deep, or through which hatch, or whether the glass on the sixth cylinder had held. She simply stood above the kerb-stone with her gloved hands folded over the empty space at her waist and waited.

"The sixth unit," Phrolova said, keeping her voice cold and taxonomic, one specialist reviewing another's bench-work across a laboratory rail. "I remember the specification sheet when the outline crossed my desk in the third year. Zero baseline affect; a hull built to take a borrowed tide and pull the seam shut from the inside. She outgrew the specification." She paused half a beat. "Was the surplus what you wanted, or did your tolerances slip?"

The Architect looked back out over the grey sheet of the ice toward the seven red flowers on the black water. When she answered, her tone was light, almost conversational—landing all the harder because she had taken the weight out of it on purpose.

"We have merely succeeded in imitating Their work," the Architect said.

Phrolova heard the capital letter on the pronoun as clearly as a pedal-point on an organ. She waited for the woman to say whether that imitation was the triumph of the program or the ruin of the trial, and the Architect added not a word to either side.

She knew who the pronoun meant. Every Overseer learned it sooner or later, late at night, over tea, in the same voice that told the fable. Nobody had a name for Them, or a world, or a face. They had raised the Sentinels and set the Exostriders walking, and nobody living could swear to more than that, except that two had been sent, and the likeliest hands to have sent them were Theirs. Instance One, which had not been heard of in longer than Phrolova had been alive. Instance Two, who still kept her shore and her lights on the far coast, patient as a lighthouse, managing a world that had never been asked whether it wished to be managed. Under the figures in every quarterly outline that had crossed Phrolova's desk lay the same intention, never written down: take the pen back from Them. She had initialled it for centuries without once looking at it straight.

"Was she meant to be the Third?" Phrolova asked.

"Mm." The Architect's gloved thumb finished its quarter-turn against her palm. "Three was never ours to number."

Phrolova turned that over and could not find which way up it went: whether it meant there would never be a Third, or that the number was already spoken for, by someone the Architect had not made.

"The early tables were built on your notes, of course," the Architect continued after a moment, turning her head back to Phrolova with the exact, scrupulous fairness of a director crediting a senior fellow on a proof sheet. "Your margins were the best method anyone had."

Phrolova's bare hands hung at her sides in the minus-twelve dark, the nail-beds still aching faintly from the stove-iron.

She did not argue, and she did not apologize. She remembered signing the courier's receipt when the three calfskin binders went down the Riseway hoist; she had traded them to the lower ward for two crates of pre-Lament resonator wire she needed to hold the bakehouse roof up in the rain.

Inside her ribs, the revulsion and a sudden, razor-thin splinter of professional pride were wedged so tightly against the same soundpost that she could not prize them apart: *my bridge held when five of hers rotted in the glass,* and directly underneath it, *they used Granny Leah's split collarbones to hang a child in a ward.* Both sentences were true, and both were in her handwriting, and she would have walked back into the forty-fathom absence before she let the woman on the road hear either one.

"Your bench-hands were clumsy with the upper harmonics," Phrolova said coldly. "They over-tensioned the fourth."

"They were," the Architect said. "Until you played for it."

A dry draft came off the southeastern scree, stirring the hoarfrost along the hem of the dark woollen coat and the bronze coin on the kerb.

"The first five rotted," Phrolova said. "And the sixth is at the bottom of a borehole with seventy metres of water on her head. Will you deploy a seventh?"

She used the institution's verb on purpose—*deploy*, the flat clerk's word from the quarterly requisition slips—and watched the woman's mouth.

The Architect did not deflect. She did not smile, or turn the sentence sideways, or offer a proverb about gates and pens.

Asked a plain question she could not answer, she went completely silent.

One second. Two. Three. Four.

At her waist, her gloved right thumb stopped halfway through its small quarter-turn against her left palm and stayed still. She stood on the frosted roadbed looking down at the grey ice, and Phrolova, who had spent eight weeks sitting at a table with witches who had no answers prepared at supper, felt a cold jolt under her breastbone because she could not tell whether the Grand Architect was guarding a file or whether, for the first time in four hundred years, the hand reaching into the cabinet behind those eyes had come up empty.

"The cold is coming down through the upper vents much earlier this season," the Architect remarked at last, her voice returning on a completely different stave, smooth and unhurried. "They opened the precipitation baffles in the dome at seven last night. Six centimetres on the fourth terrace."

Phrolova braced her shoulders against the freezing air.

She knew the structure of this symphony. She had known it since a Thursday evening four centuries ago when the tall clock on her landing struck twelve: first the quiet company in the dark, then the acknowledgment of shared craft, and then the charter laid beside the empty stew-bowl. *Your story is over, and you are still here.*

She waited for the clerk's face to let go. She waited for the thirty-year-old water-board employee to drop her jaw half an inch and put on a dead violinist's squint, or Luc's wet forelock, or to name a seat at the table in the north.

Nothing happened.

The tired, pleasant face of the municipal clerk stayed fixed in its lines. The hands remained folded in their plain dark wool.

"You aren't going to ask," Phrolova said, very low.

The Architect looked down at her from the roadbed—one step up, across the untouched bronze token on the stone.

"Phrolova," she said—using her name once, plainly, without office or ornament—"you have a house with the lamp lit. What would I offer you that isn't already on your table?"

Phrolova stood motionless on the shingle. She could not tell whether that sentence was the cleanest courtesy her former employer had ever paid her, or a dismissal so absolute it meant she had already been cross-referenced under *retired* and moved to the dead stacks—or whether sitting in the witches' kitchen under the ice was precisely where the Architect's current draft preferred to have her kept.

The Architect glanced once more toward the ten-metre circle of open black water in the middle of the lake.

"The water is very deep over the lower ward," she said—lightly, almost an afterthought, though for half a beat the pleasantness thinned around the clerk's eyelids and something sharper and hungrier looked out at the basin. "And the bedrock plays tricks with the lower register. Did anything carry through the floor when you went down?"

Whatever the woman on the road knew of tonight, she knew it the way the Fractsidus had always known things: through instruments, from needles and paper and figures copied out in someone else's neat hand.

She did not have the ear. She had not heard the four hummed bars of the Stellavento cherry-song stopping for three beats where the E-peg slipped, and she had not heard the girl laugh through twelve metres of rock until the dead fluorescent cages rattled on their chains.

Phrolova looked straight up into the ancient, waiting eyes.

"The fans in the ceiling have rusted through," Phrolova said evenly. "The F-sharp is gone. There's only the water."

The Architect held her gaze for two slow bars. Whatever she looked for in Phrolova's face met four hundred years of concert iron and came back empty-handed.

"Is it," the Architect said mildly. "Yes. Marl is hard on bearings."

They stood together at the edge of the basin while the fifth hour of the morning wore out—two women who had each spent lifetimes waiting in the dark for someone who went ahead and didn't turn back, saying no more about it, while the hoarfrost thickened along the shoulders of the clerk's woollen coat and stiffened the hem of Phrolova's skirt.

At six o'clock the milky pearl high in the fork of the Solvein Heartwood began to flush a dull, bruised red toward its morning relighting, and the lavender mist over the basin thinned to a flat pewter grey.

Across the water, four hundred metres away on the northwestern rim by the rusted culvert stake, three small figures were moving round the windbreak slab—rolling up their insulated bags, kicking frost over the ash of the resin fire, and unbolting the brass cylinder from the wooden tripod.

One of them—shorter than the tall white-haired woman, bundled in a heavy coat with a woollen hat pulled down over her ears—scrambled down the thirty-foot bank to the shingle to free the black sensor cable from the anchor stones.

Halfway through winding the stiff rubber loop around her elbow and mitten, the girl stopped.

During the last two hours of minus-twelve air, a foot-wide collar of clear, glass-smooth new ice had formed overnight around the inner lip of the ten-metre black hole in the middle of the lake, locking the seven crimson flowers flat under the surface glaze with their five wide petals open to the sky like specimens under a watch-glass.

The girl laid her cable coil down on the shingle, walked ten paces out onto the thick shore ice, and crouched by the new glaze with a pocket lens. After a moment she pulled a small bound journal and a pen from her coat and began to write with cold-stiffened fingers.

Up on the southeastern roadbed, the Architect unfastened the flap of her canvas book-bag, drew out a compact pair of brass-mounted field glasses, and held them out across the one-step drop to Phrolova.

She did not raise them to her own eyes first. As Phrolova's bare fingers took the cold brass barrels, she noticed that the woman on the road had no need of ground glass to read a hand at four hundred metres; she already knew how that coat was buttoned.

Phrolova lifted the glasses and turned the center wheel a quarter-notch.

Through the lenses, across the grey-white skin of the basin, the girl crouching by the seven frozen crimson flowers came into sharp, flat focus: nineteen or twenty, her brows drawn together in earnest scholarly irritation under her woollen hat as she wrote on the green-ink pages at the back of her book, her breath puffing white over the ice, and at the line of her jaw two stubby, uneven ends of bright orange braid where the hair had been sheared off short.

The girl on the right of the photograph. The one who had been laughing under the flowering ash with her shoulder touching Denia's.

Phrolova lowered the field glasses half an inch from her eyes and looked sideways at the Architect.

The Architect was watching the girl on the ice with her naked eyes—still, unhurried, her gloved hands resting over her coat buttons—with the exact, quiet attention of someone looking at a room she had lived in for two years and learned down to the floorboards before she walked out the door.

In Phrolova's mind, the narrow, scissor-cut hole in the middle of the last page—the missing uniform sleeve, the missing shoulder, the missing third head under the ash blossom—settled into place without a sound.

Phrolova said nothing at all. She pushed the brass bridge of the field glasses shut and handed them back across the step.

Across the basin, the orange-haired girl put her journal away, picked up her cable coil, and climbed back up the bank to the shelf. Before they started off, she walked twenty paces down the top of the south-southwest scree chute, crouched beside a turned slab of grey rock, and picked up a fist-sized chunk of stone, dropping it into her left coat pocket. She started to rise, paused with her mitten hovering over the gravel, picked up a second, smaller pebble from the same bed, and slipped it into her right-hand pocket.

Then the three of them hoisted their kit—the third one, a dark-haired girl carrying the heavy olive-drab box on a pack-frame, walking last and turning her head once to scan the empty southeastern ridge before they dropped behind the spur—and struck out eastward along the frozen shelf toward the Heartwood.

On the roadbed above the shingle, the Architect slipped the field glasses back into her canvas bag and buckled the strap.

"The morning tram runs on the half-hour from the lower depot," she said, in the pleasant, practical tone of a clerk concluding an overnight inventory. She looked down at Phrolova on the shingle, and then, for half a beat, past Phrolova's boots toward the water's edge where the unseen latch of the house lay. "Give my regards to your household, Phrolova. Keep out of the draft."

She turned on her dry heel and walked away along the frosted roadbed into the pewter mist.

Phrolova stood on the shingle below the road for a full minute after the footsteps died out on the gravel. On the flat kerb-stone at the lip of the bank, the old bronze conservatory token sat untouched in a thin ring of rime. She still could not tell whether that parting sentence was a concession that the door of this shore was closed to every skin the Architect owned, or whether she had simply been left on the shelf until the weather turned.

She left the coin sitting on the stone, turned her back on the road, and walked down the shingle into the water.

Inside the kitchen under the spur, the morning light filtering through the three-inch ceiling of shore ice was a pale, milky pewter, except dead in the middle of the rafters, where the ten-metre circle of open water hung above the glass like a round black mirror with seven dark stems locked in the new glaze around its rim.

Breakfast was on the oak table: fried salt herring, barley-bread toast, the wooden trencher of warm purple figs, and the everyday earthenware teapot steaming under its knitted cosy by the butter-dish.

At the far end from the stove, Phrolova's place was laid—earthenware trencher, horn spoon, the linen napkin pressed flat under the knife. Nobody looked up to ask where she had been since four; nobody remarked on the hoarfrost melting off the hem of her skirt onto the hearth-rug.

By the door, on the side counter, the heavy cut-glass dish of yellow-green lemon-acid sweets sat untouched in the grey light, piled high and waiting.

Phrolova hung her damp cloak on the peg by the settle, pulled out her chair, and sat down.

At the head of the board, Herta was perched on her two volumes of maritime law with the scarred calfskin household book propped open beside her saucer and her spare steel nib between her fingers, entering the morning's fuel consumption while slipping a corner of toast crust under the table-flap where her leather satchel gave a small, muffled scratch. Viviane was buttering a heel of bread in her grease-marked kidskin gloves; Black Swan was curled in the window-seat with a cup balanced on her bare knee, dry and unhurried, looking up at the black circle overhead; Elaina leaned across the board and pushed the fig trencher two inches toward Phrolova's elbow.

Phrolova ate one herring and half a slice of toast, and drank a cup of tea that had steeped five minutes too long on the trivet.

When she set her cup down in its saucer, she looked down the length of the oak table at Herta's inkstand.

"Put me down for a second hour," Phrolova said.

Herta's steel nib stopped mid-stroke over the vellum. She squinted down her nose at Phrolova from under the brim of her enormous hat. "You already have the one after dusk. Nobody in this house has two hours."

"The one before dawn," Phrolova said evenly. "Up top, on the shingle. While we are here."

For two bars the kitchen was quiet save for the copper kettle ticking on the trivet and the faint, steady sixty-one-count coming up through the bedrock forty metres below the floorboards.

Herta sniffed once, dipped her nib into the violet inkwell, and wrote a single cramped line in the margin beside the Friday column without asking a question.

"Done," Herta said, blotting it with the heel of her thumb. "Don't leave the latch off the outer stone when you come back down, the ice-draft gets under the settle."

Nobody else said anything. Viviane reached for the marmalade; Elaina tipped fresh water from the kettle into the pot.

Phrolova's bare right hand—warmed back to the nail-beds by the iron flue, a faint grey smudge of tallow-soot from the pantry hatch still caught in the crease of her thumb—reached two inches to the left of the butter-dish without her looking down from her plate, closed around the pewter salt-cellar, and set it by her bread.

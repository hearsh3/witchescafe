# THE YOUNGEST GATE

### Books One through Eleven — A Continuous Account

---

## I. THE HOUSE THAT CAME EAST

Before any of this, there were seven people, one stove, and a truck.

The stove belonged to a house in Lahai-Roi, a continent and a season away from Huanglong, and the seven who shared it had assembled the way weather assembles: out of pressure, over time, from unrelated places. Mei was the oldest thing at that table and the newest person at it — a woman with golden eyes, a Tacet Mark branched across her right hand, and no memory whatever of the twenty-odd years that produced her. Twenty-three years earlier she had walked out of her own history on purpose. She had left a sealed page with a physician named Luuk, instructed him to let her come back a stranger, and then done exactly that. She had raised a daughter in a cabin with a blue door, vanished into a long absence, and been recovered from it by that daughter's refusal to stop looking. She had died more times than anyone had a clean count for, and every one of those times she had come back.

Iuno had been High Priestess of Septimont for thirty years, which is a way of saying she had spent thirty years standing near documents that belonged to other people and reading them faster than anyone expected. She had built a calculus in which one child's annihilation could be made to sound like civic duty, and she had signed it. It had cost her an arm — she wears a crystal one now, sawn at the shoulder, cold, and honest about the weather — and eleven years, and a hundred and seventy-three names she can still recite. What ended the calculus was a wolf who put a spear through her and, years later, a woman with no arguments at all who said *no* to her in a hallway and stayed in the room while Iuno hated her for it. She loves Mei with the ferocity of somebody who has watched herself do arithmetic on people and knows the taste of it. Her failure mode is protection performed three moves ahead and disclosed afterward.

Lupa is Co-Champion of Septimont, combustion class, and sold the rest of her name. She runs hot. She sleeps four hours and four hours and has since she was sixteen. She is a disaster at comfort, which is why she is the best at it: she says something rude, hands you a flask, and never mentions it again. Ciaccona Toccata carries a lute that has been broken by playwrights and by gods, crosses borders under a surname a state once erased, and has a technique for playing badly on purpose so that strangers will correct her and, in correcting her, tell her the truth about their city. Cartethyia spent twenty years sealed in the dark naming dolls so the dark could not erase the fact that someone remained inside; she is small, she is a knight, she has crystal thorns for a laurel, and when she needs to be eight feet tall she becomes Fleurdelys and the room has to accommodate it. Chisa cannot see faces, including her own; she reads a room by echo, footfall, breath and pulse, spent twenty years inside a city that reset itself before every rainy season, and carries an enormous scissor-blade and a red string at her left wrist that she keeps out of the sun. Amy is nineteen on the outside and has twenty-nine years in her; ten of them she spent under a city in the Nihility, holding a door shut for two hundred thousand people and talking the entire time on a radio, because a load-bearing wall may as well be a funny one. She is Mei's daughter, she calls Iuno *Auntie* like a pin coming out of something small and round, and she reads everything, fast, in the wrong order, completely.

They came east because a wind asked.

The wind had been a woman once, in a Jinzhou that burned. In her printing of the world the library burned three days, the sluices failed, the lower city flooded, and a red patroller's cap came out of black water; afterward the city was described as having *held*, and she went and looked up what that word costs. She had carried a compass in her chest all her life pointed at a person she was meant to meet at a shore, in a story that had gone differently. So she gave up being a person. She unbound herself into wind at the edge of her world and crossed the dark between printings, which strips everything except what is made of sound, and arrived carrying her mother's shell and one sentence: *the Gorges of Spirits. Let her wake there.* The errand was refused. The woman was already awake, already loved, already surrounded. So the wind spent twenty years in Huanglong carrying other people's grief because it could not refuse useful work, and paid for what it needed out of its own remaining vocabulary — which is why the compositors on Jinzhou's Bookbinders' Row have been short of particular sorts for years.

In Jinzhou it acquired a junior. Outrider Yangyang files what repeats. Across one year she recorded a warm pressure at her cheek that lingered too long, air that corrected her guard in the manner of her own Mingting school, a lullaby completed when her breath ran out. She called it *shijie*, elder martial sister, and then she went to the lake and asked the lake to keep the wind itself, and then she went to her Magistrate and said out loud, in a room, that the weather is a person. She wrote forty-one letters to a stranger at an open window in the small hours because she needed somewhere to put things. That stranger was Mei.

The letter came, and the kite came, and the household did what that household does: it refused momentum. Iuno said honesty about a decision like this takes four days; Mei gave it five. They built a quorum out of named weaknesses — Mei speaks last, because her charisma turns rooms into agreement before anyone notices deciding; Lupa gives private reasons first; Chisa is authorized to call out any gap between what a person says and what their voice is doing; Amy's rule passes eight to one, that *nobody in the household is the last resort*, and if the arithmetic wants a person as payment the arithmetic can have the city. Seven chose to go. They filled in next-of-kin forms, and Mei's hand — which cannot write ordinary Huanglong, which produces the first correct stroke of any character and then stops — wrote five characters fluently: a surname, the lintel formula *there is someone in*, and a river-bend place with no road. Then the characters grew by one, and then by another, and nobody could say what was still arriving.

They drove a decommissioned twelve-tonne mobile dental surgery two thousand kilometres, named it the *Lucky Bird*, took nine days over a two-day road because nineteen strangers in twenty-three halts walked over to a truck with a lintel written on its door, and crossed the frontier under Superintendent Ruan Zhi, who read Mei's five characters, identified a stroke order abolished thirty-one years ago, and said as the truck pulled away a sentence Cartethyia carried untranslated into the city: *There is someone in.*

At the Gorges of Spirits, at fourth watch, the wind moved the hair off Mei's face and kept its palm there, and delivered the sentence it had crossed the dark to deliver. It spent one word — *Found* — and tried to write its name in wet sand, and stopped where it always stops, and Mei's hand finished the character without her knowledge. The Almanac Bureau struck *weather* and entered *family*. That evening Mei took two cups onto a roof and said *Same time tomorrow*, and the steam nodded, and the wind stayed.

Jinzhou absorbed them at speed. The city is terraced; the road becomes stairs two hundred metres in; the truck met a granite bollard in front of the entire harbour ward. The census taker asked who the head of household was and five voices said *Iuno* at once, which is worse than election, because it is affection. Amy wrote *niece* in the relation column, in front of a foreign clerk, where it can still exist in forty years. Mei's relation to Iuno went down as *co-resident*, with a remark from Assessor Lü running four lines longer than a remarks column is supposed to go. Household four-one-one, ward four, one stove, entered the twenty-first of the fifth month.

And then two things happened underneath the welcome. Iuno climbed the hill and told the Magistrate of Jinzhou, before the Magistrate had met her, that Mei does not stay dead — because a ruler facing a war will eventually run one recoverable body against a hundred and ten thousand people, and Iuno wanted the cost named before the arithmetic became convenient. Jinhsi declined to promise she would never use it, exactly as expected, and the objection was now in the room forever. Second: someone inside the Huanglong system had reassigned a Mingting berth held all season for this household, overnight, in a correct hand under a correct seal, and steered them to a frontier gate instead of the capital. No forgery occurred. Nobody knew whose hand it was.

Then Mei was summoned to City Hall and received by a bodyguard named Sanhua, who told her the Magistrate had been called away three days and had left four objects: a sugar pearl wrapped in plastic, a mangosteen, a miniature sundial, a green leaf.

That is where the story stands when it begins.

## II. FOUR OBJECTS, A SIMULATOR, AND A ROAD NORTH

*(Youngest Gate 1)*

Mei laid the four tokens on a guesthouse table and the household read them the way it reads everything: as a test, a courtesy, a dead drop, or a scavenger hunt, with paranoia they had earned. Iuno, hovering her one hand over the objects, recognized the method with horror, because it was hers: give people something to investigate so they feel autonomous while you control the frame.

Then Mei asked, lightly, whether Iuno had scared the Magistrate off, and Iuno told the room what she had done. The guesthouse went quiet in the particular way it goes quiet when nobody wants to react first. Mei asked whether it was the right call, received *I believe so*, said *Okay*, and picked up the sugar pearl. Inside an hour Yangyang sent a polite message asking Mei to see a physician. The chain Iuno had built came back link by link — the woman she briefed sent the woman she befriended to escort the woman she loves to the examination she caused — and Mei went, taking all four tokens and Yangyang for company, which was a kind of poke.

At Huaxu Academy, Mortefi cracked the sundial open on a radiograph and identified it as a mortise-and-tenon puzzle box wanting a component Xiangli Yao happened to have on a workbench. Baizhi examined Mei and found waveforms of impossible stability, near-zero Overclock risk, and hypothesized a body that consumes frequency energy internally, as though housing a mechanism. She put Mei into a Sonoro Sphere to see it under load. Communications cut. Mei stood under an enormous moon while a scythe-bearing Discord came for her and a voice said *Leave… Mother…*

The tokens resolved. The mangosteen was fruit. The leaf carried Waveworn frequencies and a set of coordinates. The pearl was an expired oral vaccine from a children's epidemic Jinzhou had barely survived twenty years ago. The sundial's sexagenary indexing sent Mei and Yangyang to an archive shelf marked *Wei*, where they found a heavily redacted resonance evaluation of a subject called the First Resonator: amnesiac, Tacet Mark on the right hand, golden glow in mark and pupils under Forte, broad Syntony, non-convergent periodic waveforms, zero risk of destabilization. That subject defeated the Crownless at the Battle of Gorges of Spirits, stood beside Jinzhou's Sentinel Jué three days later, and the city was founded that same year.

Mei carried the implication home and put a smaller version of it on the table. Chisa heard the omission immediately. Later, with the household dispersed and Amy's shower running through the wall, Iuno declined to accept *it glitched* and got the rest: the mark, the eyes, the waveforms, the founding battle, and then the moon and the two words. She said *Okay*. She did not annotate it. In the dark, after the lamp had gone out on its own, Mei tested one sentence against the ceiling — *It might not be me* — and Iuno covered the marked hand with her living one and let her have the night.

Breakfast at Panhua's supplied Iuno with an education in chili oil and the household with the next move: Mei mentioned a dream of cracked ground, a low sky, rock formations that moved wrong in the wind, and a gate with something on the other side. Yangyang set her pencil down and named the Norfall Barrens. Iuno then reported that Sanhua had registered a northern Access Pass to Mei's Terminal the previous day. The Magistrate had built the archive route and had already left a legal road north before Mei solved anything. Chixia, who could not leave her ward, sent Yangyang and a name: Captain Bao, who knows what logs omit.

At the northern checkpoint they collected a fifth traveller. Jianxin, in the grey of a Taoist order, had been standing at that barricade since the previous evening, asking each successive guard the same question because one of them might have different information. An old man had sat three hours on the steps of the east-gate letter-writer's office because his grandson Zhiyuan had stopped writing home; the letter-writer could not guarantee delivery to an active posting; Jianxin had concluded it was more efficient to go. Mei said *She comes too* before Iuno could finish an objection, and Jianxin fell into step without adjusting anything about her posture.

Beacon Seven had been stripped by three displaced civilians from the north cluster: six weeks on the road, three days without food, twenty pounds of resonance alloy under a woman's arm that she had forgotten to drop when she ran. Jianxin emptied her rations and her coin purse, counted it out flat, and held both out — enough for three tickets on the post road and meals for the journey. Then they tried to rob her. Chisa caught a wrist a second before it reached a pack strap; Lupa put herself between an old man's gutting knife and Mei and offered him, courteously, the chance to reconsider; the woman found her hand gripping a forearm that declined to move. Mei asked all three of them to look at what they had just chosen. The woman said sorry, small and raw. Jianxin said *I know* — and extended her hands a second time.

On the kilometre to the next beacon, Cartethyia worked at the part she could not follow: the woman still had a knife in her jacket, and Jianxin knew, and gave anyway, because they had not eaten in three days. Amy, who had grown up with registries and distribution points, said that in Lahai-Roi poverty had systems. Yangyang started apologizing for her country's edges and Lupa stopped her flat: *You didn't put them there.* Cartethyia, out of twenty years alone in the dark, arrived at the thing that had actually frightened her — three desperate people together can still hurt each other before they hurt you.

Beacon Nine was intact, offline, and surrounded by the dispersal pattern of four or five Discords that had retreated that morning.

The Midnight Rangers' camp had been built for three months and was entering its seventh. Captain Xiulan had eleven resonance casualties, a resupply three days late, probes at the north gate she could not answer, and twelve names on the operations board carrying stars, which is the notation for personnel who went in and whose status is unresolved. Twelve minutes into the conversation her comm crackled: an Overclocked Resonator in cascade at the third staging ground, combustion class, forty metres from the medical station.

Rong was twenty-two and had been fighting since the previous morning. Ranger Xu Jun had gone in before anyone understood the risk and had been talking to her for thirteen minutes with nothing left to talk with. Jianxin volunteered because a Resonator in cascade is a problem of somewhere for the energy to go, and she could give it somewhere to go. Lupa went in because she has been combustion class for years and knows what it feels like when the flame has nowhere except back in. Jianxin opened gradients — channels, the mathematical equivalent of opened hands. Lupa took what Rong was throwing and spread it through her own Forte. It took twenty-three minutes. Mei stood at the tent entrance and did not go in, and noticed that this was new, and stood there anyway.

The camp did not relax so much as exhale, and the household distributed itself into it without instruction. Iuno went to find Xiulan, because somebody was sitting alone with twelve names and had not been relieved in two days. Amy sat down on a quartermaster's crate and rebuilt his ledger. Chisa crouched beside a Ranger whose resonance injury had left a tone in her left ear that ruined her sense of distance, and asked where the tone was highest, and sat with her in specific silence. Cartethyia stood in front of the twelve starred names and wrote all of them into a cloth-covered notebook, because she makes dolls so names can stay somewhere and a notebook is a doll with pages. Ciaccona found the camp's oldest staff sergeant and asked him a badly formed question so that it would feel like talking. Lupa sat in the sun against the medical station wall and let her body work out what it had absorbed, and declined to mention it.

Jianxin found Zhiyuan on a supply crate at the Rearguards staging area with six days of rehearsed arguments loaded. She let him say all of it — the north valley, the friends who got out and the ones who did not, the unbearable arithmetic in which ordinary people can only watch Resonators die — and then said *Hit me*. The spar lasted eight seconds and used no Forte at all; she turned his momentum the way you turn water. She gave him her hand out of the dirt and told him he was not ready, and that this was temporary.

Before he left he took her to the memorial turret at the supply depot, where Mei was already sitting on the ground with her back against the wall. Forty-three names ran from knee height to above her head. Zhiyuan knew twenty-two of them. He told Mei who they actually were: Fang Ruoxi, a cartographer who drew maps of the camp in her off hours for no reason except that she liked maps; Bao Sheng, twenty-six, with a daughter he never met, who could cook five things badly and tried anyway; two brothers posted a week apart and dead a week apart, filed as simultaneous because nobody knew how else to write it; Wen Daoyi, twenty-four, who sat at the east wall every night off rotation because it was worse if nobody was watching the dark, and who died facing north; Kuo Liang, the worst card player in camp, a schoolteacher before the Lament, who kept chalk in his jacket to the end. Mei listened without offering consolation, without saying their sacrifice was worth something, and without locating the moment when he had said enough. Yangyang came round the corner at the seventh name and stopped, having walked past that turret on three rotations without ever feeling entitled to stop.

When he finished, Mei asked only whether he would remember them at home. He said that was the plan. She said *Good. They need somebody to.* Then Yangyang sat down near her, opened the patrol log to a clean page, and wrote all twenty-two in the second ink — the private one, the one for things she intends to keep. Mei called her *Night Window* and thanked her.

Iuno, watching Xu Jun's hands shake almost invisibly outside the medical station, worked out what the four tokens had actually been measuring: whether these people could look at what the puzzle was protecting, and decline to look away. A city founded by someone who survives being killed had learned in its bones that the cost of surviving is unevenly distributed, and had built the carrying into its structure — hand-lit lamps, hand-synchronized clocks, a manual evening — and put the part that pays for it as far north as it could, under the heading *unresolved*.

That evening on the south shelf, Mei told Amy what she had told Iuno, and Amy covered her marked hand without looking at it.

Two days later, in a village called Qichi, a man in a coat with red tails would step out of a burned threshold and say: *You're very late, Rover.*

## III. THE CAMP, AND THE SHAPE IN THE PLAIN

*(Youngest Gate 2)*

At the third bell the west transport took Zhiyuan home. Amy and Lupa quietly restocked the pack Jianxin had emptied — four meal packets, a stoppered bottle, and the gutting knife from the road wrapped in clean cloth, with instructions to put the blade in the inner sleeve because the edge catches on canvas. Jianxin observed that it would last four days; Lupa observed that the trip would last one, and told her to eat generously. Zhiyuan asked what he was supposed to tell his grandfather. Jianxin gave him the truth in three sentences: he reached the camp, his reasons arrived ahead of his training, he is coming home to prepare his body. Mei told him to put the twenty-two names in the letter. He said he would need a lot of paper. She told him to use both sides.

Yangyang wrote *twenty-two names carried south* in the second ink and drew a line under it.

The ridge road closes at dusk, so the household stayed. Iuno saw what standing had cost Xiulan and negotiated a temporary command with five lines of terms in Xiulan's own hand: two bells, messages and supplies and personnel already assigned, every deployment still Xiulan's, Yangyang countersigning, and Cartethyia empowered to say when Iuno was becoming frightening. Xiulan asked what Cartethyia's function was. Cartethyia straightened up and told her. The camp seal went on the table where everyone under the canopy could see it. Iuno's interior High Priestess observed that a younger self would have called this an appalling decline in standards, and then conceded that a younger self needed sleep.

Xiulan slept eighty-three minutes with her boots on. While she did, Cartethyia cut twelve small figures out of spoiled requisition forms, lettered each with a name, folded them at the waist, and stood them along the edge of the operations map, because the unresolved need somewhere to stand while people look for them. One leaned; she adjusted its feet and let the tilt remain.

Ciaccona brought Staff Sergeant Du under the canopy with three weeks of watch rotations in his memory, and the evening probes resolved into a pattern: they arrive in measures of four, the third measure shifts east by one ridge, each contact lasts exactly twelve minutes, and on the fourth measure the camp answers — weapon fire, retrieval teams, casualty transport — and the Discords leave on the next bar. Cartethyia moved four paper figures away from the north wall and along the medical route and said it out loud: they are learning how the camp moves. Iuno's warm hand went flat on the map. She put the observation in front of everyone before the next contact, with its maker's name attached.

Across the depot, Amy turned the quartermaster's ledger sideways and stopped counting sacks. Eighteen supper bowls for active watch, eleven soft meals for medical, six held for late rotation, one for the person doing the arithmetic. Quartermaster Hao added it up, got thirty-six, and discovered he had been assigned a place at his own table. Lupa took the cookfire off a corporal with an injured hand and built stew out of four onions, seven soft roots, ambitious greens and one wrinkled pepper. Mei hauled twenty-seven buckets, and when Amy asked for a number, gave the count and then, after using the extra second, the information that actually mattered: *I'm hungry.* Lupa put flatbread in her hand at once.

Then Chisa and an injured Ranger named Lian, sitting with three empty mess tins at measured intervals, isolated a signal inside the tone in Lian's damaged ear: three pulls, a gap, then two, repeating since midday. Yangyang identified the east patrol's distress cadence requesting a bearing. A standard flare would give them the bearing and hand the firing point to the north shelf. Ciaccona proposed a three-note answer carried on Aero resonance, which would read as field noise to anything watching for light. Yangyang's range ended eight hundred metres short in the crosswind.

Cartethyia said *I can give the signal height*, and light opened around her, and Fleurdelys lifted the brass signal frame above the south wall at full reach, higher than the relay mast. Yangyang came to attention beneath her. The familiar pressure arrived at her cheek and she told it to take a chair and dinner, and a sleeve of warm air rolled along her forearm and stayed there in reserve. Chisa moved Yangyang's right foot half a boot-width and aimed her off the returning interference. Amy called the window. Ciaccona played, and Aero resonance took the notes east along the ridge, over the quarry shelf, down beyond sight. Lian pointed east: one long pulse. They had the bearing. Yangyang's shoulders shook once and Mei held her elbow until it passed and asked for the count. Ten went out that morning; fourteen were standing there now.

Captain Bao and Doctor Tang were among them. Tang restored the medical station. Bao laid a dirt-stiff map over Iuno's probe marks: the resupply convoy was safe inside Shuanghe's ancestral hall under Ranger Ma with twelve civilians; the Discord formation had spent three evenings tracing old Tacet scars, drawing a perimeter through the barrens with the camp on its short side. Ciaccona laid Du's four measures beside the route. Iuno rotated the map a quarter-turn and the shape settled. Mei's right hand warmed. Bao told her to go to the south shelf above the water tanks at sunset, because paper gives you the pieces and the ridge gives you the whole mark, and added that today they had drawn closer to finishing.

Cartethyia stood Bao in camp and left Ma in Shuanghe facing south. Ten figures still had nowhere to stand, and everyone under the canopy could see the difference.

Iuno returned the seal at the end of her term with a status report and no request for extension. Xiulan, with a canvas seam pressed into her cheek, asked them to stay through morning.

Rong woke to Tang's lamp, Xu Jun asleep in a chair with soot on his fingertips, and Lupa on the floor with a bowl on one knee. Rong saw the singed fur along her forearm and asked what it cost. Lupa told her: an afternoon in the sun and a patch of fur, and that Rong owed her body the larger account. Rong said she wanted her post back. Lupa told her to heal with the commitment she gave the wall, because recovery is her next watch, and put the bowl in her hands.

Outside, Ciaccona found a tuning under the clatter of bowls, Du supplied a marching song about a magistrate, a goat, and an unlucky bathhouse, Amy improved the goat's legal argument, and three Rangers claimed authorship and produced contradictory older versions. Cartethyia threaded grain-sack fibre through the creases of her paper people so their feet would hold. Chisa seated Lian where the cookfire lay on one side and the relay mast on the other, so each sound kept its own place. Iuno received a bowl, discovered that rank carries no privilege at that kitchen, and sat in the dirt beside Mei. When Mei mentioned twenty-seven buckets, Iuno said twenty-five had reached their destination. Mei asked whether she had counted. Iuno said: *I count everything I love. Occasionally the figures include buckets.*

At sunset, Mei and Yangyang climbed the six natural steps of the south shelf, and the Norfall plains opened underneath them in long folds toward Jinzhou. Tacet scars crossed the country from the northern ridges, violet-black at the centre and silver along their raised edges, branching with the intimate geometry of a Resonator's mark. Life occupied every space the strokes allowed: Shuanghe around its ancestral hall, Lingshu beside its irrigation cut, white birds lifting off a flooded terrace, jujubes over boundary walls, one vast scholar tree grown through a split in the plain with half its trunk black from an old strike and its whole crown catching the sunset. Beyond all of it Jinzhou rose in terraces, City Hall's top windows burning gold, the ninth tower throwing a thin shadow down the north road.

Mei's right hand began to glow. The mark shone gold along its lower branch, and across the plains the western scar held the same turn. She spread her fingers over Bao's map and the light from her palm crossed the pencil lines. That branch had been completed that morning; Beacon Nine went dark as the formation withdrew from it. The golden edge ran south between Shuanghe's roofs and the old scholar tree, crossed the irrigation cut, and aimed at the highest terrace. City Hall sat on the line.

Mei said she wanted the archive's answer, and dinner, and night watch, and Rong's opinion of the stew, and that she could hold all four for one evening. Then she asked Yangyang who holds the Outrider's post. Yangyang listed them: Chixia's breakfast and complaints, Baizhi checking her routes against clinic admissions, a mother who sends a flute across the country when she thinks her daughter needs breath, a shijie reading over her shoulder, and a household with one stove that had filed an aggressive invitation. Mei bumped her shoulder and said *Leave a bowl there.*

The last rim of sun touched the highland. Frequency light entered the northern scar and travelled south beneath grass and road, and the scholar tree lifted every leaf as it passed under its roots, and the line went on toward the city. Mei's mark answered once in her palm. Yangyang asked which way the doors in her dream had swung. Mei turned her glowing hand toward Jinzhou, where human hands were carrying lamp flame down each terrace and village cooking fires were waking one by one inside the mark, and said: *South. The city stood beyond them.*

## IV. QICHI, AND THE FABLE OF THE BLACK LAMB

*(Youngest Gate 3)*

Morning came up over the highland with iron in it. Yangyang stood above the camp with cold tea and tasted the front on the back of her tongue — copper, smoke, wet stone, and blood carried in on a stream out of the northern ravine. The wind came up the slope and stayed low around her boots, dense with the breath of field hospitals and turned soil, and she told it that the people here already knew where the smell came from.

Doctor Tang had found something. Jinhsi's cassia leaf, sitting in his ward overnight, had taken up a residual fluctuation from the subterranean pulse: human frequencies and Tacet Discord frequencies overlapping every forty-seven seconds, repeating, and growing stronger by the hour. Laid under a transparent grid, the leaf's veins resolved to a point in the Central Plains. Yangyang put her finger beside a reservoir symbol and named it: Qichi Village. Eighty-six households on the registry. Last supply notice three months ago. Two patrols down the southern road since, both reporting empty fields and a closed gate — a word Bao wanted replaced with a better verb. Chisa told him they would bring him one.

The morning count was twenty-three. Mei asked for it and told Yangyang to keep the homeward tally warm.

They came down through terraces of grass silvered by old resonance burns, and a league on, another current crossed the iron. Yangyang stopped so hard that Ciaccona's shoulder brushed hers. Her Forte opened around the stream and the whole village arrived at once: a fingernail across stone, a mouth full of earth, a prayer through a broken tooth, a hundred private griefs pressed together until their edges became one pressure against her ribs, with malice moving bright and eager through the centre, pulling every cry into tune. She went down onto her palms and her knees. Chisa came round in front of her and gave her a narrower door and a count — four in, six out — and the village withdrew from the inside of her skull and became landscape again. Iuno put her six paces back from the forward edge with Chisa guarding her breathing, and Yangyang, who would once have insisted that usefulness requires maximum exposure, weighed the route and accepted it.

Qichi lay inside a ring of black grass, its reservoir holding a perfectly blue surface while the reeds along its banks leaned away from the water. The instant Mei crossed the ring, rain rose from the ground — silver drops climbing past her face, each carrying a voice folded inside it — and the square split open.

They fought as one instrument. Amy tracked the forty-seven-second cycle and tagged the living harmonics so nobody's strike would take them. Ciaccona struck a low chord and drew the human voices out ragged and individual so Chisa could find the black pulse braided among them and close her scissors on it. Iuno's arrow pinned the shrine bell's vibration against a roof beam and held the note as a visible silver cord. Fleurdelys came down through the crown of a Discord wearing a fan of ancestral plaques across its spine, and Yangyang caught the bursting plaques in a spiral of air before they touched the rupture, because the names were anchors. Lupa broke and burned the ritual roots under the shrine floor. On the downbeat Mei put her marked palm against a clay vessel painted with white lambs and took the pulse into her body — a crowd running through a single doorway — and carried it through her shoulder and her sword and drove it into the empty sky, where it opened like an ink flower. Fleurdelys split the vessel. The field collapsed, and the silver rain fell in its proper direction.

One Discord remained, under the dead pear tree at the gate. It stood at Yangyang's shoulder height and sloped like a person under a soaked cloak, and its dim pearl core pulsed the same torn whisper: *Brother… help…* Amy's dial read its aggression at zero. Yangyang opened her Forte toward it and heard an old man's last breath, a child choking on smoke, a young woman crying until the word tore — and beneath all of them one clear sustained call. She lowered her sword. The field had gathered the village's frequencies; most had scattered when it was purified; this one had formed around a plea that kept its shape. The creature turned its covered head toward the reservoir and walked away between two houses.

The search told the rest. In the ancestral hall, three generations of plaques hung with their faces turned to the wall; when Mei turned one, the formal name had been cut away, leaving one character at the bottom: *daughter*. Every plaque around it carried the same wound. Crescent stains under the dust marked where bodies had knelt in every household. ## V. THE SANCTUM

*(Youngest Gate 4)*

Back in the city, Chixia checked Qichi's historical files and found the old criminal case closed long ago, the responsible parties punished, most villagers relocated — and no record whatsoever of a girl called A-Ling. Iuno reversed the household's first reading of Jinhsi's role: the Fractsidus had already been preparing to lure Mei there so Scar could preach, and Jinhsi had learned of the plan and sent them along the same path first. The sundial gave up its last message. The Academy had supplied the Shichen of Wei; Qichi had supplied South and the Vermilion Bird; Mei aligned the two symbols and the box opened on a map of Jinzhou's Grand Library. Chisa asked whether City Hall was Jinzhou's centre, and declined to say why she was asking.

*You're welcome.*

## VI. HAND TALK

*(Youngest Gate 5 — 手談, the same afternoon, in two rooms four kilometres apart)*

### The sanctum

The escort took Scar down a service stair with eleven turns in it. Amy counted them without meaning to. The room he left behind had a reading table on three legs and a stack of tax rolls, a panel hanging off its top hinge, and dust still moving in a bar of light because the air in that room had been somewhere else four minutes earlier and had come back changed.

Amy picked the black-lamb card off the flagstones, held it to the light, found nothing in it, and put it in her jacket, because a thing like that left on a floor gets swept up by somebody paid four coppers a day. Then she said, standing, that she wanted the list — everything Jinhsi knew before they walked in, in order, starting from the day she left a mangosteen.

Jinhsi told her to sit down. Amy said she was fine. Jinhsi said it ran to seven. Amy sat on the flagstones with her back to a shelf-end and her arms round her knees and said *Go.*

One: two Fractsidus apertures inside the prefecture in the third month and four by the fifth, increasing at a rate inconsistent with reconnaissance. Two: an Overseer designated Scar asking after Qichi through three intermediaries since winter, one of whom is a fruit factor who has no idea what he is. Three: Qichi's file closed forty years ago in a manner she would refuse from a clerk of her own, by a hand belonging to a man now dead whose widow receives a pension Jinhsi has declined to stop. Four: a cassia leaf carrying a Waveworn signature that would put the household in the highlands within four days, with a Ranger camp between them and the village, so that whatever they found would be found in company. Five: he wants witnesses, and has wanted witnesses for eleven years, and every site he has left in that prefecture has been left legible. Six: the Grand Library's fourth level has an approach absent from every plan filed with the Ministry, and the plan it *is* on was copied in a capital scriptorium in a year when a man now working in Jinhsi's registry was a copyist there — and Jinhsi had stopped correcting the copy.

Seven was what she failed to know. The wards are four hundred years old, keyed to the lake, never once failed, and the survey in her own file is wrong. She and Sanhua were positioned for a man in a room. He took the room away.

Amy asked what the plan had been if they had stayed inside. The pause had a floor to it. *I would have written to your household.* Amy said that is a letter. Jinhsi agreed twice. Amy's knuckles went the colour of the plaster; then she let go of her knees and said *Okay. Thanks* — because Jinhsi could have stopped at six and had gone to seven. Mei watched her daughter do that and kept her mouth shut, which had taken four years and a god's dark to learn, because the old version of herself would have crossed the room and put her body between Amy and a nineteen-year-old with a city on her back, and the old version had raised a girl who copied it perfectly all the way into the Nihility.

Jinhsi cleared the entire afternoon — Censorate, harbour levy, and a general who had moved himself up the schedule uninvited — and sent for tea in the green jar.

Then she gave Mei the dangerous thing. The First Resonator file has a sealed annexe holding eleven redactions made in the ninth year of the second reign. Three Magistrates have held the key; two left it in the drawer. Jinhsi has had it four years and taken it out twice, and has never opened it, because the moment she knows, she has to write it down. A rumour she may decline; a fact she must forward. If the annexe confirms what she believes it confirms, then by the eleventh of the seventh month there is a memorandum in the Censorate, a query from Rites about the naming of a shrine, a request from the Board of War regarding the disposition of an asset, and a letter from the capital — and by the ninth month a Grand Marshal who takes an unhidden interest in this household has a policy about Mei. So she built a route that did not run through her office: a fruit, a sweet, a leaf, a piece of clockwork, and four days of Mei's own judgement, with no line anywhere in the registry saying the Magistrate told her. The key was in the third drawer, on the left, and Mei could have it.

Mei declined. Somewhere with worse chairs, she said — because opening it in that room with Jinhsi standing in it turns it into evidence, and then it becomes a thing about her that the whole city gets to hold an end of before she has had one afternoon to be sad about it on her own. Jinhsi said that was correct, all of it, and that she would have said it less well.

While Jinhsi was speaking, she turned her cup a quarter-turn on the table by the rim and settled it. A moment later Mei, with no part of her mind attending, reached out and turned her own cup a quarter-turn by the rim and settled it. The room went very quiet. Both of them saw it. Amy saw both of them see it, had four clever things available and one of them halfway up her throat, and put her teeth together and let it pass.

Jinhsi then explained the objects. The pearl was chosen because the coughing epidemic of the fourth reign was ended by a woman with a laboratory, a wheelbarrow and nine assistants over two winters, and Jinhsi wanted that fact in Mei's pocket before anybody offered her a cheaper story about how plagues end. The leaf was a warning wearing a clue's coat. The sundial was the route. The mangosteen was a mangosteen: in season eleven days, and it was the ninth, and they had come two thousand kilometres in a truck. Amy, appalled and delighted, said she had put a snack in a spy puzzle. Jinhsi said she had put a puzzle around a snack.

Then Mei told her how the pearl's meaning had changed under a lake, and gave her A-Ling's name, and Jinhsi received it as a filing: the girl's own words, her own hand, entered beside the ledger page that priced her, which is a doctrine of that office with a number attached. Iuno had already begun the paperwork.

Amy had been watching Jinhsi's left hand for eleven minutes. It had been closed since before the escort left. She said so — declining to ask what Scar had whispered, and stating only that she had clocked it, so that Jinhsi could keep it on purpose instead of by acting. Jinhsi opened the hand. It was empty, and had always been empty, and carried four nail-marks across the palm. She said she intended to verify before she repeated, because he builds distrust out of true materials. Amy asked with whom. *With the Sentinel.* Amy asked how that was going, and got told she was extremely rude, and answered that she was extremely fast and rude is a side effect.

Then she gave Jinhsi the warning nobody had ever given her. Amy is nineteen with twenty-nine years in her; ten of them she spent in a hole under a city holding a door for two hundred thousand people, most of whom never saw her face and all of whom knew her voice. What nobody tells you, she said, is that the wishing keeps going after you start answering. It gets worse, because now it works. The queue lengthens, the wishes grow, and one day you run dry and they are all still standing there wishing, and by then the wish has a face and the face is yours, and they will be so kind about it — they will bring you soup, and that is the part that gets you. Amy's escape had been a radio and a sentence: *I am a girl with a machine, and the machine has a maintenance schedule.* Jinhsi cannot say that. Jinhsi is the machine.

Mei got off the table, declined both the standing-over position and the taking-a-knee position, and sat on the floor beside the chair with her back to a shelf-end, the same as her daughter, and offered a house rule: when it gets loud, say so out loud, and there is no second part. Jinhsi asked, flat, in the voice she keeps for true things: *To whom.* Mei let it sit long enough to be honest and then said: start with the wolf. She is a disaster at comfort — she will say something rude, hand you a flask, and never bring it up again as long as you live. Best service in the house. Amy offered herself as well, on the grounds that she is up at four anyway. Jinhsi said that Counselor Changli tells her to eat. Mei said that is one person. Jinhsi said it is a very good one. Amy asked whether she ever tells her to stop, and Jinhsi turned her cup a quarter-turn and drank, and said *Once*, and declined to say when.

Then Mei asked Chisa's question. Jinhsi answered that City Hall is the geometric centre of Jinzhou: there is a stone under the floor of the room below with a hole four fingers across, the hole took the survey pin, and every wall, ward boundary, gate and one of the nine towers was laid out from that pin. The compound was the first structure; the wall came afterward, and came *to* it. The Tacet scars point at it. She has four theories about why, and three of them are the same theory in different coats.

Mei asked whether she played weiqi. *Badly and constantly.* Mei mentioned the point in the middle. Jinhsi named it: 天元, the origin of heaven — worthless for two hundred moves, because you cannot make territory in the middle and you cannot run to a side from it, and then in the endgame every fight on the board runs into it, and it is worth the game.

Then she corrected Mei's metaphor. There are four hands on this board and she can account for two. The first is the Fractsidus, who hold that the sentence was already written by somebody who never asked, and who intend to take the pen and write the next one themselves — and portions of whose material are correct, which is the single most inconvenient fact in the prefecture. The second is her own office: herself, her Counselor, a general who tells her when she is wrong, and eleven thousand people who go to work. The third is Ovathrax, which does not play; it is a hand off the board entirely, the weather over the board, and there exists a printing in which it has already won here, and the word that printing uses is *held*, and she has looked up what that word costs. The tremors past the north face stood at six days that afternoon. They had been eight in the spring.

The fourth is a stone that came off a different board. There is a wind in her city that obeys no opening she has ever read, carries the dead down to the lake at fourth watch, appears on no roster, takes no wage, belongs to no ministry, has been entered by the Almanac Bureau under 接風 and ruled out of *weather* and into *family*, and the second ruling stands. It came out of a Jinzhou that burned, walked here across the dark between two printings, arrived with a purse of about forty words, and has been spending them on other people at a rate that will finish it. Jinhsi stopped trying to read it in the fourth month and doubled the kitchen budget instead. Mei said *Every wind is met once*, and Jinhsi asked where she got it, and Mei said off a shell, one word a night, and did the punctuation herself — and added, in case anybody was still filing, that it is a good wind. Jinhsi said that is entered as well.

Amy pushed for the two unaccounted hands. Jinhsi went somewhere else first: the lake dreams backward and has for four hundred years, and the court has twice ruled that memory is admissible and prophecy is not, and she keeps that ruling framed because it is the only thing standing between her lake and the Ministry of Rites. On the night after the kite, it dreamed forward once, for the length of a held breath, and showed her a sound and declined to show her the road. The sound was the ninth tower. Eight towers answer along the wall for a column counted returning; the ninth stands on the north face, looks up the north road, and has stayed silent since the dark, because the column it is kept for never came back down that road, and a tower keeps quiet for a road with nobody on it. On the thirtieth of the fifth month, eight beats went along that wall at full morning for a column of one, on Jinhsi's order, given before she knew Mei's name.

And since the morning at the shore, the lake has shown her nothing. Nineteen nights. She goes down at fourth watch and kneels at the water and asks, and the water is water. The Ministry would call that peace. She has been calling it a held breath, in her own head, where nobody has to sign it.

So the two hands she cannot account for are these. If the Sentinel was sealed in the spring, then her office has run some part of a year on prophecy that came from somewhere, and she has been conveying it, and believing it, and does not know whose hand it was. If the Sentinel is unsealed, then it went quiet of its own accord on the morning a foreigner lay down at a waterline and let a dead woman finish a twenty-year errand, and she does not know what that means either. One of them may be a god. One of them may be a forgery. And she is obliged to run a city, a war, and a sluice with a two-hundred-breath interval in it at the same speed either way.

Mei asked: *Are you a player, or are they playing you?* The Magistrate of Jinzhou put her head back against the chair and was nineteen all the way through for exactly as long as it took dust to cross one flagstone, and said there is no difference at this point, and that no matter what, she will do what she must. Mei said there is a third thing on a board. She has been the placed stone, set down on a shore to die on a schedule, and good at it. She has been the hand, with a whole pack and a plan eleven moves deep, and good at that. Both left her exactly where she was. What moved her was somebody who came and sat down next to her and refused to leave. Jinhsi said that is unavailable to her. Mei said it is sitting on your floor.

Later, in the doorway, Mei invited her to supper at Panhua's back room. Jinhsi said the Magistrate does not eat in the harbour ward. Amy said, from the stair: *Then Jinhsi does.*

### The field

Four kilometres east, past the willows, Lupa stopped dead in the road with her nose working and said they were cutting the sorghum green — four hundred hectares of it, sap in the air where a ripe cut smells like a warm floor.

Iuno had already known. On Tuesday, in a remissions ledger opened in the harbour ward for an entirely different reason, she had found harvest-loss remission granted *in advance of loss*, for eleven villages and sixty-one households, on one day, under one chop, at a fifth above assessed value. Advance remission is what a state does when it already knows. Somebody in Huanglong had decided to lose the plain and had begun paying for it out of politeness.

Under an oiled canvas awning stood a trestle, a strongbox with a soldier reading a novel badly on top of it, three clerks, and a queue forty people long standing calm with its hats off, talking about a wedding, with water put out and a bench made that morning for the old — the cut ends of the planks still pale. In the middle of the field, in a coat the colour of a struck match, a woman held a paper umbrella over an old man who was refusing to sign.

His name was Bo. She used it eleven times in four minutes and never once as a lever. Fifty-one years, he said; his father's hands on the drill for the first row; and taking money for it standing green means it is lost, and the paper will say the year he gave up. She asked the new clerk, Wen — thirty years old, six days in the job — whether there was a schedule for seed-stock purchase for the resettlement wards. There was: Schedule Four, purchase at valuation, for displaced agricultural households. She asked whether Bo's standing crop was seed-grade. Wen walked out into the sorghum, came back with a head of it, studied it for a long time doing arithmetic that had nothing to do with grain, and said it was. She struck the remission line, thanked him for catching it, and told Bo he was selling four hectares of seed-stock to the prefecture, to be sown in the eighth month north of the lake where the ground is heavier and wants a hardy strain, on a receipt that names the strain and the grower — because it goes into the record as a supply, and somebody eats it next autumn, and the ledger says whose it was. He signed with a shaking hand and was furious about the shaking. She told Wen he had found the schedule faster than she had, which was a lie of the specific kind that costs the liar nothing and pays the other party for a year.

Lupa, at Iuno's shoulder: *She didn't lie once.* Iuno: *No.* Lupa: *That's worse.*

The woman closed the umbrella, looked at them across sixty metres of cut field as though waiting for the paragraph to end, and came over. Up close she was younger than the coat and older than the face, with burn-scars up the inside of her left forearm shaped like feathers, worn bare, the way a person wears a thing she has decided to stop explaining. Changli, Counselor to the Magistrate. She said she had wanted to meet them for some time and had twice found a reason to postpone it, which she would rather say now than have them work out later. Then she opened the umbrella over Iuno's crystal arm, explained about a woman in the dye-yards with a glass hand who loses an afternoon every summer to a headache behind that eye, and said Iuno could tell her to stop.

Iuno put five facts on the ground at once, to save an hour. The Mingting berth was reassigned by Changli. The Magistrate's three-day absence fell where it fell because Changli put it there. The four objects were the Magistrate's, and the week they were given in was Changli's. Scar walked into the Grand Library because a survey has been wrong for eleven years and somebody stopped correcting it. And fifth: Iuno would have done all four in that order with better paperwork and would have been wrong every time, and she was aware that saying so first is itself a manoeuvre, and she was doing it anyway.

Changli said yes to all five. Denial is a move that works once, and she would rather spend it in the winter.

Her reasons came in three of a possible seven. In the third month the tremors past the north face went from nine days to eight, and eight is the number at which the Bureau's model stops being a model and becomes a calendar. On the ninth of the fourth month a red kite went out of Mengzhou against the prevailing wind carrying an edition of one, and Bookbinders' Row is a very bad place to keep a secret, and the character-count of what the wind spent buying it is public gossip on that street. And a Marshal had held a berth at Mingting all season for a party out of Lahai-Roi and had begun speaking fondly of it in front of people who write things down. Mingting is the capital of a country at peace; whatever this household was going to become, it was going to become that in a room with four ministries in it and a protocol officer assigning the seats. So she moved them to a frontier prefecture with one magistrate, one counselor, one bodyguard and no protocol officer at all. Iuno said: you moved us where you could see us. Changli said: I moved you where you could be let alone, and those are the same act performed at different distances, and she declined to pretend she took no pleasure in their being the same act. Lupa said *You put my pack in a war.* Changli said the war was addressed to this gate before the household existed as a household; she chose the gate they would be standing at when it arrived, and she chose the one with the deeper kitchens, and Lupa could take that as an insult, though it was offered as an argument. Lupa took it as an argument and reserved the insult for her own time.

On the three days: the Magistrate's absence was genuine. There is a thing Jinhsi does alone, three or four times a year, which she has never described and which Changli has never asked about, and the request that took her away came from the source it always comes from. Changli moved it eleven days forward on the schedule. That is the extent of her involvement and also the whole of the operation, because an Overseer who has wanted witnesses for eleven years will keep clear of a sanctum with a Magistrate sitting in it, and will enter it in the week the Magistrate is provably out of the prefecture and the fourth level is being visited by a foreigner with an interesting hand — because that is the week the room becomes interesting. *I have never in my life chosen another person's move, Registrar. I choose the hour at which they are asked.*

Iuno sat with the size of that, because it was better than anything she had ever built. Thirty years of arranging what people knew — a document withheld, a witness posted, a corridor lit at the correct end — and every one of those is a content move, and every content move leaves a fingerprint, and the fingerprints had cost her an arm and eleven years. This woman had never touched content. She moves calendars. There is no audit for a calendar.

Iuno named what it had cost: her daughter — she used the word deliberately, having no other — and a woman she intends to marry the moment she stops being reasonable about it, in a sealed room with a man who kills people by explaining things to them, neither of them asked. Changli agreed that this was no defence and offered a reason instead, which is a smaller thing: he was going to reach Mei somewhere. Three intermediaries, eleven years, and a talent. He was going to arrive in a doorway, or a market, or a room with her daughter asleep in it. Changli chose a room with two prepared Resonators in the ceiling and a lake under the floor, and it was insufficient, because the survey was wrong. Lupa observed, drily, that this was the seventh thing on the Magistrate's list four kilometres away, and that the two of them were having the same conversation and could not hear each other, and that it was very funny from where she was sitting.

Then Changli asked the question Jinhsi had declined to ask. She left aside whether Mei comes back and went straight to the interval. *How long.* Because *does not stay dead* is a marvel and worth nothing to a general; what is worth something to a general is the interval. Four hours is a doctrine. Eleven days is a reserve. A season is a rumour, and rumours stay out of the order of battle. Iuno had run that arithmetic herself, in a hallway in Septimont, in the fourth year, with a pen. She refused, and said the Counselor would obtain it elsewhere without the part Iuno would have attached to it, and wanted that on the record between them. Changli thanked her for refusing quickly and turned, without weight, to ask Wen about the second column — which was worse than everything else she had done all day.

Then Lupa put her cup down in the dust and said *Ask me.*

She gave the numbers flat, at the pace of a woman describing weather to somebody who has to work outdoors. Blade is quickest — clean hole, up before the blood is tacky. Fire is slow and she comes back wrong for a day, will not be touched, sits by a window. Drowning is the worst; lungs argue; Lupa sat with her nine hours on a beach once and she came up swearing in a language none of them speak. Fastest clocked, under an hour. Slowest, three days and a bit, and that was a god doing it on purpose. Nine deaths witnessed, and she has come up every time — *that's the number you actually want and nobody'll say out loud, so: every time.*

Iuno went the colour of the canvas. Lupa's answer to her was that holding it back so nobody could use it was a good instinct and bad maths — Changli was getting it regardless, and then she would have a number and no terms. So now she has the number, she has it off Lupa, and Lupa is charging for it: when this prefecture spends a member of that household — and it will, because Lupa has met four of her kind and they all do — somebody says it to her face, in a room, out loud, before. A person standing in front of her, saying the words, while she still has a mouth to answer with.

Changli said the clause exists. Contingency Nine, fourth amendment, first article: the Magistrate gives the sluice order from the north wall in sight of the lower city, and never from the Heights, written by Jinhsi herself four years ago in a lantern-maker's light — *If I drown those streets I will do it looking at them* — copied fair by Changli, who has read it perhaps two hundred times. Lupa said: then say it again with my name under it. And the Counselor to the Magistrate of Jinzhou stood up off a crate in a cut field, put her hands together, and gave it in the phrasing of the frontier court, binding upon her office and her person: no life belonging to household four-one-one, ward four, committed by this prefecture without the commitment being spoken to that person's face, in a room, in advance, by a person who is present — and where she is able to be that person, she will be. Wen entered it in the day-book, out of sequence, marked *out of sequence* to spare the auditor a hunt, verbatim, then her name, then Lupa's. It took him two minutes and his hand was unsteady, and a wolf stood over a trestle in a field of cut sorghum and watched a clerk in a paper sleeve-guard put a clause into the permanent record of a foreign country.

Iuno, following the house rule, said out loud that she was angry and grateful and could not separate them and would be no use to anybody about it until roughly tomorrow. Lupa said *You'll get there. Takes you a night. Drink your tea.*

Walking them out to the road, Changli produced a rebound book — old boards oiled to the grain of a river stone, a new spine, a great many blank pages sewn in at the back. Her teacher's game record: one game recorded move by move for thirty-one years, left unfinished, with the truth said to be in it, by a Secretary of the Board of Rites who liked a joke and hated being explained to and died in a corridor of something ordinary. Thirty years of deciding whether that was a bequest or a prank. The record gives moves and never once gives a name.

Iuno, who does not play and has never had the patience for a game requiring her to let the other person finish, read it as a document, because a document has columns, and columns can be counted. Fourth page from the back: *Black plays twice.* Moves two hundred and six and two hundred and seven, both Black, no White between them, the numbering unbroken, and he went on recording for another eleven years afterward. Four people over thirty years had told Changli four things: a scribal error he was too proud to correct; a handicap game recorded at the placement of the stones; two people sharing Black with one arriving late; and somebody else sat down.

Iuno said they were all wrong. Two hundred and six is in the lower left. Two hundred and seven is in the upper right, nine lines from anything Black has touched in eighty moves. That is a man recording *two games on one board* under one numbering, because he only ever had the one book. Count the parity: even means two, odd means three — or he sat down at somebody else's board once and wrote that down too, because he had the book with him. Changli went absolutely still in the way that means a person is receiving something new, and bowed, shallowly, colleague to colleague, and said she had shown that page to eleven people in thirty years and Iuno was the first to count the columns instead of reading the game. Iuno said she does not play. Changli said: *I am aware. That is why.*

Lupa found the thing in the back: a card of Fractsidus stock, blank on both sides, no lamb, no slash, no mark of any kind, which had come in the post in the fourth month, correctly stamped through the Jinzhou east office, with a receipt, and had sat at that page for four months while Changli failed to decide whether it was an invitation or a joke. Iuno named it in four words. It is a *pass*: when a player has nothing worth playing, they hand over a stone and say nothing, and it is legal, and it is the politest thing on the board, and it means *I am still here, and it is your turn.* Changli said that is worse. Iuno agreed.

Then Iuno asked what she had come out for. Does Jinhsi know? She knows there is a channel out of her registry to the Fractsidus, because Changli told her two years ago, standing up, in her office, and was made to repeat it twice. She does not know the name. Changli does: forty-four, a daughter at the Academy, recruited through a debt manufactured for the purpose, no idea it was manufactured, convinced he is protecting her, and passing Changli four true things for every one he passes them, because Changli has spent two years choosing what walks past his desk. Give Jinhsi the name and she will act on it inside a week, because she is who she is, and Changli will have a clean registry and no eyes at all in the fourth month of a war.

And the dreams. Iuno had catalogued three stillnesses that day and twice they had been about the Magistrate. The lake's answers began arriving backward eleven years ago. Jinhsi has been told four. She was fifteen, the Sentinel had chosen her in front of the Council, and the Council was hunting for a reason to un-choose her — and a Magistrate whose god has been talking nonsense since before her appointment is a Magistrate the Board of Rites can retire on health grounds. So Changli dated the anomaly to the fourth year, in a file, in her own hand, and has maintained that date for seven years, and every runner's map that crosses her desk is copied fair before it goes up the stair. Iuno said *You forged a record for a child.* Changli said: *I forged a record for a Magistrate, which is the worse thing, and I would like it named correctly.*

Iuno stood in the willow shade with her heart in her ears, because she knew in her hands and her teeth exactly how the day of that decision had felt — the clean feeling of it, the way the pen moves, what it is to be the only person in a building who can see the whole thing and decide, kindly, at speed, with love, that the person you are protecting will work better without it. And she knew what it costs eleven years later in a hallway when it comes due. She said *Tell her.* Changli said she intended to. Iuno asked when. Changli produced silence. Iuno said: *That is what I said.*

Changli asked how Iuno had escaped the professional disease of becoming too competent to be told anything, and asked as a person rather than as an office. Iuno said there is no method: a wolf put a spear through her, and years later a woman with no arguments at all said *no* to her in a hallway and stayed in the room while she hated her for it. It is a staffing problem. You cannot appoint one; they have to already be there, and you have to have failed to drive them off. And then: *You have one. She sits in a chair on a hill, she is nineteen, and she cancels her afternoons. And you have spent seven years making sure she never gets to be the one who says no to you about anything that matters.* Changli left that where it lay.

Iuno's last question was about Tuesday. Changli had moved the remissions ledger from the bound volume to the working shelf on Friday and had stayed in that field since Saturday, signing four hundred and eleven counterfoils personally when a deputy could have signed them, keeping herself in one findable place for six days. Iuno would have found it inside a week regardless; Changli preferred to know the day. Iuno said she would like to be furious about that, and then said, hearing the sentence come out of her own mouth and disliking it and letting it stand, *I would rather be useful* — noting out loud that this was new, that it cost her something, and that the correct procedure is to mention it.

At the last bend, with the gate lamps being carried out on their poles, she asked the number. Eleven villages. Four thousand one hundred and twelve people at the spring count, and the spring count runs low because it omits the ones who live on boats and the ones who are somebody's second household. Places secured: three thousand eight hundred, north of the lake, on ground Changli has been buying since the second month through four different offices so the price would hold. And the rest: three hundred and twelve. Iuno asked what happens to three hundred and twelve. Changli said *I am still working* — the number was nine hundred in the second month and six hundred and forty in the fourth, and it comes down by fifty or eighty at a time, and every one of them is somebody's grandmother arguing about a well. She has not found the last three hundred and twelve a floor to sleep on. She intends to. And on the day the road closes, whatever the number is that morning is the number she will say out loud in a room, to a person, with her own mouth, because a wolf made her write it down in a field that afternoon. Her hands were shaking and had been for some time. Iuno told her not to decide the remainder alone. Changli said that is why they are here, and that she would have gone home and done it alone anyway if nobody had said it.

### The gate, and the tower

At the eighth hour the wind came in off the plain everywhere at once, laid the smell of four hundred hectares of cut sorghum down the whole length of the harbour ward shopfront by shopfront, lifted the corner of Wen's day-book at the out-of-sequence entry until he put his hand flat on it and thanked the sky and felt the canvas tap him once on the shoulder, went in at a high window on the library's fourth level where two carpenters were fitting a hinge, turned one page of a survey that has been wrong for eleven years and left it turned, and found Mei and Amy on the third terrace and did the thing it does, which is very little: a pressure at the temple, off the brow, back.

At the east gate Yangyang was under the lamp with the second-ink ledger open on her forearm. Iuno's first concern on seeing Mei was that she was intact; Mei's bruise was shaped like a fish and she wanted it admired. Yangyang said *Out, four.* Mei said *Back, four.* Yangyang said *Back, nine* — four of them, plus one Overseer through that gate under escort at the third hour, entered and countersigned; one clause entered out of sequence in a prefectural day-book in a field with a name under it that is not a Huanglong name; one file in a third drawer, unopened; one girl called A-Ling whose page went up the stair that afternoon and was in the archive that night beside the ledger that priced her, which is a person crossing a gate whether or not she is available to do the walking; and the wind, which came in with them and has not gone out. Mei said: *Then every one of them crosses.*

At the eleventh hour, on the north face, the keeper of the ninth tower filed an incident. She has been keeper six years and has filed four incidents, three of which were birds. The bell has hung silent since the dark, rope coiled on a cleat, cleat sealed with a lead tag renewed quarterly. The seal was intact; it is in the file; it was photographed. Between the eighth and ninth hours, with the wind coming up out of the plain smelling of cut grain, the bell moved — enough to touch the housing once on the north side and make a sound she wrote down in her own hand as *not a beat. A tap. Like somebody checking the door is still there.*

She filed it under 天氣, weather. Then she sat looking at the column for a while with the lamp going and the lake below her the colour of the inside of a shell. Then she struck it through. The second column stands blank. She has been asked twice and declined twice, politely, in writing, and the file went up the stair with the blank in it, and the Magistrate has seen it, and has written one word in the margin in her own pencil.

*wait.*

## VII. SEVENTEEN PEOPLE, NINE CHAIRS

*(Youngest Gate 6 — Panhua's back room, the same Tuesday)*

Mei remembered forty metres from the door: *Oh — Jinhsi's coming.* Yangyang kept walking three steps and then stopped in the lane. Chixia put her bag down on a doorstep, picked it up again, and established that it was a Tuesday, the house was full, and there was a man at the counter who had been drunk since the second hour and whose entire personality was opinions about the harbour levy. Amy supplied the interval: eleven minutes.

Panhua listened to thirty seconds of information delivered in nine, with a cleaver in her hand and forty percent of her attention on the ticket rail, and said: *She can sit where there's a seat.* Chixia protested that she had shut the house for foreigners in the sixth month and would not shut it for the Magistrate. Panhua told her to sit down or carry something. Chixia carried something. Later, with the count past twelve and a door laid across two barrels in the back room, Panhua reached to the top shelf and brought down a squat brown bottle. Chixia has eaten at that house four times a week for six years and has seen that bottle come down four times in eleven. She looked at the back of Panhua's head and elected to say nothing and went and got more chairs.

The Magistrate of Jinzhou got stuck behind a family leaving — five of them, a pram, a grandmother with a stick — and waited her turn in the lane in a plain coat with the rain finished and the lamps coming on. Nobody in the front room looked up. Cartethyia got there first, took her coat, warned her that the hook is a lie, and put a small folded paper person into her hand, three inches high, made out of the blank margin of something. Jinhsi asked what it was. *It's a person. Everyone gets one.* Lupa hooked a stool out with her foot and said *Sit. That end's warmer.* Amy said she was late; they had started at the third hour. Mei, mid-argument with Mrs Ren down the far end, lifted her chin and told her to have the fish. That was the whole of the reception, and Chixia stood against the wall with a tray and watched something behind her eye twitch twice and give up.

Seventeen people, a table that seats nine, and an overflow eating on the step in the lane with bowls on their knees, rotating in when somebody got up, the rotation managed by nobody and working perfectly. The drunk man at the counter tried to join the step, was absorbed for eleven minutes, and went home satisfied, having told Cartethyia everything about the barges. Mrs Ren came because Iuno had climbed two flights to fetch her with the promise of food and arguing, and spent twenty minutes going through a schedule of assessments with Iuno with the pleasure of two people who have finally found somebody who wants to. Mei went past behind them with a dish over her head, put her free hand flat on the back of Iuno's neck for two seconds, took it away, and kept going, and neither of them commented.

Assessor Lü arrived seven minutes early and stood in the lane until she was two minutes late. Three bites in, the goose came up: the bird that holds the doorway of the tax office. Chixia declared there was no record. Jinhsi supplied it — zero and seventeen, over six years, observed in the ward report. Amy leaned onto her elbows. Then Chisa, from the far side, without looking up from her bowl, dismantled six years of Chixia's life in three sentences: the goose favours the left approach because its right eye is clouded, it declines to turn its head to compensate because turning its head loses the door, so come at the right side, low, at walking pace, and it cannot track you and will hold the centre because the centre is what it is holding. Chixia asked how long she had watched it. *Once.* Chixia put her forehead on the door-table, which flexed. Cartethyia asked whether the goose gets paid. Lü recited column twelve: non-human residents, permanent; occupation, deterrent; housing status, doorway; income, exempt. She made the entry herself. Iuno observed that it is better documented than she is, meant it as a fact, and was told by Lupa that it had landed as a joke and to take it. By half past seven there was a plan for the morning involving three people, a bag of feed, a diagram drawn on the back of Lü's own notice paper, and Chixia insisting with total sincerity that she would not be participating while holding the diagram. Jinhsi said quietly into her bowl that the ward reports on that bird are the best thing that crosses her desk, that there is a file, and that she has had it rebound. In red.

Around eight, Ciaccona drew the particular breath that precedes a beautiful observation about rooms like this, and Lupa said *No*, and put a piece of pork belly in her bowl from a metre away, underhand, without missing. Twenty minutes later Mei began *I just think it's sort of amazing that all of us are —* and got *Eat the fish.* Amy announced the rule to the table: say anything true and Lupa feeds you. Ciaccona tested it deliberately and received a dumpling mid-sentence. Chisa noted it was Lupa's fourth dumpling in eleven minutes and that she had eaten none, and apologised to Lü for borrowing her line, and Lü said she might use it.

The rule got tested properly at half eight. Iuno set her cup down and began *There is something I ought to put on the table about this afternoon* — and Lupa slid a bowl in front of her and said *Tomorrow. You said a night. It hasn't been a night. Eat the soup, it's got the thing in it you like.* Jinhsi watched a one-armed foreigner start an important sentence in a crowded room, be stopped by somebody handing her a bowl, permit it, and lose nothing whatsoever by it, and turned that over for a while, and then Cartethyia asked her whether she liked bitter melon and she had to say something about bitter melon.

She ate a very great deal, and nobody remarked on it. Cartethyia asked what her favourite was — of anything, food — and Jinhsi thought about it long enough that Cartethyia's face began doing something complicated and Yangyang went very still three seats down and Baizhi wrote nothing with tremendous restraint. *I have not been asked that.* Recently? *At all.* So Cartethyia picked up the serving spoon and did it properly, and the Magistrate of Jinzhou spent eleven minutes of a Tuesday being fed nine things in sequence by a woman with a cane, giving each a verdict, and being argued with about four of them. She chose the one with the vinegar. Cartethyia informed her that was the wrong answer, that she had asked for the *right* one, and that a vinegar dish is a side; Ciaccona confirmed it was a side; Jinhsi said it is what she likes; Cartethyia said *Fine! It's noted.* Chixia leaned over to Baizhi and observed that she was arguing with the Magistrate about a pickle, and winning. Baizhi said losing — it went to the vinegar.

Somewhere in there Jinhsi got sauce on her cuff, looked at it the way you look at a thing that will be a problem for somebody in a laundry three floors below a building you work in, pushed the sleeve back, and kept eating, and Sanhua at the door closed her eyes for one second and opened them again.

Little Guo, sent out with a platter and told who was at the long table, crossed four metres of back room like a man walking a plank over a canyon, and on the second trip dropped a bowl. Lupa's hand came out sideways without her looking, caught it a foot off the boards, and set it on the table without interrupting what she was saying about a gearbox. Jinhsi asked whether there had been anything in it, was told no, and said: *Then it was an empty bowl. Everybody in this room has dropped an empty bowl.* Lupa said she had not. Jinhsi said she was showing off. Lupa agreed. Guo laughed by accident, went scarlet, fled, and came back four minutes later at ordinary walking pace.

Chisa had the back door and Sanhua had the front. Neither had discussed it; each had arrived, looked once, and gone to the correct place, and each had noticed the other take the other one. They stood like that for an hour and a half. Then Chisa moved down the wall to speaking distance, waited an appropriate interval, and asked what her eyes do — because she cannot see faces and does not know what hers does. Sanhua said: *They finish. You look once. Then you stop. You have it.* Chisa said yes. Sanhua said *Mine also.* Chisa asked whether it troubles people. *Constantly.* That was the entire conversation. Eleven minutes later Lupa came down the room with two bowls, put one in each of their hands, pointed at two places on the bench, told Sanhua that her four were outside and one of them was on the light side and she would hear it — and both of them sat.

At nine Baizhi came down the table and asked for the Magistrate's wrist with the flat courtesy of a physician in a public place. Sanhua came half off the bench. Jinhsi put her wrist on the table. Baizhi counted, then opened the notebook and asked when she had last eaten a meal sitting at a table. The silence was diagnostic. There had been a working dinner in the fourth month; there had been food *near* it. Baizhi wrote down a number and the word *near*. Chixia, in genuine distress, said that was the Magistrate; Baizhi said that was a person with a resting rate of ninety-one who had been standing since the second watch, and that if the Magistrate would rather she stopped, she would stop, and write that down as well. Jinhsi turned her cup a quarter-turn, asked what it was supposed to be, was told considerably less than ninety-one, and said to send the report to the office marked to her. Baizhi said it would be read by her clerk. Jinhsi said: *Then mark it to my Counselor. She reads everything and she keeps things.* Iuno's head came up out of column four for about a second and a half, and then went back down.

The room reached its loudest at a quarter past nine with a Rinascitan drinking song about a man who sells the same donkey four times, Amy with the words wrong, Chixia with the words wrong differently and louder, Cartethyia singing a completely separate song, and Mrs Ren, from somewhere, knowing the actual tune and having opinions about the pace. Lupa went to the hatch. She has asked twice for the crispy thing; both times Panhua said no, once kindly and once while doing four other things, with a condition attached: *when you can hear the oil.* Behind Lupa the back room was doing sixteen people and a donkey song and a bench going over and somebody catching it. She put her head slightly on one side, and her ears did the thing they do, and she said *…Now.* Panhua asked now what. *It's ready now. You're about nine seconds off.* Panhua looked at her for one second, which in a kitchen is an enormous investment, then pointed at the second wok with the cleaver and went back to her ticket rail and did not watch, which was the point.

What came out was fine — a good, hot, correct platter, carried down the back room at speed by a she-wolf holding it in both hands like something she had recently rescued from a river. Chixia took hers without looking at it, mid-sentence, and ate it, and carried on talking, and Panhua saw that happen from the hatch and went back into the kitchen. Lupa ate four pieces and said nothing at all for eleven minutes, which is her version of weeping. Then, without warning, she put a piece into Jinhsi's bowl from a metre away, underhand, without missing.

The corner by the window, under the shelf with the stitched cloth bird on it, has had a seat kept at it every week for a year. Yangyang set it before anybody sat down — bowl, cup, chopsticks, the good spoon — early and quickly and out of the flow of traffic, the way she has done it fifty-odd times, and then went and did something else. Jinhsi saw her do it. Jinhsi wrote the ruling; her seal is on the drawer the report went into; she has read 接風 in three separate hands this season and argued about it with a Ministry in writing. She sat four feet from that seat all evening and said nothing about it whatsoever. At about half nine Cartethyia reached across and put a spoonful of bitter melon into the third bowl, on the grounds that she likes this one, which Ciaccona disputed, which produced an argument about steam that ran six minutes and was never resolved. And four minutes into it, without any ceremony, Jinhsi picked up the dish of bitter melon and passed it down the table toward the empty seat, the way you pass a dish, because it had come to her and the empty place was the next place along. Yangyang saw it. She picked up her cup and drank out of it and put it down and said nothing, and her ears went pink under the hat.

At ten a woman came in with road dust on her boots and her coat over her arm, and two people in the back room recognised her: Iuno's cup stopped halfway, and Lupa's ears went round. Jinhsi's face did something nobody at that table had seen all night and it was gone in a second and a half. *You are back early.* Changli had finished the second column; the strongbox was in the cart and Wen had the receipts. She stood in the doorway of a back room with sixteen people in it and looked at the table for slightly longer than a person normally looks at a table, until Mrs Ren told her to shut the door because it was coming in on her hip.

She was absorbed in under a minute. Cartethyia took her coat and put a paper person in her hand. Somebody found a rice sack. Chixia, who had never seen her before in her life, put the crispy-thing platter in front of her and told her at length that there was extra and that she was a generous person. Amy demanded a ruling on the mangosteen — was it a code — and got the answer twice: it was a mangosteen, in season eleven days, and Jinhsi had had four crates of them put through the ward offices in the second week of the season for six years running, and there is a line in the prefectural accounts about it that has been queried at audit twice, and the query is unresolved, and the query will remain unresolved. Amy sat back on her crate and looked at the two of them with an expression Mei would still be able to describe in twenty years, and said: *Okay. Fine. It was a mangosteen.*

Changli ate standing up and then on the rice sack, took a chair off nobody, and inside twenty minutes knew the name of every person in the room and had used all of them at least once. She got the goose diagram explained to her by Chixia, who still did not know who she was and was still insisting she would not be participating, and told her the approach was on the wrong side. Chixia demanded to know how everyone knew about the eye. Lü said it was in the entry — remarks column: *Right eye clouded, 4th year. Non-hostile if approached slowly on the blind side.* Chixia has never read the entry.

Iuno was watching about a third of the room out of a habit she has stopped apologising for, so she was the only one who saw it: Changli, on a rice sack, with a paper person in her coat pocket and somebody else's chopsticks, stopping for four seconds to look down the table at a girl of nineteen who had sauce on her cuff and was being shouted at about a pickle by a woman with a cane. Then somebody passed her the vinegar and she said thank you by name.

Panhua sat down twice — once near the hatch with a bowl she had made for herself out of the ends of four things and ate in ninety seconds, and once near eleven when the front was closed and the room had gone from loud to the other thing. She said one sentence to the Magistrate of Jinzhou, which is one more than she had said all evening: *You eat too fast.* And then: *Come at four in the afternoon. Nobody's in. It's quiet and the oil's fresh.* Chixia was three feet away and heard all of it and looked at the ceiling and made a small noise.

They went out at half eleven. Changli waited in the doorway while the room emptied past her, and Iuno came out last because she had been putting Mrs Ren's ledger back into Mrs Ren's bag with the pages the right way round, and for six seconds the two of them were in the doorway together. Changli said *Tomorrow.* Iuno asked whether she wanted somebody in the room. Changli said no. Iuno said *Good answer.*

Yangyang did the count on the step under the door-lamp because there was nowhere flat left indoors. Mei guessed fourteen. Yangyang said Panhua had washed nineteen bowls, which is how she counts, and listed seventeen at the table, four in the lane who were fed and are on no list, one who came at ten, one who left at eight and told Cartethyia about barges, and the seat. Mei asked for the number. Yangyang looked at it for a while and said the number does not come out, and that it has never once come out at that house. Mei told her to write down that it does not come out. She wrote it down. Then she wrote three more words underneath it, in the second ink, and boxed them, and shut the book before Mei could look, and stood up and said the fish had been good.

In the empty back room, Panhua turned the lamps down one at a time — the long table, the window, the shelf with the stitched cloth bird — and left the one at the back left by the door hinge, as she does, because it gets knocked and it is easier to leave it. It burned by itself for a while. Then it leaned once, over toward the corner and the third seat, and steadied, and went out on its own about a minute later, which is roughly a minute longer than that lamp has ever managed.

## VIII. NIGHT TALK

*(Youngest Gate 7 — 夜話, the same Tuesday, 23:30 to 04:30, in pairs)*

**The shrine street.** Lupa told Iuno she had touched the left side of her coat eleven times since the door, and did it when she was deciding something, and had done it four times at that table, three of them while the Magistrate was talking. Iuno asked how long. *Second port.* Two hundred and eleven kilometres of silence, because it was not hers. Amy has known since the landing and shut her mouth, which for Amy is a religious act. Chisa knew before Iuno did. Cartethyia thinks there is a knife in there, because when she asked, Iuno said *it's private*, and in Cartethyia's experience the private thing is always a knife.

At the second bridge they passed a sill where a flute usually lives; tonight the sill was empty and the window shut, and Iuno noted it and filed it and kept it. At the door of Panhua's, handing back her ledger, Mrs Ren had held the other end of it one second longer than the handover required and said, in Huanglong, at conversational volume, to a woman she has known nineteen days: *Whatever's in the coat. It doesn't get lighter.* And then gone off up the lane on Little Guo's arm and said nothing else about anything at all.

Lupa's counsel came in two parts. *Feed her first* — Mei had been up since the fourth hour, had had a bad afternoon in a nice chair, and had had four Moments that day, two of which Lupa stopped herself. And: *Don't make it a Moment.* Feeding somebody is ordinary; keep the whole thing that size. It's Tuesday. Do it on a Tuesday. Iuno said it was better advice than she expected and that she resented the whole of it.

Halfway up the terraces she said the other thing, because she had decided at the second bridge that she would, and because she does that now: *You sold her interval.* Lupa said yes. Iuno asked whether Mei knew. Lupa said she will know when it is read out, because it is in a day-book with her house in it and a date on it and Lupa's name under it, which is more than it was that morning — that morning it was a thing four governments knew and none of them could point at. Iuno called that a defence. Lupa called it the reason, and said defence is what you do after. Iuno asked whether it was worth it. Lupa looked out over the terraces where the lamp-grid was down to its bones and said *Ask me in eleven years.* Iuno said she intended to. Lupa said *I know. That's why I gave you a number.*

**The other lane.** There is a chair available to the office of Magistrate, kept in a shed behind the west court with a man whose whole employment is the chair. It goes out perhaps nine times a year. It stayed in, because nobody had been told to send it and Jinhsi had not thought to say. So she walked home, with Changli one pace back on her left, which is where a Counselor walks, and Sanhua four paces behind both of them being professionally a piece of the lane.

Changli gave the day: Wen has the receipts, the strongbox goes into the second vault, the second column came out four hundred and eleven short and she knows where the four hundred and eleven is — a man who cannot read a schedule and has been carrying the difference in his head for six weeks, whom she will have taught. Seed-stock purchases went through at Schedule Four in nine villages and stuck in two, both with temple land, which she had expected, and she has a way round it that will take four days and offend a Deputy — the one Jinhsi likes. *Offend him.*

At the corner where the ward changes, Jinhsi stopped at Old Feng's shop window. It shuts at five and had been shut six hours; the shutter is a half-shutter, so the top shows. The window is funeral paper: white houses with white doors four to a rack, paper horses, a paper granary — and along the front of the display, at the height of a person's hand, in a row, eleven paper people, three inches high, folded, made to be burned. In her coat pocket, against her hip, was a paper person three inches high that a woman with a cane had put into her hand at a doorway with the information that everyone gets one. She looked at them for about eleven seconds. Changli looked at the side of her face for the whole of it, then at the lane, and said that Feng's daughter runs the counter now and that they do a good horse. Jinhsi agreed that they do, took her hand out of her pocket empty, and started walking.

Fifty metres on, with no change of pace: *Move the fourth-hour thing on Thursday.* The Bureau of Waterways. To when? *Four in the afternoon is taken.* By what? *A lane off the shrine street. The oil's fresh at four. Nobody's in.* Changli said she would have to write something in the book, and was told to write what it is, and composed it walking, without slowing, in the hand that presses hard on the down-strokes: *The Magistrate of Jinzhou will attend a noodle house in the harbour ward at the ninth hour on Thursday for the purpose of eating fried food.* Four clerks will read it and two will copy it. Yes. Changli put the book away and said it was a good entry.

At the turn where the terraces start she asked about the hand. Jinhsi said it was fine. Changli said she had held it shut since the third hour and had eaten one-handed for four hours in a room with a physician in it. Jinhsi walked to the next lamp, stopped in it, and opened it: four marks in a curve at the base of the fingers, gone from red to the colour they go. Changli looked at them for as long as it takes to look at four marks, and declined to ask what he had said — seven years as Counselor has taught her the difference between a thing a person is carrying and a thing a person is holding, and you can take the first off somebody, and reaching for the second costs you the hand. She said Baizhi saw it, and wrote ninety-one and the word *near*, and shut the book. Jinhsi closed her hand again gently, the way you close a book you are still in, and said *Tomorrow.* Her office, door shut, no clerk, no runner, no Sanhua. *Will I be angry.* Changli walked to the next lamp before answering, which is a thing she does and which Jinhsi has known about since she was fifteen and has never once mentioned. *Yes.* Jinhsi thanked her; Changli said not to, because she would want it back by the third sentence. *Will I still have a Counselor at the end of it.* *That will be your ruling.* Jinhsi called that a coward's answer. Changli said it is the only honest one she has and she has had seven years to find a better.

At the compound gate the escort came out of the dark with Deng among them, on the correct side this time, wearing the expression of a man to whom the concept of light has been explained at length and with visual aids. Changli stopped outside the gate and said *You ate a great deal.* Jinhsi said she did. *…Good.* And went off toward the second vault with a strongbox waiting and four hundred and eleven in her coat.

In the courtyard, Sanhua said the only sentence she said all night that was not about a door: *That was a good room.* Jinhsi paused on the fourth step with her hand on the rail and agreed, and then said there is a woman there who tells people to eat the fish, and that she would like her file. Sanhua said she has one. Four pages, none of it in Huanglong, two pages a Septimont billboard. *Bring it up on Thursday. Before the ninth hour.*

**The sink.** Yangyang came back at twenty to twelve, because she always does, and Panhua had the front bolted and both hands in a tub and told her to go home. Yangyang hung her hat on the nail that is not a lie, rolled her sleeves, and took the rinse side, which is hers, and has been hers for a year, and which she has never once been offered. Nineteen bowls, two platters, and the wok that Panhua does herself and nobody has ever been permitted to touch. Panhua observed that her foreigners eat like carters; Yangyang said they were carters for twenty days.

At the end of it Panhua dried her hands and did something she has done perhaps four times in the year, which is stand still in her own kitchen. *Fifty-two weeks.* Yangyang, rinsing: *Fifty-three.* Then: *You want me to keep the corner.* And, when the silence ran: *I'm asking because there's a shelf going up in the sixth month and if the corner's staying I'll put the shelf somewhere else.* Yangyang set the last bowl in the rack and said *Keep the corner.* Panhua said *Right.* That was the whole conversation. At the door, with a parcel made up without discussion and put into her hands in a manner that did not admit refusal, Yangyang asked how late the hinge lamp had gone. *About a minute. Over.* She wrote something on the step in the second ink, which she has explained to nobody and which Panhua has never asked about, because Panhua's entire theology is that a woman's second ink is her own business. Then Panhua bolted the door, went back through, took the third seat's bowl out of the rack, dried it, and put it up on the shelf beside the little stitched cloth bird — where it has been going for about three weeks now, which she has mentioned to nobody, including herself.

**The terrace stairs.** Chixia had an event on the way home, at volume, about a goose being in a remarks column in a file in a building she works in, and was reminded twice about a man on the fourth landing who had been trying to sleep since the second hour and was now going to be told about a goose. She announced she was going in at six, before anyone else, to read the entry, and was extremely clear that she was not participating in the operation at the fifth hour, and that holding the diagram is custody, which is practically legal.

Two landings up she changed register: *What are you going to write.* Baizhi gave the report — ninety-one, resting, taken at the ninth hour after four hours seated; a recommendation about hours; a recommendation about meals; a note that the subject consented and a note that the subject was told she could refuse. Chixia said *You saw something else.* Baizhi went up four more steps at exactly the pace she had been going and said she sees a great deal, and then stopped on the landing and gave Chixia the actual answer: if she ever takes Chixia's wrist in a public room, Chixia should be certain that whatever she finds goes into a book nobody reads without her name on the cover — and that certainty costs something, and what it costs is that Chixia does not get to hear this one. Chixia opened her mouth, shut it, went up two steps, and said *…Fine.* Then, immediately: *It's a left hand, though, isn't it.* Baizhi said goodnight.

At Chixia's door — a door with four separate things nailed to it, none of them a nameplate — she said the thing she had actually been carrying: that Jinhsi had sauce on her cuff and pushed the sleeve back, like anybody, like a person at a table; and that Panhua brought the bottle down and said nothing about it and just put it on the tray. She stopped with her key half-turned because the sentence was going somewhere she had no equipment for. Baizhi said *Volume*, gently. Chixia said she was not being loud. Baizhi said *I know. It was the other thing.* Chixia laughed once, wetly, said something unrepeatable about the goose, and went inside. Baizhi stood on the landing, wrote *91* and *near* — and then turned the notebook over and went in at the back, where there are four pages in a different hand that she has shown to nobody in her life, and wrote one line on the fourth of them, and went home.

**The roof.** Ciaccona got up first with the lute and did not play. Chisa arrived four minutes later and stood. Ciaccona announced she was going to say it now, and that Chisa could not stop her, because Lupa was at the stairhead and had run out of dumplings. Chisa said she had four and had eaten one, and had been holding three for an hour and a half — for anything.

What Ciaccona had been carrying since eight o'clock was this: sixteen people in one small space with a low ceiling and wet plaster, all going at once, is a chord. A working chord. It is what a hull sounds like: meant to be ugly and hold. And every chord goes somewhere; strike one string and every string tuned to it sounds, whether or not anybody touched it. So: who is tuned to that room?

Chisa said the corner has two. Two strings on the third seat, going different ways. One goes up — out at the seam over the door, past the eaves, past the weather — and that one is easy; it has been in the house since the fifteenth and it drinks the tea. The other goes north, along the crest wall, and it does not stop at the eighth tower, which is the last one with a bell in it that anybody has heard. Chisa has not walked it. It is taut, and old, and held at both ends, and it went tighter when the Magistrate put a dish down at the empty place, and it has not slackened since. The lute hummed once, untouched, three notes, low, and Ciaccona put her hand flat on the soundboard and stopped it — which Chisa had been watching her do for four days, along with the blank card in the same pocket, taken out twice and looked at from the back.

Ciaccona named her problem: a message you can answer. An empty stave. Somebody ruled five lines and sent it and is sitting somewhere with a pen waiting to see what she writes, and whatever she writes, they will play. Chisa said *Then write nothing.* Ciaccona said that is a note as well — a rest, and rests are the loudest thing in the trade. Chisa considered it with the seriousness she reserves for other people's professions and said *Then it is a good problem.* Ciaccona agreed that it was a wonderful problem and that she was having a marvellous time, and Chisa held out a dumpling, and Ciaccona said that was unfair, and took it.

**The landing.** Amy and Cartethyia planned the goose operation on the floor of the downstairs passage, because Amy's room had the rig in it and the upstairs one had two people already asleep. The diagram had gone home in the custody of a woman who was not participating; Cartethyia was certain she would be there at ten to. Amy redrew it from memory on the back of a signal log, with the courtyard as a square, the goose as an X, and eleven arrows, four of which were wrong, including one that brought them in through a wall. Cartethyia vetoed bringing Lupa to a goose on the grounds that it would be rude, and Amy could find no flaw in it.

Then the feed turned out to be sorted: barley, soaked since the third hour, drained at seven, in a cloth. The plan happened at half seven. Cartethyia had had the bag at four. Amy sat back on her heels and named it, quoting Chisa: doing the work in advance and telling nobody, so that if it happens you are ready, and if it does not happen nobody finds out you were hoping. Cartethyia went pink and became extremely interested in the folded top of the bag and said it was just barley. It was *soaked* barley. Dry barley is no good for a goose, it swells, everybody knows that. NOBODY KNOWS THAT. *Volume,* said the stairhead.

They shouted a recruitment attempt through a wall at Mei, who answered from face-down in a bed, established the hour, and declined. Amy called her a coward. Iuno pointed out that Mei was asleep. Amy pointed out that she had answered. Iuno pointed out that she had answered *asleep*. *Volume,* said the stairhead, through a floor, and the house shut up.

Amy set her watch alarm for four-forty. Cartethyia watched the operation with the frank attention of somebody from a place where time was a thing you looked at the sky about, and asked whether knowing what time it is, all the time, down to the bits, hurts. Amy turned the watch over and said she was three years in a place with no clocks. So, no. Cartethyia accepted it, and they went back to the arrows, two people who have both been somewhere with no clocks and have agreed without discussing it on how much of that comes up at any one time.

Then Amy asked the other thing, because she is the one who asks: the corner seat at Panhua's. Make one for it. Cartethyia said no. Amy invoked *everyone gets one* — said to the Magistrate of Jinzhou at a doorway that very evening. Cartethyia said she had asked Yangyang. Two weeks ago, on the wall, who it was for and whether she could make one; and Yangyang told her; and she will not say it, because it was not hers and it was given to her at the top of a tower with nobody else there. Amy, who two years ago would have gone round it four different ways and had it out by the second, sat with that and said *Okay.* Cartethyia looked at her with something close to suspicion, finished a fifth paper person, turned it over twice, and held it out. Amy asked who it was. *I'll tell you when it's true.* And went up the stairs with her cane, remembering the fourth step, leaving Amy on a passage floor at half past midnight holding a three-inch paper person she could not identify, and being, on balance, delighted. She put it on the sill next to the rig. Then she moved it, because the rig hums, and she did not want it standing in a hum all night.

**The front room.** The front downstairs room at Mrs Ren's has a window on the lane, a chair, a washstand, a hook, and one bed four feet wide, allocated by a vote of six to nil with one abstention, and the abstainer has never let it go. Mei came in ten minutes behind everybody because of the count, got her boots off by standing on the heel of one with the toe of the other, and went face-down on the bed from a distance of about a foot without breaking stride. Told the boots were in the middle of the floor, she said they were *near* the mat, and was informed that there had been food *near* a working dinner in the fourth month and a physician had written it down. Iuno hung her coat over the chair back on the hook side, where it goes, stood for a second with her hand flat against the front of it, took her hand away, and went and dealt with the boots herself.

The complaints started at seven minutes past midnight, which is standard: nineteen things eaten, four of them crispy things that had *arrived*, which Mei argued removed her agency. Iuno supplied the word for a thing that turns up without your say: weather. Then Iuno was accused of doing the voice, denied doing any voice, and was quoted back at herself in an accent four percent Septimont and ninety-six percent slander, and stood at the washstand with her back turned and let herself laugh where it could not be entered into evidence.

Then the fish. Mei sat up and presented it: a very good bruise coming in under the shoulder blade and out along the ribs, with a genuine tail, gone in four hours from a claim to a fact. Iuno brought the lamp round, confirmed it was a fish, was thanked twice, and was informed that Amy had said it was a boot. Then she went to the chair and took a small notebook out of the coat — the notebook, and only the notebook — and read the page out. Ragunna, fourth month, ribs, thrown into a wall of a Registry office by a man who apologised: four days to clear. The ninth of the fifth, forearm, the crate at the second landing: thirty-one hours. The eleventh, a four-centimetre cut across the palm, deep enough to want stitching, declined: nine hours. The second of this month, a tower wall and a badly judged jump, bruise to the right hip comparable to tonight's: two days and a bit. And tonight's: gone before the fifth hour. Mei said that was fast. Iuno said it is about half. Half of what it was. It went to about half on the twenty-first and has stayed at half since, and stayed at half *exactly*, which is what she finds interesting. Mei knew what is filed on the twenty-first. Iuno knew that she knew. Neither of them put it into a sentence, and the date sat on the quilt between them, and they both left it there. Mei asked her to keep the page and to keep doing it and to give her the numbers the same way — flat, straight off, no run-up — because Iuno is the only person who tells her numbers about herself without doing a face first. Iuno said she was going to keep it whatever Mei said. Mei said *Yeah, I know. That's why I asked. It's nice to be asked things you were getting anyway.*

The window does not latch, so it sits half an inch open all night, which is how the room stays cool and how everything else gets in. Mei lay with her head on Iuno's stomach and her feet off the end of a four-foot bed and put her warm palm flat on the crystal, which she does every night. Iuno's arm aches from about the elbow when the pressure drops and has since the frostlands; Baizhi has a theory involving the lattice which Iuno has declined to hear twice. *Weather in about four hours.* Mei called it a barometer that goes off when her aunt is coming down off the plain — because the wind takes the tea and puts her hair back off her face when she is being an idiot, and that is an aunt, that is the whole job. The curtain went in two inches and came back. Iuno predicted that acquiring a relative who can hear everything said in the open air within four hundred metres and has no capacity for tact would become a problem; the curtain went in twice; Mei defended the wind's tact; Iuno pointed out that it had read her correspondence on Sunday, and had turned the page *back*, which means it read both. Mei laughed hard enough to be shushed by two separate parts of the house.

Iuno has to be up at six because Lupa's hot tea, every morning, is a treaty, and she has kept every treaty she ever signed, and that one has cost her more sleep than the other nine put together. Four years and eleven days. She has counted. Mei called it soppy arithmetic; Iuno noted that Lupa knows the number too, checked it in Ragunna, and told her she was two days out and was right.

Then Mei prosecuted the ledger date with Mrs Ren — an hour and a half, in public, sleeves up — and Iuno defended it as an error in column four propagated through the ward's assessments for eleven years while everybody argued about column six, and went pink about it, and was made to say the name in the voice. And then Mei went quiet and said *Yeah. You would* — and Iuno stopped with her hand in Mei's hair and asked for the rest of it.

Mei rolled onto her front across Iuno's lap, which is how she does a serious conversation, and said: *You liked today.* Iuno gave it whole. She was extremely good at it. She read a man's thirty-one-year game record on a road in the evening light and found the fault in about four minutes, and watched a woman she had known one day discover that she had been wrong about the largest object in her life, and enjoyed it in her hands. There is a particular pleasure in being the only person in a road who can see the whole thing, and she had not had it in two years and had forgotten what it tastes like. And then at supper a nineteen-year-old Magistrate with sauce on her cuff said *mark it to my Counselor, she reads everything and she keeps things*, and Iuno put her head up out of a ledger like a dog hearing its own name — because that is her old job description, word for word, what she was in a building for thirty years, the best there has ever been at it, and it cost an arm and eleven years and a hundred and seventy-three names, and a child said it out loud across a table with a fish on it and something in her stood up.

Mei asked whether she wanted to be Jinhsi's Counselor. *No.* She wants to be *useful to her*, which is worse, because it has no ceiling and no salary and no clerk to notice when she has overstepped. A title is a fence; Iuno never once misused a title; everything she has to answer for she did in the gap where nobody had written down what she was. Mei thought about it in the dark and said: *So get a title. Get written down. Get a bit of paper that says what you are and what you're allowed and who can sack you, and give the paper to somebody who'd use it.* Iuno said it was a remarkably good answer, and upsetting, because Mei had it in about nine seconds and Iuno had been standing in it since half past nine.

Then Mei handed something over: Jinhsi offered her the key that afternoon, held out flat like feeding a horse, to a sealed part of a file about the first one of them, which a Magistrate has had since she was seventeen and has never opened because *the moment I know, I have to write it down.* Mei said no. Iuno asked whether she wanted it. *Course I want it.* Then why? *Because she'd have had to write down that she gave it to me. And I've spent about two lifetimes being the reason there's an entry about somebody.* Iuno said she had told her that unprompted, and that two years ago she would have filed it behind the smile and Iuno would have found it in the spring. Mei said don't make it a thing. Iuno said *I am making it a thing. It is a thing.*

About one, Mei got cold and reached without looking for the nearest heavy fabric, which was the coat on the chair back. *Not that one* came out faster than Iuno's sentences come out. Mei let go, pulled the quilt up instead, and said nothing for four seconds, which from Mei is a long document. Then Iuno kept an arrangement she has kept with herself for two years at considerable expense: she said there is something in that coat — two things, one carried since the frostlands and one since the twenty-fourth — and that she was not going to show her either of them tonight, and that she was telling her she was not showing her, because she would rather Mei had the shape of it than an evening where she moved a coat and pretended she had not. Mei asked whether it was bad. No. Whether it was paperwork. One of them is technically paperwork. Mei began an objection about carrying a form around for a month, was stopped by her name, and said *Okay. I'm not asking.* And did not ask.

She lay there with her hand on a crystal arm and did not ask, and after a while started up about the goose again, and Iuno lay underneath all of it with her heart going hard enough to be a medical event and thought: *there it is. That is the woman. That one, exactly, right there, in about nine seconds, on a Tuesday.* And the High Priestess, with no performance in it whatsoever, said: *Now. Darling. Now.*

What stopped it was entirely ordinary. Mei was explaining what the goose is actually defending — nine flagstones and a door that shuts on its own, the whole estate, and it stands on it like it has a *position* on the — and by the second attempt at the end of the sentence she had gone. Out. Mid-clause. Iuno lay there a minute and a half. *I had my hand on it. I had the sentence.* And was told, gently, four-times-a-year gently: she is nineteen hours awake, she has been fed by a wolf and shouted at by her daughter, and she has just, for the first time in two lifetimes, told you a hard thing on the day it happened. *Let her have the Tuesday.*

Iuno got out from under her by degrees and put a pillow where she had been, which Mei annexed without waking. She went to the chair and took the ring out of the coat: one facet, sawn off her own shoulder in a workshop in Lahai-Roi at a price itemised to nobody, taking hold of every colour in the room one at a time. She held it over a woman asleep on her front with a bruise shaped like a fish leaving her ribs at twice the speed it left in Ragunna. Then she put it back and took out the other thing — a folded paper on good stock with a seal space at the foot and four characters inside it, written at a gate in the morning by a woman with a tin of three hundred and six letters at home. The seal space is empty. She has known for a month what the paper says. She has been unable, for a month, to work out whose chop it is supposed to want. Four candidates; she dislikes three; and the fourth is a nineteen-year-old with sauce on her cuff who ruled that a woman with no origin is *returning* — which would require Iuno to explain the whole of it to her in a room, in advance, to her face, which is, as of a field that afternoon, a thing this household is entitled to.

She put the paper back beside the ring, on the left, over the ribs, hung the coat where it goes, stood at the half-open window in a lane above the east market at half past one, and said *Tomorrow* to the gap. The curtain went in once, about two inches, and came back, and settled.

At 02:11 the house held: a wolf awake at the stairhead, because she does four hours and four hours; a bard with a lute on the floor and a blank card face down under the lamp, weighted with a cup, where she will see it first thing; a woman with a cane asleep with a row of paper people on the sill facing out, on watch, and a torn-off blank margin under her pillow kept back for the morning, in case; a woman who cannot read a face, asleep on her back with her hands folded and her scissors within reach, and a string somewhere over the roof going north; a girl of nineteen forever, asleep with a watch wound to four-forty and a paper person moved off the sill to the shelf, away from the hum; a one-armed woman asleep for the second time that fortnight before three, with her coat over the chair back and her hand where it fell; and a woman with a bruise shaped like a fish, which by four in the morning had gone the colour of nothing at all, and by twenty past had gone.

The air went round the house once, unhurried — the stairhead, the passage, the shut door of the front room, the sill with the rig on it, the window that will not latch — and out over the east market and up the terraces and along the crest wall, tower to tower, and past the ninth, where a keeper who has filed four incidents in six years sat with the lamp going and the second column of her book still blank, and looked up when the air came through the housing. The bell stayed where it was. She wrote nothing down and went on sitting there, which is, in the language that keeper's book is written in, its own kind of entry.

In the tax office courtyard, on the warm flagstone by the north door, the goose was awake and had been since the eleventh hour. At about half past four the sky went the colour of the inside of a shell. The goose put its head up. It turned, once, to the right — the side it gave up four years ago, the turn that costs it the door — and held it for about a second, with the clouded eye, at the blank wall on its blind side. Then it turned back, and settled, and took the centre of the doorway, and waited.

## IX. THE MINE, THE LAB, AND THE WOMAN IN THE PROJECTION

*(Youngest Gate 8)*

Breakfast happened on a café terrace above the second-tier canal, chosen by Yangyang for seating capacity and defended by Chixia on the grounds that the good coffee is at Old Wan's on the fourth terrace. Iuno met a latte and set it down with the precise care of a woman refusing to be defeated by a beverage; Lupa identified it as hot milk with bean water; Ciaccona called the foam brave. Mei ate chili oil noodles at seven in the morning with the satisfaction of somebody who had discovered religion.

At eight-twelve a theatre student came round the corner with a stack of handbills, looked up at Cartethyia, looked down at a painted figure in crystal thorns titled *The Maiden of Two Names*, looked up again, and dropped everything. Within four minutes the terrace had an audience: a noodle vendor who recognized Ciaccona from the Carnevale broadsheets, an elderly woman who asked Lupa whether she was the wolf from act three, and two children who wanted to know whether the Blessed Maiden could become tall right now, please. Cartethyia held her half-finished congee like a shield and whispered that she was eating. Ciaccona confirmed that she was eating and moved her chair one inch closer. Lupa, without standing, slid her own body between the table and the crowd and blocked the sightline with the practised ease of somebody who had been famous once and hated every second, and said *Eat.*

Amy stood up and took it. *Hi! She's not doing the transformation right now, but I'm Amy, and I'll sign whatever you're holding.* Chixia, who had once been Septimont's greatest celebrity and had not entirely forgotten it, planted herself beside her and announced *Fleet Snowfluff* and that she does signings. The crowd migrated toward the interesting. Ciaccona watched Cartethyia quietly resume her congee and said nothing about it, because some victories stay victories only if nobody names them.

Mei had gone — two terraces down, at the canal rail, watching a laundry line with red cloth on it snapping in the canal wind. She said *I'm fine.* Iuno said *I know*, and stood beside her anyway, and said the same two words again when Mei said it a second time more quietly. Then Mei produced the reason: City Hall had sent her a location that morning, ruins out of town, with the message that if she did not want to open the box yet, she could go there. *Should I go?* Iuno asked what she was actually asking. Whether it is running away from the box, or toward something. She did not know which. So Iuno said: *Then go find out. Ruins don't tell you in advance. Neither do boxes.* And added the accurate part — the file is inside a building and ruins are outside, and Mei has never been able to think clearly about herself in enclosed spaces — and the term: if it turns out to be running, they walk back slowly and call that the information. Mei accused her of enabling. Iuno said if she says so. Mei said *I hate that you're funny now.* Iuno said she has always been funny. Mei said she has always been devastating, which is different.

Amy dropped into her chair, stole a noodle, and said *I'm coming.* Mei said *I know.* Yangyang read the note, said she knew that road, and folded it back with a decision made and filed in the same motion; her morning patrol ended at nine and they were leaving at ten. Chisa's shoulders had already decided. Chixia rescheduled a patrol in under thirty seconds, walked Yangyang to the steps, said something low and specific that made her nod twice, and reclaimed her scallion pancake. Lupa stayed, and said *Ten* to Amy specifically, and Amy held her gaze and nodded, and the negotiation was complete with nothing said about it. Ciaccona declined on the grounds that she had a thing, and then a thing at the market. Cartethyia watched two children draw her from the canal wall, reported that they were getting the thorns decoratively wrong, declined to correct them, and was told she therefore had a morning. Mei walked back and touched the back of Lupa's hand once — brief, specific — and kept walking.

The road north brought them, before the ruins, to the Tiger's Maw Mine, and Amy stopped walking at the crest.

She had spent ten years in the Nihility, which contained no industry and no organized extraction of anything, and before that Ragunna, which ran on festival, and before that a cabin with a blue door. The pit made persons seem like an organizational error: terraces of reddish-grey stone cut in concentric descending rings to a depth that defeated estimation, each level with its own cart-track, crew and machinery, ore carts on rope lines counterweighted against descending empties, a resonance generator at the northwest corner whose frequency made Mei's right hand twitch once without her acknowledging it, and the stamps coming down in measured sequence — boom, boom, boom-boom — arriving delayed from the sight, the way lightning separates from thunder.

Sixty years old, maybe more. Prefecture charter, three operating companies, capital from the east coast for two of them. Most of the structural iron for the northern walls. Iuno had the flag-signal system on level four mapped inside thirty seconds and said the crews rotate and the equipment never stops, and later had all six signals rather than four and mentioned it into her soup, and wrote it down, and said nothing else about it, because Mei would have found it unbearable and Iuno had decided some time ago that she does not need credit for the things she understands privately.

They stayed twenty-three minutes on the observation path, where the vibration stops being something you notice and becomes something you are standing inside. Amy asked four questions about the rope-and-counterweight system; the fourth — *but then where does the tension actually go* — produced a silence from all three companions that she correctly identified as consensus. Mei said somebody had once stood in a field and decided to take apart the inside of a hill. Iuno observed that people have said that about mountains for a long time, and that Septimont extracted things from the faithful instead. Amy said Ragunna was performances and transit fees, and the city she held up was people.

Yangyang, who has walked that rim a hundred times and filed six patrol reports on it under *infrastructure, operational, no anomalies*, said Jinzhou is grain, salt, transit revenue — and iron. Mei asked whether she found it strange. Yangyang considered it the way she considers anything she intends to answer accurately and said she did once, and then stopped finding it, and then stopped finding it at all; it is good iron and the north wall holds. Her ears went the particular pink they go when she has said something true she had not intended to say.

They ate at the settlement east of the road: three stalls under corrugated overhangs, communal tables worn to the smoothness of water-stone, four shifts of mine workers arriving grimy and direct, families in between, children trailing. Nobody managed it; it managed itself, the way things do when a hundred people have eaten lunch in the same place for sixty years. A woman at the first stall glanced at Yangyang's hat and pointed at a bench without a word, a gesture made so often the elbow barely moved — *She knows me by the brim*, Yangyang said; she has stopped there six times and the woman has fed three hundred people since the first, and the hat is more consistent. Mei ordered scallion oil noodles in the flat confident common-tongue she had been assembling from context since their first morning and got the neutral acknowledgment of somebody who has watched a thousand people make the correct choice. Amy pointed at the second item and revised the question to *what does it smell like*, ordered the congee, and examined the century egg with the scrutiny she applies to anything that looks intentional. Chisa asked which item the broth station had refilled most recently and ordered that. At the far end of the table two workers argued something technical without heat and left without resolving it. A girl of about seven ran between the tables in a pursuit with no discernible object. Chisa found a ceramic spoon at the bottom of her serving cloth, placed where her hand would find it without needing to look, and used it without saying anything. Iuno counted the marks on the bowl-rinse turnover log without being asked — three shifts, seventeen workers minimum each, eight months without a gap — said nothing about it, and rinsed her bowl properly, because somebody maintains that log and somebody has to wash the bowls, and both of those are worth doing correctly.

Yangyang set her chopsticks down and said she has reported that road six times and written *operational, no anomalies* every time, and that she did not know what she would have written instead. Mei, without looking up from her noodles: *Operational. With lunch.*

The road out of the settlement was occupied by Mei's sustained proposal that Amy take up a career in supervised extraction coordination, on the grounds of career progression, structure, regularity, benefits, and transferable skills from a radio operator who once ran seventeen relay stations at peak. Amy pointed out in escalating terms that she had held a city up for ten years and had been the load-bearing wall of an underground civilization. Mei called it a lateral transition — different underground. Chisa contributed that the level-four flag changes every six minutes, which Mei received as a point scored. Yangyang offered that the flag system communicates crew position and equipment load, helpfully and neutrally, and then said *I'm not saying the mine. I'm saying the flags*, and refused to be thanked. Iuno, whose established interval between a conversation becoming ridiculous and her speaking into it is eleven seconds across three countries, waited, and then observed that the mine workers ate in under twelve minutes and Amy took eighteen. Chisa, asked by Yangyang whether Mei does this often, said the specific mine proposal was new and the structure was very consistent, and that usually there is a capybara involved.

The ruins waited at the bottom of a shallow bowl in the hillside, sheltered on three sides, with three walls at original height, intact doorframes, the north half still holding its vaulting on stone ribs meeting at a keystone carved with a small precise diagram — an engineer's mark rather than an artisan's — and the south roof open to the sky. Mei walked through the doorframe and said, looking around: *Ruins? Looks pretty pristine to me.*

Inside: machinery at its posts. A horizontal-pivot arm the height of a doorframe. A centrifuge locked mid-rotation. An apparatus of vertical rods at graduated heights. Forty years of dust lying consistent, with no prints in it. Yangyang named the owner — the Court of Savantae, a scientific research organization principally known for Automata Mechanics, whose research had extended into Jinzhou's northern territories before her posting, and who were considered quite serious. Chisa put her palm flat against the rod apparatus and said it was not silent: a resonance in the base, old, like something still counting. Nobody asked counting what.

Then Amy said *Oh* — the rounded, satisfied one, like a key finding its lock — and stepped into the south half where the open roof made the light good, and her posture lifted, and her voice dropped half a register into the broadcasting register. Fleet Snowfluff knew the Savantae. They were a favourite topic: the bizarre experiments, the flying machines nobody could replicate, the specimen catalogues. *One day, a daring young scientist soared into the sky and never returned.* Iuno said her name, once, with the flat authority she had used to redirect cardinals mid-processional, and it had no effect whatsoever. Amy cleared her throat, assumed the broadcaster's stance, and delivered it at volume with ten years of practice behind the pause: *One day, out of the blue — every single Court of Savantae member vanished.* And the Chairman's last words: *You want my research? You can have it. I left everything I gathered in the Court of Savantae's labs. Now you'll just have to find it.*

Mei's golden eyes were very bright. *Then we have to break in.* Iuno looked at the perfectly intact doorframe they had walked through unobstructed four minutes earlier. Chisa reported a sensor under the south floor. Yangyang, looking up at the keystone from a new angle, resolved the diagram into a registry mark of the kind used for *active* facility ownership, and said the Court of Savantae holds the active deed to a building everyone believes has been empty for forty years, and that she had filed three patrol reports on this road. Mei said, with great satisfaction, *We really have to break in.* Iuno said they were already inside. Mei said metaphorically. Iuno itemized the deed, the functioning machinery, and the fact that the floor sensor had registered their entry approximately forty-seven seconds ago. Then she reached for her notebook and said, to Amy, *Tell me about the Chairman* — which cost her something small and visible, and which she paid without comment. Amy's smile was the kind that had filled radio rooms.

Chisa found the lock by weight distribution: a tile that accepted pressure differently, mechanical pins underneath. Yangyang said standard Savantae security would be a frequency key attuned to the Chairman's personal waveform, which they did not have. Mei said *Move*, knelt, and put her marked hand flat on the tile. Nothing happened. Then her fingers moved — placing, not searching. Index on the upper-left corner. Ring finger in a groove nobody had identified. Thumb on a point along the bevel that produced a faint clean click. Then a quarter-turn, precise, the motion of somebody whose body remembered what her mind did not. Two more clicks. The tile sank two centimetres and slid sideways with the oiled ease of engineering built to outlast its builders. Yangyang asked how. Mei said *I don't know*, without distress, in the voice she uses for true things that will need to be sat with later, after dinner, with somebody beside her. Iuno wrote the time in her notebook and did not ask.

The lab below was pristine. Workbenches in rows; calibration instruments on adjustable arms; storage racks labelled in a hand approaching typography; a slate blackboard with equations in three colours, the final line incomplete, the chalk resting in the tray where it had been set down mid-thought. The ceiling was higher than the ruin above suggested — the lab had been carved down into the hillside.

And in the central chamber: rain. Thousands of droplets hanging motionless in a column two metres across, from a ceiling-mounted apparatus to a collection basin, each one perfectly formed, catching the lamplight like beads on invisible thread, declining to fall and declining to evaporate, as though time had excused itself from that column and had not come back. Chisa put a fingertip into the edge and a droplet broke — real water, cold, forty years old and fresh as morning. A placard bolted to the base read *Artificial Retroactive Rain. Phase two.* Iuno repeated *Retroactive* with the emphasis she reserves for words trying to smuggle ambition past her in a lab coat. Amy let three drops break against her palm and watched the water run along her lifeline into a basin where a shallow pool had been accumulating one accidental touch at a time for decades. Then she pointed at a single recent handprint in the dust on the nearest workbench — weeks, not years, and small, with the fingers spread.

The holographic projector activated at Mei's approach — at her approach, not her touch; the sensor floor again, reading her weight, or her waveform, or something it had been told to wait for.

The logs told it in order. The Court of Savantae Committee had expelled Professor Heisen and eleven researchers, using the word *dangerous* to mean *uncontrollable*, and a patron had funded them into a hillside laboratory. The patron's name appeared only as a symbol, and Yangyang took a sharp breath and identified it as Fractsidus. Phase one: the ancient civilization of Whining Aix's Mire had produced a phenomenon the researchers called a divine miracle — a singular event that reshaped the relationship between human frequency and the physical world — and Heisen's ambition was to reproduce it artificially, on demand. Phase two: the rain. Retroactive precipitation, water carrying encoded frequency backward through its own fall, theoretically capable of reaching the conditions that preceded the original event. The suspended column was the prototype. It worked. The rain fell and hung and carried. The miracle declined to trigger. Phase two point five: the patron imposed a deadline, the handwriting in the log changed from precise to pressured, and the researchers went to their contingency — an Artificial Controllable Tacet Field. A manufactured zone of frequency disruption; designed rather than grown from accumulated trauma; controllable, deployable, aimable. Amy's arms crossed in the protective fold, elbows held.

The last logs concerned Subject A038, described as an artificial replica of a divine entity, contained in a resonance chamber at the lowest level, in the clinical language people adopt when they have stopped being comfortable with what they are doing and have decided that tone will substitute for ethics. A038 showed anomalous behaviour: frequency readings the researchers labelled, with visible reluctance, *hunger*. Items disappeared at night. Equipment was found repositioned. Sounds came from the containment level that were not mechanical and had *want* in them. The researchers increased its rations, and the log called this *appeasement protocol*, which Iuno read aloud once, flatly, and then closed her eyes for three seconds. Late entries were fragmentary: surveillance failures, a researcher found in a corridor who could not explain how he had arrived there, and a patron whose communications became less frequent and more specific, as though whatever had funded the work was now watching from a distance it had chosen rather than one imposed on it.

Then the final hologram loaded: an old man's voice, making a confession to a room he expected to be empty forever. *It's getting closer. These will be my final words, haha…* — the laugh terrible because it was genuine, a man who had spent his life finding himself amusing and could not stop even there. *If only I had more time… I thought my enemies were misconceptions, waning passion, and my own ignorance. Only in the end did I realize… time is my ultimate foe. I became blindsided by my fear of death and insatiable thirst for truth, eventually derailing our research… It was too late when I finally came back to my senses… We… We had already walked into that false 'Gate'…* Iuno's hand came to rest on the console, the way her hand rests on things when she is making herself stay. Yangyang's hand moved without her looking at the page, the way it moves when the second ink takes over. Chisa's head tilted at something underneath the words that the recording had preserved without intending to. And in its final minute the voice found the clarity it had spent years burying: *They are our 'past'… but they are also our 'future'… Had I realized it sooner, there might have been a chance to get things back on track… The future being hailing from our past… Please save us… once again… Please, keep pushing forward… until you reach… the real Gate that can change the world…*

The hologram dissolved. The chamber went dark. And then the projector activated one more time.

A figure. Blurred, at failing resolution, flickering at the edges like a candle in moving air. A woman, or the shape of one, standing where Heisen had been. Unannounced. Unintroduced. Simply there, as though she had always been there and the recording had only now found enough power to show her. She was looking down — at her hands, or at the floor, or at something below the frame. Static ate her outline and returned it. Her hair moved, and there was no wind in the hologram.

Then she lifted her head. No features resolved. No eye colour could be confirmed. No mark on any hand could be verified.

But she was looking at Mei. At the angle that tracked precisely to where Mei was standing, as though the projection had measured the distance between the past and the present and found it navigable.

Mei went still — the involuntary kind, a body recognizing something before the mind had agreed to participate. Her right hand rose two inches and stopped. Amy took one step and stopped herself deliberately, because Mei's face had done the thing it does when being reached for too quickly breaks something that needs to stay intact long enough to be understood. Iuno watched the hand and the face and declined to catalogue, annotate, or interpret. Yangyang's pen stopped on the page. The blurred woman held Mei's gaze across forty years of dust and static and failed ambition, and stayed — the way things stay when they have been waiting and have decided that the person who arrived is the person they were waiting for. Then the charge failed, and the hologram folded inward, edges first, then centre, then the face, last of all the face.

*Okay*, Mei said, eventually, which is the word she uses when something has entered her and she has decided to let it stay long enough to learn its shape. *Okay*, Iuno said, same word, same weight, and her living hand found the marked one in the dark.

Mei's hand slipped out — a redirection, the motion she uses when her body needs work before a thing finishes arriving — and she said *Let's move on*, in the operational voice, and Iuno let her. Amy let her differently: by filling the room, talking about Savantae-standard calibration arms and the orbital platform in the frostlands where the wreckage was still pressurized in sections and she and Mei had spent an afternoon in the observation module. Mei said she did not remember. Amy said *I know*, with no weight on it, and told her she had complained that the viewport curvature made her dizzy and then eaten an entire ration pack standing up because she had forgotten lunch again. Mei said that sounded right. Amy said it was very her. And she kept talking — the modular construction, the intact spectrographic array still aimed at a star that had measurably moved, the flight manual printed in three languages one of which neither of them could read and which Mei had spent forty minutes on before admitting defeat with the specific grace of somebody who enjoys being defeated by interesting problems — and Mei listened, and asked small questions, and touched equipment as they passed with the unconscious familiarity of hands that had once worked in places like this and had been allowed to forget. Yangyang walked behind them recording nothing, because there was nothing to record yet; there was only Mei walking forward and Amy walking beside her talking about a shared past that one of them could see, and the sound of it was enough.

At the fourth chamber Chisa said *Seal*, and Yangyang's lamp found a blast door recessed into limestone with hinges cut from the same stone. **SUBLEVEL CONTAINMENT — A031 THROUGH A042 — CLEARANCE: CHAIRMAN ONLY.** Amy established context loudly and admitted, when challenged, that she was stalling with context. Mei put her marked hand on the seal and the mechanism read her the way the floor had — recognition rather than key — and the door unsealed with a sound like a held breath releasing.

The smell came up first: metal, ozone, and underneath both something organic and wrong, the smell of a living system that has run unsupervised for forty years and has solved the problem of sustenance in ways its designers did not intend. Chisa's hand went to her collar. *It's awake.*

The containment level was one enormous chamber. In the centre a resonance chamber stood open, its three concentric containment rings bent outward from the inside — bent, slowly, over years, with a patience worse than the damage. A038 was in the corner, and it had built itself a body. Monitoring equipment stripped from the walls and repurposed: sensor housings as joints, cable bundles as something like tendons, screen casings stacked and fused into a torso shaped with the approximate memory of something that had once seen a person and tried to become one from available materials. Too many joints. Proportions of a child's drawing made physical. Where a face should have been, a cluster of monitoring lenses arranged in a pattern that was not quite eyes and was not quite not.

Its output hit Chisa first; she caught herself on the doorframe and pressed her palms over her ears and said *Loud and confused. It's not hostile. It's looking for something.* Mei had already drawn, and the relief on her face was almost invisible, and Iuno saw it and Amy saw it — *she'd rather fight this than sit with that hologram for one more second* — and Amy, who has the same instinct and spent two years learning to identify it in herself, declined to stop her, because some walls you let somebody hit.

A038 moved the way things move when they have taught themselves locomotion from first principles, and then screamed: a raw shapeless frequency discharge, the sound of a system designed to replicate divine resonance that has spent forty years alone with that mandate and nobody to tell it when to stop. Mei was fast. Her first cut took a cable-tendon; her second opened a seam; her third severed a joint. Yangyang called the sensory array from the doorframe. A038 pivoted toward the voice — and then ran, past her, toward the doorframe and the staircase and the lab and the suspended rain one floor above, with the desperate momentum of something that had been locked in a room for forty years and had just discovered the door was open. Chisa intercepted it: no blade, feet planted, shoulders into the mass of it, redirecting rather than stopping, buying seconds. *Now.* Mei's fourth cut split the primary sensor housing, and the lenses went dark in sequence — left, centre, right — and A038 stopped. Blind, arms hanging at their wrong angles, output dropping from a scream to a hum to something Chisa, still braced against it, described as *tired. It's very tired.* Iuno, from the stairs where she had positioned herself with precise tactical judgement about when her presence would be in the way, said *Feel better?* Mei said *Shut up.* Iuno said *Mm.*

It slumped, and the mechanical legs gave out with a sound like a drawer of cutlery being gently set down, and it sat in the doorframe like an exhausted child in a costume made of monitoring equipment and ambition. Amy stood over it and looked at the bent rings and the empty chamber. *Forty years. It built itself a body from the walls. Because there was nobody to talk to.* Mei said *I know, Amy.* She crouched beside it, and forty years of solitary engineering looked back through lenses that no longer worked, and she put her marked hand on its cobbled shoulder and said *Yeah. I know* — to it, or to herself, or to the woman in the hologram whose face she had recognized and refused to name.

The hum thinned under her palm and left. The lenses caught the lamplight once more, held her reflection in dark glass a moment longer than the moment was, and went out, and the cables were cables and the torso settled against the doorframe with the sound of somebody setting down a very heavy bag at the end of a very long walk. Chisa rubbed her ears once with both palms and said *Gone*, and then, after a pause that was almost hesitation, *It stopped looking.* Nobody asked what it had been looking for. Mei withdrew her hand and left five spread fingers in the dust on the mechanical shoulder and looked at the impression and left it there. Yangyang documented the chamber for three minutes without stopping, closed the book, looked at the doorframe, and added one line in the second ink that she did not share.

They climbed past the rain, and Amy reached in one more time and let a single drop break against her knuckle and told it *Goodnight*, very quietly, and kept walking.

The walk home took longer, with the same distances. They passed through the settlement's edge without stopping for food, at the respectful distance of people carrying something they could not yet name and did not want to set down in a place belonging to other people's ordinary work. At the observation path Amy looked down at the pit and asked nothing, the way a person looks at something very large when they have just been reminded how small a room can be.

The sun was behind the western ridge when Mei spoke, looking at the road, in the voice of somebody who had carried a thing for exactly as long as she could carry it alone and had decided that alone was one day shorter than it used to be.

*I think I know that woman.*

Nobody stopped walking. Nobody turned. The gravel went on. She said it was not from the archive, or the file, or the mark — something else. Then she told them about a girl who had been asleep a very long time, with a journal, four thousand days of writing to no one, who had written a story about a woman who gets to start over and had wanted to be that woman so badly she fell into it. She did not say Lilith; she has said that name to two people and both of them were walking beside her. *The woman in the hologram. The way she was looking down. At her hands.* Then she stopped walking, on an orange road with the mine silent behind them. *She looked like someone who'd been writing for a long time and had just realized nobody was going to read it.*

That was all. She started walking again with her hands in her pockets. Nobody interpreted it. Nobody connected it to the First Resonator file, or the mark, or the founding, or the annexe she had refused to open, or offered any of the twelve inferences the evidence could support. The interval was getting shorter. *She said it today.*

Ahead, Jinzhou's first lamps were being lit by hand, one human match at a time, because the city had decided the alternative was not worth the efficiency. The east gate was open, as it always is at that hour, and they walked through it together in the particular order of people who had not discussed the order and did not need to.

## XI. THE AIRING

*(Youngest Gate 10 — 曬書, the sixth of the sixth month)*

The Bureau of Rites posts six articles on the fifth, under a heading that counts seven. *一 出* — Out, from the fourth hour. *二 看* — You may look. *三 翻* — Turn it at noon. *四 勿讀* — Do not read. *五 落* — What falls is not yours. *六 收* — In, before the rain. The seventh was struck in the year of the reform; the block was recut without it and the gap was kept, because a ward that has been reading six articles under a heading that counts seven for four hundred years dislikes being corrected.

Iuno woke at ten past four with her right shoulder full of glass, which it does four hours ahead of weather, with the give and the take getting smaller since the plateau — a fact she has recorded and told to one person, a wolf, who received it by feeding her something. She stood at the half-inch gap in a window that will not latch, told the draught to go back to sleep, and got a refusal. Then she took a lamp out to the corner where the ward changes, past Old Feng's shuttered paper-goods shop, to read a board she has read every morning for twenty-two days as a discipline, the way other people run. She got four characters into the new notice before the lamplight showed her the street, and the street was awake at twenty past four, quietly. Every door open. Every door with a chair in front of it. Quilts, coats, boots, hats, mattresses, seed trays, medicine boxes with their lids off, paper drying flat under stones at the corners — and books everywhere, opened to the middle and stood on their spines like small tents, in rows, all the way down the terrace. An old man went past with a chest on his back and said something cheerful about her lamp.

Mrs Ren's yard had four lines up, a cauldron, two stools, a stick, and a system explained once at speed in Huanglong: quilts on the top line because they want the height, boots on the wall, books standing with their pages open, or they steam and you have brought her mould in the sixth month, and she has had mould in the sixth month and did not care for it. Cartethyia translated most of it and admitted that a fifth remark about mould had contained an insult whose grammar went past her. Amy came out backwards dragging the rig case and stopped dead at the sight of a whole household's paperwork on a rack — somebody's deed, with a red seal, under a rock, on a wall, with nobody standing next to it. Ciaccona said eleven people were standing next to it. Amy said standing *near* it is a different job, looked at it for four minutes, and said *I hate this.*

The coats went last, because Mrs Ren does the coats herself, and the reason is that a coat has to be turned out first and people are sentimental about pockets. She took Lupa's without comment, made a small hard noise at the state of Ciaccona's lining, and set Chisa's aside on its own, which nobody asked about. Then she put a hand out to Iuno and waited.

Four seconds went by, which in a yard holding nine people is a long time. Then Iuno turned away from the line, put the coat over the back of the stool, went through it herself with her living hand, and came out with two things — one flat and folded and about the size of four characters, one with a shape — and put them in the breast pocket of the shirt underneath, and hung the coat up herself. Mrs Ren watched the whole of it from three feet away with her hands folded, and when it was done she pegged the shoulder seam straight, twice, because Iuno had got it crooked, and said nothing at all, and moved on to the next one. At the cauldron, Lupa kept her head where it was and her left ear went round.

Mei's coat went up at the fourth hour on the second line from the wall, third from the end, between a quilt and a bolt of winter felt, and Mrs Ren re-pegged it at a quarter past five because the wind kept getting into the shoulder. At half past five Iuno came out for water and found Mei standing under that line with her hands at her sides. Mei turned round with the grin already on, said she was having a slow one, and went and got the water herself. Iuno decided to leave it alone, and said out loud, to nobody, that she was leaving something alone, so it would be on the record as a decision. Mei told her she is allowed to just get water.

At six the household split over the last of the congee, in the manner of a committee that has never once operated as a committee. Lupa took the west terraces, because nine houses on that stair have somebody over sixty in them and today every one of them is carrying a chest down four flights; Chisa went with her and announced that she was going because she had been asked the night before and had said yes. Ciaccona took Bookbinders' Row, because every press on that street would have its type out in trays in the sun and she had wanted to see it since the second morning, and because there is a theatre on that street, and the theatre has a trunk. Cartethyia went with her, on the grounds that the fruit stalls were shut and she could not be cheated, carrying the terrarium in both hands at arm's length like a lantern, because plants go in the sun on the sixth and this one had been in a box on a truck for twenty days and she wanted to be somewhere she could watch it happen. In case. In case of what. *In case it needs me.* The yard let that go by; Amy put her chin on the top of the terrarium for a second, which was permitted. Mei, Iuno and Amy took the east gate, because a paper trade on the sixth of the sixth will have every scrap it owns out on a board by seven, and the letter-writer shuts at eleven and is impossible after nine. Amy had been planning it since the fifth, because she reads all the boards, which is a thing she does; Iuno reads them too, and Amy gets there first, and it is becoming a sport.

Yangyang crossed them at the arch at ten past six, going the other way, in a hurry she was declining to display, and gave them three things on her fingers. One: everything is open today except the offices, so if you want a thing, ask a person, and there will be somebody on a stool outside every trade, and they will all say yes, because it is the sixth. Two: anything on the ground stays on the ground until a ward officer lifts it, and you will want to disobey that and you will be wrong. Three — and she stopped — the Bureau has posted fair weather to the ninth hour. Iuno said *Has it.* They looked at each other for a period Amy afterwards estimated at two seconds and Mei afterwards estimated at eleven. Yangyang said *You'd say the seventh.* Iuno said *I would.* Yangyang said that was what she thought, and went up the wall — and came back four paces to say, with her hat brim level and her ears going a colour under it, that it was a year today. Since her report. The whole one.

Mei walked east and said it to the paving: a year ago today somebody stood in a room and said out loud that the weather is a person, and nobody has thrown her a single party about it. Iuno warned her that she was naming a team. Mei said she was noting it.

### The east gate

Shen's table stands at the north side of the arch, out of the traffic, with a schedule of charges repainted so many times that the numbers stand proud of the wood. On the sixth she puts out six boards on trestles along the gate wall, alone, at four, and it takes her two hours because of her hands. On the boards, weighted at every corner with river stones the size of a fist, laid flat and open and face up in the early sun: three hundred and six undelivered letters.

Amy stopped eight metres short and stayed there. Iuno went still in the way she goes still at exhibitions and worked it out aloud: the letters would rot in a tin in that climate inside a decade, and Shen has held some of them forty years, so at some point a woman at a gate worked out that the only way to keep an undeliverable letter is to take it out on the day everybody takes everything out and put it in the sun with the quilts, where it looks like housekeeping. Mei said that is not housekeeping. Iuno said it is entirely housekeeping, and that is what makes it survivable.

They walked the line. People were doing it in both directions, unhurried, hands behind backs, the way people go round a market they have no money for. An old woman went along all six boards with her chin up, reading nothing, looking at every one. Two boys ran the length of it and were shouted at by three separate adults for running near paper. A carter stopped at the fourth board, took his hat off, put it back on, and went to work. Nobody picked one up.

Shen finished charging a young man for nineteen characters about a baby who had come and had a name, and then looked up. *You'll be here about the boards.* The rule is the same as everywhere else: you may look, and if one of them is yours you say so and take it, and she will want a name and she will want it out loud. In forty years, eleven have gone that way. The last was six years ago — a man from the fish market, for his mother, who spent two hours on the boards because he was looking for her hand and had forgotten that Shen had written it, and who sat on that step most of the morning afterward and then bought a second one and posted it to the same bad address. That one is on board five, second from the left. Mei looked at board five, second from the left, for a while, and said her own was not there. Shen said Mei's went into the box on the twenty-first and out of the city on the twenty-second and has three prefectures and a survey to survive; they come back in the spring, when they come back, so she will know by the plum rains and will not know before — and told her not to check the ward box every morning on her way past, except that she will, and so does everybody, and the box has a dent in the lid from forty years of people checking.

Mei asked what the plum rains are. Meiyu: the sixth month rains, which is what today is for. Everything you own comes out on the sixth, because in three weeks the air turns into a wet cloth and stays that way for forty days, and the things that go into the plum rains damp come out of them ruined. *That's the entire holiday. It's a laundry day with a Bureau attached.*

Amy tilted her head and asked for the name of the rains again, and how it is written — plum, and rain — and then whether the plum is the same character as the first of the five on the truck's door. The gate did a certain amount of ordinary noise for a couple of seconds. *No*, said Shen. Amy asked whether she was sure. Shen said she has written that surname perhaps two hundred times and has written the rains on a weather board every year for forty; they are not the same character, they are not related, and they do not sound the same to anybody who grew up saying either of them. And then: *And you'd be the fourth to ask me.* Iuno asked who the other three were. Shen said *Next*, to a queue with one person in it.

While that person took four minutes, Amy walked board six alone from the far end backwards, which is how she reads anything she is frightened of. Three hundred and six letters face up in the sun, none of them hers to read. She read none of them and looked at all of them, which is the whole permitted operation, and the permitted operation gives you a great deal: the hands, the sizes, the fold-wear, the ones with a smear at the bottom right where a thumb sat while somebody dictated. Eleven on the good paper. Two of four sheets. One of six characters, paid for and written and posted and returned from four prefectures away in a hand that had been steady the whole way across.

Then she came back and sat down on the customer's stool without being asked, which nobody at that gate has done in some years, and asked what happens to the tin — and, told to ask the question she was asking, laid it out. Shen is seventy-something. The tin is not ward property, because the ward never took it in. It is not post property, because the post gave them back, which is what makes them what they are. It is not hers to burn, because they were paid for, and a paid job belongs to the customer, and the customer is the one who never came. So it is an estate item with no owner and no column — and in this city an estate item with no owner and no column goes to the ward, and the ward puts it in the basket, and the basket sits a year, and on the sixth of the sixth the basket goes on a table where anybody may read anything in it. *Three hundred and six letters. On a table. In the sun. With people allowed.*

Shen put the brush down across the rest and said *That's right.* Amy said *You knew.* Shen said she did it in about eleven seconds, and it took the ward clerk who told her eight years ago the better part of an afternoon, and he cried in her chair, which she did not enjoy. She has known since she was sixty-two. It is the only part of the trade she has ever considered giving up, and she has considered it three times, and the third time was that afternoon. Amy said *Give it to me* — she is nineteen, she has a column for everything, she has never once lost a thing given to her to hold. Shen said she believed all of that, and that Amy had been in the city three weeks and was asking for three hundred and six households' private business, and that the reason was a good one and it was still the same request. *Sit with it a year.* Amy sat with it about four seconds and said *Fine.* Shen said *Come back on the sixth.* Amy asked which sixth. *Any of them.*

Mei went along the boards a second time on her own, slowly, and at the fourth board crouched down on her heels with her elbows on her knees and stayed there long enough that a stallholder brought her a stool she declined and then accepted.

Iuno stood in the arch with her back to all of it and held a folded paper up between herself and the low sun. Four characters, whose meaning she has known since the twenty-third. The trouble has never been the four characters. The trouble is the seal space: a square of clean paper at the lower left, standard size, faintly ruled, waiting for a chop — and a document of that kind with no chop on it is a sentence with the subject left out. She had four candidates and disliked three.

Held against the sun from underneath, at twenty past seven in the morning, the paper showed her what a fortnight of lamplight had declined to show her. Inside the seal space, in the fibre of the sheet itself, pressed in when the sheet was made and about a shade lighter than the paper around it, was a chop. Made in. Pressed at the mill, before anybody wrote a word on it. *Somebody had this paper made*, said the High Priestess, very quietly, with none of the velvet on. *Before they wrote on it. And they left the space empty anyway.*

She folded it along its old folds, put it back in the breast pocket, and turned round — and her daughter, the word arriving unannounced and permitted to stand, was coming back up the line of boards with her thumb working at something small and square in her left jacket pocket, and took it about halfway out, and put it back. Iuno filed the halfway.

As they left, Shen spoke without looking up from a farmer's dictation, in the flat volume of a professional dropping something over her shoulder: *The thing in your left pocket. Bring it when you want it read. Not before.* And: do not take it to anybody else at this gate, because two of them will make something up, and one of those two is very good at it, and Amy would believe him. Amy asked how long she had known it was in there. Shen said: *Nine times.*

### The west terraces

Lupa took the first chest at ten past seven, from a woman on the second flight who had got it as far as the top step and then had to sit down on it, and she took it the way she takes things, which is by arriving underneath it, and put it in the sun squared to the wall with the lid propped. The woman thanked her and then stood in the middle of her own yard with her hands moving against each other, getting quieter and quieter.

Chisa explained: *You've made a debt.* Lupa said she carried a box. Chisa said she carried a box for a stranger on a day the whole street can see the yard, and now the woman owes a foreigner, and she cannot pay it back over years the way she would pay a neighbour, because Lupa will be gone; and she has been standing there four breaths longer than she meant to, working out what she has in the house. Lupa said she would tell her it was nothing. Chisa said that is worse — that prices it. Lupa put the case for Septimont: there, a favour earns you the right to say so, loudly, at a party, for nine years, and that is the whole point, that is the interest — and here she has to let the woman pay her off inside a morning or she has hurt her. Chisa said it is her system upside down: hers runs the debt long on purpose, and this one closes it before dark, because everybody on this stair has to still be on this stair next year. Lupa looked at four flights, nine households, and a city with its books standing open on every wall, and said *…Huh.*

Then she went and asked the woman for a chair. She tried water first and got a face like a slammed shutter. So: a chair, out of the house, in the doorway, for an hour, from noon, so she could sit in it and eat her own lunch on a stranger's step in a ward where a step is the only real estate anybody owns on the sixth. The woman brightened up like a lamp and went and got the good one. Chisa said it was well done: she had asked for something that costs the woman, in public, that she can point at. Lupa told her to put something else on the record.

They did four more flights and eleven more chests and a mattress that took both of them and an argument. Somewhere in the third hour Chisa's pace changed by about a fifth of a step, on the flat, at the turn by the dyer's, and Lupa noticed it the way she notices everything, which is late and completely. The city had gone quiet: every wall on the west side hung with quilts, and quilts eat sound; the lane that gives back a hard flat slap off the render at the second turn gave back nothing; the rain-butt Chisa counts from had been moved into the yard to be scrubbed; the dyer's had its vats out and its door shut, so the smell was in a different place by four metres, and four metres is everything. Chisa stood at the turn and said none of that. Lupa dropped back one pace and came up on her right, on the outside, at the same speed, and put nothing into it at all — no hand, no comment, no slowing — and walked her through two lanes and a bridge.

At the far end, level, facing forward, Chisa said the whole city had moved its furniture into the street, that every mark she uses is somewhere else today, that the walls have gone soft, that there is no echo anywhere in the ward, and that she had been lost for forty minutes. Lupa said *I know.* She had known at the dyer's. She had waited because Chisa would say so when she wanted to say so. And then Chisa gave the other half: *It's also the most of Jinzhou I have had.* Everything in every house is out where she can hear it. Nine households on that stair and she knows what all nine of them own; four keep something in a lacquer box and three of those boxes have hinges mended by the same hand; the house at the second landing has a child's abacus with two beads missing and a set of surgical scales, and nobody in that house is a doctor or a merchant, and both objects are older than the plaster. Lupa asked whether that was something to do something about. Chisa said no. *It's the best day I've had since the shore.*

Baizhi had the clinic's eleven years out on nine trestles above the third landing, in date order, weighted, with a boy of about twelve stationed at the corner to shout if the wind came, which the wind did not. She greeted their arrival by continuing a task and putting a cup at Lupa's elbow without pausing the sentence, and sat her down for four minutes on the grounds that she had been on a stair since seven, runs high, and it is the sixth of the sixth. Chisa stayed standing with her chin at the angle and said Baizhi had brought everything out except one: a small soft-cover book inside her coat on the left, on the warmest morning of the year, adjusted twice on the steps, about four pages written and the rest empty — which Chisa knows from what it does when Baizhi moves. Baizhi finished turning a sheet, squared it, put a stone on it, and observed that this was a remarkable amount of information to take off a coat, and that Chisa would notice she had not asked how. The book stays in the coat; it is not a clinical record and is not required to be aired, and if it were required she would air it and would still not enjoy the day. Chisa declined to press, and gave the reason: *I have a book like that.* Baizhi looked up for the first time, went back to the trestle, and about ninety seconds later said, without preamble, that four pages is a great deal when a person writes at the rate she writes.

At the ninth house on the stair, at the top, an old woman had one line up in a yard three feet wide, and on it a summer school uniform — jacket, skirt, white collar, ironed, sized for somebody about fourteen. The youngest person in that house is fifty-one. She was going along the collar with a small brush, doing the seams.

Chisa stopped at the gate the way a person stops when the floor changes, and stayed stopped, and Lupa put the chest down and said nothing at all for two full minutes, which for her is a feat of engineering. Then: *Twenty years.* Honami never got to the sixth month; the loop turned over before the rains every time, so nothing in that city ever needed drying, because nothing in that city ever got wet twice. Chisa's hand had gone to her left wrist without instruction. She had been thinking all morning that this was a foreign custom. Her mother kept her room for twenty years. Her summer uniform is in a box on the top shelf of it. Her mother told her it was in a box; she did not tell her it was ironed. *She has been doing this. Every year. On her own. In a house where nobody came back.* Lupa let that sit exactly as long as it needed and then did the only thing she is any good at: *She's not on her own this year.* And then: *Write to her tonight. I'll sit there while you do it. I'll say nothing. I'm very good at it, you've all agreed.* Chisa made a small sound that on anybody else would have been a laugh.

The old woman came to the gate with her brush still in her hand, looked at the tall one, then the small one, then the red string wound loose around the small one's left wrist, and said something short. Chisa translated: the string wants the sun. Red goes first. Everybody keeps the red out of the light and then in eleven years they have a pink one and they are upset about it, and the way you keep red is to put it out on the sixth with everything else and let it go the way it goes. Chisa has kept that string out of the light for four years. She knows what sun does to a dye. It has been a deliberate and daily operation, and nobody has ever asked her about it, and a woman with a brush in her hand had just described it to her in a language she is on lesson seven of. She asked Lupa to ask what you do if it is already faded. Lupa asked, badly, with three words and both hands. The old woman answered and went back to her collar. *She said faded red is still red.* Lupa said *Huh.* Chisa said she said it like it was a fact about dye. Lupa said it was a fact about dye. The string stayed on the wrist. It stayed out of the sun as well; Chisa turned her hand over as they went down the steps, without any apparent decision, and neither of them mentioned it, and it went into the day's account as a thing considered.

### Bookbinders' Row

Every press on the street had its type out — trays on trestles down both sides, four hundred metres of small metal in the sun, laid out by frequency the way type is always laid out, which from the top of the street makes a grey river with a pattern in it. Ciaccona sat on a step and played badly on purpose for eleven minutes, using the entire technique: right notes, flat bridge, tempo arriving a hair early, the specific irritating competence of a person who could fix it and has decided against. In Ragunna it takes four minutes; in a Mengzhou tavern it took nine. Today the whole street went past her without a flicker, because everybody on it was carrying trays with both hands and had been up since three, and a foreigner playing a flat bridge was, in the professional judgement of Bookbinders' Row, weather. *This city is rude.*

At minute twelve an old man came out of a shop four doors down with a stool, put it on the pavement in front of her, sat on it, folded his arms, and looked at her for about thirty seconds. *The bridge. You've done it eleven times and it's been wrong eleven times in the same place, which means you can do it. So do it.* Ciaccona put her head back and laughed at a shopfront and then did it properly, twice, and the second time she took the flat bridge and let it be flat and put the fix underneath it instead, which is the thing she learned inside a cosmic horror from a girl with a laurel of thorns. Old Feng listened with his arms folded, got up, took his stool, said *Better*, and went back into a shop that sells funeral paper.

Cartethyia followed him in and came out four minutes later with a bundle of offcuts the size of a loaf and enormous private satisfaction, having paid four times what it was worth — because he told her it was four times what it was worth while he was taking the money, and said he was taking it because she had come into a paper shop on the sixth of the sixth and asked for the bits, and then gave her two extra sheets of the red, which is the expensive one, so really it was three times. Ciaccona began an objection. Cartethyia said: *He told me, Cia. Nobody in Ragunna ever told me.* She was already folding as she walked, offcuts coming out of her hands as small people, one every forty seconds, arms out, no faces. She gave the first to a child on a doorstep, the second to a compositor holding four hundred pieces of metal who took it in his teeth, and the third to a dog, who had had a hard morning.

Then the trays. Ciaccona walked the Row from bottom to top and back, and got what she came for in the first eighty metres, which is that you can see the holes. A tray of type in a case is a full grid; a tray in the sun on the sixth is a full grid with gaps, because a working press has sorts out in the formes and sorts worn down and sorts that went into somebody's pocket in 'forty-one, and the compartments they came from sit open and empty with the felt showing at the bottom. She counted them at the fourth press, at the seventh, and by the eleventh she had stopped counting and was standing very still with her hand flat on a trestle edge.

*How many is arithmetic. Which is the question.* Every press on that street is missing sorts, which is the trade. And every shop on one street should lose the same letters in the same order, because wear is wear and they all set the same language, and the common sorts go first. Eleven shops. Eleven different holes. And four of those holes are in compartments the case says should be full, because those are rare sorts — the ones a shop buys once and keeps for ninety years — and they are gone out of four separate shops, and nobody has replaced them, and every compositor she had asked in the last twenty minutes had said the same sentence. *It went.* Eleven compositors, one sentence between the lot of them, and it is the sentence you use about a cat. *It went. On its own. Somewhere.*

Cartethyia found one turned over: third from the end, face down, when all the others are face up because you cannot read them face down and the whole point of putting them out — and her hand went toward it, and Ciaccona caught her wrist four centimetres short, without hurrying, the way you stop a friend reaching into a fire she has not noticed. The compositor at that trestle, a woman of sixty with her sleeves to the elbow, watched them do it from the far end and went back to her work. *Ask her first.* Cartethyia asked, in her decent bad Huanglong, whether the turned one was turned for a reason, listened to the whole answer, said thank you twice, and came back. It has been face down since before her, and she has been at that press thirty-one years; her teacher put it that way and told her not to bother turning it, because a sort that is face down is a sort that is not being used, and a sort that is not being used does not need reading. And then the compositor said the second thing on her own, without being asked, which is how you can tell it has been sitting in her for thirty-one years: *It's out in the sun with the rest of us. That's all it's owed.*

Ciaccona looked at the tray a long moment, took her hand off Cartethyia's wrist, put it in her own pocket where a blank ruled card has been in four pockets in four days, left it there, and started walking. *Come on. There's a theatre.*

### The Plum Rain Troupe

The troupe's yard, behind the fourth press through an arch, held the entire contents of a theatre: costumes on forty metres of line, hung by the shoulders, all facing the same way, so that walking under them is like walking under a queue; boots in ranks; two dozen headdresses on poles; a rack of swords being oiled by a boy who was doing it correctly; drums on their sides. And in the middle, on four trestles pushed together, with the lid off and the contents out flat in rows and weighted at the corners, a trunk of drafts.

Master Qu is eighty-one and does the trunk himself, sheet by sheet, from a stool, at the speed of a man who has calculated how long the sun will hold and has allocated accordingly. Without looking up, he said the costumes were on the line, the boy would show them the sword rack, and he would rather they did not sing in the yard — because she had a lute on her back and a face like a Ragunnan, and this was the third year running that somebody had come through that arch on the sixth and offered to sing him something. Ciaccona asked what happened to the first two. *They sang.*

She sat on the edge of a trestle at the far end from him without taking the lute off her back and looked at four hundred sheets of paper drying in a yard, in the order he made them, weighted, with the rejected ones in the same rows as the kept ones and no visible distinction between the two. And somewhere around the middle of the third trestle there was a run of nine sheets, in a row, all of them blank. Ruled. Margined. Headed. Empty.

*You went straight to them*, Qu said. Ciaccona said they were the loudest thing in the yard.

That is the second act. Of a commission he took eleven years ago and finished nine years ago and has never staged. The first act is behind her and the third is under her left hand, and the second act is nine sheets of nothing and has been since the year he understood the assignment, and he airs it every sixth of the sixth with everything else, because it is part of the work and the damp does not care whether a page has writing on it. Cartethyia asked why nine. *Because nine is how long that act runs.* He timed it: he is eighty-one and has been in the trade sixty years and has never once put a thing on a stage without knowing how long it takes. Nine minutes. The doors stand open. There is no one on the stage and nothing is said and the house does whatever the house does. Ciaccona asked what the house does. *I've no idea. I've never staged it.*

What stopped him: the commission was to write the wind. He spent two years trying to get it onto a stage and the entire two years were a mistake, and he found out why in the fourth year from an attendant who cleans that yard, who did not know she was telling him anything. The subject already attends. Regularly. Back door, standing, through the domestic scenes, gone before the battle. *You do not write a play about somebody who is in the audience. You open the doors and you shut up for nine minutes and you let them have the room.* Cartethyia said that was very good. Qu said it was very cheap — it costs nothing and takes nine minutes and he has never had the nerve. Ciaccona asked why. He looked at the nine blank sheets in the sun. *Because a rest is the loudest bar in any piece of music, girl, and if you put nine minutes of it in front of four hundred people and nobody comes, then you have found out.*

Ciaccona did not move for a while. Her hand went into her pocket, and stayed there, and came out empty. Then she asked one more thing, and Qu told her she would ask who paid, and she said she would ask who paid. Anonymous. Extravagant. In coin. He tipped his head three degrees toward a small tray on the corner of the second trestle, out with everything else, being aired the way you air anything metal that has sat in a box for eleven years, which is to say for no reason at all except that it was in the box. Half-coppers. Old minting, all of one year. He has never spent one. Cartethyia crouched beside the tray with the terrarium in her arms and asked how many. *Enough to keep a troupe eleven years.* How many exactly. A pause of about the length of a man deciding whether to be irritated. *Three hundred and six.*

Ciaccona's hand closed on the strap. Neither of them said anything at all, and the boy went on oiling the swords, and eighty metres away at the east gate a woman of seventy-one was laying three hundred and six letters flat in the sun for the fortieth year, and there is no reason on this earth those two numbers should have anything to do with one another. Cartethyia began *Do we —* and Ciaccona said no: they put it somewhere and leave it there and do not go running at it, because everything in this city has a column and the column is usually a coincidence. And, getting up off the trestle: *And if it isn't, it'll still be true on Thursday.*

### Noon

At noon a bell goes at the ward office, and then at the next ward office, and then at the one after that, four seconds apart, all the way down the terraces, and a hundred and forty thousand people turn their paper over. It arrives as a long dry hiss from everywhere at once and it has a shape: it starts at the top of the city and comes down, ward by ward, four seconds at a time, and takes about ninety seconds to reach the harbour, and then it stops, all at once, and the gulls complain.

Amy was on the wall above the east arch, on her back, boots on the parapet, hands behind her head, with a receiver in her jacket she had not taken out. *That's forty thousand roofs. Nobody's running it.* Mei said there is a bell. Amy said there is a bell and then ninety seconds of a hundred and forty thousand people all doing the same thing without anybody talking — and she knows what that costs, because she ran half a city, at night, and only the ones who could get a set; and the thing that took her four years to build was two thousand people doing something at the same time on a cue, and it broke every eleven weeks, and she had to be talking the entire time or it fell over. *Here it's a bell and four hundred years and nobody has to say anything at all.*

Mei lay down on the parapet next to her, which is a thing a magistrate would have views about, and put her hands behind her head in the same way and looked at the same sky, and asked how long she had been holding it. Since twenty past four that morning. *It's fine.* *Amy.* *…It's a bit loud.* *Okay.*

And then the real one, said to the sky rather than to her mother, which is how Amy says the ones that count: *There's no job.* Everywhere they have been, she arrives and inside a day she knows what she is for — nobody is counting, or nobody is talking, or somebody needs a voice at two in the morning. And this place counted itself before she was born and will be counting when she is — and she left the end of it in the air, where it sat, being a whole sentence. Mei said *Yeah*, and then *That's the good version, though.* Amy said *I know. That's what's annoying about it.* Mei laughed at the sky with her whole chest and a gull left the parapet in disgust, and Amy put her boot against her mother's boot and left it there.

Iuno was on the step of the arch with her back against the stone and a shoulder she had decided at about eleven to stop describing. Yangyang came down off the wall at a quarter past twelve and asked. Iuno said *The seventh hour* — at six that morning it had been a professional opinion; at a quarter past twelve it was a fact about her arm, and she disliked both of those sentences and was saying the second one anyway, because a woman she lives with has made a rule about it.

Yangyang did the arithmetic with her face entirely still. Books come in at the sixth hour if they move now: four hours of sun lost off the top of the day, and half the city gets a second airing next month if the plum rains break early, which they will not, because they never do. And if they do, a ward is a ward and a book is a book and the Bureau of Rites answers questions at the frontier court until the ninth month. Then she named her actual problem. *I want a column.* She can file *wind rising* off her own eyes and they will shorten the day inside forty minutes. She cannot file *a foreigner's arm*. There is no field for it. If she writes it in remarks the Bureau will strike it and enter her own observation in its place, and she will have got what she wanted and lied to get it. Iuno looked up at her with an expression a number of people in two countries would have recognised and been afraid of, and said it was the most honest procedural objection she had heard in eleven years. Yangyang said it was a nuisance. Iuno got up off the step badly, using the wall, and gave her the instruction: do nothing, post nothing, go up the wall and watch her own weather with her own eyes and file what she sees when she sees it — and if the arm is right she will see it at about half past six and will have her forty minutes. And if she sees nothing until seven, the city gets wet, and Iuno will have been right, and being right will have been worth nothing at all, which is a lesson she paid thirty years for and can recommend.

Yangyang said she would be on the ninth tower, which has the north face. Iuno said she knew what it has. Four paces on, Yangyang stopped without turning round and said: *She's holding it.* The air over the city has been sitting still since the fourth hour — nine hours — and there are eleven hundred lines up on the west terraces and every one has hung dead all day. It is the sixth: the one day of the year this country wants its weather to sit down and be quiet, and she has been sitting down and being quiet since before it got light. Iuno asked what that costs. Yangyang said *I'll find out tonight, when she can't do the flute*, and went up the wall.

At the second landing of the west stair, from noon, a she-wolf sat in a borrowed chair in a stranger's doorway and ate her own lunch for one hour, with the chair squared to the step and her boots on the stone, while nine households went past and every one of them looked at the chair and clocked whose chair it was and who was in it and adjusted their accounts accordingly — and the woman who owned the chair came out twice for no reason at all and stood in her own doorway with her arms folded, being seen. By the fortieth minute Lupa had worked out that she was having an extremely good time and had begun to resent it. *I sat in a chair for an hour and now everyone on this stair thinks she's magnificent.* Chisa said she is: she gave a chair to a foreigner on the sixth, and she paid Lupa in an hour of being the sort of household that has a chair to give, which is a better rate than Lupa was asking. And when Lupa laid out the Septimont version — nine years of a wolf who owes her, dining out on it, and carrying the box when she died — Chisa named what she disliked: that here it is settled, shut, with nobody carrying anything about it into next year. And then the rest of it, which Lupa invited: she has been on that stair since seven and has carried nineteen chests and a mattress and has not put one thing of her own in the sun today, because the medal is on a wall in another country and everything else she owns is in a fire, and there is no article on that notice for people who keep their dead somewhere the weather cannot get at them. The stair went on being a stair for a while. *…Eat your rice.* *I've eaten it.* *Eat someone else's.*

At twenty to one an old woman on the fourth landing had one line running from her lintel to a hook on the opposite wall at the height of a raised hand, and the pole she uses to lift it had gone in the plum rains of the year before last, and her quilt was on the ground. Cartethyia offered to help, in Huanglong, correctly. Whether she asked first is disputed — she maintains she asked; Ciaccona maintains she asked the quilt; she clarifies that she asked the general area. Fleurdelys stood eight feet tall in a lane four feet wide with a wet quilt in one hand and the line in the other and her head somewhere in the second storey, and the old woman looked up at her with complete and untroubled professional interest and asked whether she could do the ones on the next lane as well. Ciaccona said to tell her no. Cartethyia had already said yes, because there are four houses on that landing and every one has somebody over seventy in it and the pole broke two years ago. By ten past one there were eleven quilts up on three lanes at a height at which no quilt on that terrace had ever hung, and a queue, and an argument at the back of the queue about precedence that resolved itself along lines of seniority so ancient that nobody had to say them out loud, and a small girl sent for, running, from two streets away, to look. The Blessed Maiden of the Order, godslayer, blade of a six-pilot god, hung out eleven quilts and one carpet and a bag of seed and the summer bedding of four households, in the archaic register, at volume, because she had discovered by the third that announcing each one made the queue laugh. *BEFALL.* *That's a pillow.* *IT HAS BEFALLEN.* Ciaccona sat on a step guarding a camellia in a foreign lane and laughed until she had to put her forehead on the soundboard.

### The Ge house

The Ge house is on the last lane before the ward changes and it has a yard with a gate, and the gate stood open, because they all stood open. In the yard, on two trestles, weighted at eight corners with river stones, out flat in the sun, eleven generations long: the family scroll.

Cartethyia stopped at the gate to look, because looking is the second article, and turn it at noon is the third, and the fourth is the one about reading, and she has known all six since half past four that morning, and she is a person who keeps rules. She looked for four seconds. Then she said, out loud, in decent bad Huanglong, at conversational volume, from a stranger's gate: *Somebody wrote that name and crossed it out before the ink was dry.*

Ciaccona was eleven metres up the lane and heard the shape of it before the words, which happens to her about once a year and has never once been good news. By the time she reached the gate there was a man in it. Ge Ruilin is about fifty and had been carrying a table, and he put the table down and said *Say that again.* Cartethyia began to apologise and was told she had said it at his gate on the sixth and could say it again.

She said it level, which is the thing that costs her. The seventh generation has four sons and three names. The fourth has a stroke through it. The stroke is the same ink as the name, the same brush, the same hand, and it went on while the character was still wet, because it has spread at the crossing the way a wet line spreads and a dry one does not. Somebody entered a person into that family and struck him out inside the same minute, and then went on and wrote the eighth generation underneath in the same sitting.

Nobody in the yard moved. There were four people in it and eleven in the lane, because a raised voice at a gate on the sixth of the sixth is the most interesting thing that will happen on that terrace all year.

*We know*, Ruilin said. Everyone in that house has known since they were nine. Everybody on the lane knows. The ward office has a copy and the clerk knows. Nobody says it at the gate. Nobody says it in the yard. It comes out one day a year because paper rots, and for that one day the whole street walks past it and looks at it and does not read it, and that has worked for a hundred and fifty years — and then a foreigner arrives in the sixth month and does it in nine seconds. Cartethyia said *Yes.* He said she was not going to argue. She said *No. You're right.* He asked why she did it. And Cartethyia, who has been asked worse and answered honestly, answered honestly: *Because a name was missing and I saw where it went.*

Ciaccona had reached the gate by then and had done nothing, which took an effort she would be describing to a wolf later at some length. She has a technique for a room like that. She has four. She could have had the lane laughing inside a minute and the man at the gate would have taken it, because people take it, because that is the trade. She stood in a lane in a foreign country with her hands off the lute and let her friend be wrong in public. *I'm not going to sing you out of this one.* *I know.* *I could.* *I know. Don't.*

Ge Suqin is seventy-four and came out of the house at the point where her nephew was deciding what kind of man to be about it. She came slowly, with a stick, and stopped at the scroll on the way and put two fingers on the seventh generation without looking at it, the way you touch a doorframe going through, and told Ruilin to go and carry the table. He stood there four seconds and then went and carried the table, and that is the last thing he did in this account, and he did not look at Cartethyia again, and he has not forgiven her, and there is no version of that day in which he does.

Suqin came to the gate and looked at the small foreigner with the crystal thorns for a while, in the sun, at a quarter past two. She asked how old she was and was told it was complicated, and said everybody's is. Then: she is seventy-four; her grandmother told her about the seventh generation when she was nine, in a kitchen, once, and never again, and had it off her own grandmother the same way, and that is four women in a row keeping an agreement made by people none of them met. It is a good agreement. It has held a hundred and fifty years, it has cost the family nothing, and it has kept the yard quiet. *And I have been waiting fifty years for somebody to say it out loud where I could hear it, and I had entirely given up, and I want you to understand that what I am is something with no name on it, and that grateful and angry would both have been simpler.*

Then: *Did you read it.* The character under the stroke is still there — one line through, not four — and whoever did it was in a great hurry or wanted it left legible, and she has never in her life been able to decide which. *Did you read it.* Cartethyia said yes. *Then say it to me. Once. I'm the last one who's allowed.*

The lane had eleven people in it and every one of them was looking somewhere else with enormous concentration. Cartethyia went in through the gate and stood next to an old woman and said one thing, quietly, into the side of her head, and it took about a second and a half. Ge Suqin closed her eyes and stood like that for a while, in her own yard, with a hundred and fifty years of her family out flat on two trestles behind her. Then she opened them and went to the scroll and picked one of the eight river stones off the eighth corner and put it into Cartethyia's hands. *That's held that corner fifty-one years.* Cartethyia began to refuse. *There are eight and the paper needs six.* And, already turning away: *Go on. My nephew's going to shut the gate in about a minute and he's entitled to.*

He shut the gate in about a minute.

At the top of the lane Ciaccona said *Well*, and Cartethyia said she is a terrible guest and always breaks things, and Ciaccona agreed that she does. And then: *That's the second one.* Second name she is carrying that she will not say. Amy asked her about a seat at a table two weeks ago and she had been told and would not repeat it, and Amy let it go, because Amy has manners that frankly none of the rest of them can account for. And now there is a second, and it is in the same girl, and Ciaccona wanted it on the record that she was also letting it go and that it was costing her considerably more.

Cartethyia walked a while with a river stone in both hands and said they are not the same kind. One of them is a name somebody is keeping *for* a person. The other is a name somebody kept *from* one. She will hold both until they are true. *That's all I've got, Cia. I know it's rude. I did twenty years where the only thing I had was the names I gave things, and the way I stayed a person was that I didn't spend them.* Ciaccona walked eleven or twelve paces and said *…All right* — and then that she cannot stand it, that it is a fifth in the middle of a perfectly good chord and will sit there for weeks and she will hear it every time Cartethyia opens her mouth, and that Cartethyia is right, and it is hers, and Ciaccona would rather have her out of tune than have her spent. *Come on, there's a table in ward four and I am reliably informed that everyone else is going to be at it.*

### The register yard

Ward four's office was open and the registers were out: four hundred years in twelve courses of paper, flat under stones on trestles in the courtyard, with two clerks and a patroller and a boy walking the rows, and the public admitted, because the public is admitted on the sixth and the second article says you may look.

Iuno stood at the end of the first trestle with her hand at her side. Amy came up beside her a minute later, having read four hundred years of ward four's arrangements upside down in eleven minutes flat by walking the rows at a normal pace and never once stopping, and pointed out that eleven-fifty is on the fourth trestle — the plague year, the whole ward's household returns, under a rock, with nobody standing next to them.

*I am aware of what is on the fourth trestle.* And then, to the first table: for thirty years, Iuno's entire professional method was to be standing near paper that belonged to somebody else. That is the whole of it. That is what a High Priestess of Septimont is — a woman who is always plausibly in the room where the documents are, who reads at four times the speed anybody expects, and who has never in her life needed to be handed anything. There are twelve trestles in that courtyard and she could take the whole of them off in about ninety minutes and nobody there would know it had happened, and she would have broken none of their six articles, because she would have looked.

Amy said *So look.* Iuno said she intended to look at nothing at all, and to stand at the end of that table until they left, and she would like Amy to stay there and watch her do it. Amy asked why her. *Because you are the only other person in this courtyard who can do it, and because in about four years somebody is going to hand you something you should not have, and I would like there to be a memory available to you of a one-armed woman being extremely boring in a yard.*

They stood at the end of the first trestle for twenty-two minutes while a foreign city's four hundred years of paper dried in front of them, and neither of them read a line of it, and it was, by a considerable margin, the hardest thing either of them did that day. At minute nineteen Amy said, without turning her head, that on the fourth trestle, third course, the returns are bound in eights and have been rebound at some point in a different thread. Iuno said she saw it when they came in. And then: *Say nothing about it to Chixia.* Amy protested. Iuno said she was going to say it to Chixia within the hour, in a way that would have sounded like a joke — and that she has done that exact thing four hundred times, and it is a very good technique and it works, and she would like Amy to have a better one.

### The table of the fifth article

The fifth article works like this. A page that leaves your line stops being yours. You leave it where it lands. A ward officer lifts it, folded once, unread, and it goes into a basket at the ward office, and the basket is sealed at the ninth hour and put on a shelf. It sits there one year. And on the next sixth of the sixth, everything in it comes out on a table in the courtyard, and anybody at all may read anything on that table, because a page nobody has claimed in a year has stopped being private.

Ward four's table stood in the shade of the east wall with a cloth on it and eleven things on the cloth, and Clerk Wen behind it on a stool with a day-book, since noon. Chixia was leaning on the wall beside him with her arms folded and her hat pushed back, in the posture of a patroller who is off duty and has come anyway, and delivered the rules: everything on it is a year old, everything on it is fair game, you can read all of it out loud and people do and some of them do voices; you cannot take anything; and if it is yours you have to say so in front of everyone, and then it is yours, and then you have to explain to your whole ward why you lost it, which is why almost nobody does. Ward four, eleven years: four claims. One deed, one contract of engagement, two letters. Ciaccona called it a museum of things nobody wants. Wen said it is a museum of things nobody will stand up in front of a courtyard about, which is a different museum and much better attended.

Eleven things on a cloth. A page of somebody's accounts for the ninth month of the year before last, in a clean hand, with a total wrong by eleven, circled by the writer and left wrong. A recipe with four amendments in four inks. Half a letter, torn on the fold, beginning in the middle of a sentence about a dog and never reaching its point. Two pages of a boy's writing exercise, the same character ninety times, getting worse. A page torn from a printed almanac with three days ringed. A receipt from a paper shop, itemised, for one funeral's worth of goods, folded small.

And a shopping list, which has been on that table eleven times, and which everybody in ward four knows, and around which a small permanent crowd of six or seven people has stood all afternoon every year for eleven years. Six lines. Five of them crossed through in a different hand each time. Beside it, on the ground, in a shallow open basket: five objects.

The ward buys them. It came off a line eleven years ago and nobody has ever claimed it, so the first year old Auntie Pei bought the coal — three catties of the small coal, right there — and put it in the basket, and crossed it off, and everybody said she was mad. The year after, somebody did the needles. Then the oil. The comb was four years ago. The thread was last year and still has the shop's paper on it. Five of six, over eleven years, by nine different people who have none of them ever discussed it.

The sixth line is one character. It sits under the thread, in the same hand as the other five, the same size, the same spacing, the same weight of brush; the only difference is that it is one character where the others are three and four — and a ward of four thousand people has been arguing about it since the year it arrived. Wen recited the state of the argument the way a man recites something he has recited a hundred times and privately enjoys. Reading one: *give it back* — an instruction to whoever finds the list, making the whole thing a note to a servant or a child with a last line about a borrowed thing that wants returning; that is the ward office's reading and it has the advantage of being boring. Reading two: *still*, *yet*, half of a longer word; the person was interrupted, or their brush ran dry, or they were called away, and the sixth line was going to be a word and never finished being one. Reading three: a rare surname held by about four hundred households in the prefecture — on which the last item is a person, and the list means coal, needles, oil, comb, thread, and go and see so-and-so. That is Auntie Pei's reading, and she has held it for eleven years against three clerks, and Chixia maintains that she is wrong.

Chisa came round the end of the table with her chin at the angle and asked whether she might read it out. Anybody may.

She read it in Huanglong, at lesson seven, badly, in the flat unbeautiful voice of somebody working, one line at a time, with a small pause between each while the character resolved. *Three catties. Small coal. Needles. The middle size. Lamp oil, half. A comb. Thread, black.* And then the sixth, in exactly the same voice as the other five, at exactly the same pace, as an item on a list of things a person needs:

*— still.*

Nobody said anything. The courtyard went on doing what a courtyard does. Around the shopping list, seven people did not say anything for a while. Chixia said that is not one of the three. Chisa said no. *You read it as a thing you'd go and get.* *That's the grammar of the other five.* *Nobody reads it like that, because you can't get —* And Chixia stopped, standing in her own ward's courtyard with her arms coming slowly uncrossed, and worked out at the age of twenty-four, in about three seconds, why the ward had been arguing for eleven years about a word every one of them could read. *…Oh.* *Mm.* *Oh, that's — no. No, that's horrible.* Chisa said it is the fourth reading and probably wrong, and that she is on lesson seven and got the coal wrong — it is the small coal and she said the little coal, and there is a difference, and Cartethyia will tell her about it for a week. Cartethyia confirmed that there is a difference.

Clerk Wen had picked up his brush during the reading and had been sitting with it loaded for the better part of a minute. He wrote four characters in the remarks column, read them back under his breath, and did not strike them. *Fourth reading offered. Foreign. Not ruled on.* It will go up to the prefecture with the year's returns, and somebody at the prefecture will read it in the tenth month with a cup of tea in his hand and it will annoy him for the rest of his life, and that is the entire and only pleasure available in his profession.

Lupa asked the operational question, because somebody always has to and it is generally her: why has nobody bought the sixth thing. Because nobody knows what it is. Lupa said somebody has guessed — eleven years, four thousand people — somebody has put something in that basket and been told to take it out again. Chixia's mouth shut. Wen looked at the wolf with something that in a ward clerk passes for delight and said *Twice.* Year three, a man put in a length of cord, on the surname reading, because the household of that name upriver are rope-makers, and the ward took it out and gave it back and nobody was unkind about it. Year eight, a girl of about eleven put in a paper flower and would not say why, and the ward left it in for four days and then the clerk before him took it out at night so that she would not have to watch it be taken out. And she is twenty now, and she comes every year, and stands where they were standing — the one in the blue at the end — and she has never put anything in it again.

Cartethyia's hands went still around the river stone. She looked at the basket with five things in it for a while and asked what would happen if somebody put a sixth thing in and it was wrong. The ward would take it out and give it back and nobody would be unkind about it. And then Wen advised against it, on the grounds that it is a very good basket with five things in it, and a basket with five things in it and an argument attached has kept four thousand people company for eleven years, and he does not know what a basket with six things in it is for.

Nobody put anything in the basket.

Amy read the whole table twice, the way she reads anything, and at the second pass stopped at the seventh item, which is the one nobody stops at. It is a page of foreign writing. It came off a line in the harbour ward a year ago in a season of foreign shipping and has been on the shelf ever since, and no clerk in ward four can read a word of it, and it has stayed on the table all afternoon every sixth for one year with nobody near it, because a courtyard that comes to hear the shopping list has no use for a page of letters. On the evidence it is a list of nothing: eleven short lines, two of them numbers, one struck through and rewritten identically underneath. And in the top right corner, in ink, a single brushed shape.

Amy stood in front of it with her left hand in her pocket and her thumb on the edge of something small and square that has been in that pocket, or in a rig case, since Lahai-Roi, and asked Wen whether the mark on the corner is a character. Wen came round and looked at it with the professional lack of interest of a man who has looked at it eleven times. *No. That's a fold-crease with ink got into it.* Chixia said her father says it is a paper-maker's mark. Wen said her father says a great many things; Chixia said he is right about paper; Wen said the clerk before him had it as a compositor's sort-mark and wrote half a page about it in 'nineteen and was told by the prefecture not to write half pages. *Three of us, three answers, one corner. Take your pick, and take it away with you, because it is the only thing on that table nobody has ever asked me about and I have been rather looking forward to it.*

Amy looked at the corner of a stranger's page for a while. Her thumb went round the edge of the small square thing in her pocket about four times. She said *…Thanks.* And she wrote nothing down. Iuno, eleven feet away, at the end of the first trestle, where she had been standing without reading anything for the better part of an hour, watched her daughter fail to write something down, and filed it, and kept it, and said not one word about it for the rest of the day.

One other thing happened at that table, because Mei asked to see her own. Wen sent the boy: household four-one-one, ward four, entered the twenty-first of the fifth month, seven names, one stove, head of household a one-armed woman by acclamation. It is the only thing in that courtyard she was permitted to read. Amy stood at her mother's shoulder and read her own line, which says *niece*, which is what somebody wrote down in a hurry on a wet afternoon and which has therefore been true for sixteen days. And in the remarks column, in Assessor Lü's small even hand, going on four lines longer than a remarks column is supposed to go on for, was the note about name-giving to a person of unestablished origin. Mei read it standing up, in the sun, with people going past, and read it twice, and handed it back to the boy and said thank you in her four bad words of the language and got two of them wrong. Amy asked what it said. Mei said: *It says a man in an office spent four lines being careful about me. On a wet Tuesday. When he could have written two words and gone home.* Amy said that is not what it says. Mei said it is what it says.

### In, before the rain

At seven in the evening Iuno's shoulder stopped hurting. She was on the step of the ward four courtyard with a cup of somebody's tea she had been given and had not asked for, and it went from four hours of ground glass to nothing at all inside about two minutes, the way a pressed nerve lets go. She sat with it for a while. Then she walked to the ninth tower, which is twenty-two minutes at her pace and took her fourteen.

Yangyang was on the north face with her route case open on the parapet and her glass up and nothing at all in it. *There's nothing coming.* The north was clear to the ridge; it had been building at five and she had had it at five and had been going to file at half six, and it had gone. Iuno said it was the seventh — and then gave her the rest: her arm went quiet twenty minutes ago, and she has had that arm eighteen months and it has never once been wrong and it has never once gone quiet *early*. It does not stop when the weather passes. It stops when the weather stops.

Yangyang put the glass in the case and looked north along the crest wall, past the eighth tower — the last one with a bell in it that anybody has heard — at a sky over four hundred kilometres of ridge that had been going to rain on her city at seven o'clock and had been asked not to. *She's had it since the fourth hour.* Fifteen hours. And a whole country's laundry on top of it. Nobody down there knows. A hundred and forty thousand people will take their books in at nine on the schedule the Bureau posted, and every one of them will think the Bureau got it right. And the Bureau *did* get it right — the forecast was fair to the ninth hour, and it was fair to the ninth hour, and the record will be correct. Iuno said that is the worst sentence either of them would say that day. Yangyang closed the route case and said she hates that country's paperwork. Iuno told her she is the second-best clerk Iuno has ever met, and that she includes herself in the field. Yangyang asked who the first was. *A woman at a gate who has three hundred and six letters in a tin and has never once written a false character in her life.*

The bells went at the ninth hour and a hundred and forty thousand people took their paper in. It takes about half an hour and it is the least dramatic thing in Huanglong: nobody hurries; the books come off the walls in armfuls with the spines closing all down the terrace like a very long applause going the wrong way; the quilts come down, the lines come down, the chairs go back in; and the lane by the ward change was empty and swept and ordinary by twenty to ten, and by twenty to ten it looked exactly like a lane.

Mrs Ren's yard came in in eleven minutes, because Mrs Ren had a system and, for the first time in twenty-two days, seven foreigners did what they were told in the order they were told to do it. Lupa took the lines. Cartethyia took the top line, because she could reach it, and did it at eight feet without being asked and put herself away again immediately afterwards, at speed, in the yard, which made Little Guo drop a stool. Chisa did the boots by feel in the dark faster than the two people doing them by lamp. Ciaccona did the books, which took her longest, because she opened four of them. Amy ran the stairs. Iuno counted, out loud, and was correct. Mei carried whatever was put in her arms and put it exactly where she was told, which anybody who has known her for more than a fortnight would recognise as a state of shock.

The coats came in last. Iuno took hers off the line, and it was dry, and it smelled of eleven hours of a foreign city's sun, and she put it on in a yard, in the dark, with two things still in the breast pocket of the shirt underneath. Lupa went past with an armful of quilts and said one word without stopping: *Tuesday.* Iuno said it is Wednesday. Lupa said *I know what day it is.*

Four minutes after the last quilt was through the door, it began to rain.

They went up on the wall anyway, because Mei had noted something at ten past six that morning and had spent the entire day declining to do anything about it in a manner everybody in the household had correctly identified by noon. Seven of them, in the rain, on the ninth tower's stair, at half past ten at night, with two lamps and a covered basket Panhua had handed over the counter at nine with the words *I heard*, and no further explanation, ever, to anybody.

Yangyang was on the north face with her mother's flute and had been trying it about ten minutes. She got three notes and the fourth came apart. She did it again and got three notes and the fourth came apart, and the air over the wall, which had been sitting on four hundred kilometres of weather since before it got light, could not take it up, could not finish it, could do nothing at all except be present, which it was, entirely, all along that parapet, at a temperature about a degree above the rain.

Yangyang lowered the flute and stood there with her hat brim running. *…She can't.* And then: she has spent the day and now she cannot do the note, and Yangyang cannot do it either, because she has been on a wall since six that morning, and it is a lullaby, it is four bars, her mother plays it at a stove with a spoon in her other hand — and Mei said her name, and asked how many of them were up there. Yangyang counted, because she is who she is. *…Seven.* Mei said *Eight.*

What they sang was the donkey one, because nobody except Yangyang knows the lullaby and Yangyang could not get through four bars of it. It is a Huanglong drinking song of no distinction whatsoever, four hundred years old, known to every person in the prefecture, with eleven verses of which nine are about a donkey and two are about the man who owns him, and Amy had it off Panhua's back room three weeks ago and had been singing it wrong ever since and was singing it wrong now, at volume, in the rain, on a wall. Ciaccona harmonised around the wrong notes, which is a professional skill she has been developing on that particular girl for three weeks. Cartethyia was flatly, gloriously, unrepentantly out of tune and sang the loudest. Chisa did not sing, knew all eleven verses, and supplied the words to Lupa four beats ahead each time, which is why Lupa came in on the beat and sounded, for the first time in her life, like a person who can sing. Iuno sang two verses and stopped and stood at the parapet with her one hand around a cup of something hot that had appeared in it.

Yangyang got about halfway through the fourth verse before her voice went, and by the sixth she had her hat off, which she does not do outdoors, and by the ninth she was sitting on the wet stone of the ninth tower with her back against the parapet and her mother's flute across her knees, being sung a stupid song about a donkey by eight foreigners on the anniversary of the only report she has ever been afraid to make.

Somewhere around the seventh verse the rain over that one tower thinned out by about a third, in a circle roughly eleven metres across, and stayed thin for four verses, and then came back.

Nobody said anything about it. Mei looked up into it once, with her face wet, and said, in the tone of a woman noting a fact at a dinner table: *That's enough of that. Come and sit down.*

And the rain came back over the tower.

And the wind sat down.

Iuno walked home last, with Mei, with a ring in her shirt pocket and four characters folded next to it and a chop in the paper that somebody made before they wrote. Mei observed that she had been holding her coat shut all day. Iuno confirmed it, and said she was aware that Mei knew, and that everybody knew, and that she had been aware since the second port, apparently, which she had learned on Tuesday from a wolf at midnight in a shrine street, and had not recovered from. There had been a moment that morning in a yard where she very nearly did something enormous because an old woman put her hand out for her coat. Mei asked *And?* And it is the sixth of the sixth month, and there are eleven hundred lines up, and a hundred and forty thousand people turned their paper over at noon, and half the ward watched a woman put her family on a table and a girl read a shopping list to a courtyard, and *I am not asking you anything at all on a day like this.*

Mei walked about four paces and asked why. And Iuno said it to the lane, in the rain, without any performance on it at all, which is the one thing thirty years never taught her and eighteen months did: *Because I want the small one.* She has had the enormous ones. She has had a cathedral and a chain and a prophecy and a city on its knees, and every single one of them was a Tuesday somebody else chose. *I would like a bad evening in a rented room with the window that doesn't latch and nothing on at all.*

Mei said *Okay*, and then that she was going to be unbearable now, and was informed that she had been unbearable since the fourth hour and had stood under a washing line for forty minutes that morning claiming to be having a slow one. Mei laughed at the rain and agreed that she was having a slow one. And then: *…Iuiu. Same time tomorrow?* Iuno stopped in the lane for about a second and a half, because she has heard that sentence four times now and has never once managed to find out where her partner got it. *…Same time tomorrow.*

### The basket

## XII. THE PLUM RAINS

*(Youngest Gate 11 — 梅雨)*

The first drop struck Deng Rui on the back of the hand while he was trying to thread a needle.

He was mending the waterproof cover of a field receiver, which had split beside its buckle, and Sergeant Du had given him waxed thread, a needle, and an account of how much better these covers had been in the year he enlisted, and had told him to hold the thread up against the sky, because you get more light through the eye that way. Above the northern ridge a narrow strip of brightness lay under the cloud.

The drop on his hand rose. It pulled into a bead, lifted from the small hairs, and travelled upward past the needle to join several others hanging at different heights beneath the receiver mast. Through one of them Rui could see the north watch platform, including the roof that had burned down four years earlier.

Du walked past carrying two bowls and said the captain takes his on the platform and will come down when somebody brings him something hot, and stepped between three surviving posts onto a remembered stair over a slope of loose shale. Rui caught his belt. Both men went down; one bowl broke against a post; the other rolled beneath the platform and went on ringing against the stones. Rui drove his heels into the mud and hauled, and Du fought him with the irritated strength of a man whose hands were full of supper and asked for a moment to put these down.

The deep northern drum sounded. Rui felt it through his teeth, and beyond Du's shoulder a black shape unfolded from the ditch beside the mast — it had followed the rising drops across the watch line — and he pulled the sergeant under him. Green light crossed the platform; the Discord struck a post, folded around it, and broke apart under Jiyan's blade; and its claw left Rui through the back of his jacket as it dissolved.

Jiyan caught him before his head reached the ground. For several seconds Rui went on trying to pull Du uphill, and Jiyan had to uncurl his fingers from the sergeant's belt, and told him to stay with his voice and let him carry the weight. Rui looked past him at the suspended water and said there was a needle somewhere near the receiver and that Tang would want it out of the mud.

By the time Doctor Tang reached them, the blood had filled the hollow between Rui's shoulder and the ground. She knelt beside Jiyan; they worked together while two Rangers stretched a tarpaulin over the posts; under the cover, Du sat holding a bowl in each hand, one of them a handle and a crescent of enamel, keeping his hands level. Tang finally laid two fingers over Jiyan's wrist. The receiver crackled behind them — somebody had reached the southern relay — and the waxed thread swung from its torn cover, gathering drops. Jiyan drew his hand out of Rui's jacket, and Tang folded a clean cloth over it, and he used the cloth, carefully, and then helped her lift the body onto the stretcher.

Du watched them carry it away and said: *He was speaking to me. I heard him from the platform. He wanted the pepper out of his bowl. His stomach always gave him trouble before a march.*

Across the camp, a green command banner hung over the medical shelter, and rain passed through it and briefly made another banner — black, torn along the lower edge, snapping above men who had been ordered to advance. Jiyan's right hand reached for the medical satchel he had carried in those days and found the guard of his weapon. He kept looking until he could see the green cloth again. Then he told Du that he had heard the old advance call and had followed it as far as the mast before he saw Rui holding him, and asked Du to tell him what he saw now. Du studied the bowls. *This one's broken. I think I've been holding it together.* Jiyan told him to put both pieces beside his boot, and that Tang would examine him there. The sergeant placed them down with care.

Captain Bao came in under the tarpaulin with water running off the end of her braid onto her map case: the west watch had three men following the old quarry road; Xiulan had sent pairs after them; the new recruits could still read the route flags, and the men from the last campaign kept seeing the bridge. Jiyan looked beyond her at the quarry bridge that had fallen during that campaign, on which sunlight lay along the rail, in the rain. His orders: put a guide from each group together; move the wounded through the lower passage and cover the assembly point; and every order goes through an officer standing beside the people receiving it. Bao proposed the south shelf, which gives room to withdraw the outer posts, while holding the current line keeps Shuanghe's carts covered another hour. Jiyan brought the posts to the shelf and took the ridge himself while the carts passed, and watched her mark the abandoned positions, and then made himself look at the road those positions had protected.

The drum reached them again. Bao checked her watch: nine minutes and forty seconds. The evening before it had given them almost nineteen. The spoon inside Du's surviving bowl trembled against the enamel.

Jiyan rose. *Send the Magistrate our full report. Retroact Rain at the highland camp, spreading south along the Tacet seams. Include the change in the interval.* And then the casualty slip: Deng Rui's family lives above the east ropewalk; his sister should hear from an officer before the ward board goes up; find somebody who knew him. Bao said he taught the night signallers and that she could send one of them. Jiyan signed the slip on the map case, and the paper took the wet impression of his thumb beneath his name, and he went back into the rain.

By the twenty-seventh of the sixth month, Mrs Ren's front door needed a shoulder to open it. Mei supplied the shoulder, was carried halfway into the yard with an empty tea tray, and received advice about the hinge from upstairs, which Cartethyia translated in part — there had been a remark about people who possess hands, and Cartethyia had judged the hinge to be the useful part. Three weeks earlier that lane had held quilts, open books, and trays of type; now the walls smelled of wet plaster, the books in the front room stood on thin wooden slats to keep them clear of the shelf, and Mrs Ren had made everyone carry their boots upstairs. Lupa came out with a bowl of coarse salt for under the linen cupboard and told Mei to lift while she pulled, because the bottom was catching; Mei said she thought that was the point of a door, and was told that by supper she might qualify to use the cupboard. She got it shut, and Amy applauded from inside with the slow deliberate rhythm she reserves for achievements she intends to mention again.

Iuno sat with the river observations and a cup held against a shoulder that had acquired a deep ache in the night; she had told Baizhi, taken what Baizhi prescribed, and handed the heaviest of her cases to Mei before anyone could develop a theory about her independence. Mei asked whether the cup helped. *It gives me something warm to complain into. Your breakfast is under the other saucer.* Lupa had left an egg, two pieces of fried dough, and a small dish of plum sauce.

At the window, Cartethyia turned her terrarium a quarter-turn: a pale tip had appeared on the camellia cutting, and she had spent the previous evening showing it to people, including the delivery man who brought the lamp oil. Chisa, with Ruan's care roll spread beneath her scissors, had moved her chair twice to hear the hinge above the gutter, and reported that the courtyard sounded deeper, that water was collecting under the paving by the rear step, and that Mrs Ren should have someone lift that stone; Lupa said they could do it after breakfast if Chisa showed her which end gives.

Ciaccona, working on the quarry song at the far end of the table, set down her pen. Inside the little field terminal, Tollhide had drawn its four feet together and its bronze throat quivered, and a low note entered the table through the terminal's base. She took the ruled card from her sleeve: three grey marks on the stave now — and as she watched, the distance between the second and the third shortened. The paper under her fingers stayed dry. Amy reached across and turned the room receiver down, and the note continued through the wood, and she asked for the terminal on folded cloth so she could hear the floor by itself.

Yangyang knocked with the hilt of her sword because both hands were occupied. Jiyan had pulled the outer posts back to the south shelf; the highland camp was seeing Retroact Rain; his report had reached the relay twenty minutes ago. Amy's hand closed around the receiver dial and asked whether it was the kind they had found under the Savantae lab. Baizhi wanted the observations they had brought back from there and had sent for the recordings. The Magistrate asked for Mei and Iuno at City Hall; Chixia was opening the ward shelters and was on her way with the admission sheets.

Mei looked at the courtyard, where rain was making a steady ordinary pattern in the water beside the step. Her hand remembered cold droplets hanging above a basin, and the curve of a machine's shoulder under her palm, cable and metal assembled over forty years. She turned that hand over on the table and asked how many people were still up there. Yangyang said the camp was moving the medical station and Bao was collecting the Shuanghe convoy, and that she would have a count when both groups reached the covered road. Mei ate the egg in three bites, wrapped the remaining dough in a clean cloth, and put it in her jacket, and Amy watched her do it and then reached for the street receiver they had dried and cleaned on the sixth: she would be at the freight apron, because the ward bells cover the terraces and the dock speakers reach the people under canvas, and Captain Chixia could have her voice until her own got there. Iuno told her to bring somebody onto the channel who knows the households; Amy said Panhua knows them by what they owe her, which is an excellent identification system. Lupa put two flasks beside the receiver, one into Amy's bag and one toward Yangyang. Mei kissed her daughter's hair on the way past and got a palm pressed against her cheek and a report that she had sauce on her mouth, and said she was leaving her provisions.

At City Hall, Changli had laid the first casualty message beside Jiyan's route map. Jinhsi read it twice, turned it facedown, and put a small weight on it, and asked whether his sister had been told. Sanhua said the signaller was on the ropewalk stair and had asked the patroller there to take him up. Jinhsi rested her fingers on the weight for a moment.

Then the work. Shuanghe's convoy can reach the city through the lower service road; Jiyan's withdrawal brings the northern approaches under their own guns; once the convoy passes he gives up that cover to join his troops. Iuno put her river notes down beside the route: the lower road crosses the spill channel, and the day's reading puts water at the foot of the crossing by late afternoon. The sluice office expected to lower it before noon — on a reading from the upper gauge, and there is a second flow entering below it, from an old feeder whose mouth sits behind the reeds, which the man who reads that gauge showed her, and for which he asked for a second measuring post last spring. Changli read the page through and said she would send his request with the works order, and open the eastern spillway to relieve the crossing.

That water reaches the drying ground. Three households are using it for seed-stock; Wen has their names; the stock can be purchased and what is bagged can be moved. Jinhsi sent Wen at once with the forecast and the time the sluice needs to open, to bring their answer directly back. Changli noted that the engineers need forty minutes to clear the spillway, and that if a household disputes the order, that time continues to pass. Jinhsi reached for her pen: *Tell them that too. We will open it at the necessary hour. I want their objections while there's still time to act on them.* And Changli added a question for Wen to carry — ask who uses the ground after the seed is moved, because the household register covers the storage sheds and the people working between them will have their own arrangements.

Mei had stayed standing at the window, where the lower terraces were already gathering water. She asked what the rain does to somebody who grew up there. Baizhi, coming in with the camp's exposure notes, answered: it gives their memories more material to work with. The rain carries condensed Remnant Energy from the Etheric Sea; past events survive in that energy; contact allows a person's frequency to resonate with them; exposure matters, and so does attachment to the place. Two recruits saw a damaged platform. Sergeant Du saw his former captain eating there. Jiyan reports hearing an order from the previous campaign. Each of them occupied the same few metres.

Mei looked down at City Hall's oldest foundation stones and asked: *And if the person remembers with their hands before they remember anything else?* Baizhi's gaze followed hers. *Then I would ask them to keep somebody beside them who can observe their hands.* Iuno moved closer to the table and left room beside Mei.

Jinhsi drew a sheet from the bottom of her folder — a small plan of City Hall with one chamber outlined beneath the oldest part of the building — and said the foundation gauge had begun answering the northern pulses, that they had confirmed the correspondence that morning, that the sealed annex may tell them why, and that Mei could have access whenever she chose. A warm line opened across Mei's palm. She said she wanted to read it with somebody who can explain the language, that she would ask questions as they went, and that she would like time to write down what she remembers. Sanhua could arrange the room that night. Mei said she would come back for it — and then put her finger on Bao's map: right now there are people trying to cross a place where the rain can put a bridge back, and Yangyang knows the road, and she would like to go with her. Jiyan had requested help receiving the convoy at the south end and had assigned the ridge to his own troops.

Then Mei put her finger on the convoy's destination inside the wall and asked where they sleep after they get there. Changli pulled the accommodation sheet from beneath the transport orders: forty-three people still had wagon places and a covered transit courtyard, and their second night depended on two buildings whose occupants had been asked to share them — the west school, and the cloth hall above the canal.

Iuno said she could go to those meetings. She knows the families on the school stair; she has measurements for the cloth hall; Mrs Ren knows what its roof does in this weather. Changli took up a fresh sheet and asked what authority she would need. Access to the allocations, and a clerk who can enter an agreement when people make one. A term ending tomorrow at noon. Recommendation of expenditure up to an amount Changli would write in the margin. *You and the Magistrate can end the appointment earlier.* Jinhsi reached for the sheet before Changli had finished the first line — and Iuno added one clause of her own: *The families can ask for another representative as well.* Changli asked, and Iuno inclined her head. *Please write it in.*

*A career with an exit printed on the front*, observed the High Priestess. *We shall have to live on our charm.*

Iuno signed beneath the clause while Mei waited beside her, damp-haired, eating the last of the fried dough out of its cloth.

The cloth hall's caretaker had been told to expect bedding. She had swept the floor, unlocked the side door, and put a bucket under the leak, and when Iuno arrived with the proposed allocation she took one look at the word *vacant* and went upstairs. Iuno followed. Six sleeping places occupied the loft. Shoes stood beneath the beds. A man's work jacket hung from a rafter with the sleeves turned inward to dry. On a box beside the nearest pillow sat a cup, a comb, and half a peeled orange. *They unload the night barges. They've slept here since the second month. The warehouse lets them have the loft for keeping the rats out of the stock.*

Iuno stood at the top of the stair with the allocation in her hand, while below her the clerk turned a page and said the return lists storage above the hall. There is storage too, on the dry side, said the caretaker, pointing at the stacked cloth — and she has been asking for that roof to be done since winter. Iuno folded the allocation so the total faced inward and asked when the men would be awake. Two were at the landing; the others came off shift at seven. *Then I'll speak to the two at the landing first. May I leave the bedding downstairs while we work out the room?* The caretaker looked past her at the porters with their bundles and said to stack it on the boards, and that the corner takes water when the north gutter fills. By the time Iuno reached the landing, her clerk had added six people to the accommodation sheet, and she sent that sheet back to City Hall with the loft's address on it.

At the freight apron, Amy had the street receiver open on a crate with Chixia kneeling opposite holding a wire at the angle Amy had asked for, both of them with rain running off their elbows. *Your speakers are older than the plugs. Someone adapted these by winding copper around the pins. I respect the confidence.* That was Chixia's predecessor, who would want her to know they worked through the last flood; Amy said she could see the flood in the socket and asked for another handspan of cable. Under the canvas beside them NYX stood with its lifting clamps fitted while two works crewmen argued about securing a pump in its hands; Amy looked up long enough to settle the question and went back to the receiver.

Cartethyia arrived with a basket of clean cloths and a furious little boy who had an objection to the school: they had put beds where the projects are, and his is a bridge, and the glue is still drying, and they picked it up by the roof. Chixia kept the wire steady and asked which classroom. Cartethyia winced and offered to carry it by the base and let him show her where he would like it. He wanted it in his classroom. The rain drummed on the receiver's case. Amy let it go on a moment, then slid a tool toward Chixia and stood up. *Let's look at it. If the glue's wet, we'll need a flat place. You can stay with me until we find one.* The boy measured her against the people who had already moved his bridge and said it has a middle that lifts and you have to keep your fingers off the string. *Good thing you came down to supervise us.* She gave Chixia the receiver and followed him uphill with Cartethyia and the basket, listening to his account of what the middle was supposed to do.

Chixia turned the handle and waited for the dock speakers to wake. Her first announcement told people where to bring bedding; her second gave the addresses of the open shelters; before the third she moved the paper further from the rain and read Jinhsi's warning again. *The northern patrols have confirmed Retroact Rain. If you see someone from your past, or a street begins to look the way it used to, take the arm of the person beside you and get under cover together. A patroller will come through every lane. Tell us what you're seeing. Stay with somebody while it passes.*

At the ropewalk, a woman halfway through closing her shutters stopped with her hands on the wood. The young signaller stood in her doorway with Rui's field cap under his arm. She listened to the rest of the announcement. Then she turned back to him and asked what Rui had said.

The lower service road runs between the old scholar tree and the spill channel. Water spread under the tree's roots in a shallow brown fan; Mei remembered its crown in the sunset, the broad green spread above a split in the earth; today the leaves hung heavy enough to brush the top of Yangyang's hat. Lupa and Chisa caught up at the second marker, Lupa with a rope coil and a sack of meal packets, Chisa with her scissors under a fitted oilskin Ruan had made out of three smaller covers and several opinions about people who leave good steel in bad weather.

The crossing is a low stone causeway broad enough for one cart, with water over its northern edge — and fifty paces downstream a timber footbridge stood on tall posts with a roof over the middle and a girl in a yellow jacket waiting beneath it. Mei looked at the footbridge and said that would keep people clear of the water. Yangyang said the covered bridge was taken down before she came to Jinzhou, and that today's crossing has six stone markers, and that the first was beside her boot. Mei lowered her gaze to a stone painted blue on its upper half — and could feel the footbridge's planks under her soles, warm from a sun that had gone behind the rain. She reached for Iuno before remembering who had come with her. Lupa offered her forearm. *Use mine. Chisa's finding the rope anchors.* Mei took it.

Under the tree, Bao had five carts and the people travelling with them, canvas sagging over the loads, and one driver sitting with both feet in a bucket because he had mistaken it for the little brazier his wife used to put beside his chair. Bao helped him take his feet out and placed his boots where he could see them. Sixty-one civilians. Eight wounded Rangers, four fit to walk. Ma with the rear cart. She had given the drivers shelter when the bridge appeared. Yangyang reported that Jiyan's relay had the ridge still covered and the eastern spillway opening, and that they should see the channel fall before the next watch. Bao glanced at the people beneath the canvas and said several of them go home every time they stand up.

An elderly woman had turned toward the bank with a name moving on her lips, and beside her a younger woman held a bundle of household papers to her chest and kept saying that the pears would spoil in this weather. Her husband grew pears; their old yard is somewhere beyond that stand of reeds. Mei crouched beside the elderly woman and asked whether she would like company while she sat there, and the woman looked at the hand Mei had rested on her own knee and said *He's brought the small basket. He always says he'll carry the large one and he always comes back with the small basket.* Her daughter pressed her mouth against the papers. Mei sat on the edge of the cart with them while rain dripped through a pinhole in the canvas between her boots, and the woman described the pear tree and a wall her husband had built too close to its roots, and became angry about the wall in the middle of remembering it, and her daughter laughed once and then began crying.

Across the clearing Chisa knelt at a stone marker, where the rain had filled the channel with footsteps and carts rolling along a covered bridge and harness bells ringing from a road she had already checked with her own hands. She held one blade against the marker and closed her eyes and asked Lupa to walk on her right and keep walking while she listened, and followed the pressure of each footfall through the ground until she could separate it from the traffic on the old bridge — and then a second set of Lupa's steps came toward her from the water. Chisa opened her eyes and stood. *The rain has your walk now. Put your hand against my shoulder while I set the rope.* Lupa did, and together they secured the first anchor.

The drum sounded while they were tightening the knot. Every droplet hanging under the tree shivered, and at the rear of the convoy a mule began to pull against its traces. Bao checked her watch and gave the interval: eight minutes and twelve seconds. Yangyang transmitted it to the city with one hand on the blue marker, and the reply came through three layers of static in Amy's voice — the school is taking the first wounded, Chixia will meet the carts at the east arch, there is a dry room for the people who need somebody to sit with them — and, with a rustle of paper across the channel, *Tell me what else you're bringing. We can still move furniture.* Bao looked at Mei sitting between the two women on the cart and said several families will want to stay together during examination. Amy said she would tell Baizhi, and that the tables are long enough and they can put chairs on both sides. The transmission ended, and through the receiver a faint harness bell went on ringing, and Yangyang turned the volume down and kept her hand on the stone.

At the eastern spillway, Wen found a woman carrying trays of silkworms out of a shed omitted from the survey. The three seed-stock households had signed the purchase; the woman had spent the interval between the warning and his arrival getting the trays as far as the dry bank, with mulberry leaves stuck to her wrists and her youngest daughter carrying the empty frames one at a time, sideways, through the doorway. Changli reached them with the engineer while Wen was reading the household declaration aloud. *We work here during the wet season. The ground stays warm under the wall. They eat better here.* Changli looked inside; the lower shelf had already darkened with seepage; she asked how long moving the remaining trays would take. With her daughters, an hour. With somebody who holds them level, less.

Changli took off her outer coat, handed it to Wen, and said *Show me where to put my hands.*

They carried the trays to the raised path, each frame held against the flat of her palms, the silkworms moving slowly beneath their torn leaves. At the fourth trip the woman looked at her bare arm and the old feather-shaped scars and said she would want a cloth there, because the edge catches, and her daughter brought one, and Changli let the girl fold it over her arm.

The engineer called the time from the sluice platform; water had begun lifting loose earth beneath the gate. Two trays remained, with a sack of feed and the low charcoal stove. The woman counted them from the doorway and said *Take the trays. I can buy another stove.* Changli and Wen carried the trays out together; the woman went back for the feed; her daughter caught the sack at the bank and pulled while Changli steadied them both.

The gate lifted. Water came through the shed at ankle height, brown and quick, and caught the stove and rolled it onto one side and filled it, and mulberry leaves spread across the surface. The woman watched until the roof reflection broke apart. Wen stood with the declaration open and rain making the figures shine, and said he could enter the stove and the feed under the same assessment.

*Put the room down too*, she told him. *My eldest was born in that room. When you ask me what it cost, put the room down.*

He turned to a fresh page. Changli stayed beside her while he wrote.

At the causeway the channel began to fall — first the blue paint on the second marker, then a ridge of stone between the running strips of water. Bao brought the drivers together beneath the tree and they walked the visible route with Yangyang, holding the rope, before anyone took a cart onto it. The elderly woman with the pear tree went in the first cart beside her daughter, with Lupa leading the mule and Chisa at the wheel with one hand on its rim, feeling the turn and the changing weight. Mei waited beside the third marker to help each load through the deepest water.

The first cart passed. The second carried two wounded men and a woman with a bandaged hand, and Mei steadied its axle while a wheel climbed the submerged lip of a stone.

Then the rain opened around her.

Sun on the back of her neck. Somebody frying dough nearby. A child with a shaved head running between two loads of timber with a plum in each fist, and a workman calling after him to mind the lime. The scholar tree at shoulder height.

Mei knew the workman. His name pressed against the back of her teeth. She knew the way he would wipe the lime from his brow with the inside of his wrist, and that his thumb had healed crooked from an injury he laughed about while his wife was in earshot. Her palm rested on something broad and smooth — a fitting, an opening — and she had stood there before with that part waiting in her hands. Beyond the timber stacks people were putting up the frame of a house; a woman on a ladder held the upper beam while two men argued about its angle; smoke from the cookfire reached them; the child offered one of his plums to somebody just outside her sight.

The gate in front of her opened toward those houses.

Her right hand turned. Gold entered the water around the marker. Across the channel, every suspended drop swung toward her at once. Bao shouted. The second mule reared between its traces.

Mei could hear the workman's wife laughing. She knew where their house would stand when it was finished. She knew which window would catch the evening.

*Mei, put your weight into my arm.* Chisa's voice from a great distance; a hand closing on her left forearm; the scissors ringing once against stone. Mei tried to turn toward the sound and the fitting moved under her palm, and far below her feet something enormous began taking up its slack. She said *There's a house behind it. They've got the roof beam up.*

Chisa's grip tightened. *I've got the cart against my hip. The wheel is slipping. I need you here with me.*

The workman's crooked thumb was guiding her hand over the fitting — a warm hand, callused along the base of the fingers, showing her the direction the gate would travel. Mei lifted her right hand. It rose through the memory slowly, and cold water closed around her wrist, and the house frame thinned, and she saw the cart's axle in front of her, tilted toward the edge of the causeway.

She caught it with both hands. The mule found its footing. Chisa shoved the wheel upward, and together they brought the cart onto the next stone, where Bao took the shaft and guided it clear. Mei stayed bent over with her hands on her knees while the child with the plums crossed the water once more, and then the scholar tree's old roots filled the place where he had been standing.

Yangyang reached her from the south end, and air gathered around her hat, warm enough to lift the wet hairs off Mei's forehead, and the rain slanted away from them, and for a few breaths Mei could see every stone on the causeway.

Then the wind faltered.

Water flashed high above the road: a city reflected in the air, windows open on black floodwater, a red cap turning slowly beneath a broken lamp. Yangyang dropped to one knee as if somebody had struck her behind it, and her hand went up toward the air beside her cheek.

*Stay close to my shoulder. I'll take you across.* The warmth pulled north, toward the carts still waiting beneath the tree. Yangyang turned her hand palm upward and held it steady. *Bao has the drivers. Lupa has the animals. I need help finding the south marker, and I want you with me when I reach it.*

A small pressure settled into her sleeve. She stood carefully, and Mei took her other arm, and they walked the rope together, and behind them the rain resumed its full weight over the crossing while Bao called the next driver forward and Chisa answered from the third marker, telling him where to place the left wheel.

At the south bank, under a green canopy, Mei asked a Ranger for a cup of water, and her fingers shook enough to tap the rim against her teeth. Yangyang transmitted the report: a severe episode at the third marker; Mei under cover and speaking; her Tacet Mark answered something beneath the channel. Mei held out her left hand for the mouthpiece and said *Iuiu, if you're near the set, I'd like to talk to you.* The response took a little while, and Amy kept the channel open, and Mei watched the drops beneath the canopy's edge and counted the ones that fell all the way to the ground.

Iuno's voice arrived with street noise behind it. *I'm here. Tell me where you're sitting.* South bank. A green canopy. Someone has given her water. *I knew the people I saw. I knew where they were going to live. My hand started opening something while I was looking at them.* Iuno let her finish and then asked what her hand was doing now. Mei looked down. *It's holding the cup. I'm using both hands because it keeps shaking.* *Keep doing that. Bao can use the other people at the crossing. I'll be at the east arch when you arrive.*

The drum came through the ground beneath the canopy and water quivered in the cup. *I wanted to follow it. Even after Chisa spoke. I was so close to remembering his name.* A cart rattled across paving at the far end of the channel, and Iuno waited for it to pass. *We'll write down what you saw when you're home. You can tell me as much of him as you have.* Mei pressed her thumb against the cup's handle with the warmth of the radio case in her palm and said: *Bring my dry coat. The one Mrs Ren finally trusts me to hang up.* *It's already over my arm.*

Jinzhou received the first cart while the west school was still moving its desks. The boy's bridge had acquired a shelf in the head teacher's room, and he sat beside it working the lifting section for a wounded Ranger who watched with the solemn attention the demonstration deserved, with a bowl cooling on the desk between them. Downstairs, Baizhi had screens arranged around three examination places, and Cartethyia held one upright while a carpenter fitted its foot and introduced the room to every new arrival in Huanglong and then in the common tongue and pointed out where families could sit. An old man stopped in front of her and said his wife had come in ahead of him, carrying the blue quilt. Cartethyia looked at the several quilts over the admission rail, took his arm, and brought him to a chair beside the door. *You can tell me about her while we wait. Chixia is checking everyone who comes through.* He described a woman who had been buried beside the north road six years earlier, and Cartethyia listened with a hand on the chair so that he could feel where she was.

Ciaccona sat at the window with her lute across her knees. The classroom had bad acoustics — hard ceiling, bare floor, too many voices in one small space — and she tried a chord, listened to it return, and moved her chair. Then the rain beyond the window answered with an older song, and she knew it: her father had played it with the flat bridge, smiling at a mistake he intended to improve next time, and her fingers went toward his fingering.

Then the boy upstairs dropped the lifting part of his model, and a wounded Ranger laughed, and somebody below asked where the toilets were.

Ciaccona put a different chord under the room. She played softly enough for Baizhi to go on asking her questions; when Cartethyia started humming, she followed her pace; the old man by the door tapped a finger against his knee. The song outside went on for a while. Ciaccona's eyes filled, and she kept playing the one inside.

At the cloth hall, two night workers moved their beds against the upper wall, having agreed to share the loft with the people who could climb the stair; in return the city had hired a carpenter for the roof and guaranteed them a place to sleep through the morning shift. One of them watched Iuno's clerk enter the agreement and asked what happens to them when the rain ends and the hall is needed again. Iuno put a date beside the terms. *This expires in seven days. You'll be at the table when we discuss the next seven.* He said he would be unloading a barge. She looked at the shift list nailed beside the stair and changed the hour of the meeting.

Mrs Ren arrived with curtains, two extra blankets, and a complaint about the loft's ladder, and while the carpenter listened, Iuno crossed to the east arch with Mei's coat over her living arm.

The sixth cart came through near dusk — borrowed from the service depot to carry people whose legs had begun giving way after the crossing — with Lupa walking beside it in mud to her thighs and Chisa holding the rear rail with one hand and Mei's wrist with the other. Mei climbed down slowly. Her right hand had begun to glow again on the last approach to the city, and through the rain the foundation beneath City Hall shone with the same thin gold. *I saw it twice more. Shorter each time. Chisa kept asking me what I was carrying.* Chisa said she changed the answer halfway there, and that Bao gave her the medical case. Mei held it up with her knuckles white around the handle.

Iuno took it, set it on the cart, and opened the dry coat, and Mei put her arms into it and stayed a moment with her forehead against Iuno's shoulder while Iuno fastened the top clasp. There is a room at the school where she can lie down, and Amy saved her a bowl. *I'd like the bowl first. Then you can tell me how many buildings you've acquired.* *I've obtained the use of part of a loft. The negotiations have damaged my reputation for speed.* Mei laughed into the damp collar.

Beside them Chixia checked Bao's count, reading each household back and waiting for somebody from it to answer, and passed the sheet to the school clerk, who carried it uphill under his coat. The elderly woman from the first cart caught Mei's sleeve under the arch and said *He came as far as the turn. Then I lost the basket*, and Mei sat beside her until the daughter had fetched their bedding. By then Iuno's tea had gone cold in its flask, and she poured it anyway, and Mei took a cup in both hands and drank while the rain moved through the arch in slanting silver lines.

At City Hall the northern reports began arriving on sheets written on both sides. Jiyan had withdrawn the highland medical station; Xiulan held the new assembly ground; the engineers were cutting drainage trenches between the gun positions; the armourers had begun bringing damaged weapons in from the abandoned line. The rain had raised a second army between the watchfires — men at old posts, men carrying old banners, men walking through earth that had covered them for years — and the current army worked among them. Jiyan went from position to position with revised watch orders, and at the western gun found a young officer waiting for a captain who had died in the previous campaign, and sat on the ammunition box beside her and had her take him through the placement of her own troops, one squad at a time, until her hand steadied on the map. Afterward he returned to the command shelter and asked Xiulan to read his orders back to him, and she did, including the withdrawal from the ridge where Deng Rui had died.

Beyond the shelter, something struck the earth far to the north. The lamp flame flattened. On the map, a bead of water travelled across the Norfall plains and stopped against the weight holding Jinzhou down. Xiulan looked at her watch: seven minutes and thirty-six seconds. Jiyan marked the time, and beneath it wrote the name the dispatches had been approaching all day.

*Ovathrax, Threnodian of War.*

He sent the sheet south with the next relay. Jinhsi received it standing beside the council table, with Changli newly back from the spillway, mud on her hem and a folded cloth still tucked over her scarred arm, reading it over her shoulder. For a little while they listened to the rain hitting the roof. Sanhua went to shut the outer window and stopped with her hand on the latch.

The deep impact reached the city through the foundation. Jinhsi felt it in the soles of her feet, and her pen rolled across the table and struck the inkstone.

*Send Jiyan the ammunition reserve. Open the second medical store and put the armourers on rotation. The eastern district can draw lamp oil from City Hall until the ward deliveries reach it.* Changli placed the dispatch beside the accommodation sheet and said the city will ask how long the stores will last. *Give them the current figure and the rate we're using them. Read it at the ward meetings. They should be able to ask about their own streets.*

Jinhsi picked up the accommodation sheet. Forty-three had become forty-nine when the loft's occupants were added; new arrangements had brought the number down again; below the revised total the clerk had started another column for the families expected from the north tomorrow. She ran a finger down the names and stopped where one had been squeezed against the rule. *We'll need the larger register.* Changli looked up and said she had had it brought from the records room that afternoon and that it was on her chair. Jinhsi turned. The book lay under her folded cloak, heavy enough to bow the cushion. She carried it to the table herself.

On Bookbinders' Row, a compositor was setting the warning for the morning posting and ran short of one sort. He checked the drawer, the floor beneath it, and the dish where his daughter puts the pieces she finds while sweeping. Then he ruled a small space in the forme and reached for a brush.

The woman waiting at his counter watched him and asked whether the ward would accept a sheet finished by hand. *They'll accept a warning people can read. Bring your receipt over here; I'll see what you're owed.* She set it down. It covered several purchases of type over the previous month, all paid in advance. He glanced at it, then toward his cases, and said he could fill part of it after the rain, because the notices had taken the rest of the afternoon. *Take the time you need. I'll be in the city.*

His daughter came through the back door carrying a kettle with both hands, stepped around the trays on the floor, poured for her father first, and put the second cup on the counter beside the woman's receipt. *It's very hot. The handle stays cool if you take it here.* The woman wrapped both hands around the cup.

Outside, the rain had gathered in the lip of a broken paving stone, and for an instant the puddle held a mown field — stubble against bare arms, a small finger hooked around another, warm and certain, and the light above it spreading until it filled the place where a face should have been.

The woman's little finger moved against the porcelain. The girl behind the counter asked whether she wanted sugar.

*Two, please*, said Nivora. *And another cup for yourself, if your father can spare it.*

The compositor's brush went into the space left by the missing type. His daughter fetched a cup with a chipped rim and sat on the stool beside him. Nivora drank while the hand-lettered warnings dried.

At the school, Mei finished her bowl with her back against the warm side of the stove, and Amy sat on the floor beside her with the receiver between her boots, having given the late announcements to two dock workers and the school caretaker, whose voices came through in turn, each with its own speed and its own way of pronouncing the street names. Lupa arrived from the kitchen with another pot, put it down, rubbed both hands over her face, and leaned against the doorframe while Cartethyia found bowls: Panhua says there is enough for the late watch, and also that somebody has been cutting her scallions with medical scissors. Baizhi said they were kitchen scissors when she borrowed them and that she sterilised them afterward, and was told she could explain that in person, because Panhua had saved her supper. Ciaccona laughed and shifted her lute so a child could squeeze past her chair. Chisa sat near the doorway drying Ruan's hinge with the cloth from his care roll; the red thread at her wrist had darkened with water, and she eased it clear of the steel and went on working. Yangyang occupied the bench below the window with her mother's flute in its open case, and the curtain nearest her shoulder lifted occasionally, barely enough to stir the air around her cup.

Mei watched it for a while. Then she leaned against Amy, who accepted the weight and went on listening to the receiver, and said she had made an appointment at the archive — that night, after this — and had asked for somebody to read the annex with her. Amy turned the receiver a little lower and said she would like to come, and could sit outside while Mei read if Mei would rather have Iuno in the room. *Come in with us. You can keep the questions I forget to ask.* Amy rested her head briefly against Mei's.

Iuno was at the far end of the room helping Mrs Ren divide the dry blankets, and looked over when Mei stood, and asked whether she was ready to walk up. *Give me a minute. Chisa left her coat in the cart.*

Mei went down to the arch, found the coat beneath the driver's bench, and shook the water off its oilskin cover. Through the open gate the road ran north between lamps set low enough to show the mud, and a keeper was lighting the next one with a long pole, and stopped to let a stretcher pass, and raised the pole again.

Above him, the bell in the ninth tower trembled on its beam.

Mei felt the answering warmth across her palm. She closed her hand around Chisa's coat and waited for it to pass.

Beside the school door, Chixia had pinned the weather notice beneath a square of oiled paper. Rain gathered along its lower edge. The two large characters at the top stayed clear.

梅雨

Iuno lifted the curtain from inside. *Mei, your daughter has found the questions already. You may want to hear them before we walk up.* *I'm coming. Help me get this coat somewhere dry.*

Iuno took one sleeve. Mei kept the other. Together they lifted it clear of the wet step and carried it into the room.

## XIII. WHERE IT STANDS

At the start of this, seven foreigners were strangers in a terraced city on a ninety-day stamp, holding four unexplained objects that a Magistrate they had never met had left on a table with a bodyguard's apology. Nobody in Jinzhou had a column for them. Mei knew a mark on her hand, a language she could speak and could not write, and one certainty: that she was going to be very careful about what she asked for. Iuno knew where every document in a building was and had learned, at the cost of an arm and eleven years and a hundred and seventy-three names, to stop reaching for them. Amy knew that ten years underground had made her load-bearing and was frightened of being left behind again. The wind had finished its errand and had been given a name jointly made and a life after purpose. The ninth tower had been silent since the dark. The tremors past the north face were eight days apart and closing.

Twenty-odd days later the shape of everything has changed, and almost none of it changed by conquest.

Mei has stopped being a rumour and become an entry. Household four-one-one, ward four, entered the twenty-first of the fifth month, seven names, one stove, a one-armed head of household by acclamation, a *niece* written in a hurry on a wet afternoon, and four lines of an assessor's careful remarks about name-giving to a person of unestablished origin. She has read that entry standing in a courtyard in the sun. She has walked into a room where a nineteen-year-old held out a key to her own sealed history and declined it, because taking it would have made a Magistrate write down that she gave it. She has come back to it anyway, on her own schedule, in a building whose foundation gauge has begun answering the drum in the north, and has asked for a reader, and for time to write down what she remembers, and for her daughter in the room to hold the questions she forgets to ask. Her body has begun answering things her memory cannot reach: a puzzle floor that recognised her hand, a blast door that recognised her hand, a hologram of a blurred woman who looked at her across forty years of failed ambition, a gate fitting under her palm in a rain that gives back the past — and each episode has been shorter than the last, and each time somebody has been standing beside her whose job is to watch her hands. Her healing halved on the twenty-first and has stayed halved, exactly, and neither she nor Iuno has said the obvious thing out loud. She still declines to be the last resort, and she still comes home for supper.

Iuno has done the thing she was most afraid of, which is want the work. She stood at the end of a trestle in a public yard for twenty-two minutes and read nothing, with a witness she had recruited specifically so that the memory would exist. She emptied her own coat rather than hand it over. She named a cost out loud before paying it, several times, on the record, to nobody. And she has taken a title at last: a written appointment with a term ending at noon, a spending limit in the margin, two people empowered to end it early, and a clause she added herself allowing the families to ask for somebody else instead. In her shirt pocket she carries a ring cut from her own shoulder and a folded paper with four characters and a seal space that turned out to have a chop pressed into the fibre when the sheet was made — because somebody had that paper made before they wrote on it, and left the space empty anyway. She has decided against the enormous version. She wants a bad evening in a rented room with a window that will not latch.

Amy has discovered a city that does not need her voice — a bell and four hundred years moving a hundred and forty thousand people at noon without anybody talking — and has said so, to the sky, on a parapet, and been told by her mother that this is the good version, and agreed that this is what is annoying about it. She has been told to sit with a thing for a year and has said *Fine*. She has stood in front of a stranger's page with a brushed mark in the corner and three official answers to choose from, and taken her hand out of her pocket, and written nothing down. She has stopped extracting what people will not give her: from Cartethyia, from Shen, from a Magistrate whose left hand stayed shut all afternoon. On the twenty-seventh she is running a city's shelter announcements off a socket with copper wound around the pins, and taking a boy's glued bridge seriously, and saving her mother a bowl.

Lupa sold the one number that belongs to Mei's body and bought, in four sentences, a clause in the permanent record of a foreign country: nobody in that household gets spent without being told to her face, in a room, in advance, by a person who is present. She has been told to ask again in eleven years whether it was worth it, and expects to be asked. She has learned an economy where debts close before dark and hated it and used it correctly, and she got the second wok. Ciaccona has had her flat bridge corrected on a public step by an old man with a stool, and has let her friend be wrong in public without singing her out of it, and carries a blank ruled card that now has three grey marks on it, with the interval between the second and third shortening. Cartethyia carries two names she will not repeat, a river stone that held a corner for fifty-one years, a camellia cutting that has put out a pale tip, and a shut gate on the last lane before the ward changes that will stay shut. Chisa has been lost for forty minutes in a city that moved its furniture into the street and called it the best day since the shore; she has been told that faded red is still red; she has read a shopping list to a courtyard and given a ward its fourth reading of a character it has argued about for eleven years, and a clerk entered it and declined to strike it. Her mother has been ironing a summer uniform once a year for twenty years, and Chisa is going to write.

Yangyang has had her anniversary noticed. A year ago she stood in a room and said out loud that the weather is a person; now she counts a household through a gate twice a day and cannot make the number come out, and has stopped trying. She has learned to ask for a column and to refuse to lie to get one. And she has watched the wind hold four hundred kilometres of weather off a city for fifteen hours so that a laundry day could run its posted hours, and then fail at the fourth bar of a lullaby, and be sung to instead by eight foreigners in the rain until the rain thinned over one eleven-metre circle for four verses.

Jinhsi has eaten a meal sitting at a table. She has been asked what food she likes and discovered that nobody had asked before. She has been fed by a wolf and argued with about a pickle and handed a paper person at a doorway and told that when it gets loud she should say so out loud, and asked *To whom*, and been given three names. She has moved a Bureau of Waterways meeting so that the official book can record the Magistrate of Jinzhou attending a noodle house for the purpose of eating fried food. And she is running a city, a war, and a sluice with a two-hundred-breath interval in it while carrying a whisper she will not repeat until she can verify it with a Sentinel that has told her nothing for nineteen nights and counting. Her Counselor has promised her a conversation she will be angry about. That conversation stands between them still.

Changli's calendar has been read by somebody who does not play. Her teacher's thirty-one-year record turns out to hold two games under one numbering. Her blank card turns out to be a pass, which means the player is still there and it is her turn. She holds the registry traitor's name and has kept it for two years of a war she can see coming; she forged a date for a fifteen-year-old and has maintained it for seven; she has been told to tell her, and has answered *I intend to*, and has not answered *when*. She has carried silkworm trays out of a shed with her scars uncovered and a stranger's daughter folding a cloth over her arm. The three hundred and twelve have wagons now — eleven of them painted, numbered 301 to 311, thirty-four more being fitted — and the number is still not zero, and she has undertaken to say whatever it is out loud, in a room, to a person, on the day the road closes.

Scar sits in a Jinzhou cell, having lost the room he took and left behind a black-lamb card, an unrepeated whisper, and a promise that Mei will come to the Fractsidus voluntarily once she understands what she is. Under a drained reservoir, a girl called A-Ling is in the archive in her own hand, beside the ledger page that priced her at healthy, single, low future yield. Under a hillside on the north road, a laboratory stands with its rain still hanging in the air, its blackboard still unfinished, and a small handprint in the dust that is only weeks old. Its patron's symbol was Fractsidus. Its phase-two product is now falling on the highlands and spreading south along the Tacet seams, raising a second army out of the ground between the watchfires and putting a fallen bridge back over a quarry road.

And the drum has closed from nineteen minutes to nine forty to eight twelve to seven thirty-six in a single day, and a general has written the name under the time and sent it south: Ovathrax, Threnodian of War, which does not play, which is the weather over the board, and which has already won here once in a printing where the word for it was *held*.

The ninth tower's bell was tapped once, at the eighth hour, on a night with cut grain in the wind, and a keeper struck out *weather* and left the second column blank, and a Magistrate wrote *wait* in the margin. On the twenty-seventh of the sixth month it trembled on its beam while a lamp-keeper let a stretcher pass, and a woman with a mark on her right hand closed her hand around a borrowed coat and waited for the warmth to go out of her palm.

On a shelf in the ward four office, second course, at the level of a clerk's own eye, a sealed basket holds eleven pages. One of them is a letter that begins *To you, whom I haven't met*, found at the foot of the wall below the ninth tower at eleven at night by a patroller who declined to give her name. One of them is a ruled foreign sheet with printed headings struck through in blue crayon and relettered by a child, thirty-four numbered entries and one more on the reverse, which its owner has been searching for since she was six years old and which she walked past by eleven feet without knowing. In three hundred and sixty-five days, that basket goes on a table in a courtyard where anybody at all may read anything on it.

On Bookbinders' Row, eleven shops are missing eleven different sorts, and every compositor gives the same two words about it — *It went* — and one sort has lain face down for thirty-one years because a sort that is not being used does not need reading, and is out in the sun with the rest of them, and that is all it is owed. In a theatre yard behind the fourth press, nine blank sheets are aired every year: a second act, nine minutes long, doors open, nobody on the stage, never staged, paid for in three hundred and six half-coppers of one old minting that have never been spent. At the east gate, three hundred and six letters go out on boards one day a year so the paper will last, and the woman who wrote them will not hand the tin over, and has told a girl to come back on the sixth. Any of them.

And on the twenty-seventh, at a counter on Bookbinders' Row, in the rain, a woman with a receipt for a month of type bought in advance drank tea from a cup with a chipped rim while a compositor filled a missing character in by hand.

The plum rains have forty days to run. The wind is in the house and has spent fifteen hours it did not have. Four hands remain on the board and Jinhsi can still account for only two. The annex is unsealed and waiting, in a room being arranged for tonight, with a reader, a daughter, and a woman whose job is to write down the numbers flat.

And every evening, one human hand at a time, the lamps of Jinzhou still come on.

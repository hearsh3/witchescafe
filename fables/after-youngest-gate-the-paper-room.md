# The Paper Room

By morning, somebody had washed the north road.

Jinhsi watched a man push his broom through the gutter below City Hall. Water gathered around a discarded strip of bandage, lifted it, and carried it against a wheel. He propped the broom against his shoulder and bent to free it with two fingers.

The lamps along that road had burned all evening while the column came through. She remembered the light touching a signalman's cheek; the way he turned toward a voice and then hurried to recover the step he had lost. For a moment she had wanted to tell him he could take his time. Baizhi's figures were in her coat pocket. She had walked beside him and kept the figures there.

Now the man with the broom straightened. A woman above him shook a cloth out of her window and called down an apology. He waved the apology away with his wet hand.

Jinhsi had slept long enough for the street to acquire morning noises. She could account for sleep by the change of shift outside her door and the dry patch on her pillow where her hair had lain. Her body felt as though she had spent the interval standing.

On the dressing table, Gao Shun's chopstick rested across a folded cloth.

She had washed it herself. A grain of rice had dried near the blunt end, and she had worked it loose with her thumbnail, carefully, over the basin. She remembered his hand trying to close around it. She remembered the small pleasure in his face when he understood why his mother had always put the pot back on early.

That was the part that came back while she washed her hands. The pleasure.

She dried each finger. Then she took the Loong Scale from its case and sat beside the window.

The familiar invocation came easily. Her mouth had learned its shape while she was still learning how to receive a petitioner twice her age. She finished it, closed her eyes, and held the scale between her palms.

A barrow rattled over the paving below. Somewhere in the compound, Sanhua spoke to the officer taking the morning watch.

Jinhsi waited through both voices.

The scale warmed against her skin.

“They came through the north gate,” she said. “All one thousand nine hundred and seven.”

She had said that number to clerks, ward representatives, the gate captain, a woman holding a child's sleeping weight against her shoulder. Each time there had been another person's face before her, another breath arriving after the words.

She watched the pale edge of the scale.

“I wanted to tell you myself.”

The sweeper began working under the wheel. Bristles scraped stone in long, uneven strokes.

After a while, she put the scale away and went to the paper room.

***

Changli had placed her chair on the far side of the table.

Jinhsi saw the distance as soon as she entered. Usually her Counselor sat at the corner, near enough to turn a page while Jinhsi held the preceding one open. Today the whole width of the table lay between them. The resignation envelope sat beside the inkstone. Its edges had softened from being moved between rooms.

Clerk Wen occupied the smaller desk by the partition. Morning light came through the paper behind him, showing the darker rectangle of a recent patch. Somebody had brushed paste beyond its edges. The excess shone when Jinhsi passed it.

She took her seat.

Changli's hair was pinned with its usual care. There was a narrow red mark across the bridge of her nose, where her reading glasses had rested too long, and the cuff over her left wrist had been turned once to keep it clear of the ink. Jinhsi knew that fold. She had copied it as a child, on sleeves that were forever falling into things.

Wen read the resumption of civil authority into the day-book. Changli set the seal on the red cloth and pushed it across. Jinhsi checked the impression against the last entry, signed beneath it, and gave Wen the page.

The motion occupied her hands for almost a minute.

They reviewed the corrections to the prophetic chronology, the circulation list, and the transfer of the original records into joint custody. Luo Zemin's hearing had a date. His daughter's protection order remained in force. The Council would receive Changli's account with the index of affected decisions appended.

“The Council secretary asked whether the appointment as Acting Magistrate superseded the inquiry,” Wen said.

“Record the inquiry as continuing,” Jinhsi replied. “Attach the terms of the appointment. He has the answer in the last clause.”

Wen entered it. His brush had begun catching on the same stroke every few lines; each time he paused and worked the bristles back into shape against the dish.

Changli watched him finish.

“A second copy of my private index is with the depositions,” she said. “Its references can be checked against the originals. I have also recorded the places where I relied on memory.”

Jinhsi read the covering page. The handwriting was the same hand that had once put little circles beside passages she might enjoy.

She set it down.

“Thank you, Clerk Wen. Please leave us the room. We will call you back for any further examination of the records.”

He blotted the page, closed the day-book, and stood with one hand briefly braced against his desk. Jinhsi waited until he had straightened.

“After you deliver that, ask Gao to take the next returns. Go home for the afternoon.”

“My daughter has begun asking the gate watch for my whereabouts. I would prefer to reach the house before she extends the inquiry.”

Changli's mouth softened. “Then you had better take the lower stair. She was in the east gallery when I arrived.”

Wen glanced toward the door with immediate professional concern. He gathered his things more quickly.

His footsteps receded along the gallery. Through the partition came the scrape of a chair in the adjoining work room, then a drawer opening.

Jinhsi looked at the envelope.

“I have read the account twice,” she said. “This morning I began it again. I was hoping a third reading would tell me how to speak to you.”

Changli rested her hands on the table. “You can begin wherever you wish. I have kept this conversation waiting a long time.”

“That is one of the things I keep finding in it. You had time to prepare. You knew what I would learn, and you knew I would be hurt. You wrote your resignation before I asked the first question.”

“I thought having it ready would spare you the difficulty of asking for it.”

Jinhsi lifted her eyes.

“And where had you imagined going?”

Changli's right thumb moved along the seam of her cuff.

“I had considered temporary rooms near the southern archive. Close enough to attend the inquiry and answer requests from the office.”

There it was: a street, a building, a useful distance. Jinhsi could picture her making the arrangements, asking after the windows and the writing desk, finding the route with the fewest steps.

“You had a room ready for the person I would become when I found out.”

Changli looked down at the envelope. “I had thought about the consequences I could organize. Those were easier to spend an evening with.”

Jinhsi turned her cup by the rim. The quarter-turn brought a small flaw in the glaze toward her. She touched it with her thumb and left the cup where it was.

“At the gate, when I saw you, I was glad.”

Changli's fingers stopped at her cuff.

“I had been thinking about the road and the rain and whether the people at the rear would reach us in time. Then you were standing under the arch, and I wanted to give you everything I was carrying. I wanted you to know what had happened to Chixia. I wanted to tell you about Gao Shun.”

Her voice had begun to hurry. She brought it back under control, and the effort hurt her throat.

“I was angry with you all the way home. I still looked for you first.”

Changli drew a breath through parted lips. She held it briefly before answering.

“I looked for your hair above the column. Every time the road bent, I thought I had found you. There was a standard with a strip of white cloth tied beneath it. I followed that for nearly ten minutes.”

Jinhsi could see the standard. She had walked beside it for part of the descent. A Ranger had tied the cloth there so the cradle crews could judge the crosswind.

“You could have asked the relay.”

“Wen had already asked. I knew where you were.” Changli's mouth pulled faintly at one corner. “My eyes kept choosing the cloth.”

The drawer in the next room shut. Both women heard it, and for a few breaths that small domestic sound occupied the space between them.

Then Jinhsi said, “When the chronology fell out of the packet, I saw your initials. My first thought was that you would be able to explain the mistake.”

She pressed her thumb into the flaw in the cup.

“I had already turned toward you.”

Changli's gaze dropped to the hand around the porcelain. Jinhsi saw the old impulse travel through her: the attention to pressure, the assessment of what might break. Her Counselor brought her eyes back to Jinhsi's face.

“I am sorry,” she said. “You came to me with your doubts for years. I knew how much trust there was in that, and I accepted it while I held the originals upstairs.”

Jinhsi had imagined a longer answer. She had prepared for the Council and the threatened inquiry, for the names of men who had written about her first flood order as though an open storehouse were a symptom. She knew how to answer those men now. She had spent part of the night composing what she would say.

The apology left her sitting with the younger version of herself, who had brought a page upstairs because Changli would know where the difficulty lay.

“You used to ask me to show you my working,” she said. “Even when I had the right answer.”

“You often arrived there by a route worth keeping.”

“I showed you all of it.”

The words came out with a child's plain sense of an agreement.

Changli's shoulders lowered. Her hands lay open on the table, the feathered scars running pale into her sleeve.

“You did,” she said. “I made that a condition of teaching you. You had every reason to expect the same care from me.”

Jinhsi looked toward the paper wall. A shadow passed beyond it carrying a stack of bowls. The top bowl tipped; a hand came up, steadied it, and continued along the other side.

She waited until it was gone.

“The Council draft frightened me,” Jinhsi said. “Even reading it now. They had collected such small things. A meal served late. A request I sent back because a village had been left off the list. I remember making some of those decisions. I remember feeling pleased that I had noticed.”

Changli looked at her closely.

“They had agreed on the conclusion before they assembled the evidence. I knew the men drafting it. I had watched them prepare inquiries in the capital, and I knew how quickly a concern could acquire enough signatures to become an order.”

Her voice had gained its familiar steadiness. Jinhsi heard the Secretary-General in it: a woman accustomed to entering a room where a decision was already taking place, finding the person with the pen, and changing the afternoon.

“You were downstairs asking about the kitchens,” Changli continued. “I remember hearing you through the floor. You had learned the steward's name, and you were asking him to explain the difference between the stores we owned and the stores we could actually distribute. Upstairs, they were writing that you lacked judgment. I was furious.”

She drew the folded cloth a little farther beneath her wrist.

“I knew exactly how to stop them. The amended chronology went into their packet, and by the next sitting they were arguing over another matter. You stayed in your chair. The kitchens opened. For a long time, I could put those events beside each other and feel satisfied.”

Jinhsi leaned back. Her shoulder blades met the chair, and she realized how rigidly she had been holding herself away from it.

“I am glad you were there,” she said. “I have been trying to find a way to say that since I read the draft. I keep worrying that you will hear it as permission.”

“I remember how tightly you held my hand before the first hearing,” Changli said. “I was glad to be the person walking in beside you. I let myself keep deciding for that girl long after you were ready to answer them yourself.”

“You were the person I could understand in that room. Everyone else seemed to be speaking from somewhere I would have to reach when I was older. You sat beside me and explained what they wanted.”

She remembered Changli making a diagram in the margin of a trade petition: three storehouses, two roads, the river between them. Jinhsi had been ashamed to ask what the petitioner meant. Changli had used the back of the page and drawn the roads wide enough for her to follow with a finger.

“Later I learned to understand them myself,” Jinhsi said. “I think you enjoyed that. Sometimes you would look at me across a meeting, and I would know I had found the right question.”

Changli smiled then. It arrived quickly, with a memory of its own.

“The salt assessment. You asked the deputy why his warehouse required winter fuel in the seventh month. I had been saving that question for the end.”

Jinhsi's mouth moved before she could help it.

“He spent the rest of the meeting explaining ventilation.”

“Very thoroughly. I still have his diagram.”

The smile stayed between them for a moment. Jinhsi felt its familiarity in her chest, almost painful in its ease.

She lowered her eyes.

“You let me grow up in some rooms. In others, you went on deciding what I could bear.”

Changli's smile faded slowly. She placed the cloth flat with the side of her hand.

“When you understood the deputy, I knew how the meeting would end. When I imagined giving you the originals, I had to imagine you deciding what my judgment was worth. I kept attending to the dangers outside the room. They gave me a great deal of work to do.”

“You were afraid I would send you away.”

“I was afraid you would look at me differently. You might keep me here and weigh every word I said.”

Jinhsi looked across the table at her.

Changli's breath caught. Her thumb pressed once against the pale skin above her wrist, then loosened.

“That is what I have given you,” she said. “I can feel you doing it now.”

Jinhsi lifted her cup. The tea was cool enough to drink quickly. She swallowed too much, and the last mouthful sat heavily in her stomach.

She wanted Changli to pour the next cup. The desire irritated her so sharply that she reached for the pot herself.

Changli moved the inkstone out of the way.

It was a small, useful motion. Jinhsi watched it and found herself trying to decide what it meant. Her hand faltered; tea struck the saucer and ran toward the edge of the table.

Changli held out the cloth.

Jinhsi took it. She wiped up the spill, folded the wet part inward, and set it beside the pot.

“This is exhausting,” she said. “Even the tea.”

“I see that.” Changli's voice was quieter. “Would you like the pot on your side?”

“I would like you to pour it when I ask you.”

Changli set the pot between them, its handle within reach.

For a while they listened to the office through the wall. A clerk read out a list of supplies. Another corrected the number of axles. They discussed which yard had received them, and Jinhsi could picture the narrow passage where the last wagon would be blocking the drain. Her mind began arranging the order in which the loads should come out.

She put both feet flat on the floor.

“Did you want to tell me when you came back from the fields with Iuno?”

“I did. She recognized what I had done. She told me what it had felt like when she did it, and what came afterward.”

“You were able to tell her.”

“She was a woman I had met that afternoon. I could bear losing her good opinion.”

Jinhsi rubbed a damp spot on her sleeve between finger and thumb. Outside the window, a porter called for somebody to hold the gate.

“At Panhua's, Lupa put a bowl in front of Iuno while she was trying to begin something serious. Iuno took the bowl. She ate from it. Later they went back to their conversation.”

She could still see the table, the rice sack beneath Changli, the places changing as people got up and sat down. She had spent the first part of that evening trying to locate the rule that allowed everyone to speak so freely.

“I kept watching her,” she said. “She looked annoyed. She looked perfectly at home. I tried to imagine doing that to you.”

Changli glanced at the wet cloth beside the teapot.

“You brought food into my office often enough.”

“And stood there while you thanked me. Then I went back to my work. I used to think that was how adults took care of each other.”

Changli closed her eyes briefly.

“I taught you a very efficient way to leave me alone.”

“You kept telling me you had everything in hand. I believed you. I was grateful.” Jinhsi looked at the softened corner of the envelope. “Sometimes I wanted to stay, and I thought staying would give you another person to look after.”

Changli shifted in her chair. The movement brought her knee against the table leg with a dull knock. She put a hand there, almost absently.

“I liked having you in the room,” she said. “Even when you were reading and I was working. I would look up to find you turning a page, and the afternoon would feel easier.”

“You could have told me that.”

“I should have told you many times.”

Jinhsi drew her cup toward her with both hands.

“Mei looks for Iuno too. I watched them at the field kitchen. Mei could barely keep her eyes open, and she kept following Iuno's hand whenever it went near the dressing. Eventually she said something about it. Iuno answered her while she was eating.”

“They have had some difficult conversations.”

“They seem able to continue having dinner through them.”

Changli looked at her, and the tenderness that came into her face made Jinhsi's eyes sting. She bent her head over the cup.

“I would like to learn that,” Changli said. “I have been very good at finding work that keeps me here after the kitchen closes.”

“You will have to let me be unpleasant company sometimes.”

“I have shared a table with three successive revenue ministers. There is experience to draw on.”

Jinhsi breathed out a small, unwilling laugh. Changli let her own smile come, and for a few moments they sat in the warmth of something each had contributed.

Then Jinhsi looked toward the envelope again.

“I need you to understand that I may keep asking about the same evening. I remember another part of it, and the whole thing changes. Something kind you said. A question you helped me put away. I keep finding you there.”

Changli leaned forward, resting her forearms on the table.

“Then we can take that evening again. I remember a great deal of it too.”

“You will be tired of answering.”

“Some days I expect I will. You have seen me tired before. I would rather you have an honest answer from a tired woman than another carefully chosen week in which to ask.”

Jinhsi held her gaze for several breaths. There was something awkward in the room now, a future she could picture only in pieces: asking again over breakfast, disliking the answer, needing the door open for the next question. She had been able to write Changli's temporary authority in a single sitting. Her mind kept reaching for a similar page here, with an ending she could sign.

She took the resignation envelope and moved it to the end of the table.

“The Council has your account,” she said. “I will give them mine. I intend to tell them that I want you to continue as Counselor under the restrictions already entered. They will have their own questions, and we will answer those in the hearing.”

Changli's gaze followed the envelope.

“I would like to continue,” she said. “I know the work. I care about the people whose names arrive here. And I want to see you when I come up the stair.”

Jinhsi's fingers stayed on the paper a moment longer.

“When you think I am about to be hurt, tell me what you see. You can argue with me. You have always been persuasive.”

Changli's mouth moved at one corner, then settled.

“I can do that.”

“You will have to do it while you are still frightened.”

Changli looked at the younger woman's hands on the envelope, then up at her face.

“I am frightened now,” she said. “I have been thinking about how much of your life you may want to keep from me after this. There are questions I used to ask very easily. This morning I spent nearly an hour wondering whether I could ask if you had slept.”

Jinhsi withdrew her hands and folded them in her lap.

“You may ask me.”

“Then tell me how the night was.”

Jinhsi looked at the shining patch on the paper wall.

For several seconds she could hear the gutter outside her window, and the long drag of the sweeper's broom.

“I washed the chopstick,” she said. “There was rice on the end. I stood over the basin picking it off, and then I had to sit down.”

Changli remained leaning toward her.

“His father brought it across both hands. I carried it all the way north. When I found Gao Shun, he tried to take it from me. He was very careful. He kept adjusting his fingers, as though there were some ordinary difficulty with his grip.”

Jinhsi opened her right hand in her lap. She watched her fingers close around the remembered wood.

“We found a bowl. I tapped the rim for him. He looked so pleased when the steam came toward him.”

The clerk beyond the wall asked for another sheet. Paper whispered across a desk.

“I wanted his mother to have that part. His face. I have words for it, and she will want every one of them, and I have been trying to choose them since last night.”

“You can tell her how long you have been thinking about it. She may want to hear that too.”

Jinhsi pressed her lips together. Her eyes had begun filling, and she looked at the window until the bright rectangle steadied.

“And then there are the people who walked north with us. I wake up with Chixia's leg in my hands. I can feel the scarf going wet. She was talking to me the whole time, explaining where the shots had come from. I wanted to ask whether it hurt, and I kept looking at how much blood there was.”

Changli's hand closed slowly over her own wrist.

“Deng Hao was carried past us. Later I wrote his name on the back of my address. I knew what to do with the page. I had things to enter, people to send for, a family to inform. That evening, when someone thanked me for bringing the column home, I saw his name again.”

She pulled the handkerchief closer, still folded on the table.

“Hsin let the man who gave the signal walk away. There was a bargain, and a price, and a reason we are supposed to learn in Mengzhou. I stood beside them with my seal in my hand. I am still standing there when I try to sleep.”

Changli's eyes lifted to hers. Jinhsi could almost hear the explanations assembling: the boundary of the writ, a Sentinel's authority, the bargain's possible purpose. Her Counselor sat with her hand around her wrist, breathing carefully.

“I was at the receiver,” Changli said. “Your voice came through very clearly. I heard you object, and I heard how they answered. I wish I had been close enough for you to turn toward me.”

Jinhsi pressed the folded handkerchief flat with her palm.

“I would have wanted that,” she said. “I think I would have been furious with you too.”

Changli's fingers eased at her wrist. “We could have sat together while you were furious. I would have had a few things to say myself.”

Jinhsi followed a crease in the handkerchief with her finger.

“By evening, there were people waiting in the yard with things they had brought from home. One woman had a comb. Another had her husband's coat over her arm. They had heard the announcement, and the ward officers had explained what the rain would do. I watched the woman with the coat hold a sleeve open for her husband.”

She had looked away for a moment, then made herself look back. The woman was still holding the sleeve. Her husband had bent his head close to hers, his mouth moving beside her ear. Jinhsi remembered thinking she should move farther down the line and give them room. Her feet had felt enormously heavy.

“I keep thinking about how long she must have kept that coat. She had sewn a new tie onto it. I could see where the cloth was brighter.”

Changli looked down at her own hands. “She stayed near the west wall afterward. Her sister came for her. They folded the coat together.”

Jinhsi's breath broke on the way in.

She reached for the cloth beside the teapot, saw the dark patch of tea, and put it down again. Changli took a handkerchief from her sleeve and laid it within reach.

Jinhsi pressed it beneath her eyes.

“I wanted it to last longer,” she said. “I knew the figures. Every time Baizhi measured the rain, I asked for the result. Then we reached the gate, and I started counting the minutes as though I could keep one.”

Changli drew her chair a little closer to the table.

“When the last of them faded, I watched the road beyond the arch,” she said. “There was still light on it. Wen had closed the column. I stayed facing the bend until somebody came to take the lamps in.”

Jinhsi lowered the handkerchief. “I saw you there. I thought you were waiting for the rear escort.”

“I was having trouble leaving.”

The answer settled slowly. Jinhsi had seen her Counselor beneath the gate and given her the task that would explain her position. She had done it as naturally as reading a heading on a page.

She looked at the woman across from her: the red mark on her nose, the cuff turned clear of the ink, the hand resting too carefully on an old injury.

“Does your arm hurt?”

Changli glanced down, then returned her gaze.

“It aches this morning. I carried too much at the spillway and spent the next day pretending that writing would count as rest. The physician gave me a dressing. I left it in my room.”

“Please use it after this.”

“I will bring it to lunch. You can supervise my considerable medical expertise.”

Jinhsi's mouth trembled around the beginning of a smile. She pressed the handkerchief to it.

The tears came then, warm against her fingers. She bent forward, trying to breathe through her nose, and the effort made her shoulders shake.

Changli's chair moved an inch.

“Would you come round the table?” Jinhsi asked. “It feels a long way to speak across.”

Changli rose. She took the chair by its back and carried it to Jinhsi's side, setting it down carefully in the narrow space between the table and the wall. Her sleeve brushed Jinhsi's shoulder as she sat.

Jinhsi caught the edge of it.

She had reached for that sleeve in crowded rooms when she was small. Changli would turn, lowering her head to hear the question, and the people waiting to speak to the Secretary-General would briefly have to occupy themselves. Jinhsi remembered a man's elaborate belt, the little metal leaves on it trembling while he shifted his weight. She had been asking where the washing room was. Changli had taken her there herself.

Now the cloth folded into her fist, and Changli put her right arm around her shoulders.

Jinhsi leaned into her. The chair was badly placed; its wooden arm pressed into her ribs. Changli shifted, moved the handkerchief clear of Jinhsi's mouth, and held her while she cried.

Through the wall, someone began reading the next shelter return. The voice reached the number of occupied rooms and continued into the temporary terms. Jinhsi heard the clerk turn a page. Work went on a few feet from her, in a voice she recognized, at a pace she could follow.

She let her forehead rest against Changli's shoulder.

“I thought Jué might answer when the bell rang,” she said. “I heard the first beat, and I was already waiting for the voice. Then there were seven more. I stood there listening after everyone began moving.”

Changli's hand rested warm between her shoulder blades.

“This morning I took out the scale. I said the number of people who came through. I thought perhaps I should say it as I used to, when I brought the wishes. I could remember exactly how to hold my hands.”

Her fingers tightened in the sleeve.

“I wanted to hear my name.”

Changli rested her cheek lightly against Jinhsi's hair. They sat through the end of the return. In the adjoining room, a woman asked when the next delivery was due, and the clerk consulted someone farther away before answering.

“I miss being able to ask for help and know what the answer sounds like,” Jinhsi said. “Even when it was something difficult. There was a voice. I could be frightened of what it meant and still be glad to hear it.”

She lifted her head a little.

“I keep wondering what Jué would have said about the road. About Geshu Lin. I had to answer him, and then I had to come home and decide what to tell his city, and this morning I wanted someone to tell me I had done well.”

Her voice thinned on the last words. She rubbed the handkerchief across her cheek with sudden irritation at herself.

Changli took her hand gently and lowered it.

“I heard you on the relay when the wards could hear their families suffering. You told them what was happening. Then you let each room decide how much it could bear. I was standing beside the receiver, wanting very badly to make that decision for them.”

She paused. Her thumb rested against the back of Jinhsi's hand.

“You gave them time to answer. I am proud of you for that. I have been proud of you in rooms where all I could contribute was a page you had asked me to bring.”

Jinhsi's face crumpled. She lowered it against Changli's shoulder again, angry at how much she had wanted the words, grateful enough to ache.

Changli held her more firmly.

“And I wanted you home,” she said. “I had a bag of mangosteens brought up yesterday. I kept moving it because every flat surface had papers on it. At one point I put it in the drawer where I keep the visiting cards, and the drawer stuck.”

Jinhsi made a wet sound against her sleeve.

“The bottom catches when it is full.”

“I discovered that while a harbor representative was explaining a matter of considerable importance. I had to ask him to begin again.”

Jinhsi's hand loosened a little.

“I was thinking about how you open them,” Changli continued. “You always choose the largest and then complain about the size of the segments. I wanted to hear you complain about a piece of fruit. I kept the bag.”

For a while Jinhsi cried quietly, with the handkerchief crushed between their hands. Her breathing gradually lengthened. She could feel the heat of Changli's body through two layers of cloth and, beneath her cheek, the occasional uneven breath her Counselor was taking.

She raised her head.

Changli's eyes were wet. A tear had reached the corner of her mouth. She wiped it away with the heel of her free hand, glanced at the ink on her finger, and let out a breath that almost became a laugh.

“There is probably a mark now.”

“Hold still. You will spread it.”

Jinhsi found a clean corner of the handkerchief and wiped the small dark streak from beside her mouth. Changli let her do it. At that distance she could see the fine lines around her teacher's eyes, the tired skin beneath them. She had seen those eyes across a table for most of the life she could clearly remember.

Her hand slowed.

“I have more things I will be angry about,” she said. “I can feel them waiting.”

Changli kept her arm around her.

“Tell me when they arrive. I will be here for lunch, and I will bring the fruit.”

Jinhsi folded the handkerchief. Its creases had become soft and confused, and she pressed them together until it would fit in her hand.

“Please bring the dressing too. I intend to be tiresome about it.”

“Then I shall come prepared.”

They sat a little longer. Jinhsi's hand rested over the scars at Changli's wrist, light enough to feel the warmth coming through the skin.

Later, in her own room, Jinhsi put Gao Shun's chopstick into a narrow box. She lined it with clean cloth and wrote his name on the lid. His parents would come after the morning ward meeting. She moved their appointment to a room with a door onto the garden, where they could leave at their own pace.

The work took several minutes. Afterward, she sat with her hands beside the box.

Mei and her household would eventually take the road west. Yangyang had written Mengzhou into her plans. There would be packing and arguments about luggage, a last meal that grew crowded, somebody remembering an errand as the others reached the gate. Jinhsi could imagine herself seeing them off, checking that the travel papers were in order, coming back up the terraces with the sound of their departure still in her ears.

She wanted another evening at the crowded table before they went. She wanted to ask Mei what it felt like when the room grew quiet afterward and a person she loved began speaking again. She could ask over dinner, perhaps, while Amy disputed something with the wolf and gave them both enough time to answer.

Jinhsi took a sheet from the drawer and wrote an invitation. She gave the day and the hour, then added that she would like to eat at Panhua's, if there was room. She read it through and let Sanhua take it downstairs.

The Loong Scale lay in its open case.

She held it for a while. Clouds moved over the terrace, changing the light across its surface. Beyond the window, the city made room for carts and neighbors and a delivery arriving at the wrong gate. Somewhere along the lower road, a child was calling repeatedly for someone to come and see what she had found.

Jinhsi listened until an adult voice answered.

Then she laid the scale back in the case. The lid remained open beside Gao Shun's box.

At noon, Changli arrived carrying a bowl of fruit and the physician's packet. She stopped in the doorway long enough for Jinhsi to look up.

“The kitchen has sent lunch to the little room. I asked for enough for both of us.”

Jinhsi glanced at the papers on her desk. She placed the marker where she had stopped reading and set the book down.

“Bring the fruit with us,” she said. “I would like the largest one.”

Changli looked into the bowl. Her mouth began to curve.

Jinhsi reached for her sleeve, and they went to eat.

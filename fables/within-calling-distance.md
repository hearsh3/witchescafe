# Within Calling Distance

Beyond Xuanfang’s gates, the road entered a cloud which had forgotten the mountain. The driver lit the lamps. Their yellow circles rested on the mist without reaching the ground. Behind the transport, the stone leaves remained open under Jué’s key; a Ranger stood in their shadow, one arm raised, watching the rear wheels cross. He grew faint before he grew small.

Suisui drew Yangyang’s coat closer. Within its folds, the shells touched softly. The Wind lay beneath her scarf, making a cool place against the warmth of her throat.

“You can come out when the road improves,” she murmured. “I shall be quite unbearable about keeping you here until then.”

A strand of hair lifted behind her ear.

Across from them, Amy had one knee on the seat and a cable between her teeth. Mei took the cable away before answering the question Amy was trying to ask around it. Iuno steadied the map with her living hand. At the back, Cartethyia had put her terrarium between her boots; Ciaccona was explaining why holding it there did not make it less likely to break. Lupa listened with one ear turned toward the road. Chisa had stopped listening to them altogether.

“The wheels have gone over that joint twice,” she said. “The second time, I didn’t feel it beneath us.”

Mei looked toward the driver. Above the road, something moved within the white. Lupa was already standing when a metal arm came through the canopy.

It struck the forward axle. The transport slewed; the map rose; a lamp swung across Iuno’s face. For one clear instant everyone could see the Autopuppet walking beside them, its old crest polished by the weather, one hand sunk to the wrist in the machine it had been made to escort. Then the parapet gave way.

Mei caught Amy before Amy’s feet left the floor. She turned with her daughter against her chest, putting her own back toward the breaking timber. The canopy beam split lengthwise. A pale spar came through the space they had just occupied, and Mei’s hold tightened once, terribly.

Lupa’s rope ran taut between her wrist and Mei’s belt. She planted a foot against the rear bench. Ciaccona struck the floor beside her; Lupa caught her collar with the other hand. Through the torn canopy, a second weapon descended toward Suisui. The Wind left the scarf.

She met the blade with all the force she could gather in that narrow place. Its edge turned aside, shaved the coat’s outer fold, and cut the rope above Lupa’s wrist. The rear of the transport fell away. Chisa caught its iron rail as Lupa and Ciaccona went with it.

Cartethyia saw the driver disappear under the forward bench. She stepped toward him, grew too tall for the collapsing roof, and drove both shoulders through it. The front half plunged beneath her.

Iuno’s moonwheel opened across the space where the floor had been. She reached for Mei and caught a handful of torn canvas. A current struck her from the side; the wheel turned, carrying her and the Wind through a whitening cleft between two rocks. Suisui slid from a seat that was no longer attached to anything.

The Wind’s last push set her feet toward the bank. She landed hard, kept the coat beneath her arms, and rolled through wet grass. Something blue passed above her head. She heard Amy call for her mother. She heard Lupa answer from what seemed the same place. She rose toward both voices, and the road between them filled with pine trees.

Behind the gates, the Ranger lowered his arm. He could still hear the engine. Inside the fog, it had stopped. The Rangers entered after it. A minute later, they came out of the white behind their own sentry.

***

Mei came to rest against a stone sluice, half sitting, with Amy folded between her knees. For a moment Amy thought the timber lay across them. It had the innocent colour of newly opened wood. Rain touched its splintered end; farther down, where it entered Mei’s coat, the colour changed. Mei moved her hand over Amy’s head, finding the skull beneath the hair.

“Tell me where you’re hurt. I felt your shoulder hit something.”

Amy looked up.

“My shoulder can move. Your stomach has a piece of the roof through it.”

“I noticed that part.” Mei tried to shift and stopped with her mouth open. After a while she drew enough breath to continue. “I need you to get out from under my knees. Slowly, sweetheart. I seem to have become badly arranged furniture.”

Amy crawled clear. The spar had passed through Mei’s abdomen and caught against the broken sluice behind her. Each breath moved her along it by a little distance which Amy could not bear to watch.

She watched.

Mei put one hand around the wood. With the other she reached behind herself, feeling for the place it was trapped.

“Give me a moment. If I can free the back of it, I should be able to—”

Her voice went away. Amy caught her wrist before she tried again. Mei looked at the hand holding hers. Gold stirred beneath the torn cloth. Her body had already begun its patient work; it found the wood in its way and closed against it. The spar moved. Mei bent her head until her forehead touched Amy’s shoulder. The sound she made had no words in it.

Amy had heard great machines break. She had listened to a city crying through her headset. This small sound, made against her own collar, took the strength from her legs. She sat down before they failed.

“You told me it takes an hour now,” she said.

Mei breathed against her shoulder. “When there’s room for it to mend. I can’t promise an hour while this is still here.”

Amy looked at her Terminal. The screen had cracked across the time. She started the little counter in the corner anyway, because her hands needed something they knew how to do. Then she opened the pack channel.

“This is Amy. Ma is alive, and I’m with her. She has a serious wound. We’re beside a stone watercourse, below a broken retaining wall. I need someone who can help us move her.”

Her own voice answered from the speaker.

It reached the word *alive* and began again.

Amy changed channels. Somewhere in the mist, Mei laughed with her whole chest, as she had laughed ten minutes earlier over the cable between Amy’s teeth. Beside her, the real Mei could scarcely breathe. Amy turned the speaker down.

“I was trying to get it over with,” Mei said.

“I could see that. I was trying to think how to stop you without moving it.”

“I thought if I was quick, there would be less of it for you.” Her fingers closed weakly on Amy’s sleeve. “That was a foolish thought. I’m sorry.”

Amy looked at the wood. She had a knife, dressings, a broken receiver, the strength of her own hands. Somewhere above them, Nyx Ravenhollow lay in the transport’s carrying cradle. The transfer indicator still glimmered. Amy could enter five metres of white-and-gold armour; her body would fall still against the stones, and seven minutes would pass here before she could return from twenty-four seconds there. Mei followed her gaze.

“We don’t know where Nyx landed,” Amy said. “I might spend those minutes finding out that she’s facing a wall.”

“And I would rather you stayed where I can hear you.”

Amy’s chin began to shake. She resented it with a sudden, childish fury.

“Then you’re going to hear me crying. I can do the other things while I cry, but I don’t think I can stop.”

Mei’s hand moved from the sleeve to the back of her neck.

“Come close enough that you don’t have to try.”

The fog passed above the sluice. In its whiteness, a wheel continued to turn.

***

Iuno landed on a stair whose upper half had vanished. Her moonwheel struck the wall and rang. The Wind folded beneath her, took what she could of the fall, and broke into cold fragments over the stones. Iuno’s living hand scraped down the wall. Her crystal shoulder met the stair’s edge. For several breaths she remained where she had fallen, listening to something drip from the crystal.

*A splendid entrance,* the High Priestess observed. *We shall have to send someone ahead with flowers if we intend to make a second one.*

Iuno pressed her palm to the stair. It was wet, rough, and obligingly still.

“I can stand,” she told the air. “Give me a moment to discover whether I can do anything after that.”

The cold gathered by her knee. She rose with the moonwheel against the wall. Below, the stair descended into an abandoned dye yard. Long poles leaned over empty vats; the fog had hung itself there in the place of cloth. A red thread caught on one pole moved against the current. The Wind went toward it. Iuno followed two steps, then stopped.

“Is that the way Suisui fell?”

The thread snapped straight. Across the yard, a woman called softly for Yangyang to keep her head still while she plaited her hair. The Wind rushed forward. Iuno swung the moonwheel across the stair. Its light reached the yard floor and showed the stone rim of a vat where the voice seemed to be. Below the rim lay a depth the light could not finish measuring.

Cold struck Iuno’s wrist. For an instant it was a grip, furious at the obstacle.

“There is no floor beyond the third stone,” Iuno said. “You may be able to cross it. I cannot follow you there.”

The current tightened. Iuno kept her hand where it was.

“I don’t know whether that is your sister. I know where I’m standing. That is all I can give you at present.”

The voice began again with the same caught breath before *Yangyang*.

The Wind stayed over the empty vat until it had repeated three times. Then she returned to the stair, drawing the loose thread behind her. It had come from an old sleeve, bleached on one side by years of daylight. She let it fall. Iuno sat down on the lowest whole step. Above her marked shoulder, the new dressing was growing warm.

“I need your help with this fastening,” she said. “My hand won’t reach without pulling the wound open.”

The air was still.

Iuno waited. Somewhere behind the mist lay thirty years and a file with *Weather* written in its margin. She had carried the knowledge into rooms where people could answer her; here the woman she had wronged possessed no mouth with which to refuse a request.

She brought her hand back to her lap.

“I can brace the cloth against the wall if you would rather leave it.”

After a while, the loose end lifted. It passed through the buckle, slowly, and drew the dressing close. Iuno held the buckle steady. The cold lingered over the place where the blade had opened the crystal at Norfall. She could feel every small movement. Then it withdrew.

She said, “Thank you for helping me,” and let the sentence end there.

***

Lupa had six metres of rope and nobody at the other end. She pulled it in once, hand over hand. The cut was clean. A little piece of Mei’s belt had caught in the final knot.

Ciaccona sat beside the broken rear bench, holding her lute away from the wet ground. Chisa had one hand on the iron rail. The three of them had come down together on a terrace of dead mulberry trees; above the terrace, the transport’s rear wheels rested upside down against the sky. Lupa drew breath through her nose.

There was Mei’s blood on the wind. Amy’s hair. Tea from Iuno’s broken flask. Each scent came from three directions. She turned toward the strongest, took four strides, and found the bench in front of her again. Ciaccona looked up.

“You passed behind me that time. I watched you go the other way.”

Lupa’s tail had gone still. Chisa set the scissor-blade against the earth. A faint tremor travelled through it, then another, arriving too soon.

“The terrace is folded over something,” she said. “I can hear ground underneath us, but I haven’t found the edge.”

From beyond the trees came the sound of Lupa’s boots. Chisa turned toward it. The real Lupa stood beside her, motionless. For a little while Chisa said nothing. Her fingers went to the red thread at her wrist.

“I thought that was you,” she said at last. “I would have followed.”

Lupa took her hand and put it against her own sleeve.

“Then use this until we know what the ground is doing. You’ve found enough roads for us to let someone else take a turn.”

Ciaccona tried one note. It travelled through the mulberries. A second lute answered, then a third, each a little farther along the terrace. Beneath the third came a metal footstep. She damped the strings with her palm.

“That brought something.”

Lupa turned toward the noise. A small Autopuppet emerged between the trunks with a torn length of packing canvas caught in its weapon. She met it before it reached the bench. Her flame showed the old pruning scars on every tree at once. When the machine fell, the fire continued briefly along her spear. Two familiar shapes moved within it, kept company with her hand, and were gone.

Farther away, more feet began to strike the earth. Chisa listened with her eyes lowered. Every human face had always been beyond her; the world had given her other ways of knowing. Now it returned those ways too readily, footsteps without feet, breath without the body which should have needed the next one.

“I can’t tell you that the next voice is ours,” she said.

“Neither can I,” Ciaccona answered. “We can still make a place where somebody might find us.”

Lupa looked toward the scent of blood. Her mouth opened. Ciaccona waited for the objection.

“I don’t want to be sensible about this,” Lupa said. “I want a direction, and I want to get to them before anything else does.”

Ciaccona rested her good hand over the one holding the cut rope.

“I want Cartethyia to tell me she has broken something expensive. I’m finding all this silence very difficult to forgive.”

Lupa’s ears lowered. She looked at Ciaccona properly, and the bard let her see. They stood that way until Chisa found the edge beneath the mulberry roots.

***

Suisui had lost one shoe. The remaining shoe filled with water as she walked. She took it off at the first low wall and carried it by the heel. Yangyang’s coat stayed over her arm. She checked the shells after every fall, though they had not struck each other since the road broke. The blue bird waited on the wall.

It was smaller than the one in the old gate carvings, round through the breast, with a little mechanical stiffness when it folded its wings. A Rafter Kite. She had seen them above market roofs at home, going about errands too small to interest anyone until they failed to arrive.

“Have you seen the others?” she asked.

It tilted its head.

“There are several. One is considerably taller when she’s upset, and one will be making a great deal of noise about this.”

The bird hopped to the other end of the wall. Suisui stayed where she was. Beyond it, mist gathered over a flight of steps. She could hear a woman running up them, although the wet dust held no footprints.

She had tried going back. The bank on which she landed had become a stand of bamboo. She had tried following a voice. It had led her to a gate fastened from the side on which she stood. The bird opened its beak and gave a small, impatient note.

“I understand that you would like me to come,” she said. “You must allow me a little time to become foolish enough to follow another thing I don’t understand.”

She sat on the wall and turned the shoe upside down. Water ran out onto her bare foot. The bird watched closely. For reasons she could not have explained afterward, this steadied her. It seemed a thoroughly ordinary interest. She tipped the shoe a second time, found a pebble inside, and held it out. The bird declined it.

“Dumpling, then,” she said. “You look like one, and at least I shall have something to call if you leave me behind.”

Dumpling went three steps ahead and stopped. Suisui put on the wet shoe. She would have liked to cry while someone told her which road to take. There was nobody beside the wall, and the bird had already waited longer than she expected. She rose.

At the foot of the steps she drew a loose thread from her own sleeve and tied it around a twig. The knot was clumsy. She left the ends long enough to see.

“I’m going this way,” she told the empty place beneath her chin. “If you come after me, I shall try to leave you something better than my voice.”

Above the steps, a pagoda roof floated on the white.

***

Cartethyia had been alone long enough for the dark to become familiar before she understood where she was.

The transport’s front had fallen through the roof of a buried storehouse. Broken beams lay across its engine housing. Below them, the driver’s legs were trapped beneath the seat; his head had fallen against the little bag he had hung there before departure. His lunch had come out of it. Rice lay among the glass.

She had grown into Fleurdelys while falling. Now her back filled the space between the engine and a ceiling beam. One knee rested in spilled grain. The other would not fit beneath her. She could lift the engine. When she did, the beam above it tilted, taking the remaining roof with it. She could hold the roof and the engine where they were. She could not reach the driver from that position.

She considered becoming smaller. The weight shifted as soon as she began, and the driver’s breath caught beneath the seat. She became tall again. Dust drifted down her arms. The man breathed. Far away, someone was moving a cart along a road which might have ceased to exist before she was born.

“I’m here,” she told him. “I’m going to need some help getting you out, but you should know someone is here.”

He did not wake. The terrarium had broken against her pack. She could feel wet soil down the back of her neck. A camellia leaf rested near the man’s cheek, bright green upon the grey dust. For a moment the sight was so miserable that she almost laughed. Then she began calling.

Her first call came in Fleurdelys’s voice, deep enough to stir the grain at her knee. It returned from the broken rafters with the solemnity of a congregation. Nothing answered from beyond them. She tried again, naming her friends. The engine settled. Her arms trembled with the adjustment. Somewhere inside the housing, a little wheel turned against a bent tooth and stopped.

She knew how to live in a place without answers. The knowledge came readily: count what can be counted; give names to what the dark would make alike; decide which small work will occupy the hands until morning. Her hands were full now. She counted the driver’s breaths. At forty-one she lost her place.

There had been forty-one dolls. She had known the feel of every one in the dark. Several still needed better clothes when she came out. She began counting again and found that she hated doing it.

The hatred was fresh. She had been shopping yesterday. Ciaccona had wanted another hour in bed. There was a leaf growing on a plant someone had expected her to bring home. She had no wish to become good at this place.

“I want to get out,” she said.

The driver breathed against the rice scattered by his mouth. Cartethyia listened for him, gathered the air she could spare, and called again. This time she used none of the Maiden’s gravity.

“Cia, I’m under the front of the transport! There’s a man with me, and I can’t let go long enough to move him. Please come and find us. I’m frightened of how quiet it is down here.”

The words struck the beam above her face. She left them as they were.

***

Amy found the outer medical pouch caught on a thorn bush. It was three paces from Mei. She had to turn her head after the second to make certain the distance still contained her mother. Mei lifted her fingers without being asked.

On the way back, Amy gathered a strip of fallen canvas and put it beneath Mei’s shoulder. There were small comforts available. She attended to each with a concentration which had once held an auditorium quiet. The counter showed seventeen minutes. Mei had been looking at her own hands. When Amy knelt beside her, she held one out.

“Will you help me with the spar? I can tell what my body is doing, but I can’t manage both ends of this. I’ve tried twice, and I’m making it worse.”

Amy took the hand.

“I don’t think I can be gentle enough.”

“I don’t think there is a gentle enough.” Mei swallowed. “We can stop when we need to. You can tell me when you need to.”

They worked within the little shelter of the sluice. Mei knew wounds; Amy knew how to listen when terror made every instruction seem to arrive from a great distance. Still, the broken wood caught. Still, Mei’s body tried to mend before they were finished. Once Amy had to put her face against her own forearm and wait until the stones stopped tilting. Mei waited with her.

The spar came free whole at last. Amy laid it well beyond her mother’s reach, though there was no reason Mei should want it. She covered the wound. Gold moved under her hands, gathered, and thinned. Mei’s head slipped against the stone.

“Ma, keep talking to me. Tell me if this is what usually happens.”

Mei’s eyes opened, unfocused.

“There isn’t a usual enough for me to promise you. I’m here.”

Amy felt for the pulse in her wrist. It was there, hurried and slight. She kept her fingers on it until Mei moved her hand.

“Your knees are in the water,” Mei said.

“I’ve noticed several things about the accommodation.”

Mei tried to laugh and could not. Amy shifted onto the canvas without taking her hands away. Above the sluice, the mist darkened around a moving shape. Metal scraped stone.

Amy lowered herself over Mei before she had decided to move. One arm lay along the dressing; the other found the knife at her belt. The Autopuppet’s shadow passed over the channel. Its head turned toward the sound of the old engine still travelling through the fog, and it went after that. They listened until its footsteps stopped returning.

Mei’s gaze had cleared. She was looking at the arm Amy had laid across her.

“I wanted to get between you and it,” she said.

“You aren’t in a very convenient position for that.”

“I know. I’m finding it difficult.”

Amy put the knife down within reach. Her hand shook afterward, when it no longer had a job.

“I spent ten years being the person who stayed,” she said. “When I saw you try to pull that thing out by yourself, I thought you were going somewhere I couldn’t follow again. You were sitting right in front of me, and I was already trying to work out what I would say when the others asked where you’d gone.”

Mei’s mouth trembled. For a moment Amy thought she was about to make another promise.

Instead, she said, “I’m afraid too. Will you put my head somewhere that isn’t this stone?”

Amy made room in her lap. It took them time. Mei needed to pause twice; Amy had to move the canvas with her heel. When they were settled, Mei’s hair lay across the wrist where Amy was still counting her pulse. The skin around the wound had begun to close. Neither of them looked at the counter.

“Do you want me to sing?” Amy asked.

Mei watched the broken line of sky above the channel.

“I would like to hear you complain about something.”

Amy bent over her. A wet, astonished laugh escaped before she could help it.

“I have an extensive list. You are in an extremely poor position to interrupt.”

“That sounds like what I need.”

Amy began with the transport’s suspension. It was a subject on which she had already possessed strong opinions, and the morning had supplied further evidence.

***

The edge Chisa found led beneath the mulberry terrace to an old courier house. Its threshold was worn low in the middle. A carved horse above the door had lost both ears; under the eaves, the brackets for three signal lanterns remained. Lupa lifted a fallen shutter away from the entrance. Inside, a table stood upon three legs and a stack of roof tiles. Ciaccona put her lute on it.

She played a measure softly. The room gave it back with the dull, close voice of wet plaster. Beyond the threshold, the same measure returned bright and thin, wearing the sound of a larger hall. Chisa crouched at the door.

“Keep playing in here. I need to hear where the room ends.”

Ciaccona laid a small Realm along the inside of the walls. Nothing appeared that had not been there already. The table steadied; a loose strip of paper ceased lifting beneath a wind which belonged to yesterday. She held to the room’s unlovely acoustics, the shortened note beside the damp corner, the faint rattle of the broken shutter.

Chisa set one blade across the threshold. She listened through two measures, found the place where the larger hall pressed against the courier house, and cut. Outside, the mulberries shifted several feet to the left. Lupa looked from the trees to the doorway. She fetched the broken shutter, wedged it beneath the hanging lintel, and tied the cut rope around the soundest pillar.

“If anyone reaches this place, they should have something they can put a hand on.”

The other end went through Chisa’s belt. Lupa held the middle as they began examining the ground outside. Six metres allowed them the length of the yard. At its far edge, a stone drain passed under the wall. Chisa put her hand against it. Someone was speaking through the stone. She could hear the rhythm without the words: a long complaint, interrupted by a much quieter answer.

Then a shout came from beneath the opposite bank. Lupa turned at once. Ciaccona’s hand stopped above the strings.

*Please come and find us.*

For several breaths the three women remained exactly where they were. The fog had used their own voices already. The shout did not repeat. Ciaccona drew in a breath which hurt visibly.

“Cartethyia, if you can hear me, tell me what you’re holding.”

Silence ran along the bank.

Then, from beneath it: “Most of the front of the transport, and something which I think used to be a ceiling. I’d appreciate another opinion about the ceiling.”

Lupa was moving before the sentence ended. Chisa caught her sleeve.

“Let me find where the ground meets that room. You can hold on to me while I do it.”

They went together. Ciaccona carried the room’s low measure as far as the doorway and let it rest there, small enough to keep without both hands. The bank opened beneath Chisa’s blade. A slit of darkness appeared, full of dust and the smell of hot metal. Fleurdelys looked up from under the beam.

Her face was filthy. Blood marked one knee. A green leaf had stuck to her cheek, and her eyes filled when she saw the light.

“Please take him first,” she said. “His feet are underneath the seat. I’ve kept the space open, but I couldn’t reach any farther.”

Lupa knelt beside the opening. “I’m coming down to him. Keep telling us when something shifts.”

Ciaccona held out her hand into the dark. Cartethyia could not free either of hers to take it. The bard stayed with her arm extended anyway, where Cartethyia could see it. Chisa worked at the broken seat while Lupa reached the driver. A seam parted; a piece of iron moved; the man cried out and opened his eyes. Ciaccona spoke to him until Lupa could lift him clear.

Only then did she look back beneath the roof.

“You can come now. I’ve been holding this hand out for you, and I’m beginning to feel theatrically excessive.”

“It’s a very good hand,” Cartethyia said. Her voice shook. “Please leave it there.”

Chisa found a way to brace the beam long enough. Fleurdelys lowered the engine the little distance it could bear, became Cartethyia, and crawled through the opening on her elbows. She reached Ciaccona with both hands at once. Behind her, the grain store fell in. Dust blew over the terrace and whitened the mulberry bark.

Cartethyia held on through the noise. Her shoulders shook so hard that Ciaccona put the lute aside and sat down with her in the mud.

“I kept thinking how little time it had been,” Cartethyia said. “I knew you couldn’t possibly have found us yet. Knowing didn’t help very much.”

Ciaccona smoothed the dirty hair back from her brow.

“We’ve found you now. You can take some time being angry that we weren’t quicker.”

Cartethyia gave a damp laugh against her neck.

“I’m going to need you to describe my heroism more favourably than this.”

“I shall mention the ceiling. It will be practically an architectural ballad.”

Lupa was checking the driver’s breathing. From inside Cartethyia’s torn pack, Chisa lifted a clot of soil with the camellia cutting still rooted in it. One leaf was gone. She wrapped the roots in the damp lining of a supply pouch and put the bundle where Cartethyia would see it when she lifted her head.

***

Iuno and the Wind found Suisui’s thread at the foot of the pagoda steps.

They had passed through the dye yard by a route neither could have travelled alone. Iuno’s light showed the stone beneath the old images; the Wind moved along it until she found air coming from an actual opening. At each turning they waited for one another. Once the moonwheel carried Iuno upward and returned her to the step she had left. She abandoned the attempt, leaned against the wall, and let the Wind see how badly her legs were shaking.

The thread was blue. It had been tied around a twig with too many turns. The Wind loosened one end and knew the impatient little tug with which Suisui had tightened it. Above them, the steps divided. One flight ended at a closed wall. The other entered a courtyard where a bead of rain hung beneath a leaf without falling.

Iuno’s moonwheel stopped at its edge. The light bent back upon itself. Beyond it stood another door, too far away for the size of the courtyard. The Wind went forward, returned to Iuno’s wrist, and went forward again.

“You think she’s in there,” Iuno said.

Cold gathered against the opening. Iuno set the moonwheel upright on the stair. Its rim lit the blue thread, the wet leaf, the place where she could safely sit.

“Then go and look for her. I’ll stay beside this light. If I have to move, I’ll mark the way on the stone.”

The Wind returned. She moved up Iuno’s arm, paused over the dressed crystal, and pressed into the hollow beneath her living shoulder. Iuno had felt that small weight settle against other people; she had watched them arrange their clothes around it. For a moment she did not move.

*Careful, darling,* said the High Priestess, very softly. *We know what we would like this to mean.*

Iuno lifted the edge of her collar to shelter the cold.

“You can rest here before you go,” she said. “I shan’t mistake that for an answer to anything else.”

The Wind remained for several breaths. She could feel the unevenness in Iuno’s chest. A living body, trying to be quiet under pain. The body belonged to the woman who had read her wandering and closed the file. It also belonged to someone who had stopped at every turning, had told her where the ground ended, had waited while she learned that a voice could resemble her sister’s without being her sister.

The anger had a place in her still. So did the brief rest beneath the collar. She rose when she could travel again. At the courtyard’s edge, she took the loose end of Suisui’s thread and laid it across Iuno’s knee. Then she passed the hanging drop. Behind her, the stair grew distant without moving.

Ahead, a bird called from somewhere within the dark. She followed as far as the closed door. There was no opening at its foot. She drew a loose scrap of cloth over the stone, let it scrape, then lifted a little grit and tapped it against the wood. The bird answered. She tried again.

***

At the courier house, the driver gave his name as Shen Rong and asked after the people in the back. Lupa told him whom they had found. She left the other names as they were, still being sought. He listened with his head on a folded coat, then tried to rise. Cartethyia put a hand on his shoulder.

“You can help from there. We heard someone through the drain. Where does it go?”

He swallowed. His gaze moved toward the wall as though a map might have been painted upon it.

“There’s a settling sluice behind the lower storehouses. The water runs under this yard before it reaches the dye vats.” He closed his eyes for a moment. “My daughter used to put boats in it. They never came out where she expected.”

Chisa knelt beside the channel again. Ciaccona played the three notes Amy had once left in her keeping, very softly, into the dark mouth of the drain. For a while they heard only the water. Then Amy sang them back, and went on past the place where the old song ended. Ciaccona bent over the opening until her hair touched the stones.

“We’re in the courier house. Chisa is finding a way along the wall to you. Keep talking if you can; we need to hear where you are.”

The answer came through stone, thin enough to hurt.

“Ma’s awake. We got the wood out, but she needs carrying. I’ve kept her warm as well as I can.”

Lupa put down the flask she had been holding. She did it carefully, with her whole hand around it until it touched the table. Cartethyia stood.

“I can carry her.”

Ciaccona looked at the trembling in her legs.

“Come with us. We’ll decide how much each of us carries when we get there.”

Chisa followed the water with her palm against the wall. At the first bend the passage attempted to become a stair. She stopped, listened back toward the room Ciaccona was holding, and found the cut where the two places had been laid together. It took three such cuts to reach the sluice.

Amy was still talking when Lupa came through. She had reached the matter of Mei’s hot chocolate and was defending it against criticism which her mother was too weak to offer. Lupa saw the discarded spar. She went past it and knelt in the water.

Mei raised her hand. The she-wolf took it, placed it against her own cheek, and held it there for the length of one breath. Her ears were laid flat.

“I’m going to lift you,” she said. “Amy can keep hold of you while I get my arm under. Tell me where I can put it.”

Mei looked at the rope tied around her wrist. She touched the severed end with a finger, then showed Lupa where the dressing lay. They brought her out together. Lupa took most of the weight; Cartethyia steadied her legs; Amy walked backward along the wall until Ciaccona put a hand at her waist and guided her around the first turning. Chisa kept the return passage open one short reach at a time.

Halfway back, Mei began to apologize for being heavy. Lupa adjusted her grip.

“I spent the last hour wanting this exact problem. Give me a little time to enjoy having it.”

Mei put her face against Lupa’s shoulder. For the rest of the passage she was quiet, except when someone asked how she was hurting.

***

The drop let go of the leaf. Before it struck the next step, the Wind came back. Iuno had scarcely had time to close her hand around the blue thread. Yet the cold arriving against her wrist carried the faint scent of extinguished incense, and it shook as though it had travelled all night.

Suisui followed with Yangyang’s coat over one arm and a small brightness held in both hands. Dumpling perched on her shoulder. Her face had changed in the way a room changes after somebody has wept in it, though all the furniture remains where it stood. Iuno rose too quickly. The wound in her shoulder caught; the Wind braced beneath her elbow.

“Has she hurt you?” Iuno asked. “Tell me what happened before we try to go any farther.”

Suisui looked down at her own breast, then touched it with two fingers.

“I thought she had. There is a great deal to tell you.” She drew breath and looked at Iuno’s dressing. “You’re bleeding.”

“The bleeding has slowed. I was waiting for your sister.”

The Wind moved from Iuno’s elbow into Suisui’s scarf. One fold rose; Suisui bent toward it without taking her eyes from the woman before her.

“She came back for you.”

Iuno looked at the thread in her hand.

“She did. I told her where I would wait.”

From below the stair came a tremendous crash, then Lupa’s voice objecting to a piece of masonry in terms too particular to have belonged to the old war. The Wind lifted from the scarf. She circled once over Iuno’s light, gathering her bearings, then went down toward the sound.

Suisui and Iuno followed. At the first uncertain step, each offered the other an elbow. They looked at the awkward arrangement and nearly laughed. They managed it by going slowly.

***

By the time they reached the courier house, Mei’s wound had closed. Her ruined coat lay beneath the table. Amy had wrapped her in a blanket and was sitting close enough that their knees touched. On the cracked Terminal, the counter read sixty-three minutes. It continued to count; nobody had thought to stop it. Iuno saw the blood first. She stopped inside the door.

Mei turned toward her. Relief broke over her face so openly that Iuno had to look down for a moment before she could cross the room.

“Tell me where you were hurt before I touch you.”

Mei moved the blanket aside. The skin beneath the dressing was whole, flushed with its newness. Iuno’s living hand hovered above it and came to rest on the uninjured part of her waist.

“Amy helped me,” Mei said. “I couldn’t manage it by myself.”

Amy had been watching Iuno’s hand. She looked up then, and the face she had held together through the sluice and the carrying came apart. Iuno sat on the edge of the bench. She opened her living arm. Amy came into it with such force that Mei reached to steady them both.

For a while Iuno could say nothing except that she was there. She said it in complete, ordinary sentences, as often as the girl against her shoulder needed to hear it.

Suisui stopped beside the table. Her gaze rested on the bloodied blanket, the splints around Shen Rong’s leg, Cartethyia sitting with a little bundle of wet earth in her lap. Chisa’s sleeve had torn at the collar. Ciaccona kept flexing the hand with which she had held the room. She had taken their strength into Hsin’s chamber as though she could produce it from her bag when asked.

The Prism grew warm between her fingers. Mei looked up from Amy’s hair.

“Come and sit with us. You’ve lost a shoe.”

Suisui sat. Only then did she realize how badly her foot hurt. She put the Prism on the table, where everyone could see it.

“Hsin has given me a way to call Yangyang,” she said. “If she can answer, it may show us a passage. I need to understand what each of you can still do before I ask you to take it.”

The Wind settled along the coat in her lap. Under the cloth, the shells made their small sound. Lupa brought a stool closer with her foot.

“You can start by telling us what you need. We’ll answer for ourselves.”

***

They could hear Autopuppets moving beyond the mulberry terrace. Lupa took the doorway with Chisa beside her. They had already seen how the machines followed sounds which had outlived their makers. Ciaccona set a little refrain walking through the empty dye yard; the nearest footsteps turned after it. She stayed within the courier house, listening for the moment she would have to let it go.

Cartethyia put the camellia on a high shelf and went to examine the transport’s fallen carrying cradle. Nyx’s signal had steadied as Chisa cleared the nearest folds. If they needed the armour, Amy could enter it with people beside her body now.

Shen Rong watched the door from his bedding. When the metal footsteps passed beneath the yard, he told Lupa which paving stones covered an old pit. She thanked him, badly, and went to inspect it. Mei tried standing once. Her knees folded before she was fully upright. She sat down, waited for the dizziness to pass, and told Suisui she would need carrying farther if the road opened soon.

Nobody mistook the healed skin for an answer to that. Iuno drew the lamp closer to the shells. One contained the sea Yangyang had carried since her youth; the other held the distance crossed by the woman now sheltering against her sister. The Prism stood between them. Its light divided and returned, pale within the curves their mother had smoothed.

Suisui followed the instruction Hsin had given her. She went slowly, stopping when the warmth in the Prism sharpened. At first they heard many roads. A cart rattled across a bridge. Someone called for a gate to be opened. A woman laughed from a courtyard, and Suisui’s hands tightened before she could prevent it. Iuno put her pencil across the corner of the cloth before it slipped from the table.

“Tell her where you are now,” she said.

Suisui looked at the crooked table, the ruined coat beneath it, the women waiting within the lamp’s small circle.

“Yangyang, I’m in an old courier house beyond the outer gates. The others are here with me. I have your coat, and shijie is beside my hand.”

The roads continued to pass.

“I meant to visit you in Jinzhou. I arrived rather late.” Suisui swallowed. “I would like you to know that I’m trying again.”

The light faltered. Far away, a door opened and closed. Amy moved her receiver closer to the Prism, listening to the faint disturbance without trying to make it louder. Ciaccona let the refrain in the dye yard go. The room waited in its own uneven silence. Then someone coughed. The Wind rose so quickly that the lamp flattened toward the wall. The cough ended. A breath followed it, painfully drawn.

“Suisui, can you hear me? I thought I heard you say you had my coat.”

Suisui pressed both hands against the table.

“I have it here. Baizhi told me to bring it.”

Another breath. The voice was hoarse, much weaker than the one Suisui had imagined hearing when she set out from home.

“Is Chixia alive?”

Mei leaned toward the light.

“She was alive when we left Huaxu. Baizhi was sitting beside her. The Wind stayed until she could give her into Baizhi’s hands.”

At the far end, something was set down too quickly. They heard a woman tell Yangyang to lean back, that she would hold the cup. For a while there was no reply. Suisui stared into the light. The Wind pressed against her wrist, cold and shaking. Neither could cross the distance by wanting. Then Yangyang spoke again.

“Please tell shijie that I heard. I’ve been thinking about the gorge.” Her breath caught. “I have so much to tell her.”

The older shell turned beneath the lamp. Suisui bent over it, trying to find her voice. Across the table, Amy put a hand over the receiver so the far room would not hear the sob she could not quite swallow. Mei drew the hand away gently.

“She can hear us all from there, sweetheart. Let her hear you.”

Amy bowed her head. The little sound went through. Yangyang said her name, and then Mei’s. Lupa came in from the doorway when she heard them. Cartethyia stopped beneath the shelf with both hands still raised toward her plant. For a few breaths nobody attended properly to the thing she had been doing. The Prism cast a narrow line across the floor.

It went under the door and ended before the far wall of the yard. Beyond it lay the fog, the broken roads, the machines still walking their ruined patrols. Yangyang had answered; reaching her remained work they had yet to do.

“Can you tell me what is near the house?” she asked. “Liangyu knows the old courier roads. She’s coming to sit beside me.”

Suisui looked toward the door. Rain had begun to touch the threshold. Chisa put her palm against the first lit stone and waited before placing a foot upon it.

“There are mulberries above us,” Suisui said. “A dye yard below. Someone has carved a horse over the lintel, though it has had an unfortunate time.”

From far away came the scrape of a second chair being brought to the table. Suisui settled the coat across her knees. She found the torn cuff with her thumb and held it, listening while her sister began to describe the road.

Simulacrum of the Heart 2

Past the crossing, the red-lacquered pillars of Huizhen Plaza ran on without an end.

First came another teahouse with its bamboo blinds rolled halfway to the lintel and dry osmanthus twigs sitting in a stoneware jar on the counter; then a cobbler’s open front where forty pairs of black cloth-soled shoes were lined up toe-to-toe along the bench, every sole stitched with the same spotless white hemp; then a cramped noodle stall with its low pine stools turned upside down on the trestles and a wooden ladle resting dry across an empty iron cauldron. Along the shallow drainage groove at the edge of the cobbles, the shed flakes of black vine-scale went on ahead of their boots in a dull, greasy ribbon, smelling faintly of cellar-rot and bruised winter pear whenever the draft stirred.

Mei walked on the left side of the gutter, keeping her shoulder tucked inside the round shadow of Suoming’s oil-paper umbrella.

Halfway past the cobbler’s rack, her jaw gave a sudden, traitorous creak.

Mei clapped the back of her wrist over her mouth, tried to swallow it down through her nose, and failed completely. The yawn cracked her mouth wide open until her hinges popped and water sprang into the corners of her golden eyes. She blinked the blur away, took two steps past a pair of hanging brass scales, and yawned a second time, longer and deeper than the first, her shoulders hitching up toward her ears before dropping heavy as wet wool.

Beside her, Yangyang’s right boot caught on the lip of a flagstone that was completely flat.

It wasn’t a stumble any passerby would have noticed—only a dry scuff of leather on dressed granite and a quick half-step to recover—but her right hand left her sword-belt at once and pressed flat against the side of her tunic where the linen strapping had come off that morning.

Ahead of them, the iron ferrule of Nine Shadows went *click*.

Three steps.

*Click.*

Earlier, on the loops through the gold trees below the ridge, the steel tip had rung out on every second pace, crisp as a watchman’s clapper, and the brown rim of the canopy had stayed half a stride in front of Suoming’s nose so the stone would settle before her toe reached it. Now the bamboo shaft rested low in the crook of her collarbone, the white linen bandage Yangyang had tied around her right forearm showing stiff and rust-brown from elbow to wrist where she held the wood against her plum coat. The round shadow on the cobbles trailed behind her heels instead of leading them.

Mei dug into her jacket pocket with her left hand and pulled out her Terminal.

Behind the spiderweb crack across the top corner of the glass, the little white numerals in the status bar read *11:42*.

It had been *11:42* when she took the picture of Yangyang blinking in front of the fan stall. It had been *11:42* when they sat on the stone bench by the headless Autopuppet to bind Suoming’s arm. Above the green-tiled eaves at the end of the arcade, the white glare in the western sky sat nailed to the exact same hand’s-breadth above the ridges where it had hung when they climbed the brass stair, pouring hard, shadowless noon straight down into the street.

Under Mei’s grey cotton shirt, the three-inch ridge of new skin across her left abdomen felt tight and hot, like a boot-strap pulled two holes too short.

Mei slid the Terminal back into her pocket and planted her heels on the cobbles.

“Hey, Suoming,” Mei called out.

Suoming took one more step before stopping. She didn’t turn all the way around; she only angled her head under the brown paper ribs so one pale eye showed past her coiled red hair.

“Wait up a second,” Mei said, putting on a bright, breezy grin and pointing her thumb at a carved wooden hitching-post beside a closed oil-shop. “Look at that post. That is a prime sitting post. What do you say we call it a day right here and camp out until the sun—well, until whatever passes for tomorrow morning gets here?”

Suoming looked from the hitching-post to the black flakes of vine-scale leading past the oil-shop’s threshold.

“The rot’s still moving,” Suoming said in her flat voice. “If we sit, it gets deeper into the wood.”

“It’s been moving all day, and right now it’s moving faster than my knees,” Mei said cheerfully, resting her palm against her side and leaning her hip against the hitching-post to prove her point. “Besides, what’s it gonna do if we catch it right now? Watch the three of us fall asleep on its shoes?”

Suoming’s mouth didn’t move. She shifted the bamboo shaft a fraction of an inch higher on her shoulder, and as she drew breath to answer, her chest gave that slow, dry catch low on the left side—the scrape of an iron bar against a worn staple.

The grin went off Mei’s face.

She looked straight at the small woman’s back: the faded plum wool pulled tight across narrow shoulders that hadn’t dropped an inch since the Sanctum floor gave way, the dark blood dried into the cuff below Yangyang’s bandage, and the angle of the brown umbrella still tipped backward so every drop of the hard white glare hit Suoming’s own boots while Mei and Yangyang stood in the shade.

Mei took her hip off the hitching-post and stood flat on both feet.

“We’re stopping,” Mei said, without any bounce in her voice at all. “My side’s pulling, Yangyang’s ribs are sore, and your arm’s bled through the wrap. If we walk another mile down this street, I’m gonna end up sitting in the gutter anyway, and you’re gonna have to drag me.”

Suoming stood still in the middle of the swept stones.

Beside Mei, Yangyang let her hand drop from her ribs. Her face was pale under the brim of her hat, and the white tips of her hair brushed her collar as she looked at Suoming.

“She’s right, Elder,” Yangyang said plainly. “My legs have another ten minutes in them. After that I’m no good to anyone with a blade.”

For three breaths only the unlit paper lanterns stirred overhead, their silk tassels brushing dryly against the cords.

Suoming looked at Mei’s boots, then at Yangyang’s chin. Then she turned her head and looked slowly up and down the length of the arcade.

Directly opposite the cobbler’s bench stood an open noodle stall with three-sided canvas screens; ten paces further down, on the shady side of the street, stood a two-storey timber inn with dark cedar shutters, a stone threshold swept clean of vine-scale, and a painted board hanging from an iron crane that read *THE THIRD FERRY REST* below an unlit red globe.

“Not by the oil-shop,” Suoming said. “The drain’s right under the wall.”

She turned on her iron-plated heels, walked ten paces down the flags to the inn’s front step, and pushed the unlocked double doors open with the ferrule of her umbrella.

“In here,” Suoming said over her shoulder, as if she had spent the last quarter-mile looking for the signboard. “And shut the door behind you. The glare’s coming in.”

Mei blinked at the open doorway. Across from her, the corner of Yangyang’s mouth twitched once before she ducked her head and followed.

Inside, the common room was cool and smelled of cedar shavings and dry straw. Four square pine tables sat on the swept flagstones with their benches tucked neatly beneath the aprons. There was no dust on the counter, no abacus by the ledger-stand, and no innkeeper behind the screen. When Mei walked through the open arch at the back of the hall, she found a brick kitchen with a wide iron cauldron set into the masonry. Beneath the grate, shaved pine curls and dry split birch were already laid in a neat criss-cross waiting for a flint.

On the scrubbed butcher’s block sat a covered stoneware crock two-thirds full of short-grain white rice, a willow basket of crisp green mustard-tops with the root-earth washed off, a porcelain bowl holding six brown eggs, and a squat brown-glazed jar sealed with a salted cabbage leaf and tied with hemp string.

Mei rested both hands on the edge of the butcher’s block and let out a long breath that turned into a third yawn halfway through.

“Okay,” Mei mumbled around her knuckles. “Nobody’s home, and the pantry’s full. I’m calling that an invitation.”

High above the terraced orchards, the chisels of Mengxuan Vale went on ringing against white stone.

In the center of the broad cedar square, the great brass astrolabe turned on its pivot with a slow, heavy *clank—pause—clank*, like a waterwheel pushing through thick winter slush. Around the steps of the pavilion, seven or eight Celestials in ink-stained cuffs stood in a knot with their Terminals held flat on their palms, arguing in rapid, even voices about the load on the western sluice-register while a mason on a low scaffold tapped a wooden mallet steadily against an unlaid ridge-tile: *tock, tock, tock*.

Nobody looked up at the copper-white sky. Nobody glanced toward the kitchen lane or rubbed their eyes.

At the foot of the white-tiled gatehouse steps, Qingxiao stopped walking.

Her left sleeve was still creased around the forearm where Ruoyun had hung onto the silk during the drop from Yuan Fortress. She did not smooth it out. While Mingxiao stood ten paces away explaining the southern array’s fault-lines to a pair of scribes, Qingxiao lowered herself onto the bottom cedar riser, rested both wrists across her knees, and sat still.

A breath later, her chin dipped half an inch, and she yawned.

She did not raise her moon-white cuff to hide it. Her mouth simply opened, her chest lifted under the worn red cord of jade beads at her throat, and when she blinked afterward, her pale blue eyes stayed half-shut for two beats against the shadowless glare off the white tiles.

Three steps away, Ruoyun stopped mid-sentence in the middle of berating a junior clerk about the mortar-mix.

Her head whipped around so fast her small golden horns glinted in the light. Through the round brass rims of her spectacles, her bright blue eyes fixed on Qingxiao sitting hunched on the bottom step like a wayfarer waiting for a mule-cart, and the blood drained right out of Ruoyun’s sharp chin, leaving two hard spots of red high on her cheekbones.

“Mingxiao!” Ruoyun barked.

Mingxiao broke off talking to the scribes and turned, her hand still on her carpenter’s square. “Yes, Ruoyun?”

“Where is Chen’s nephew?”

“In the third west room, I think, checking the—”

“Get him out of it,” Ruoyun snapped, marching straight across the cedar planks with her pleated skirt swinging sharp around her ankles. “That’s my room, that’s my cot, and I didn’t sign the lease over to a boy who can’t even bevel a mortise without splitting the pine! Tell him to take his blankets down to the drafting hall before I throw his spare shoes into the conduit!”

“Ruoyun, we don’t sleep,” Mingxiao said, blinking at her in bewilderment. “He only put his box of plumb-bobs on the mattress because the shelf was full—”

“Then his plumb-bobs can sleep on the floor!” Ruoyun snarled, grabbing Mingxiao by the ink-stained elbow and spinning her toward the side alley where the old brick cookhouse sat with its chimney cold and dry. “And tell whoever’s holding the storehouse key to open the millet bin and put a spark to the third hearth! Now! Do I have to speak in four-character verse before anyone in this village moves their feet?!”

Within ten minutes, a thin, bewildered plume of blue woodsmoke was wavering up out of the cookhouse flue for the first time in a generation, and three junior Celestials were standing outside in the lane holding a crate of brass plumb-bobs while Ruoyun slammed the wooden shutters of the third west room shut from the inside—*bang, bang, bang*—cutting the hard copper noon down to a murky twilight.

Out on the stone porch beside the square, Qingxiao sat at a low bench while Ruoyun marched back up the steps carrying a chipped earthenware bowl in both hands, her ruffled white cuffs pushed high up her forearms.

She set the bowl down on the stone in front of Qingxiao with a hard *clack*.

It was plain yellow millet boiled in water with two shreds of dried turnip floating on top. Because nobody in the Vale had watched a pot since the Fortress war, the bottom of the pan had caught on the iron; the steam rising from the gruel smelled distinctly of scorched grain and wood-ash.

“Eat that,” Ruoyun ordered, planting both hands on her hips and glaring down over her spectacles.

Qingxiao looked down at the grey-brown gruel, then picked up the wooden spoon. She blew on the first spoonful—her own breath steaming faint and warm against the hot water—and put it in her mouth.

“It’s burned,” Qingxiao said mildly.

“Then chew the burned bits faster! The idiot boy at the hearth tried to fan the coals with a ledger-board until I took the poker to him. Eat the whole bowl, A-Qing, or I’ll have them boil a second pot and make you drink the water.”

Qingxiao ate in silence, lifting the wooden spoon at an unhurried, steady pace until the bottom of the earthenware bowl scraped clean.

When she set the spoon down, she did not ask Ruoyun what Mingxiao had said about the upper courts, and she did not look west toward the huge, still shadow with its arms folded across the valley. Instead, as she rose from the bench to walk toward the shuttered door of the third west room, her gaze fell on the corner table under the pavilion eaves.

Centered on the stone sat a four-legged kaya-wood weiqi board.

There was no dust on its grid. Spread across the nineteen lines were nine black handicap stones on the star-points, a tenth on the third-line shoulder, and two white clam-shell discs—the exact position she had left on the gatehouse embrasure at Xuanfang Hold before the thunderclap broke the night.

Qingxiao stopped beside the table.

Ruoyun came up behind her shoulder, took one look at the board, and her mouth went thin and ugly.

Qingxiao lifted the wooden lid off the white bowl. She took a single clam-shell stone loosely between her index and middle fingers, held it for a breath over the fourth line beside her unanswered stone, and set it down with a small, dry *click*.

For five breaths she stood watching the grid.

No stone answered from the black bowl. Across the square, the great brass astrolabe gave another slow, dragging *clank*, and the mason’s mallet went *tock, tock* on the roof.

Qingxiao put the lid back on the white bowl, walked into the dark west room, took off her shoes by the footboard, and lay down on her side on the narrow hemp cot with her moon-white sleeve tucked under her cheek.

Within three minutes her breathing slowed, deepening into a long, even draw that lifted the indigo oilcloth over her shoulder.

Ruoyun did not pull the door shut. She dragged a three-legged wooden stool into the middle of the threshold, sat down on it with her spine straight as an ash pike and her Mary Jane shoes planted square on the sill, folded her arms tight across her teal dress, and glared out across the bright, sleepless street, daring anyone within three terraces to tap a chisel loud enough to carry through the door.

In the back kitchen of the Third Ferry Rest, Yangyang laid her scabbarded sword on the dry corner of the bench and rolled her sheer cloud-worked sleeves up to the elbows, tying the long cyan ribbon-tails behind her waist so they wouldn’t trail in the soot.

Across the flagged floor, Mei walked straight to the butcher’s block, hooked her fingers under the wooden lid of the stoneware rice crock, and lifted it off.

She got as far as reaching for the bamboo measuring-scoop.

Then Mei’s hand stopped in mid-air. Down at her left side, under the loose grey shirt, her abdomen gave a small, tight twitch as she leaned over the counter, and she stared into the white rice for two seconds with her jaw set.

Slowly, Mei laid the wooden lid back down on the block, hooked her boot-toe around the rung of a three-legged stool by the kitchen table, dragged it out, and sat down on it with a loud thump. She planted both elbows on her knees, propped her chin in her palms, and pointed one finger at the stove.

“Okay,” Mei announced to the kitchen. “Two scoops of rice, six bowls of cold water from the pump, and don’t touch the lid once it boils or Lupa will smell it from three miles away and come through the wall.”

Yangyang paused with the flint-striker in her hand and looked over her shoulder.

Mei was sitting with her back very straight on the stool and both hands locked tightly under her chin so they couldn’t wander back toward the counter.

A small, warm pull tugged at the corner of Yangyang’s mouth. She turned back to the brick firebox and struck the steel against the flint.

“Six bowls of water for two scoops makes soup, not congee,” Yangyang said, blowing gently on the pine curls until the orange flame caught and licked up around the dry birch sticks. “In Mingting we use five, and you stir it once with the back of the ladle when the starch rises.”

“Stir it?!” Mei sat bolt upright on her stool. “If you stir the bottom before the grains split, it turns into wallpaper paste! Tell her, Elder!”

At the end of the scrubbed pine table, Suoming sat with Nine Shadows held vertically between her knees and her iron-heeled boots swinging two inches clear of the stone floor.

“Long as it’s hot,” Suoming said.

Yangyang took the wooden bucket out through the back door into the small square well-yard to pump the water.

Here between the high whitewashed walls of the inn’s wings, the air was warm and still, smelling of wet well-moss and the sweet, heavy drift of autumn forest from the hills above. Every time she brought the iron pump-handle down—*clank-shhh, clank-shhh*—clear cold water splashed into the cedar stave-bucket, though when she paused with her hand on the iron, she didn’t feel the slightest hollow rumble under her breastbone where a day’s hunger ought to have been. Her stomach felt nothing at all, as if she had finished a bowl of noodles ten minutes ago, while her knees and the small of her back ached with every single step down from the ridge.

Through the open kitchen doorway ten feet away, she saw Mei lean sideways off her stool toward the end of the table.

“Hey, Suoming,” Mei said, her voice dropping out of its kitchen shout into something softer and a little too bright. “While the water’s coming up, let me take a look at that wrap on your arm. You got rust and sap in it back by the ditch, and I’ve got a clean strip of boiled linen in my pocket from when Amy took my stitches out.”

Suoming looked at Mei’s hand. For a second she didn’t move.

“I’m not gonna poke it, promise,” Mei added quickly, holding up two empty palms. “Just a dry layer over the top so your coat sleeve doesn’t stick to the scab when you bend your elbow. Come on, my kid’s practically a surgeon, I watched how she ties the square ones.”

Suoming shifted the bamboo shaft of the umbrella against her left knee, unbuttoned the brass frog at her right wrist with her left fingers, and laid her small, bandaged forearm flat on the pine table between them.

“Mm,” Suoming said.

Yangyang lifted the full bucket off the stone well-hook and carried it back inside, pouring five—and then, after a brief look at Mei’s narrowed golden eyes, a half-bowl more—of cold water over the washed rice in the iron cauldron before setting the heavy wooden lid on top.

While Yangyang stood at the block slicing the mustard-greens into two-inch lengths with the kitchen cleaver, Mei carefully untied Yangyang’s blood-stiffened knot at Suoming’s wrist and laid a fresh white linen pad over the soaked center of the dressing.

Suoming didn’t look at her own arm. Sitting with her chin barely higher than the tabletop, her pale, light-sore eyes rested on the inside of Mei’s right hand—where a thick, pale ridge of old callus ran across the base of the index knuckle and down the pad of the thumb, worn smooth and deep into the skin long before any beach or any mountain.

“You hold a heavy blade,” Suoming said.

Mei didn’t look up from tucking the tail of the linen strip under the wrap. “Huh? Oh, yeah, I guess so. Single-edge mostly.”

“Who taught you the low thumb-grip?”

Mei pulled the square knot snug with her teeth and her left hand, patted the clean linen once, and sat back on her stool.

“Couldn’t tell you,” Mei said easily, without a ripple in her voice. “Woke up with the calluses already there. Whoever it was, though, they made me do a lot of wrist turns, because my right forearm is way thicker than my left if you look close.”

Suoming looked at Mei’s face for two beats, then drew her small arm back into her plum sleeve and buttoned the brass frog.

“Mm,” Suoming said.

Twenty minutes later, the iron cauldron was bubbling with a thick, fragrant *plop-plop* of split white rice, and Yangyang brought the earthenware pot, a platter of glossy green mustard-stems stir-fried with crushed garlic, and six golden-fried eggs to the table, setting out three coarse blue-and-white bowls and three pairs of bamboo chopsticks.

Nobody reached for the food like starving soldiers. They picked up their bowls because the steam smelled like ginger and toasted oil, and because after walking down a mountain with a monster ahead of their boots, a table needed bowls on it.

Yangyang took one mouthful of the congee. It was scalding hot, smooth, and tasted cleanly of real rice and well-water.

Before Yangyang had finished chewing her first bite, Suoming reached out with two small fingers, hooked the bottom rim of the brown-glazed pickle jar, and slid it six inches across the pine plank so it stopped right beside Yangyang’s left wrist, without looking up from her own bowl.

“Thank you, Elder,” Yangyang said, lifting a piece of crisp salted radish onto her rice.

Across the board, Mei had two fried eggs buried under her congee to warm the yolks. Halfway through her bowl, her eyes flicked over the rim toward Suoming’s place.

The small woman was eating steadily and methodically—one chopstick-pinch of greens, a spoonful of rice, a sliver of radish—until the blue-and-white porcelain of her bowl was completely bare, without a single grain left clinging to the glaze.

Mei’s spoon paused an inch above her own bowl. She looked at Suoming’s empty dish, blinked once, and then without saying a word used her clean chopsticks to slide the third fried egg from the center platter onto the edge of Suoming’s saucer before going back to her soup.

Suoming looked at the egg, picked it up with her chopsticks, and ate it in three bites.

“I hope Amy found a steamer,” Mei said into her bowl, stirring the last of her congee around with her spoon. “When Iuno gets that look in her eye where she’s doing a map in her head, she’ll walk right past four bakeries and forget she has a mouth unless somebody shoves a bun in her hand.”

“Amy was with her when the whistle came from the north road,” Yangyang said quietly, resting her wrists on the warm edge of the table. “And later I heard Lupa shouting in the valley before the giant moved. They found each other.”

Mei’s shoulders dropped a fraction of an inch. She reached into her pocket with her clean hand, pulled out her cracked Terminal, and propped it against the pickle jar, thumbing the screen awake.

In the warm yellow glow from the kitchen stove, the four pictures from the East Arcade came up one by one: Yangyang with her eyes shut tight against the sun; Yangyang with her mouth open and the tips of her ears pink beneath her hat; the two of them cheek-to-cheek with the green awnings behind them; and the last shot, where the curved, oiled-paper edge of Nine Shadows cut across the left third of the frame like a dark moon, throwing a cool band of shade across both their brows.

Mei tapped the fourth picture twice to zoom in on the corner of the umbrella ribs, her thumb lingering on the cracked glass.

“See?” Mei said softly to Yangyang. “Told you it was a great shot.”

Five miles north-west, the living bough that had grown out of the treetop nest sloped down out of the mist and laid its leafy tip flat across the packed-earth yard of an old grain-mill above the flooded paddies.

The bough did not stop there. It ran straight across the dry flagstone threshold, climbed over the low mud-brick garden wall on the far side, and went on winding south-west through the pale pink plum hills until it disappeared into the gold haze.

Lupa’s boots hit the packed dirt of the mill yard five paces behind Chisa.

For the first two miles out of the high tree-crown, where the woven green branch had hung three hundred feet above the bamboo tops with nothing on either side of its bark but open sky, Lupa had crawled on her hands and knees with both eyes squeezed shut and her nose six inches from Chisa’s heels. Halfway down the plum ridge, where the bough had dipped low enough to brush the crowns of the orchard trees, Lupa had stood up without a word, dusted the shredded green bark off her knees, and walked the rest of the way with one thumb hooked inside the back of Chisa’s belt.

In the middle of the yard, Iuno didn’t even look at the mill-house door. Her wool coat was dark around the hem with pond-water splashed from Cartethyia’s skirt, the jagged crystal at her left shoulder was spiderwebbed with dull white frost-lines from the fight on the granary deck, and her right hand rested on the notched rim of the moonwheel at her hip.

She walked straight past the well-curb toward the garden wall where the green bough continued south-west.

“The lotus needle is still five points west of south,” Iuno said, her voice crisp and level in the unmoving afternoon glare. “If the bough holds this grade down through the paddies, we can reach the lower arcade before the light changes.”

Beside the wooden water-wheel, Amy stepped over a stone gutter, caught her heel on nothing at all, and went down on one knee in the dry chaff with a flat *thud*.

“Hold it,” Lupa barked.

Iuno stopped with her boot on the low wall and looked back.

Where Iuno’s right fingers rested on the brass catch of her belt-hook, the tendons across the back of her hand were jumping in a fine, rapid tremor she couldn’t quite still against her wool skirt.

Lupa marched three steps across the yard and planted her ash spear butt-first in the dirt directly in front of Iuno’s toes.

“The light isn’t changing, princess, because the damn sun’s nailed to the ceiling,” Lupa said roughly. “Your hand’s shaking like a wet dog, the kid just ate dirt on flat ground, and Carte’s got half a frog-pond sloshing around in her shoes. We’re done walking.”

“Mei is less than a league south—” Iuno began.

“And she’ll still be south when you can hold your wheel without dropping it!”

Behind them, Amy pushed herself up off the chaff, walked four dragging steps to the open timber doorway of the mill-house, and sat down flat on the stone sill with both legs stuck straight out across the entrance and her back braced against the jamb.

“I’m not moving,” Amy announced to the yard. “My knees are made of wet noodles. If you want to go down that branch, Auntie, you have to step on my shins, and I’ll scream the whole time and tell Ma you kicked me.”

*She has us at a tactical disadvantage, darling,* the High Priestess remarked inside Iuno’s head, dry as old paper. *Sit down before you fall down.*

Iuno looked from Amy’s boots blocking the threshold to Lupa’s scowl, and then down at her own trembling right hand. Slowly, she took her foot off the garden wall and let out a single, quiet breath through her nose.

“Thirty minutes for a meal,” Iuno said.

“Eight hours, princess,” Lupa said, stepping over Amy’s ankles into the dim mill-house kitchen. “Stove. Now.”

Inside, the mill-house had a wide brick hearth built against the gable wall, a dry wood-box stacked to the lip with seasoned oak splits, and a pantry shelf lined with jars of dried hill-mushrooms, buckwheat flour, and coarse salt.

Lupa didn’t bother with flint or kindling-curls.

After an entire afternoon of holding her spear-fire back on rotten rope bridges and climbing up the inside of a giant salad-tree, she knelt in front of the iron grate, shoved four thick oak logs into the firebox at once, and blew a single, joyful tongue of howling red-gold arena flame straight off her palm into the wood.

*WHOOMP.*

The oak caught with a roar that sent a bright orange wash dancing across the lime-washed walls. Lupa sat back on her heels, baring her teeth at the blaze as the heat hit her damp knees.

Within a quarter of an hour she had a gallon of mushroom-and-buckwheat noodle soup boiling hard in the iron pot, a bamboo rack on top steaming the four squashed red-bean buns from Iuno’s coat pocket back to plumpness, and the clay jar of salted plums from the pond-hut open on the board.

Nobody had an empty stomach, and nobody left a drop in the pot. Cartethyia ate two bowls of noodles and three salted plums, lining the plum-stones up in a neat row beside her saucer while Lupa went round the room slamming the heavy wooden window-shutters closed and dropping the iron bars into their brackets until the room was lit only by the red glow of the oak fire.

When the bowls were stacked in the dry trough, Iuno did not sit down on the bench.

She walked to the north shutter, checked the iron bar with her right hand, walked to the hearth to lay her notched moonwheel on the warm brick hob, and then stood by the table with her fingers resting on the wood, looking toward the shuttered south-west casement.

Amy sat curled on the bench with her chin propped on her drawn-up knees, watching the firelight glint along the fresh white frost-cracks in the jagged crystal at Iuno’s bare left shoulder. For twenty minutes Amy hadn’t made a single crack about video games or dungeons.

“Hey, Iuno,” Amy said quietly.

Iuno turned her head. “Yes, Amy.”

“Back at the Hold, when the transport went down in the fog,” Amy said, picking at a frayed thread on her boot-lace with her thumbnail, “Ma had her arms around me before the timber even broke the roof. She didn’t have anybody holding her when the floor opened up there today.”

The oak logs popped in the grate.

Iuno walked back across the flags and sat down on the bench beside Amy, leaving a hand’s-breadth of warm air between their shoulders.

“In Septimont,” Iuno said in a low, even voice, “for thirty years, I knew which roof would fall on Thursday before Monday’s bread was baked. When the Sanctum floor dropped under our feet today, I did not know whether there were flagstones ten yards down or four miles of empty air.”

She looked down at her right palm lying open on her knee.

“I am terrified every time she turns a corner out of my sight, Amy,” Iuno said plainly. “And I still prefer it to knowing the end of the page before she walks onto it.”

Inside her breastbone, the High Priestess did not make a joke.

*She has the girl with the blue feathers and the small woman with the thunder-cane, darling,* the bronze voice murmured, low and steady. *And her stitches have been out for days. Let the child lean on you.*

Amy blinked once at the hearth, then slid sideways along the pine bench until her pink head rested squarely against Iuno’s good right shoulder. Iuno put her arm around her and held her tight.

Across the room by the north post, Cartethyia sat on a low grain-chest with her knees together and her hands folded in her lap. She wasn’t humming, and she hadn’t touched the hem of her damp skirt since supper. Her chin was tilted slightly upward, facing the blind timber wall that looked north toward the high orchard terraces where a lute and a brass bell had rung out hours ago.

Two paces to her left, Chisa sat cross-legged on a woven reed mat with her cased scissor-blade across her thighs, her head angled toward the floorboards as she listened past the crackle of the oak fire to the vast, slow turning of the water-wheels deep beneath the hill.

“Stop sitting up like a fence-post, Carte, you look creepy!” Lupa barked, coming out of the back room with an armful of coarse grey wool blankets and flinging one over Chisa’s shoulders before marching toward the grain-chest. “Get your boots off before you rot your toes—”

Lupa stopped two steps from the chest.

Cartethyia hadn’t blinked. Sitting bolt upright against the cedar pillar with her crystal laurel slightly crooked on her brow and her hands still folded neatly in her lap, she was fast asleep, her breathing soft and even through her parted lips.

Lupa let out a rough snort, shook out the heaviest wool blanket, and tucked it carefully around Cartethyia’s shoulders up to her chin.

Iuno eased her arm from around Amy’s shoulders, rose from the bench, and knelt on one knee in front of the grain-chest.

Using only her right hand—bracing the wet leather heel between her own knees so the boot wouldn’t slip—Iuno picked open the sodden, water-swollen knots of Cartethyia’s laces one eyelet at a time, drew both pond-soaked boots gently off her stockings, poured a quarter-cup of muddy lotus-water out of the left one into the ash-pan, and set them upright beside the warm hearth-bricks to dry.

Within ten minutes, Amy was asleep on the wide bench with her cheek on Iuno’s folded coat, Chisa was breathing evenly under her blanket by the wall, and Iuno sat with her back against the table-leg and her eyes closed.

Lupa stayed on her knees by the dying red embers of the stove.

When nobody else in the room moved, she reached inside her coat, past the leather case over her ribs, and drew out the metal lotus. In the dim red glow from the grate, its filigree petals stood open, and the hair-thin needle of rose-gold at its heart pointed steady and unshaking toward the south-west shutter.

Lupa carried it across the flags, set it on the inner ledge of the south-west windowsill with the needle aimed straight down the valley, and lay down on the reed mat beside Chisa’s boots, keeping her yellow eyes open on the tiny gold point in the dark until her lids dropped shut.

Halfway up the orchard mountain, Buling kicked a fallen loquat with the toe of her black cloth shoe.

The bruised orange fruit bounced twice down the dressed-stone steps, stopped on the third riser, and immediately rose straight back up through the copper mist to snap itself neatly onto the twig above her hat.

Ten feet behind her on the stair, Ciaccona stopped dead, leaned her good right shoulder against the dry-stone terrace wall, and slid all the way down the masonry until her backside hit the grass with her velvet lute-case standing upright between her boots.

“That’s the fourth time,” Ciaccona said flatly. “That is the exact same branch, that is the exact same dent in the wall where the mason dropped his chisel, and if I climb one more flight of stairs in this valley, Buling, I am going to take my lute out of the case and hit you over the head with it.”

Buling stood on the third step with her hands on the hips of her royal-blue silk cheongsam, glaring up through the copper fog at the white-tiled roofs of Mengxuan Vale—which sat two terraces above their heads, clear enough to see the brass spire of the great astrolabe, and not a single foot closer than they had been an hour ago when she turned around to go uphill.

“Wait first,” Buling muttered, marching three paces left to squint along the stone drainage ditch. “Earlier the water was running east-north-east, now the ditch is facing the white tiger. Somebody shifted the gate-post.”

“Nobody shifted the gate-post,” Ciaccona said, resting the back of her head against the rough stone and closing her eyes. “You told me your little Celestial village was five minutes up the hill. We have walked up twelve flights of stairs, we have walked down nine flights of stairs, and we are still sitting in the middle of the fruit salad. How do you work in a place for a hundred years and not know where the front door is?”

Buling’s jaw set under her yellow paper talisman. She turned her back on the upper terrace and kicked a pebble into the drainage ditch.

“The only road I ever looked for down here was the one going out, okay!” Buling snapped at the grass.

Ciaccona opened one eye.

Buling was standing with her small shoulders hunched up around the brim of her round black winter hat, her silver braid hanging down her back and her red eyes fixed furiously on the repeating chisel-marks along the curb.

The next remark died on Ciaccona’s tongue. She let out a long, tired sigh through her nose and patted the flat top of the stone wall beside her knee.

“Forget the village,” Ciaccona said. “If I have to look at another Celestial with a carpenter’s square tonight, I’ll start singing tavern songs in parallel fifths until they throw us off the cliff. Look at the pavilion over there.”

Twenty paces along the wide earth dyke between the loquat trees stood the stone rest-pavilion with the half-laid roof.

Because the terraced hillside couldn’t agree on an hour of the day, the eastern half of the pavilion—where the grey roof-tiles were still stacked in unmortared piles beside the bare cedar rafters—sat under a blazing, brass-bright noon sun. Ten feet to the left, under the finished western half of the roof where the curved eaves hung low over a stone bench, the air was a cool, deep indigo twilight with three pale evening stars glimmering through the gap above the balustrade.

Buling looked from the unmoving white glare overhead to the dark, quiet corner under the western eaves.

“Fine,” Buling grumbled, trudging across the grass and dropping onto the twilight bench with her black-wrapped shins stretched out in front of her. “No pillow also. Tomorrow my neck going to be stiff like dried fish.”

Ciaccona dragged herself up off the ground, carried her lute-case into the indigo shade under the finished half of the roof, and sat down on the dry flagstone floor with her back against the corner pillar. As soon as she crossed the line between the third and fourth rafters, the baking heat of the orchard vanished into cool evening air that smelled of night-stock and damp slate.

For a while neither of them spoke. Below the terrace wall, the endless rows of drainage ditches glimmered faintly in the mixed light, and far off to the west, the great silhouette of the fox stood quiet against the sky with her arms folded across her chest.

Ciaccona reached over, unlatched the brass clasps of her velvet lute-case with her good right hand, and lifted the lid an inch.

Tucked into the padded hollow beside the spare gut strings—where she had shoved it five seconds before the thunderclap broke the Sanctum doors—was a folded square of coarse garrison linen.

Ciaccona pulled it out and opened the cloth on her knee.

Inside lay a single glutinous-rice cake from Wen’s supper platter, squashed completely flat between the neck of the lute and the case-lining until the red bean-paste fox in the middle looked like a run-over badger.

Ciaccona looked down at it for three full beats. She hadn’t eaten since the noon walnut-paste at the storehouse, and she had defended smaller pieces of pastry from Lupa with a fork.

Then she glanced up at Buling, who had pulled the brim of her *nuanmao* down over her forehead and folded both arms tight across her royal-blue chest with her chin tucked in.

Ciaccona picked up the flattened rice-cake, broke it cleanly down the middle of the mashed bean-paste fox so the bigger half stayed in the linen square, and held the cloth out toward the bench.

“Here,” Ciaccona said.

Buling lifted her chin an inch and squinted under her yellow paper talisman at the squashed white disc.

“What is that,” Buling said. “Look like somebody sat on a dumpling.”

“It’s a Waking Moon rice-cake from the third storehouse, and it rode down a hole in my lute-case,” Ciaccona said dryly. “Take it before I change my mind and eat both halves in front of you.”

Buling didn’t say thank you. She reached out, took the linen square, flipped the yellow paper talisman up onto the round brim of her hat with two fingers, and bit into the cold, sticky rice.

They ate in silence in the six-foot patch of twilight while high noon shone on the stacked roof-tiles four paces away. When the last crumb was gone, Buling wiped her thumb on her napkin, tucked the cloth neatly into her sash, and let the talisman drop back down over her nose.

Ciaccona lifted her lute out of the case and laid it across her thighs, keeping her splinted left forefinger straight out clear of the maple neck.

With the side of her right thumb, she touched the lowest string.

*D.*

It hummed low and warm against the wood of her ribs, without the vast, terrifying roar of the falling city’s water-wheels behind it—only a single gut string in a quiet corner.

“She’d better have taken her boots off,” Ciaccona murmured to the peg-box. “When she goes wading in her good leather and sleeps in them, it takes three days of tallow-grease before she stops squeaking down the hall.”

On the stone bench above her shoulder, Buling lay down on her side with her knees drawn up, tucked one palm under her cheek beneath her round winter hat, and shut her eyes.

“The tall wolf is over there with them,” Buling said into her sleeve, her voice already thick and slow. “She’ll yell at her until she takes them off.”

Ciaccona’s mouth curved slightly in the dark. Using only her three uninjured left fingers on the lower frets, she began to thumb a slow, quiet Ragunnese boat-air across the bottom three strings—so soft it barely reached the sunny half of the pavilion roof—and before she reached the second turn of the melody, Buling’s breathing had gone long and still against the stone.

Up the narrow pine stair of the Third Ferry Rest, there were four doors along the gallery, and Suoming opened three of them before she stopped at the corner room at the front.

Its single wide casement looked straight down onto the cobbled arcade where the black vine-scale ran toward the western archway, and through the side-slit between the eaves it commanded the well-yard below.

“This one,” Suoming said, stepping over the sill with the ferrule of Nine Shadows clicking once on the scrubbed pine floorboards.

Mei and Yangyang followed her in.

The room was twelve feet square and spotless. Against the inner timber wall stood a single four-post bedstead made up with a thick indigo-dyed cotton quilt and one long buckwheat-hull bolster. Built into the front bay under the casement was a deep cedar window-seat, barely four feet long from arm-rest to arm-rest, cushioned with a flat woven rush mat.

Suoming did not look at the bed.

She walked straight across the floorboards to the window-seat, stepped up onto a low footstool, and pulled the two heavy cedar shutters closed against the blinding white daylight outside—*clack, clack*—dropping the iron hook into its eye so the room dropped instantly into a cool, amber-streaked dusk. Only a single razor-thin line of white noon leaked through the middle louver-slat and lay across the rush mat.

Then the small woman sat down on the window-seat, swung her iron-plated boots up onto the cedar bench—which fit her from the crown of her coiled red hair to her heels with three inches to spare—and laid the furled bamboo shaft of her umbrella across her knees.

Mei and Yangyang stood side by side in the middle of the floor.

Together they looked at the single bedstead, and at the single indigo quilt, and at the single buckwheat bolster.

Yangyang’s small cerulean collar-feathers gave a tiny, involuntary lift along her jaw before she made them lie flat.

Mei rubbed the back of her neck with her right palm, looked at the mattress, looked over her shoulder at Suoming sitting three paces away in the shutter-gloom, and then turned back to Yangyang.

“Okay,” Mei said out loud, her voice entirely cheerful and a little too loud in the small room. “For the record, I am telling Iuno about this first thing when we find her, right after I tell her about the fan-stall pictures.”

Yangyang unbuckled her sword-belt with both hands and laid the scabbard carefully on the low chest at the foot of the bed. Her ears were faintly warm under the dark blue locks at her temples, and her voice stayed level and clear.

“Good,” Yangyang said. “So am I.”

From the dimness of the window-seat three paces behind them, Suoming spoke without turning her head from the shutter-crack:

“Take your boots off first. The wall side’s out of the draft.”

Mei let out a short, breathless snort, dropped onto the outer edge of the mattress, and bent carefully from the waist to unlace her left road-boot—slower than she had done it in front of the pallet at dawn, her breath grunting softly through her nose as the new scar pulled across her stomach.

Before Mei could reach for the right boot, Yangyang knelt on one knee on the floorboards in front of her, took Mei’s right heel in her palm the way Iuno had done before breakfast, and loosened the rawhide thongs hook by hook, sliding the heavy leather off Mei’s foot and setting both boots neatly beside the chest.

“Thanks,” Mei said softly.

“Let me see the side before you lie down,” Yangyang said, staying on one knee by the mattress.

Mei lifted the hem of her grey cotton shirt three inches above her belt.

In the thin white line of light slanting from the shutter, the three-inch ridge of pink skin across her left abdomen was dry and whole, without a drop of blood around the eight tiny pinpricks where the silk threads had come out, though the flesh along the upper bank was flushed warm red from the climb up the uneven stair. Yangyang laid the cool backs of her fingers lightly beside the ridge for a heartbeat to check the heat, then pulled the cotton shirt smoothly back down over Mei’s waist.

“It didn’t split,” Yangyang said. “How does it feel?”

“Like somebody sewed a piece of boot-leather into my shirt, mostly,” Mei said, and patted the edge of the mattress beside her hip. “Come here. Your turn.”

Yangyang sat on the bed with her back half-turned and unfastened the outer frog of her tunic under her left arm, parting the inner linen wrap she still wore loose under her clothes to keep the wool from chafing.

Beneath her seventh rib, the skin was no longer purple, only a faded greenish-gold bruise over the bone, and four inches below it ran the older, silver-white seam she had carried back across the dark in her feathers. Mei didn’t poke the rib; she simply rested her warm, calloused palm flat over the side of Yangyang’s ribcage, her thumb brushing once over the edge of the old white seam as Yangyang drew a breath in and let it out.

“Still clicking?” Mei asked quietly.

“No,” Yangyang said. “Just tired.”

Mei gave her ribs one brief, gentle squeeze and let go, sliding across the mattress to the outer edge—leaving the warmer wall side for Yangyang despite what Suoming had said—and pulled the indigo quilt up to her waist.

Yangyang took off her own boots, unpinned her hat and laid it on the chest beside her sword, and lay down between Mei and the timber wall, resting her head on the far end of the buckwheat bolster. With her left hand, she unfastened the folded silk sash around her waist and laid the flat square of Hsin’s Prism on the mattress between them, where its steady, hearth-stone warmth soaked slowly into the quilt.

Mei rolled onto her right side facing the wall, tucked her right hand under her cheek, and let out a long, shuddering sigh that seemed to let go of every step from the Sanctum down to the arcade.

“Wake me up if the umbrella starts crackling,” Mei mumbled into the pillow.

Her eyes shut on the last word. Her left hand slid three inches across the quilt, her fingers brushing against the back of Yangyang’s right wrist beside the warm Prism, and before ten seconds had gone by, Mei’s breathing had dropped into a deep, dead-weight sleep.

Yangyang lay still against the wall.

Her ribs were glad of the flat mattress, and her legs were heavy as lead beneath the cotton quilt, and her eyes stayed wide open in the dimness.

Under her cheek, the buckwheat hulls in the bolster rustled faintly whenever she breathed. Through the floorboards came the tiny, dry *tink* of the kitchen stove cooling downstairs; outside the shuttered casement lay the silent, endless street where nobody walked and no supper-fires were lit; and across the quilt, three paces away in the bay of the window, Suoming sat upright against the cedar frame with her faded plum coat still buttoned to the throat and the bamboo shaft of Nine Shadows resting across her knees.

Ten minutes passed. Twenty.

Mei did not stir, her warm fingers staying curled loosely against the bone of Yangyang’s wrist.

Slowly, so she wouldn’t jostle the mattress, Yangyang eased her wrist out from under Mei’s hand, pushed her corner of the quilt back, and stood up in her white stockings.

The pine floorboards were cool under her soles. She walked three quiet steps across the room to the low baggage-chest that sat at right angles to the window-seat, and sat down on its lid with her hands resting in her lap.

The single hairline slit of white daylight between the cedar shutters fell straight across Suoming’s knees and the rust-spotted linen bandage on her right forearm. Suoming was not leaning her head back against the casement; her spine was clear of the wood, and her pale eyes were open in the shadow, watching the crack in the shutter.

“Can’t sleep, Watcher?” Suoming asked, so quietly it didn’t carry to the bed.

“Not yet,” Yangyang said.

For a little while neither of them spoke. Over on the bedstead, Mei gave a tiny snort into the bolster and settled deeper into the ticking.

Yangyang looked down at the white tips of her hair resting over her shoulder, and then up at the small woman sitting across the strip of light.

“On the sluice-bridge,” Yangyang said in a low, plain voice, “when the rain started at the Hold. You asked what the Fox made me swear in the Skyworks.”

Suoming’s eyes shifted from the shutter-crack to Yangyang’s face. She didn’t nod, and she didn’t interrupt.

“I told you the three things,” Yangyang said. “There was more before that.”

She kept her voice low and even, the way she had learned to give a border report when her throat was dry after a two-day ride.

“When Muyu’s Snare was coming down on the gallery,” Yangyang said, “everything in the room stopped moving. The falling stone, the fire, everyone’s breath. Hsin spoke to me out of the Prism in my sash.”

On Suoming’s knee, her small left hand rested motionless on the oiled bamboo of the umbrella shaft.

“She called me by a title,” Yangyang said. “Only the word after *Little* wasn’t there. It sounded the way a stone looks when someone takes a chisel and scrapes a character out of the middle of a line—just a hollow place in the air where the sound belonged, the same as the gap in the eleven characters inside my mother’s shell.”

Suoming did not blink.

“She asked me what I meant to do with her stone and the one inside Muyu’s Snare,” Yangyang went on quietly. “I told her I was going to put them together to hear who was still breathing inside the machine, and pull the living out before we broke the frame. She told me to swear the three things first. And then she told me to remember that I was one of the people coming out of that room, and that when the roof got heavy, I was to open my mouth and ask the others for a hand.”

In the narrow band of white light between them, the dust motes hung still.

Yangyang looked directly at the rust-brown bloodstain on Suoming’s bandaged right sleeve, and at the dark hollows under the small woman’s light-sore eyes.

“You aren’t going to lie down tonight,” Yangyang said.

“Someone keeps the door,” Suoming said flatly.

“Then wake me for the second watch,” Yangyang said. “My ribs are whole enough to sit on a bench for three hours, and your arm needs to stay still before it bleeds through the second cloth.”

Suoming looked at her across the three feet of shadow.

For four slow breaths the room was completely quiet except for Mei’s deep, steady breathing from the bed and the faint catch low in Suoming’s left side—*draw, catch, let go*.

“Second watch is too early for a cracked rib,” Suoming said in her flat, quiet voice. “I’ll wake you at the third.”

Yangyang inclined her head once. “Thank you, Elder.”

She didn’t get up from the chest right away. Her fingers folded together in her lap over her sheer cloud-worked cuffs, and she turned her head slightly toward the shuttered window.

“There’s one other thing,” Yangyang said softly. “Up in the Pagoda today, when the bronze rings turned and the floor dropped out from under us.”

Suoming’s pale eyes stayed on her face.

“Mei had my left hand,” Yangyang said. “My right hand was empty because I’d thrown the Prism to you. Just as my boots left the wood, someone caught me by the right cuff and pulled me down through the gap so the closing beam wouldn’t clip my shoulder.”

Yangyang looked down at her own right wrist.

“It was a small hand,” she said. “Warm. And right beside my ear, closer than the wind in the shaft, someone let out a long breath, like they’d been holding it up the whole stair.”

On the window-seat, Suoming’s small left hand closed around the bamboo shaft of Nine Shadows until the knuckles showed white in the slit of light.

“I’ve been hearing it all day under the bamboo and the water-wheels,” Yangyang said, her voice dropping almost to a whisper. “And since the giant out there lowered her arms and folded them across her chest, it’s slower. Like someone who set down a heavy load on the step and hasn’t got the strength back in their arms to lift the latch yet.”

Yangyang stopped.

For a long time Suoming sat still against the dark cedar casement.

When she breathed in, the old iron-bar rasp caught once against the bottom of her left ribs, and she looked away from Yangyang toward the razor-thin line of white glare between the shutters.

“How close is the breath now?” Suoming asked.

“Further west than the street,” Yangyang said. “Steady. Not failing.”

“Good,” Suoming said to the wood.

Yangyang waited a beat in the quiet. “The promise you made her when the plum tree split,” she asked gently. “Has she called it in?”

“Not yet,” Suoming said.

She took her white-knuckled hand off the bamboo shaft of the umbrella, flexed her fingers once in her lap, and looked back across the shadow at Yangyang.

Her face was completely flat—the exact same expression she had worn when she asked for a meal ticket in the Hold’s market lane.

“You and the golden-eyed one,” Suoming said, nodding her chin half an inch toward the bedstead where Mei lay dead to the world with one knee crooked over the quilt. “What’s that.”

It wasn’t an accusation, and her voice didn’t go up at the end. She asked it the way a garrison commander asks which corporal has the gate-key.

Yangyang didn’t look away from her eyes, and she didn’t look down at her hands.

“She’s with Iuno,” Yangyang said plainly. “They have a daughter.”

She glanced once toward the dark shape of Mei’s shoulder on the mattress, then back to Suoming.

“What we have doesn’t have a title on the roll,” Yangyang said. “Iuno knows all of it. When we go back to Jinzhou, Mei and I are walking down by the bird-bridge on the canal.”

Suoming looked at her for two beats in the shuttered dusk, her pale eyes unreadable under her red hair.

“Mm,” Suoming said.

She reached up with her uninjured left hand, tugged the collar of her faded plum coat half an inch higher around her neck, and leaned the back of her head squarely against the cedar window-frame for the first time since they had come upstairs.

“Go lie down before your stockings get damp on the boards, Watcher,” Suoming said. “I’ll wake you at the third.”

Yangyang stood up from the chest.

She walked quietly back across the pine floor, climbed onto the wall side of the mattress without creaking the ropes, and lay down on her side facing the room, pulling the indigo quilt up over both her own shoulder and Mei’s.

Through the narrow crack between the closed cedar shutters, far out across the quiet roofs and the gold trees at the western end of the valley, the white glare still burned without fading—except that the great silhouetted head of the fox above the mountain ranges had dipped a little lower toward her folded arms, her chin resting down against her breast and the tall tips of her ears angled forward in the mist, the way a tired sentry’s head nods when she has fallen asleep standing up at her post.

Under the hush of the paper leaves outside, Yangyang heard the slow, deep breath rise and fall across the valley—in, and out, and in again.

Beside her on the bolster, Mei shifted once in her sleep, let out a soft sigh through her nose, and slid her warm, calloused fingers across the quilt beside the glowing Prism until her hand closed fast around Yangyang’s wrist, while three paces away by the shuttered window, Suoming let her eyelids drop half-shut with the bamboo shaft of Nine Shadows resting across her knees.

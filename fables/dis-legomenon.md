# Dis Legomenon

The four steps down from the west footbridge landing into the attic kitchen had been bridged in September with two lengths of two-inch scaffold plank screwed to oak cleats, which Rebecca had liberated from a municipal tramway repair site at one in the morning and laid with a spirit level so the lip wouldn't catch a caster.

At twenty to seven on Wednesday evening the stairwell latch clicked twice, the planks gave a single solid thrum under pneumatic tyres, and Mornye rolled across the threshold with the olive-drab aluminium geophone case strapped across her thighs and Buddy sitting squarely on the top latch between the brass toggles.

Sigrika and Lynae, who were at the end of the table clearing the oilcloth, both came half out of their chairs at the same instant.

"Sit down," Mornye said, before either of them had cleared the bench. "Three separate people between Engineering South and the west lift have already offered to carry an eleven-kilogram box for me this evening, including a first-year from Metallurgy whom I do not teach and who attempted to lift it while I was moving."

Sigrika sat down so fast her knees bumped the table underside. "Sorry, Professor."

Mornye locked her left wheel by the corner near the radiator, glanced down once at the scaffold ramp behind her—noting the bevelled entry edge and the countersunk screws with a single brief nod—and reached for the buckle of the lap strap.

At the stove beside Rebecca, Chiyo had come over at six in her dark woollen dress with a clean white pinafore tied over it for the seven o'clock snow. Before Mornye had the strap undone, Chiyo walked across the linoleum with a folded linen coaster and a heavy porcelain cup of roasted barley tea, set both down by Mornye's right elbow on the clear corner of the table, inclined her head half an inch, and went back to turning the cabbage in the colander without once looking at the chair or the case.

Mornye's hand came up two inches in the automatic gesture she used to wave off lab assistants, met the back of Chiyo's grey bun at the sink, and stopped.

"Thank you, Madam," Mornye said, slightly stiffer than usual, and put her fingers round the warm porcelain.

On the lid of the aluminium case, Buddy stood up on his two stubby legs, stretched both short arms over his round head with a faint amber flicker, and hopped down onto the oilcloth. He waddled past the salt cellar, stopped in front of the grey stoneware crock of Halla's berry jam, and patted the cut beeswax string twice with one little hand as if testing it for tension.

"He is going to get jam on the level," Mornye said, unlatching the two brass toggles and swinging the lid back against the salt cellar. "Sigrika. Look at the collar before I take the head out of the foam."

Sigrika pulled her chair in and leaned over the open box, her red and blue pens already clipped to her collar.

Nested in dark grey die-cut foam sat the cylindrical brass housing of the three-axis geophone, its three spirit tubes set into a machined flange around the top cap, with the coiled black sensor cable and the digital readout box tucked into the side slots.

"The digital inclinometer on the readout box has a three-degree software compensation window," Mornye said, tapping the circular spirit bubble at the crown of the cylinder with the tip of her mechanical pencil. "Do not trust it. Every degree the processor has to correct in software costs four decibels of sensitivity at the bottom of the register. Level the tripod manually until the spirit bubble sits dead inside the inner hairlines before you switch the board on."

"Inner hairlines first, then power," Sigrika repeated, writing *Level: manual to inner ring before digital init* at the top of her pad. "What if the frost heaves one of the tripod shoes while it's running?"

"If the clay is frozen through, it will not heave in six hours," Mornye said. She lifted the padded canvas sleeve containing the lithium-iron battery pack from the lower compartment. "This weighs three point two kilograms. Below minus eight degrees, if you set the bare metal baseplate directly onto frozen slate, internal resistance doubles and the voltage drops off a cliff at hour four. Keep it in the canvas boot and set the boot on the felt pad."

At the far end of the table, Lynae had a half-sheet of lined paper torn out of her statics pad propped beside the chopping board and was ticking lines off with a flat carpenter's pencil.

Mornye's eyes moved from the battery sleeve to the carpenter's pencil. "What is that."

"Spares list," Lynae said, not looking up as she checked a line. "I pulled the tripod out of the shed at four to check the leg clamps."

Mornye reached across the oilcloth with two fingers, turned the half-sheet round, and read it upside down.

*1. Spare brass thumb-screw, leg clamp (threads on #2 are chewed)*
*2. 30cm steel grounding spike + lump hammer (clay's too hard for the brass pins)*
*3. Two rolls black friction tape*
*4. Spare whip antenna*

Mornye looked at item two for three seconds. Then she clicked the lead of her mechanical pencil out a millimetre, wrote *5. Silicone grease, tube (battery terminal O-ring splits below -10°C)* underneath Lynae's fourth line in her small, square hand, and slid the sheet back across the oilcloth without looking up.

"The thumb-screw in the top pocket of the canvas bag has a metric thread," Mornye said to the geophone case. "Do not mix it up with the imperial ones on the transit stand."

"Already swapped the chewed one out," Lynae said pleasantly, and added a little checkmark beside Mornye's fifth line.

"For the radio schedule," Mornye went on, turning back to Sigrika and tapping the compact shortwave transceiver clipped to the lid, "you will call Engineering South on forty-one point four megahertz on the hour, every hour, beginning from instrument lock. I will be in 304."

"You don't need to sit by the set for all six hours, Professor," Sigrika said quickly. "If the bubble stays centred and the voltage holds, I can just log the hourly readings in the book and call you at the end of the run—"

"You will call me on the hour," Mornye said. "Do not skip a transmission because nothing has changed. Nothing changing for sixty minutes is half the paper. And if the battery drops below eleven volts at four in the afternoon, I prefer to know it at four rather than when you walk back through the gate on Friday."

Rebecca came over from the stove carrying the big enamelled iron pot in two folded tea towels and set it down on the cork mat in the middle of the table. When she lifted the lid, a cloud of steam smelling of smoked mutton bone, browned onions, and marjoram went up to the rafters.

"Eat first, calibrate later," Rebecca said, setting a stack of five deep bowls by her elbow and picking up the ladle with her right hand while her wide chrome left hand steadied the pot rim. "Chiyo put barley in it so it sticks to your ribs. Professor, you eat mutton?"

"Yes," Mornye said. "A small bowl, please; the salt in smoked meat makes my fingers swell in cold weather."

Chiyo was already setting a small shallow dish of pickled radish and a plate of steamed buns beside Mornye's elbow, and Rebecca ladled out five portions, dropping the marrow-bone piece into Sigrika's bowl because Sigrika was already reaching for the bread basket with her left hand.

"Are you coming as far as the gate tomorrow, Rebecca?" Mornye asked, blowing on her spoon.

"Nope." Rebecca rapped the back of the ladle once against the yellow rules sheet taped to the lath between the spice rack and the calendar, right where her own thick black marker had gone over the ninth line at the bottom: **9. There is someone in the house.** "Somebody's gotta be in the house. Plus it's minus twelve down that hole and I don't hike in a freezer unless somebody's paying me hazard scale."

She dropped into her chair, hooked one heel over the rung, and pointed her spoon at Sigrika across the pot.

"Bring me a rock, though."

Sigrika paused with a piece of bun halfway to her mouth. "A rock?"

"Yeah, a rock. From outside." Rebecca chewed a piece of mutton and waved her chrome fingers toward the window. "Everything in this place is poured concrete or dressed tile or that grey crushed stuff Maintenance dumps on the tram tracks out of a hopper. I haven't seen an actual piece of dirt nobody engineered since we came down the shaft. Bring me back an ugly rock that didn't come out of a machine."

"Greywacke or slate?" Sigrika asked seriously. "The upper shelves are mostly fractured slate, but down by the culvert dyke there's—"

"Whichever one looks less like a sidewalk, Siggy. Surprise me."

Sigrika ate her stew at her usual speed—three spoons of broth to every bite of bun, her left hand keeping Buddy from stepping into the pickled radish—until Lynae nudged the water pitcher against her knuckles to slow her down.

At two minutes to seven, Chiyo set her chopsticks across her empty bowl, wiped her hands on her apron, and looked up at the round wall clock above the wireless.

"It is nearly the hour," she said.

Rebecca shoved her chair back first, wiping her mouth on the back of her sleeve, and went to the wide casement window over the fourth terrace. Sigrika and Lynae followed, Sigrika carrying Buddy cradled in the crook of her right arm like a warm loaf of bread, and Mornye released her brake and wheeled quietly into the clear space at the left of the frame where the sill had been cut low.

Down below, the fourth terrace had emptied out of its shops and kitchens into the street.

The noodle-shop man from the ground floor was standing on the kerb outside his steamed-up window in his striped apron, his arms folded over his chest and his chin tilted up toward the dark vault above the streetlamps. Across the tram tracks, four boys from the bakery were sitting in a row on an upturned flour crate, and along the iron parapet that looked down into Tier Five, a dozen students and two railwaymen in their heavy winter greatcoats stood shoulder to shoulder with their hands in their pockets.

Nobody was talking. Even the 4B tram, halted at the chemist's stop post thirty yards down the line, had its compressor switched off and its driver leaning out of the cab door with his cap in his hand, the whole tier gone quiet in the particular way an underground city goes quiet when five thousand people are waiting for the same thing at the same time.

High up in the indigo dark above the amber lamp-cones, where the great steel ribs of the dome trusses spanned the gloom, a long pneumatic sigh ran from west to east—a sound you felt in the soles of your shoes through the floorboards before it reached your ears.

Then the baffles opened.

At first it was just a pale glimmer high up in the structural dark, like dust shaken out of a cloth, and then the first flakes came down into the warm yellow cones of the streetlamps—fat, slow, six-sided crystals drifting down between the iron railings and settling onto the black flagstones, the overhead tram wire, and the shoulders of the noodle-shop man's apron without melting.

Down on the terrace, somebody let out a long, low breath that went all the way along the railing.

Mornye lifted her left wrist and tilted her watch face to the window light.

"Nineteen hundred hours and eleven seconds," she said quietly. "The third-sector manifold is lagging by a quarter-bar. I told the utilities committee in September that the check-valves on the east truss were sticking."

"Shut up a second, Professor," Rebecca whispered, and threw the brass window latch up with her thumb.

She pushed the right-hand casement wide to the cold air and stuck her chrome left hand straight out over the sill into the falling white.

A flake the size of a thumbtack came spinning down out of the amber dark, landed flat in the middle of her brushed-steel palm plate, and sat there for three full seconds—every feathered arm of the crystal distinct and sharp against the grey metal—before the heat coming out of the kitchen window took the points off it and turned it into a clear bead of water on her knuckle seam.

Rebecca pulled her arm back inside and stared at the wet spot on her metal hand as if somebody had picked her pocket.

"What the hell," Rebecca said, her voice rising half an octave. "That's actual snow! I thought they were gonna blow shaved ice out of a fish-dock crusher or drop white soap flakes on us or something. They're making real sky-water up in the rafters?"

"It is condensed atmospheric vapour nucleated on silver-iodide mist at minus fourteen degrees in the upper plenum," Mornye said, watching another flake settle on the outer iron guard-bar. "If you drop crushed ice from ninety metres, it arrives as hail and breaks the greenhouse glass."

"That's completely mental," Rebecca said, and stuck her chrome hand back out to catch three more.

In the crook of Sigrika's arm, Buddy gave a small, urgent chirrup. He scrambled up her woollen sleeve onto the wooden windowsill, balanced on his two stubby legs with his round belly pressed against the sash frame, and reached both short arms out into the cold draft after a fat flake that was drifting past the lintel.

The flake caught the warm air rising off the kitchen radiator, bobbed two inches to the left, and sailed past his nose. Buddy lunged half an inch sideways, missed it completely, and tipped backward off the sill into Sigrika's chest, where she caught him with both hands before he could roll into her coat buttons.

Sigrika held him against her cardigan and stood looking out over the top of his head at the fourth terrace.

The flagstones around the telephone kiosk at the head of the Tier Three steps were already turning grey-white between the wet black wheel-ruts of the tram. Across the street, the baker's boys had scraped enough snow off the top of the flour crate to make a ball the size of a walnut and were arguing over whose turn it was to throw it at the lamp-post. The air coming through the open casement smelled of wet iron, cold stone, and the clean, metallic nothing of water that had never been through a pipe.

She didn't say anything at all for a full minute. Her right thumb rested on the scarred pad of her index finger, feeling the small hard ridge in the skin where the cold air caught it, and she watched the white gather along the kerb until the old wheel-marks were covered over.

Behind them at the sink, Chiyo dried the soup ladle and hung it on its nail.

"If you are going down the shaft at six," Chiyo said to the room at large, "you should shut the window before the damp gets into the blankets."

Rebecca pulled her wet chrome hand in, wiped the palm on her jeans, and pulled the casement shut until the latch bit.

"Right," Lynae said, reaching for her dish towel to dry the plates. "Bed by nine, Siggy. And don't stay up till two checking the board—if anybody argues about custard tonight, let 'em kill each other."

At quarter to six on Thursday morning the snow on the fourth terrace was six centimetres of dry, squeaking powder under the streetlamps, but down at the mouth of the Riseway the air coming up out of the shaft had scoured the roadway bare to the stone.

Four Collective sentries in long grey greatcoats stood by the lowered steel drop-bar between two coke braziers, the frost thick as sheep's wool on the cloth of their breath-masks and the brass compass badges on their caps. Behind the bar, the great arched throat of the tunnel went down into the mountain, and out of it rolled a steady, noiseless river of minus-fifteen air that smelled of old slate and deep ice.

Two motorcycles stood idling in the red pool of the gate lamps: Hiyuki's heavy dark road-frame in front, and Lynae's lighter street machine three paces back with the olive-drab geophone box lashed flat across the pillion rack and the canvas tripod bag strapped along the left pannier rail.

The sergeant of the watch came out of the brick guardhouse with a hand-torch under his arm, his breath smoking through his muffler, and took Mornye's yellow triplicate permit from Sigrika's mittened hand.

He held the clipboard under the torch beam and read the top sheet all the way through. Then he lifted the corner of the yellow page with a gloved thumb, checked that the carbon impression on the pink second sheet matched Mornye's signature at the bottom, and read the *Purpose of Deployment* line a second time.

"Calibration of instrument floor variance," he read out, his voice muffled by the wool round his chin. "Below the cordon line."

"Kilometre fourteen-point-two," Sigrika said, keeping her chin level above her scarf. "The borehole basin rim. Professor Mornye countersigned the equipment release at Engineering South."

The sergeant shone the torch beam down at the aluminium box on Lynae's rack, then brought the light back up—past Sigrika's shoulder, to where Hiyuki sat astride the lead machine with one felt-lined boot resting on the frozen stone.

Hiyuki wore her white shrine-keeper's jacket over dark woollen hakama, her long white hair braided into a single heavy plait down her back to keep it clear of the wind, the small bronze bell at her hip catching a dull red glint from the brazier coals. Her red eyes met the torch beam without blinking.

Three paces behind her, Lynae leaned her forearms on her handlebars, blowing into her cupped palms as if she were half-asleep. From under the brim of her knitted cap, her eyes moved once, without her head turning, along the row of four sentries by the drop-bar—checking the salt-stained tide-marks on the leather of their boots, the way the third man on the left was shifting his weight off a chilblain on his heel, the standard-issue clips on their carbines, the frosted grease around the drop-bar pivot.

The sergeant lowered the torch two inches so it wasn't shining in Hiyuki's face. Whatever orders the Collective's sub-committee had sent down from the third floor in October, they had not included a form for turning a Miko of the Left Behind back at five-forty-five in the morning when she had a full professor's signature in her hand.

He flipped the clipboard over on his forearm, stamped the top sheet hard with the gate iron, tore off the pink flimsy for his spike, and handed the yellow and white copies back to Sigrika.

"Keep off the east verge past the third culvert, Madam," he said to Hiyuki, stepping back and waving two fingers at the sentry on the counterweight. "The frost got behind the retaining masonry on Tuesday night and dropped two tons of rubble into the ditch. When are you due back up?"

"Before noon tomorrow," Hiyuki said.

"I'm on the morning turn at six," the sergeant said, watching the steel bar swing up into the dark. "Don't make me send a sled down there, it's colder than a witch's well past the vents."

"Mm," Hiyuki said, and kicked the starter over.

Sigrika climbed onto the seat behind her, settling her satchel against her hip and taking hold of the steel grab-rail with both hands as Hiyuki let the clutch out.

They went down into the shaft.

The ancient stonework closed over their heads, the red gate lamps dropping behind them at the first turn, and the real cold hit them at the second gallery where the great ventilation adits opened to the mountain flank—so sharp that the inside of Sigrika's nose went stiff at the first breath and she had to duck her chin behind the back of Hiyuki's white shoulder.

Thirty metres behind them, Lynae's headlight swung round the descending curves in their wake. Every time the grade steepened toward a hairpin and Lynae touched the rear pedal to keep the eleven-kilo box from pushing her tail round on the frost, the back drum shoe let out a thin, high *eeee-ik* that ran thirty yards down the stone vault ahead of them.

On the third bend it did it twice.

Sigrika felt the breath go in and out of Hiyuki's back against her chest. Hiyuki's helmetless head did not turn by a fraction of a degree, and she did not alter her throttle hand on the grip; she simply took the fourth bend on the inside line in a silence so pointed that Sigrika had to bite the inside of her lip under her scarf to keep from laughing.

Down they went, past the deep galleries where the air pressure equalised with a dull click in their ears, past the rubble heap at the third culvert where orange warning lanterns burned in the frost, until the dressed masonry gave way to black natural rock and the mouth of the shaft opened out onto the Dimmr Plains.

Hiyuki cut the engine on the shelf. Behind them, Lynae rolled to a stop with one last small squeak of the rear shoe and clicked her ignition off.

The morning out on the plains was white.

The week of sub-zero air pouring down through the upper rifts had locked every floating island and fractured slate shelf in a two-inch coat of crystalline hoarfrost. There was no snow down here—only the lavender mist that rose from the abyss between the shelves, and as the mist touched the minus-fourteen stone it froze into long, feathered fern-fronds of violet-white ice along every ledge and crevice. Far out at the centre of the bowl, the vast gnarled trunk of the Solvein Heartwood stood sheeted in pale rime, its embedded eye dimmed to a milky winter pearl, the slow eleven-count coming up through the frozen bedrock under their boots like a clock in another room.

They rode the last three kilometres along the graded track to the Heartwood shelf, parked both machines under a low overhang of slate, and took the kit off the racks.

Before Sigrika had the canvas tripod bag unbuckled from the pannier rail, Lynae had already slipped both arms through the webbing harness of the pack-frame and hoisted the geophone case and the battery sleeve onto her back with one neat pop of her knees.

"Wait," Sigrika said, turning round with the tripod in her arms. "I'm signed on as the field operator, I'm supposed to carry the—"

"You've got the tripod, the cable reel, and about four kilos of dead lexicographers in that satchel," Lynae said, cinching the waist belt tight over her padded jacket and bouncing once on her toes to settle the box against her shoulder blades. "And I've got to pass the physical for the reserve track in December anyway. Consider me a pack mule with student ID. Which way's the path?"

They struck out south-southeast past the great roots of the Heartwood, Hiyuki leading at her steady, water-smooth pace, Sigrika in the middle with the folded wooden tripod across her shoulder, and Lynae two steps behind.

Every bootstep rang on the frost-locked slate like an iron poker dropped on an anvil. Their breath hung behind them in the windless air in small white clouds that took ten seconds to thin.

Where Hiyuki's boots touched the long feathers of hoarfrost along the path, no fresh ice formed around her soles at all; instead, the white needles at the very edge of her tread went dark and glossy for an instant before the air took them again, and Hiyuki walked on with her hands loose at her sides and her chin level.

Twenty minutes past the first scramble, where a spar of widow's ladder stood encased in clear ice like sugar-spun glass, something small and olive-brown flicked out from under a split boulder with a sharp, metallic *tsik-tsik* and went bounding low over the frost on two wire-thin legs.

Sigrika stopped so fast that Lynae nearly walked the geophone box into her shoulder blades.

"That's a rock pipit," Sigrika whispered, her eyes wide above her woollen scarf as she craned her neck after the boulder. "That cannot possibly be a littoral rock pipit, they winter on the kelp wrack down on the coast, what is it even *eating* down here—"

"Rocks," Lynae said, stepping round her on the shelf without breaking stride. "It's right there in the name, Siggy. Keep walking, if I stop moving with this battery against my kidneys I'm going to freeze to the frame."

"They're insectivores!" Sigrika called after her, hitching the tripod higher and hurrying to catch up while looking over her shoulder twice at the boulder. "There must be dormant midge larvae in the lichen crust by the vents—if a coastal passerine is using the Riseway shaft as a thermal chimney in November—"

"Write a letter to the bird paper when we get back to the radiator," Lynae said. "Watch your left boot, that slab's loose."

Half an hour further on, where the shelves flattened into the wide grey corridor before the outer floor, they came across the forty-metre stretch of plain where the departure loop had stood in the spring.

There was nothing there now.

The hoarfrost lay unbroken and even across the pulverised slate from one side of the shelf to the other; the pressure in their ears did not change by a fraction of a millimetre, and the air held no stain and no fold.

Nobody slowed down.

Without looking round or altering her stride, Hiyuki moved half a pace to her right so that she was walking directly beside Sigrika's shoulder, close enough that the white wool of her sleeve brushed Sigrika's elbow at every second step, and stayed there for the sixty paces it took to cross the flat to the rising ground beyond, before drifting quietly back to the lead on the climb toward the ridge.

They came up over the lip of the Borehole Four ridge at twenty to eleven.

Below them, the shallow bowl of the basin lay white from shore to shore. The frost had taken the water in a single still sheet—opaque grey-white ice three inches thick, dusted with frost-flowers that caught the pale pearl light from the distant tree—except dead in the middle of the bowl.

There, sixty metres out from the shingle, a circular hole about ten metres across stood completely open.

The water inside the ring was black as pitch and flat as an oil bath. Though the air over the basin was eleven degrees below zero, not a wisp of steam rose from the surface, and where the grey ice met the black water the edge was as clean and round as if it had been turned on a lathe.

Nobody said anything about it for the five seconds they stood on the crest.

"Ground's good here," Lynae said, tapping the heel of her boot against the flat shelf of clay and pulverised slate ten yards west of the old culvert post; it rang like a cast-iron stove lid. She unbuckled the pack-frame, lowered the aluminium case onto the canvas tarp, and drew the thirty-centimetre steel spike and the lump hammer from the side loop. "Where do you want the legs, Siggy?"

Sigrika set her satchel down, took a sighting across the basin with her prismatic compass to line the north-south mark on the tripod head up with the open water, and marked three points in the frost with the heel of her mitten.

Lynae knelt and drove three shallow conical seats into the frozen clay with the spike and hammer—*clink, clink, clink*, the sound bouncing once off the far southeastern spur of the basin and dying out—and Sigrika unfolded the heavy ash legs of the tripod and seated the brass shoes into the holes.

She pulled her mittens off, tucking them under her arm so her fingers wouldn't stiffen before she was done, lifted the brass geophone cylinder out of its foam cradle, and screwed it onto the centre post.

The spirit bubble was off to the northeast by half a ring. She loosened the left leg clamp, eased the strut down two millimetres against the frozen grit, tightened the new metric thumb-screw Lynae had put in, and leaned over the top cap until the little bead of air sat dead inside the etched black hairlines on all three axes.

"Set the battery boot on the felt pad," Sigrika said, her breath smoking over the brass flange as she plugged the multi-pin umbilical into the digital box and ran the black sensor cable down the bank to anchor it under three flat stones at the edge of the shingle.

When she came back up the bank and flipped the main toggle on the readout box, the red power diode came on steady and the three liquid-crystal bar graphs settled at the bottom of their scales, twitching once every second—*tick, tick, tick*—just above the noise floor.

Sigrika unclipped the hand-mic from the strut, pressed the transmit bar, and said, "Rim Station to Engineering South. Do you read, Professor?"

For two seconds there was only the dry hiss of the carrier wave across the four kilometres of glacier and rock to the Riseway relay. Then the speaker clicked.

"Engineering South," Mornye's voice said, crisp and small in the huge quiet of the plain. "Read you five-by-five, Candidate. Give me your tilt and voltage."

"Spirit bubble centred to the inner ring before power-on," Sigrika said, reading off the small screen with her bare thumb held inside her palm against the bite of the air. "Digital tilt reads zero-point-zero on X, plus zero-point-one on Y. Battery under load at twelve point six volts, casing insulated. Primary oscillation is visible on the vertical axis."

A pause of four seconds while the strip-recorder in Room 304 pulled paper.

"Telemetry locked on all three channels," Mornye said. "Your baseline is clean—six decibels quieter than the North Ridge array. Mark eleven hundred hours. Do not touch the tripod legs or the cable anchor until seventeen hundred. Call me at twelve. Mornye out."

Sigrika clipped the hand-mic back onto the strut, blew into her mittens, and pulled them back on.

"Six hours," Lynae said, rolling her shoulders now that the fourteen kilos were off her back. "Hiyuki's got the resin stove going behind that slab. Come get your fingers warm before they drop off."

"In a minute," Sigrika said, already reaching into her coat pocket for the folded slip of tracing paper from the Survey Office basement. "The culvert post is right over there."

She walked the fifty paces along the rim to the rusted iron stake stamped `14.2` that leaned out of the frozen gravel at the head of the old boundary dyke. Below the stake, where the dyke had slumped when the basin subsided decades ago, a long fan of frost-whitened greywacke talus spilled down the forty-degree bank toward the ice line.

Sigrika held the prismatic compass to her eye, sighted two hundred and five degrees south-southwest down the middle of the slide, and started down the scree.

Lynae came after her with the spare aluminium tripod leg unscrewed from the kit bag and a two-metre coil of cargo webbing over her shoulder. Hiyuki followed along the firmer ledge at the side of the slip, her pack unslung and the small shuttered oil lamp in her hand.

"Forty metres south-southwest in the old fissure scree," Lynae quoted, kicking the frost off a flat boulder that turned out to be native black slate. "Your surveyor in eighty-four had a very generous idea of a landmark. There's ten thousand rocks in this ditch."

"Greywacke breaks square," Sigrika said, thirty feet down the slope on her hands and knees by a heap of rubble, brushing hoarfrost off a block with her forearm. "And it'll have point-chisel marks along the arris—no, this one's a broken culvert cap, it's got mortar on the underside."

It took fifty minutes.

They worked their way down the fan in three overlapping lanes, their boots skittering on the frozen shale, turning up two rusted iron tie-rods from the old roadbed, a split mile-post with no numbers left on it, and four separate slabs of greywacke that were just unworked dyke-fill. Halfway down the lower pitch, Sigrika's left heel went out from under her on a patch of black glaze and she slid six feet on her coat-tails into a bank of frozen gravel, coming up with her woollen hat twisted sideways over one ear and a grey streak across her chin.

"Graceful," Lynae called from the ledge above. "Nine-point-two for form."

"Shut up and bring the spike," Sigrika said, not getting up from where she had landed.

She had both mittens against the underside of a flat, massive block of pale grey stone that lay wedged face-down against an outcrop of dark slate fifteen metres above the shore ice. Along the exposed upper edge, four inches clear of the frozen gravel, ran a straight, two-centimetre chamfer cut with a flat mason's chisel.

Lynae scrambled down the talus with the steel spike and the strap.

Together they chipped the frozen grit away from the upper lip of the stone until they could feed the loop of cargo webbing under its nose. Lynae jammed the heavy aluminium tube of the spare tripod leg over a boulder for a lever, took a turn of the strap round the shaft, and braced both boots against the slate outcrop while Sigrika took the free end of the webbing in both hands.

"Watch your toes," Lynae said. "One, two, *up*—"

They threw their weight back together. For two seconds the stone held fast in its fifty-year bed of ice; then the frost-seal under the belly parted with a sharp, dry *crack*, the slab came up on its lower edge, balanced for half a heart-beat against the fulcrum, and flopped over onto its back in the scree with a heavy thud that sent a puff of white frost-dust into the air.

Sigrika dropped the strap and went to her knees beside it.

The stone was a little over a metre long and ninety centimetres wide, split irregularly along its left-hand margin where the dyke had sheared it from its pair. In the flat, sourceless white light of the plains noon, the shallow grooves on the weathered face were almost invisible, lost in the grey grain of the rock.

Without a word, Hiyuki stepped down onto the gravel at the head of the slab, slid the shutter of her oil lamp back, struck a match against the brass base, and held the lamp two inches above the left-hand margin so the yellow flame threw a long, raking shadow across every depression in the surface.

Every stroke stood out black against the lit stone.

Sigrika pulled her right mitten off with her teeth, spat it into her lap, and brushed the loose frost crystals out of the upper left corner with the side of her thumb.

Right at the broken left edge, where a thin white vein of quartz crossed the fracture, stood two shallow strokes angled down: `//`.

"That's the join," Sigrika said. Her breath came out fast and white across the lamp glass. "Look at the quartz vein through the lower tick—it's the exact same four-millimetre band that runs off the right edge of your doorstep at the shrine. It's the same rock. They cut the sentence across the full width of the slab and then split it down the middle for the near and far posts."

Her bare index finger—the pale horizontal scar across the pad white with the cold—moved across the stone from left to right, following the straight, leaning staves cut into the greywacke.

"Read it," Hiyuki said quietly, holding the lamp steady against the rock.

Sigrika read it out loud, the way you read an unseen squeeze at the blackboard when the faculty is watching—first the staves as they stood, then the grammar:

"*Fyrir útan haldizt teigr at—*"

Her finger stopped five inches from the right-hand edge of the slab.

"*Fyrir útan* is the compound preposition," she said, her voice dropping into the flat, quick register she used when she had forgotten anyone was listening. "'Beyond here', 'on the outer side'. *Haldizt* is the third-person singular mediopassive of *halda*—'is kept', 'is held open'. *Teigr* is the nominative singular subject: 'the strip of field', 'the ground'. And then the preposition *at*, which takes the dative case for the person a piece of land is held in trust for."

Her finger did not move from the last word on the line.

On the wrist of every child in the Roya, at the top of the first spiral laid in soot and sheep-fat at six years old, that word was compressed into a single five-stave bind-mark with no preposition before it and no vowel-length stroke—an isolated cipher that had sat blank at Entry Twelve in Thorbergsen's grammar for sixty years because it had never once been found on stone.

Here, with a yard of flat greywacke at his disposal and a legal boundary formula to finish, the cutter had separated the bind-mark into its five constituent staves.

Across the third stave, cut deep with the corner of the chisel so the raking lamplight filled it like an ink-trench, ran the diagonal long-vowel stroke. And at the tail of the word, matching the preposition *at*, stood the single stave of the weak dative singular ending `-a`.

Sigrika stayed on her knees in the gravel without breathing.

"Siggy?" Lynae said from behind her shoulder, leaning on the aluminium tube. "What's the last word?"

"It's Number Twelve," Sigrika said.

Her voice sounded very clear and very small against the slate.

"The one from the wrist spiral," she said, touching the rock half an inch below the diagonal cross-stroke without putting her finger into the groove. "The one nobody in the valley can read because on skin it's written as a cipher at the end of the line with no grammar around it. Here it's written out in full in the dative slot after *haldizt at*."

She looked at the five staves, taking the morphology apart piece by piece in the yellow light of Hiyuki's lamp because the stone was in front of her and a scholar does not guess when the endings are on the rock.

"The root is *mæta*," she said. "To meet. With the dental suffix it's a passive participle, inflected in the dative singular to agree with *at*. And the cross-stroke through the third stave marks the long stem-vowel. It's the *stefnu*-mood—the meeting-mood. The same long vowel my grandmother used on the telephone."

Hiyuki did not move the lamp by a hair's breadth. "How does the whole formula read?"

Sigrika looked from the two continuation ticks at the fractured quartz vein on the left to the dative ending on the right.

"With the near stone on your step at the shrine," she said, "it runs across the break: *Halt. This far. Beyond here the field is kept for—*" She paused for one second to get the English of the mood exact. "*—for the one who is going to be met again.* We don't have a single word for the *stefnu* participle in Common. It means the person who hasn't arrived yet, and whose arrival isn't in question. The one expected back."

She sat back off her knees onto her heels in the frozen scree, her bare right hand resting flat on her coat knee.

Every grandmother in the Roya, sitting in a kitchen with a birch needle and a saucer of soot, had finished the first spiral on a six-year-old's wrist with a boundary-law participle that meant *the ground is being kept for when you come back*. And the slab of roadbed ballast at the top of the ridge—the threshold stone of a shrine with no lock on the door and a maintenance broom propped behind the post for whoever walked up the path—said *stop* on the doorstep, and the rest of the sentence down here in the gravel by the water said *except for whoever is on their way back*.

Sigrika's left hand went automatically to the cuff of her own coat, her thumb pressing through the heavy wool against the bare skin of her lower forearm and the two shallow continuation ticks where her surviving ink began.

In the kitchen in Bjartr, the clay jar of lamp-soot was sitting by the back door with its rim wiped clean, where a seventy-nine-year-old woman who would not speak into a telephone walked past it eleven times a day.

Hiyuki lowered the shutter of the lamp half an inch and looked down at the five staves in the greywacke.

"That is a better doorstep," Hiyuki said.

Lynae crouched at the corner of the slab, got two gloved fingers under the chamfered edge, and tested the weight; the stone didn't even shift in the grit. "Well, it's three hundred kilos of greywacke in a forty-degree ditch, so unless Hiyuki's bike has a crane attachment I don't know about, it's staying here."

"We don't move boundary stones," Sigrika said, reaching into her satchel for the cardboard tube and the cake of heel-ball wax. "You leave the far stone where it lies and you take an impression."

They unrolled the sheet of tough grey shrine paper across the face of the slab. Lynae held the top two corners pinned against the rock with her thumbs while Hiyuki weighted the bottom margin with the brass base of the lamp, and Sigrika worked the flat of the black wax cake across the paper in firm, overlapping diagonal strokes until every stave, the quartz vein at the fracture, the continuation mark, and the deep cross-stroke of the *stefnu* vowel stood out dark and crisp on the grey fibre.

When the rubbing was finished, Hiyuki laid the sheet flat on the smooth back of the outcrop beside them. She drew the small brass ink-case and short brush from her sleeve, knelt on the gravel, and wrote along the lower margin in her upright architectural characters: *25 November. Far stone of the threshold pair, Kilometre 14.2, Borehole Four scree, Dimmr Plains. Read in situ by S. Sigrika. Witnessed: Hiyuki.*

She breathed once on her vermilion seal, pressed it squarely below her name, and handed the sheet to Sigrika.

It was the first witness sheet Sigrika had asked for in four weeks, and when she rolled it loosely into the cardboard tube and buttoned it inside her coat against her ribs, her fingers did not shake at all.

Twenty feet up the bank, where Lynae had climbed back to retrieve the steel spike and coil the cargo strap, Lynae stood for half a minute with the strap over her elbow, not looking down at the slab—her eyes moving slowly along the high crest of the south ridge and the grey-white curve of the basin rim against the mist.

By twenty to five the light in the Heartwood's eye had sunk to a dull copper coal, and the lavender mist rising between the outer shelves had begun to give back its stored silver glow.

Up on the rim, with twenty minutes left before the six-hour baseline ended, Hiyuki was feeding another knot of smokeless resin onto the coal in the lee of the windbreak slab while Lynae checked the terminal grease on the battery pack.

"I'm going down to check the cable anchor before the seventeen-hundred call," Sigrika said, pulling her woollen hat down over her ears. "If the shore ice thickened since noon, it might be pinching the lead against the gravel."

She scrambled down the thirty-foot bank to the shingle at the edge of the basin.

The grey-white ice had crept another two inches up the stones in the afternoon freeze, locking the lowest anchor rock in a collar of clear rime. Sigrika crouched at the water's edge, eased the black rubber jacket of the cable free of a sharp corner of slate so it wouldn't chafe, and was just dusting the grit off her mittens when she heard footsteps crunching on the frozen shingle thirty yards to her left.

The young woman from the Tier Five sweet stall was walking along the shore.

She wore the same plain grey woollen shawl crossed neatly over her dark dress, no heavy coat, her brown hair braided low and pinned at the nape of her neck, and over her left forearm hung the flat wicker basket with a folded white linen napkin laid across it. Though she had come along a shore of frost-furred slate forty kilometres outside the city cordon in minus-eleven air, the hem of her skirt was completely dry and her light leather shoes carried not a speck of grey dust on the welts.

She saw Sigrika crouching by the sensor cable and stopped at once, her face brightening with easy, unhurried delight.

"Oh, hello!" she said, stepping across the shingle as comfortably as if they had met on the west arcade steps outside the stationer's. "It's you—the girl from the sweet stall! With the left eye. What a lovely surprise."

Sigrika stood up slowly beside the anchor stone, her hands half-raised in her mittens, staring from the grey shawl to the wicker basket. "Hello."

"Did the snow come on time last night?" the woman asked, stopping two paces off and tucking her ungloved hands into the warm fold of her shawl. "Up in the city. Somebody told me they'd put a notice up at the Exchange for seven o'clock."

"To the minute," Sigrika said. "Seven o'clock. Six centimetres."

"Good," the woman said, giving a small, pleased nod as though that settled a question they had been discussing indoors.

Sigrika glanced past her shoulder along the bare, frost-whitened curve of the basin, trying to remember where the Survey Office's hydrological huts were marked on the eighty-four map. "Did the boiled sweets go down all right?"

The woman laughed—clear and warm in the still air. "Oh, we haven't opened the bag yet. They're for breakfast, and breakfast has been running a little late this week. I've put them in the glass dish on the counter by the door so nobody misses them on the way in."

She lifted the corner of the white linen napkin on her basket and drew out two large purple figs, their dark skins plump and unwrinkled, each sitting on its own broad green leaf. She held them out across the space between them.

"Here," she said. "For your supper. And give the second one to your tall friend with the white hair up on the ridge—she looks as though she appreciates fruit that hasn't been in a cold-store."

Sigrika held out both palms and took the leaves. Through the wool of her mittens, the figs were faintly warm, as if they had been picked an hour ago off a south-facing brick wall in August.

"Thank you," Sigrika said. "Are you—do you have a lamp for the walk back to the station? The light goes down at seven, and the cordon guards at the Riseway—"

"Oh, I'm just across the way," the woman said cheerfully. "No distance at all."

She turned her head for a moment and looked out across the grey-white sheet of the ice toward the ten-metre circle of open black water in the middle of the basin. Her expression was quiet and entirely ordinary—neither solemn nor troubled, simply the look of a person glancing at a kettle on the hob or checking that an upstairs window has been left on the latch.

"Mind the gravel on the bank if you come down in the dark," she told Sigrika with her tidy nod. "Good evening!"

She turned and walked on at an unhurried stroll along the curving shingle toward the far southeastern side of the basin, where a low spur of fractured slate ran out into the silver mist.

Sigrika stood by the cable anchor for five seconds with a warm fig in each hand, then turned and climbed back up the thirty-foot bank to the rim.

At the top of the bank, Lynae was standing on the flat rock beside the tripod with her compact field-glasses held in her right hand, staring out across the basin toward the southeastern spur with the colour drained clean out of her face.

"Who was that," Lynae said.

Her voice was flat and hard as the clay under their boots.

"The woman from the sweet stall on Thursday," Sigrika said, coming up onto the shelf and holding out the two green leaves. "In the Tier Five market—remember? The one who asked me to test the lemon-acid drops. She must be keeping house for the hydrology crew over on the south cut. She gave us figs."

Lynae looked down at the two dark purple figs on Sigrika's mittens. Then she lifted her eyes back to the southeastern shore.

The shingle along the far curve of the ice was bare, frost-whitened stone for half a kilometre in every direction. There was no hut, no trail, and nobody walking on it.

Lynae had been sitting on this rim with an unobstructed three-hundred-degree view of every ridge and scree-chute since eleven in the morning. Nobody had come down the north dyke, nobody had crossed the eastern flats from the Heartwood, and nobody had walked out behind the spur.

For the space of two breaths Lynae's thumb pressed so hard against the brass hinge of the field-glasses that the knuckle went white. Then she blew air out through her nose, dropped the glasses into her coat pocket, and put a crooked grin on her mouth.

"Right," Lynae said. "The hydrology crew. Famous for growing fresh August figs in a permafrost sinkhole. Did you check if she had hooves under that hem, Siggy, or were you too busy comparing grocery lists?"

"She was very polite," Sigrika said, frowning at her, and carried the larger fig over to Hiyuki by the resin fire. "She said this one was for you."

Hiyuki looked at the dark purple fruit resting on its broad green leaf, and then lifted her red eyes across the basin toward the southeastern spur and the open circle of black water in the middle of the ice.

She took the leaf from Sigrika, peeled the skin back from the stem in three neat strips with her thumbnail, and ate the fig in silence. When she had finished, she wiped her fingertips on a square of tissue and folded the green leaf twice before setting it in her pack.

"It is a very good fig," Hiyuki said, and remained looking across the ice at the far shore for ten full seconds after she had spoken.

At seventeen hundred hours the eye in the Heartwood dimmed to its night ember, the silver ceiling of the mist closed over the shelves, and the thermometer on the tripod strut dropped past minus twelve.

The transceiver box gave two sharp clicks.

"Engineering South to Rim Station," Mornye's voice said through the static. "Six-hour baseline complete. Stand by on frequency while I run the cross-correlation."

Sigrika, Hiyuki, and Lynae sat close round the smokeless resin coal in the lee of the windbreak slab, their breath hanging white between their faces while the carrier wave hummed in the dark. Upstairs in Room 304, four kilometres away, they could hear the rapid, rhythmic *clack-clack-clack* of Mornye's mechanical calculator and the scrape of a steel ruler on graph paper.

Three minutes passed. Then the microphone keyed again.

"Rim Station, here is the reduction," Mornye said. Her voice was quiet, level, and stripped to pure measurement. "Triangulating your three axes against the South Weir and North Ridge sensors places the primary oscillation at eleven point eight metres below the bedrock floor of the basin, directly beneath the central opening in the ice. The acoustic reflections around the source do not correspond to solid granite or natural karst. The boundary returns meet at ninety-degree angles. It reads as a rectilinear structural void with plane walls."

Sigrika had her journal open on her lap and the red pen in her mittened hand; the ink was cold-thickened in the nib, so she had to score the paper hard to get the numbers down.

"There is a second finding at the bottom of the short-baseline trace," Mornye went on after a pause. "Within a fifteen-metre horizontal radius of the primary source, the three-axis head resolves five distinct low-amplitude acoustic signatures at the floor of the array. They have no periodicity. They do not keep a fixed count. They displace laterally inside the rectilinear boundary at irregular intervals."

Sigrika pressed the transmit bar on the hand-mic. "Displace how, Professor? Like fluid percolation?"

Two seconds of flat carrier hiss came back from Engineering South.

"I am speaking on an unencrypted municipal band, Candidate, and I do not traffic in analogies," Mornye said tartly. "They move from one side of the room to the other, stop, and move again. That is the whole of the physical observation. Switch the battery lead over to the reserve pack and leave the geophone running through the night so I have a twelve-hour nocturnal trace to set against the April tape. I have a camp bed in 304 and the speaker is on my desk. Next scheduled call is oh-six-hundred. Mornye out."

The radio clicked back to standby.

Sigrika closed her journal over her red pen, swapped the umbilical plug onto the second battery sleeve in its felt-lined boot, and came back to the fire to eat her fig and a roll of barley flatbread with Halla's mutton. The fig tasted of late summer and warm stone; she ate it in two bites before remembering to go slowly, and licked the sugar off her thumb.

By eight they had unrolled the insulated sleeping bags under the overhang of the windbreak slab.

Sigrika crawled into the inner bag with her woollen hat pulled down to her eyebrows, her boots tucked into the foot-sack so the felt liners wouldn't freeze stiff, and her right hand resting inside her coat on the cardboard tube that held the rubbing of the far stone. After fourteen hours in the cold she was asleep in four minutes.

On the outer edge of the shelf, Hiyuki lay down on her woven ground-cloth with her white cloak drawn over her shoulders and her back to the low resin coal, her red eyes lowered toward the grey slate at the lip of the bank, not quite closed.

Lynae took the middle watch.

At two in the morning the silver mist hung motionless over the basin, and the only light on the rim was the pale violet glow from the coal and the tiny red diode blinking once every ten seconds on the geophone box.

Down at the foot of the ridge, where the old dyke track met the plains road, a boot crunched once on frozen gravel.

Someone came walking up the forty-degree scree slope toward the rim—not picking a route, not using her hands to steady herself on the outcrops, simply walking up the shifting frost-rimed talus at the even, unhurried pace of a person coming up the library stairs after lunch.

She stepped onto the shelf at the edge of the silver light: a woman in a dark woollen coat with the collar turned up and a canvas book-bag slung over one shoulder, carrying a tall stainless-steel thermos in her gloved hand.

She was wearing the pleasant, slightly tired face of a thirty-year-old clerk from the third-tier municipal offices—the same coat from the telephone kiosk on the fourth terrace, the same dry leather welts on her boots despite the two inches of hoarfrost on the scree.

Lynae knew her the second her shoulders cleared the bank.

Sitting cross-legged on the corner of her tarp between the resin coal and Sigrika's head, Lynae did not reach for the knife in her boot welt or the flare pistol in the bike pannier, because she had spent enough years in the trade to know when a reach was just a way of dying with your arm tired. Her spine came up half an inch against the rock behind her, her left heel tucking under her hip so her body stayed squarely across the line to Sigrika's sleeping bag.

The woman in the dark coat stopped at the edge of the shelf, glanced once at the dark woollen hump of Sigrika's shoulder to make sure the blanket was tucked in at the neck, and sat down on a flat boulder across the resin coal from Lynae as though they had agreed on two o'clock in advance.

She unscrewed the brushed-steel cup from the top of the thermos without a squeak of metal and set it on the stone between her boots.

"Good evening, Naeun," she said, her voice pitched in a low, courteous register calibrated to carry across three feet of cold air and no further. "You needn't keep your weight on your left heel like that; my hands are on my knees, and I have no intention of waking her."

Lynae did not take her weight off her left heel. "You were at the kiosk by the Tier Three steps."

"I was consulting the tram timetable," the woman said pleasantly. "You noticed the dry welts on my boots from the far side of the tracks while appearing to listen to an account of a dead epigrapher's eyesight, and then waited until the car cleared the bathhouse curve before crossing over. When the Arbiter left you on this post before going east, I assumed the choice was largely sentimental. It turns out to have been very sound staffing."

Lynae's jaw went tight; the approval sat on her skin like cold fat. "What are you doing out here."

The woman looked past the blue-white ember of the resin knot at the curve of Sigrika's woollen hat, where one cropped braid-end lay against the canvas of the bedroll.

"Is she eating properly?" she asked.

Four seconds went by in the silver dark, because it was the exact question Lynae asked herself at seven every morning while she chopped greens into Rebecca's pot, and hearing it spoken in that precise, polite cadence with no mockery in it made the back of Lynae's throat go sour.

"She eats fine," Lynae said.

"That is the answer she gives," the woman noted mildly. "I was hoping for a more objective report, given that you feed her on Wednesdays." She inclined her head a fraction toward the wooden tripod standing ten feet away on the frozen clay, its red diode winking in the dark. "What is Professor Mornye's instrument reading?"

"Rocks," Lynae said. "Go ask Mornye."

The woman smiled slightly—unhurried, entirely unoffended. "I would rather not disturb Engineering South at two in the morning; she has very firm views on unscheduled visitors. As for the basin, I only walked up from the road to look at it from the rim. I shall not go down to the shingle."

She turned her head and looked out over the dark drop of the bank at the pale grey floor of the ice and the ten-metre circle of black water open at its centre.

"The shore is spoken for," she said in the same even tone, and added nothing to explain what she meant.

Without turning her head toward the outer edge of the shelf where Hiyuki lay with her back to the coal, she raised her voice by half a tone: "I am wearing a clerk from the municipal water board tonight, Shrine-Keeper. No one you have ever known, and no one you have buried."

From the white cloak on the outer ground-cloth, without a fold of the wool shifting, Hiyuki said:

"Good."

Nothing else.

They sat across the low coal in the minus-twelve dark. Nobody spoke; nobody mentioned the dormitory, or the eastern provinces, or the rooms under the water. Two metres away, Sigrika let out a long breath in her sleep, shifted her cheek against her arm where the cardboard tube lay inside her coat, and went on sleeping.

At eleven minutes past three, the small speaker on the transceiver box clipped to the tripod leg gave a dry static *pop*.

Mornye's voice came out into the night—wide awake, crisp, speaking on the open frequency to the shelf and to whoever else had an ear on forty-one point four megahertz:

"Engineering South to Rim Station. Unscheduled log entry, oh-three-eleven hours. At oh-three-oh-eight, the primary floor oscillation ceased entirely for three point eight seconds. It resumed at sixty-six cycles per minute for a duration of ninety-two seconds, and has now settled on a new baseline of sixty-one point two cycles per minute. Variance is logged to the permanent tape. Do not adjust the gain. Mornye out."

The relay clicked off.

Across the resin coal, for the space of one second, the thirty-year-old clerk's face on the woman in the dark coat stopped working—the small muscles at the corners of the mouth and the lower eyelids going completely blank, stripped of four centuries of timing, like a clockwork movement that has slipped a tooth on the main wheel.

It was the first unperformed thing Lynae had ever seen on any face she wore.

Then the woman looked down at her gloved hands resting on her knees.

"I built that frequency," she said, so quietly that the words barely crossed the resin coal, spoken almost to the frost between her boots. "I calibrated the resting rate myself on the bench before the collar was closed. The number I set was fifty-two."

Four full seconds went past in the silver dark. From the Heartwood far out on the plain, the slow eleven-count came up through the rock and died away.

"Fifty-six in April," she said to the coal. "Fifty-eight on Tuesday. Sixty-one tonight. None of those numbers is anything I made."

She stood up, smoothing the front of the dark woollen coat with the practical, slightly stiff motion of a third-tier clerk rising from a bench at the end of her lunch hour. She screwed the steel cup back onto the top of the thermos and left the cylinder standing upright on the flat boulder where she had been sitting.

"The tea is clean," she told Lynae with the same exact courtesy she had brought up the slope. "It is black tea from the commissary annex, and your wrists have been shivering since half past two." She paused at the lip of the scree, looking once more at the bundled woollen shape under the overhang. "I would ask you not to mention to Sigrika that I walked up here tonight."

"I wasn't going to tell her a damn thing," Lynae said between her teeth.

"Yes," the woman said gently. "I know. That is the job."

She stepped off the shelf onto the frosted talus. Her boots made two quiet scrapes on the loose greywacke in the mist, and when Lynae got to her feet three seconds later and looked over the edge of the bank down the white slope to the culvert track, there was nothing moving between the basin and the Heartwood.

Lynae sat back down on the corner of the tarp. Her hands were shaking so hard inside her sleeves that she had to jam both wrists between her knees and press her boots into the gravel to keep her teeth from chattering out loud.

From the outer ground-cloth, without turning round, Hiyuki said:

"Mm."

Lynae let her breath out in a long, thin scrape through her teeth. "Yeah."

When the eye in the Heartwood began to redden toward its six o'clock relighting and the silver mist thinned to a flat pewter grey, Lynae was still sitting in the same place with her chin on her knees.

She looked at the stainless-steel thermos standing on the boulder across the cold ash of the resin knot for ten minutes. Then she reached over, unscrewed the cup, poured four fingers of steaming amber tea, and drank it down before Sigrika's sleeping bag stirred. It was scalding hot, dark and faintly smoky, and better than anything in the dormitory cupboard. She rinsed the cup out with a handful of clean hoarfrost, tucked the thermos into the bottom of her pannier roll where Sigrika wouldn't see it, and stood up stretching her shoulders just as Sigrika unzipped her bag and sat up blinking in the grey light.

At oh-six-hundred Mornye called on the radio, confirmed the nocturnal tape was complete and holding steady at sixty-one point two cycles a minute, and cleared them to strike the tripod.

Sigrika pulled her boots on, took her notebook, and scrambled down the bank to the shore to free the sensor cable from the anchor stones.

Halfway through winding the stiff black rubber loop around her elbow and mitten, she stopped and looked out across the grey-white skin of the basin toward the centre.

Where the inner lip of the ice met the ten-metre circle of open black water, a collar of clear, glass-smooth new ice about a foot wide had formed overnight in the minus-twelve freeze. And frozen into that clear new glaze right along the water's edge—not piled together by a drift, but spaced out along the curve with their faces turned straight up toward the mist ceiling—were seven large, deep-red flowers with dark stamped centres, their five wide petals open flat under the surface of the ice.

They had not been there at dusk.

Sigrika laid the cable coil down on the shingle, walked ten paces out onto the thick shore ice, and crouched to look at them through her pocket glass. Then she pulled her journal and the green pen from her coat.

She flipped to the green hypothesis pages at the back and wrote with cold-stiffened fingers: *26 Nov, 06:20. Borehole 4 basin, central ice margin: 7 specimens, corolla deep crimson, dark central disk, 5-merous, encased in overnight surface glaze. Absent at 16:40 yesterday. Flora, red, 7, in ice. Unidentified.*

She stared at the line for five seconds, her brows drawn together under her woollen hat.

"They look like day-hibiscus or rock-rose," she muttered to the ice, genuinely annoyed. "Neither of those grows within eight hundred kilometres of the frost line, and they only keep one day's hours before the corolla drops."

She put the journal away, picked up the cable coil, and climbed back up the bank to the shelf, where Lynae was snapping the brass toggles shut on the geophone case.

"Did you sleep at all?" Sigrika asked, handing her the coiled lead to stow in the side slot. "It got cold after midnight."

"Like a baby," Lynae said easily, latching the lid down. "Didn't hear a thing all night."

At the end of the shelf, Hiyuki tied the neck of her pack and said nothing at all.

Before they started back toward the Heartwood, Sigrika walked twenty paces down the top of the south-southwest scree chute to where the far stone lay turned face-up in the gravel, its five separated staves at the right-hand end already dusted over again with fresh white hoarfrost.

She crouched beside the slab and picked up a chunk of frost-split greywacke about the size of her fist—heavy and dark, with a rough orange crust of dead lichen on the weathered side and a clean, glittering break of quartz on the other—and dropped it into her left coat pocket for Rebecca.

She started to straighten up, paused with her mitten hovering over the talus, and picked up a second piece—smaller, flat and oval as a river pebble, split off the same bed—and slipped it into her right-hand pocket without giving it a name or a reason.

The walk back to the Heartwood went fast on the descending grade, their boots ringing out across the frozen shelves while the eye in the great tree brightened behind them. They strapped the case and the tripod to the racks, rode up the long echoing throat of the Riseway—Lynae's rear brake silent on the climb—and rolled out under the red lamps of the cordon gate at half past eleven.

The same sergeant was standing by the drop-bar with a tin mug of soup in his hand; when he saw Hiyuki's headlight come round the top gallery and counted three riders intact, his shoulders came down two inches inside his greatcoat, and he signed off the yellow permit without even checking the seal on the aluminium box.

Inside the gate, while Sigrika and Hiyuki wheeled the bikes toward the east maintenance shed to hand Mornye's case over to the engineering runner, Lynae stopped by the steam-pipe stanchion with one boot on her pedal and drew her Terminal from her inner pocket.

She opened the encrypted relay line to Xuanfang Hold, typed one line to Mei—*She was in the city. Came up to the rim at 02:00, listened to the 03:11 radio check, didn't go near the kid, left before dawn.*—hit send, locked the screen, and caught up with Sigrika by the greenhouse corner with her hands back in her jacket pockets.

By nine o'clock on Friday night the soup at the house had been eaten, the mutton bone boiled down for Saturday's stock, and the bowls washed and stacked.

Rebecca had received the fist-sized chunk of frost-split greywacke at the kitchen table in both hands, turning it over under the pendant lamp like an appraiser with a diamond, declared the crust of dead orange lichen on the back "gnarly as hell", and set it in the exact middle of the windowsill between the lute case and the string bag of garlic.

Now Sigrika was back in her own room down the hall, the radiator under the casement ticking steadily against the second night of the winter schedule, her woollen cardigan still smelling faintly of Hiyuki's pine-resin fire.

On the corner of her blotter, beside the stoneware jar of Halla's berry jam and her three aligned pens, sat the second, smaller piece of flat greywacke from her right-hand pocket.

She took her field journal out of her satchel, laid the cardboard tube containing the sealed rubbing beside the lamp base, and opened the back cover to the black card endpaper.

She lifted the folded compliment slip from Window Three—I. Fenn's nine words—and set it carefully to one side on the blotter so the black card lay flat and unobstructed.

When she tilted the open cover thirty degrees toward the amber bulb of the desk lamp, the matte white pigment she had written there in the spring caught the raking light:

*[1 word, illegible]*

and after the closing bracket, at the right-hand margin of the page, the two shallow strokes angled down.

In epigraphic convention, a square bracket marks a lacuna where the surface has perished; when a second attestation of the formula is recovered on a pair stone, the editor enters the restoration inside the bracket and cites the stone in the margin.

Sigrika picked up Rebecca's white paint-marker from the pencil groove, shook it twice until the small metal ball inside clicked cleanly against the barrel, and pulled the cap off.

Directly beneath the spring line, where the lamp caught the grain of the black card at an angle, she set a new pair of square brackets. Inside them, in neat, upright staves with the diagonal long-vowel stroke cut squarely through the third letter, she wrote the twelfth word from the far stone at Borehole Four, and beside it in her smallest apparatus hand: *cf. km 14.2, far stone; stefnu part. dat. sg. ("expected back")*.

After the closing bracket, at the very edge of the black card, she drew the two shallow continuation strokes angled down—leaving them standing at the break, because the two strokes at the edge of the surviving ink on her own left forearm were still there, and a sentence with the go-on at the end is not finished yet.

She blew on the white paint for ten seconds until the titanium pigment dried flat and invisible against the black card except when tilted to the bulb, laid Fenn's compliment slip back in its place over the endpaper, and closed the journal.

She pressed the cap of the white marker all the way down with both thumbs until it snapped.

Outside the glass, the second night of the Weather Office's posted snow was coming down through the amber cones of the avenue lamps—six centimetres nominal, steady through Sunday—settling on the bare branches of the Solistrees and along the fourth-terrace tram wires, while on the corner of the desk beside the berry jam the small flat piece of plains slate sat warming slowly to the temperature of the room.

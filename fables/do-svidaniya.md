## i.

The room was the white of things before they are named.

She woke smiling. The machines had known for an hour; the smile was for the machines. Above her, a ceiling of forty-eight tiles, and by the second day she had named them all after desserts, because a ceiling you can name is a ceiling you can live under, and she had exactly seven days of living to arrange.

Seven. She had audited the number twice. Under her heartbeat ran a second arithmetic, patient, professional, counting down — a collar fastened at the moment of adoption. That is what adoption meant, where she was made: at the hour she accepted the gift, a hand had closed around her frequency and kept a copy of the key. The gift had gone home to its true vessel through a hole in the world. The hand remained. The collar had begun, politely, to close.

Through the glass, the Spacetrek Collective came and went in laundered coats. They held clipboards at the angle of people who believe angles are neutral. *Subject declines contact*, one wrote, and she read it upside down and corrected the spelling in her head. They needed her — the collar was a machine of a kind their instruments had circled for years, and here it was, installed, live, wearing a girl. They said *cooperation* through the intercom in the tone of people offering water in a desert they had mapped and owned.

She turned her face to the tile called Mille-Feuille and performed sleep. She was extremely good at it. She had rehearsed in a hundred lectures.

On the third day a supervisor with silver temples pressed the intercom and explained, with real feeling, everything her data could prevent.

"There will be others," he said. "Others like you."

"Then be kinder to them," she said, to Mille-Feuille, "than you are being to me," and the intercom clicked off, and the smile stayed where it was, gentle, omnipresent, doing its work.

## ii.

Sigrika rehearsed in the corridor.

Through the glass Denia watched her mouth move — paragraph one, paragraph two, back to one, a small frown, a correction, the whole document run twice with her chin set at the angle of a girl who has decided to try her hardest, which was the only angle Sigrika's chin had ever learned. She had a folder. Of course she had a folder. She had probably formatted it.

The door hissed. Sigrika sat in the visitor's chair as if the chair were an examination, folded her hands, and began.

"The Collective has asked me to convey—"

"You skipped paragraph three."

Sigrika stopped.

"The one where they promise it won't hurt," Denia said. "You skipped it in the corridor too. Both times."

"...It's the only part I couldn't say with a straight face."

"It's the only lie in the document." Denia tilted her head; a strand of hair went behind her ear, idle, ancient habit. "The rest is just optimism with a budget. Sit closer. You're holding the folder like a shield."

Sigrika set the folder down. Under it her hands were shaking, and had been, Denia understood, for the whole rehearsal, for the whole corridor, possibly for weeks.

"You called twenty-three times," Denia said.

"Twenty-four. One didn't connect."

"It connected." A beat. "I was asleep. I heard it anyway. It sounded like you."

Sigrika's face did the thing it did in examinations when a question arrived from outside the syllabus: a flicker of panic, then the decision to answer anyway. "They said you were refusing everyone. I told them you'd refuse me too. They sent me anyway. I'm the — " her mouth found the word and hated it — "the *leverage*."

"You're the leverage," Denia agreed, pleasantly.

"I know what this is. I know what they're doing. I read the request three times and I still came, and I hate that I came, and I'd come again." The words arrived in a rush, exam-hall speed, ink before the bell. "So refuse me. Say it to my face and I'll go back and tell them their leverage is defective, and they can—"

"Thursday is my birthday."

The sentence sat down between them and folded its hands.

"...What?"

"Thursday." Denia examined her own fingernails with vast leisure. "I'd like to spend it at school. With you. A regular day — classes, the courtyard, the terrible cafeteria custard, people. One day, start to finish. Then the Collective can have all the rest. The mapping, the marrow, whatever the needles want. Everything. I'll sign it in triplicate. I'll sign it in *quadruplicate*, which I suspect is a word I just invented."

Sigrika stared at her.

"That's the counter-offer," Denia said. "You came to negotiate. Negotiate."

"You're — " Sigrika's voice performed steadiness, a brave front over open water. "You're serious."

"I'm a professional slacker with one week of runway. I have never been more serious about anything, which is admittedly a low bar."

"...The doctors will say it's a risk."

"The doctors say the ceiling is a risk. Thursday, Sigrika."

Sigrika looked at her for a long moment — at the smile, at the sum under it, at the whole gentle architecture — and Denia watched her decide, visibly, the way she decided about blank exam questions: that an attempt entered in the wrong formula was worth infinitely more than an empty box.

"Deal," Sigrika said, and put out her hand, and Denia shook it the way you shake something you are memorizing.

At the door, folder retrieved, chin re-set, Sigrika paused. "Why Thursday?"

"It's my birthday," Denia said. "Keep up."

The door hissed shut. Denia lay back and looked at Mille-Feuille and let the smile rest — one second, two — before putting it back on for the machines.

She had picked Thursday because Sigrika's timetable was free.

---

For three days, then, she was legible.

She let them read her. Needles, arrays, the long cold tunnel of the resonance mapper, her frequency laid out on their screens like a coastline surveyed at last. She made jokes into the recording microphones so that the archive would have something kind in it, for later, for whoever had to listen. When the technicians winced on her behalf she reassured them in the tone of a hostess, and one of them, a boy barely older than she looked, had to leave the room, and she pretended for him that she had noticed the ceiling instead.

"The collar," said the supervisor with silver temples, on the second evening, studying the map. "It completes on frequency failure. Wherever you are."

"Wherever I am," she agreed.

"The discharge signature matches the residue at the Stridergate seam." He said it carefully, a man setting down glass. "It wants to go home."

"Everything answers to something," Denia said, and asked for the light to be dimmed, and named the last four tiles — Pavlova, Kasutera, Medovik, Snow — and slept, for once, honestly.

## iii.

Thursday came up mild on the artificial sky, and the Collective sent a shadow.

The shadow had a rifle, excellent manners, a scar with a story it declined to tell, and a fixed distance of forty meters. She had signed the escort forms as *Leva* and everything else on the page as a line drawn through the box. At the ward door she looked Denia over once, top to bottom, the assessing sweep of a professional, and said only: "Recovery outing."

"Routine," Denia agreed.

"Nothing about you is routine."

"That's the pretense, sweetheart. Sign here."

Leva signed. Something moved through her scarred face — recognition, or its cousin — and she took up her forty meters like a coat she had worn all her life.

The Academy in mid-morning: bells, holo-boards, the smell of solder and cheap noodles, the crowd's ten thousand small collisions. Sigrika walked at her elbow with the posture of a bodyguard and the vocabulary of a tour guide, narrating renovations Denia had been present for, and Denia let her, because the narration was for Sigrika, a rope of words to hold, and Denia had spent her whole life leaving ropes where people's hands would be.

First stop, by request: Auditorium C. The public lecture. Thermodynamic Ethics, Professor Halvard, attendance credits for the desperate.

Denia took her old seat, fourth row, aisle, dozed off inside six minutes, and jolted awake at minute nineteen against the seat-back with a crack that turned forty heads. The laugh went up — the old laugh, the one with her name in it. Halvard peered over his glasses.

"Miss Denia graces us."

"Present," she said, rubbing her head, and bowed sitting down, and the row applauded softly, and she memorized the laugh the way other people photograph things. On the way out, a boy from the back called *dream well!* and she turned and gave him the smile and said, "Wake me when it matters," and the whole doorway laughed, and she filed the joke as delivered: a goodbye, wearing a bit.

The study room next, third floor, the corner crew — Petra, Ilyos, Wen — barricaded behind Advanced Royan Syntax and losing. Denia surveyed the wreckage of flashcards.

"Drop it," she advised, sliding into her chair. "Take Intro to Napping. Running away makes everything so much easier."

"You always say that," said Wen.

"Because it's always true," said Denia, and picked up the flashcards, and quizzed them until noon, correcting Ilyos's declensions with the patience of tide. When she stood to go, her annotated notes — three semesters of margins, the good marginalia, the kind people photocopy — were in the bottom of Petra's bag, deposited there by a hand nobody saw. They would find them in a week and understand in a month.

"Next year," she announced from the doorway, "we're all taking Napping. I'll tutor."

"You'll sleep through your own tutorial."

"That's the curriculum."

They laughed. She kept it.

In the medical annex corridor, a candy dish stood on the counter outside Dr. Herssen's door, refilled that morning, as it was every Tuesday and, lately, every day. Denia took two, considered, and put one back.

Luuk was in the doorway. White gloves; eyes that had kept watch over this city for twenty years and were currently declining to make her say anything.

"For the other thief," she said.

"Tuesdays," he said.

"Noted," she said, and pocketed the sour one, and walked on, and behind her the doctor stood in his doorway for a long time with his gloved hand resting on the rim of the dish.

---

The photograph happened in the west courtyard, under the flowering ash.

She arranged them — the study crew, two dormmates, the girl from the aviary club whose name was a bird call, Sigrika at her right. She arranged them the way she did everything, lazily, precisely, a director pretending to be a guest. Then she stepped into the frame and left a gap at her left shoulder. A person-sized gap.

Sigrika looked at the gap. Sigrika, who kept in her desk drawer a photograph of three girls with *Best friends forever!* in her own handwriting across the bottom, a photograph in which one girl was dying and one girl had been a costume with centuries inside it — Sigrika looked at the gap, and understood it, and kept the understanding, and stood a little closer to the edge of it, so that the gap would read, later, as almost filled.

"Everyone say *credits*," said Denia.

The shutter clicked. Somewhere it is still the courtyard, still Thursday, still almost.

---

At the terminal booths by the east gate she made a phone call.

"Mm — mama. Yes. Yes, I'm eating. The custard, mostly. Thursday, that's right — no, don't send anything, the postage costs more than I'm worth—" a laugh, pitched for an audience of one, tilted so it would carry through the booth glass — "next year. I promise. Tell papa. Mm. Love you too."

Sigrika stood outside the booth, close enough to be kind, far enough to be polite, and through the glass, on the terminal's face, the call clock read 00:00, unconnected, and had read it the whole time.

Sigrika looked away first.

She held the looking-away very carefully, both hands, all afternoon.

---

In the aviary courtyard a groundskeeper was scattering seed.

New face. Volunteer's vest. A wrist that turned exactly so at the end of the throw, a metronome of a wrist, and Denia had watched that wrist feed these birds under three different faces across three semesters.

She drifted over. Leva's forty meters held; Sigrika was at the fountain, journaling a finch.

"New job?"

"Volunteering," said the groundskeeper. "Birds skip the credential check."

"They should run one. You'd pass. You always do."

The groundskeeper tilted her head. Denia tilted hers, the same angle, the same tempo — the mannerism meeting its manufacturer — and for a moment the courtyard held two copies of one gesture and the birds ate on, unmoved by provenance.

"Thursday, then," the groundskeeper said.

"You built the calendar."

"I built everything, dear." Seed left the wrist, a perfect arc. "The collar, the coma, the week. I confess the birthday surprised me. A nice flourish. Whose idea?"

"Mine. I'm told I take after my maker."

A bird landed between them and the groundskeeper watched it with the interest of a woman inventorying a prop. "You understand it changes nothing. The play closes Thursday night. It was always going to. The venue was booked before you were."

"I know," said Denia. "I read the contract from the inside." She reached out then — unhurried, the way she did everything — and laid two fingers on the volunteer's sleeve, on the wrist, on the pulse a thousand faces had shared.

The groundskeeper went still the way theaters go still.

"Thank you for the seating chart," Denia said. "Whatever you meant by it."

"I meant a tragedy."

"You'll get one." She lifted her fingers, tucked the strand behind her ear, and gave her maker the smile the maker had installed. "Watch closely."

"I always do," said the Grand Architect, and scattered seed, and was, by the time Sigrika looked up from the finch, a stranger in a vest whom nobody would remember hiring.

---

On the long bench by the reflecting pool she found the Rover.

Mei sat the way the whole campus knew she sat now: coffee going cold in her hands, gold eyes on the middle distance, a woman with a daughter home and a body that had stopped needing to run. She looked up. Recognition crossed her face and pulled several other things behind it.

"You're awake."

"Barely. It's a lifestyle." Denia sat, uninvited, comfortable, and let the pool do a minute of the talking. "I saw the broadcast, you know. In the ward, they loop the good ones. Your daughter jumping. Eyes shut, arms out." She turned the image over with something close to reverence. "Best thing I've ever seen."

"She'd be embarrassed to hear it."

"She should be proud. Everyone should be exactly what they are at maximum volume once." Denia stretched, laced her fingers, and delivered the payload the way she delivered everything, wrapped in idleness. "I owe your pack. The strings. Tell the one with the scissors: clean edges. A professional appreciates it."

"Tell her yourself. She's in the east commons every—"

"And tell the wolf," Denia went on, gently, over her, "that it counted. Eleven days on my own hands. Best days on record."

Mei set the coffee down. The bench got smaller. "Denia."

"Mm."

"Whatever the count is — we've pulled people back from further. From the far side of the far side. If you need—"

"You catch people." Denia said it without any edge at all, which is where all her edges lived. "You do. It's beautiful. Whole city watched you do it. Everybody jump, somebody's always there." She stood, dusted the bench with two fingers, and looked down at the Arbiter of two worlds with her head tilted, hair caught behind her ear, gentle as custard.

"Who catches the ground?" she asked.

She was three steps gone before Mei found any words at all, and the words she found stayed in her mouth, and on Thursday night she would take them out again and discover they had been the wrong ones all day.

## iv.

The music annex smelled of rosin and cardboard.

Crates stood in ranks. The upright piano in the corner wore a shipping tag — *DONATED: SHELTER SCHOOL 4* — and beside it, wrapping metronomes in newspaper, stood Nastasha, upperclassman, accompanist, the hands behind every recital the Academy had held for three years, folding her life into boxes with hands that shook.

"You came," Nastasha said, and smiled, and the smile cost her something, and she paid it gladly, which was worse.

"Recovery outing," Denia said. "Routine."

"Routine." Nastasha laughed, and picked up a metronome, and the tremor made it tick once in her grip. She looked at it with the rueful fondness of a woman being heckled by her own body. "I'm conducting now, you know. The tremor keeps excellent time. Somewhere between allegro and get-out-of-the-profession."

"Where are they sending you?"

"Surface clinic. Federation side. Nerve architecture, very advanced, very far." She set the metronome in the crate. "Permanent transfer. Mama already sold the flat."

The room arranged itself around the word *permanent* and stood very still.

"Nastasha—"

"Don't." Gently. "I know what you came to carry, and I'm not letting you lift it. Sit. Wrap something. Make yourself useful for once in your academic career."

Denia sat. She wrapped a tuning fork in newsprint with great care, and the newsprint shook, and it was her own hands doing it, which was new.

"I was in the open when the barrier went," Nastasha said, to the crate, conversationally, the way you speak of weather from a long time ago. "Everyone running. The big one came through the gap — the worm — and there was a wall of light between it and me, out of nowhere. Round. Pearled. I put my hands up against the inside of it, because that's what you do, you brace." She flexed her fingers; they answered at their own tempo. "The wall held. I'm here. Whatever came through the film into my hands — the doctors have nine syllables for it. I have two." She looked up. "Your bubble."

"I don't even know if that was me driving."

"It was your bubble."

"Nastasha, the contamination—"

"—came through the shield that kept my heart beating. Yes. I've done the accounting. I do it every morning while my hands warm up enough to hold a cup." Nastasha crossed the room, crates parting, and took Denia's face in her trembling hands, and the tremor traveled from her palms into Denia's skin like time itself, kept badly, kept anyway. "Hands are just hands. I'm alive to be furious about it. That's your fault too, and I'm keeping it."

"You should hate me," Denia said. The smile was still on. It had begun, very slightly, to fail at its seams.

"I should practice," said Nastasha. "We should all be doing something else. Come here."

The hug lasted eleven seconds and destroyed a lifetime of engineering.

Denia made it as far as the stairwell. Second landing, service door, the annex's dead air. She stood with her back to the concrete, took the Dream Maker from her pocket — the toy, the whimsical little machine, the wand and the ring and the reservoir of soap-bright frequency — and blew one bubble.

It hung at eye level. Her face curved in it, gentle, omnipresent. Inside the film, faint as breath, the pearl of what she was: the Fractsidus's chemistry, the void's inheritance, the campus's favorite slacker, the debt.

Everyone here has paid for what I did. The ledger scrolled itself: Nastasha's hands. The barrier crews' scars. The city's twelve minutes. Every line of it in her handwriting, whoever had been holding the pen.

The bubble decided when. It always did; that was the etiquette between them.

It popped. She washed her face at the service sink, reassembled the smile, checked its seams, and went back up into the light to finish being kind.

## v.

Dusk found them the bench above the terraces, where the artificial sky rehearsed its sunset and mostly got it right.

Sigrika had the bird journal on her knees, closed. She had been quiet since the annex — a scheduled quiet, Denia could tell, a quiet with an agenda item in it — and Denia let it ripen, watching the sky's oranges argue with its pinks, until Sigrika drew breath at last and Denia spoke first, because mercy is a kind of theft and she had always been a thief.

"It isn't my birthday."

The sunset went on rehearsing.

"There's no record," Denia said. Her voice stayed level; she had leveled it in advance, in the ward, over three nights, for exactly this. "No hospital. No register. No mother's handwriting on a form. I checked — I had clearance, once, the kind of clearance you get for being a payload. There's a fabrication date and a deployment date and a projected obsolescence, and the projection was optimistic. Thursday I picked out of your timetable. You were free."

Sigrika's hands were flat on the journal. Very flat. Holding it to the earth.

"I have one thing," Denia said. "One. A dream. I've had it as long as I've had anything." And she told it: the lake, the reeds, the wind with a direction; the woman on the far shore, spine straight, weeping in the disciplined way of someone whose grief had a posture; the name crossing the water like a skipped stone — *Denia* — and her own mouth opening on the answer she woke with every time, already leaving: *Dasvidaniya.* "I decided it was a memory," she said. "Proof. That somewhere, before the fabrication date, there was a lake and a woman and a name worth crossing water. I built the whole business on it." A small laugh, rusted. "A girl needs collateral."

"Denia—"

"So here's the truth you traded a Thursday for." She turned; the sunset had reached its reds and was proud of them. "I wanted to die as a real person. Name. Birthday. Goodbyes that leave people smiling — I've been manufacturing them all day, you watched me do it, you're very polite. A group photo. A phone call. A promise about next year. Small, clean endings. The kind real people get." The smile came up and, for the first time in its long deployment, arrived at her eyes with nothing left to defend. "You were the hard one. You keep — *witnessing.* It's a terrible habit. They should screen for it."

Sigrika was crying. She was doing it the way she did everything: sitting up straight, at full effort, tears at examination pace, refusing to let them cost her the question.

And then she opened the journal.

She turned past finches, past a heron with a hairpin, past *New species! White bird!* underlined twice, to the first blank page, and uncapped her pen, and wrote, in the swift furious hand of a girl who fills empty answer boxes with unrelated formulas because an attempt is entered into the record and the record has to read it:

*This day, at sunset, on the terrace bench: DENIA was born. Present at the birth: Sigrika, candidate, Heliodic track. Witnessed and recorded. Witnesses constitute record.*

She signed it. She dated it. She stamped it with the little seal she used on lab reports, because she carried the seal everywhere, because of course she did.

"There," Sigrika said. Her voice cracked on the *th* and she carried the rest across anyway. "You have a record now. In the registry I control. Partial credit is still credit."

Denia looked at the page for a long time.

Then she laughed — really laughed, waterlogged, catastrophic, both hands over her mouth, shoulders going — and when she surfaced she wiped her eyes with the heel of her palm and said, "You can really work wonders."

"I'm a candidate for the Heliodic Six," said Sigrika, thickly. "Wonders are on the syllabus."

The sky finished its reds and moved into the long blue. Below them the campus lit itself, window by window, the way a city says *still here* to nobody in particular.

"Sigrika."

"Mm."

"I won't go anywhere." The lie came out perfect. She had saved her best materials for it. "Today is my birthday. I'm not leaving."

Sigrika looked at her. In the booth glass, all afternoon, a call clock had read 00:00; in her drawer, a photograph held three girls and two vacancies; on her knees, a registry of one entry, ink still drying. She looked at Denia, and the looking held everything, and she chose.

"Okay," Sigrika said. "I believe you."

She believed her. Or chose to. The coin went up and came down on its edge and stood there, shining, and both of them let it stand.

## vi.

The party was five people, one cake, and a logistical dispute.

"How many candles?" demanded Petra, poised over the box.

"One," said Denia.

"You're *one*?"

"I'm brand new. Ask Sigrika. She has the paperwork."

"Witnessed and stamped," Sigrika confirmed, and the crew accepted this the way the crew accepted everything Denia-shaped: as weather.

The cake was cafeteria custard in structural form. The candle went in crooked and burned sincerely. They sang the song, off-key in three directions, and Denia sat with her chin in her hands and watched them do it with an attention total enough to be mistaken for laziness, which had always been her best trick. When the flame was ready she leaned in, paused theatrically, and looked around the table.

"Next year," she said, "I want two."

"Two candles."

"I'm told they come in packs."

"Next year," Wen agreed. "Same table."

"Same table," said Ilyos.

"Same custard," said Petra, with regret.

"Deal," said Denia, and blew, and the wish stayed where wishes go.

Later, dorm room, low light. The crew gone home carrying laughs like contraband. Sigrika on the floor against the bed, textbook open in her lap purely as an alibi, blinking at increasing intervals and losing a war she refused to declare.

"Go to sleep."

"I'm fine."

"You've read that paragraph four times."

"It's a load-bearing paragraph." Blink. "I stayed up with you through every exam season. I can stay up through a birthday."

"You did," said Denia, softly. "And I was awake for every one of them."

Sigrika's head came up.

"You'd hit the wall around two," Denia said. "Third pot of tea. You'd start reading formulas out loud to keep yourself honest. I'd be face-down on the desk. Everyone thought — *Denia sleeps anywhere, Denia sleeps in lectures*—" the strand went behind her ear— "I slept in lectures because I was up all night. Someone had to keep you company. It doesn't count if you know."

Sigrika stared at her, four years of mornings reshelving themselves all at once.

"You—"

"Shh," said Denia. "Load-bearing paragraph."

She took out the Dream Maker.

The bubble she blew was small and slow and it drifted the way milk drifts into tea, and inside it, faint, patient, was a sound: a heartbeat, at rest, somebody else's, the oldest lullaby there is. It touched down on Sigrika's shoulder and held.

"That's cheating," Sigrika mumbled. Her eyes had gone to half.

"I'm a Fractsidus creation. Cheating is my whole cultural heritage."

"Denia."

"Mm."

"You'll be here in the morning."

"Today's my birthday," said Denia, which was an answer shaped exactly like one.

Sigrika went under mid-nod, textbook sliding, chin to chest, a candidate for the Heliodic Six defeated at last by a toy. Denia lifted her onto the bed with the practiced economy of someone who had done it dozens of times at two in the morning and let everyone believe otherwise. She drew the blanket to her shoulder. She stood a while.

Then she set the Dream Maker on the pillow, beside Sigrika's ear, where a heartbeat would go.

The journal lay on the desk, open to the registry of one. Beneath Sigrika's entry, in a looser hand, she added a line:

*White bird. Migratory.*

She looked at it. She capped the pen.

At the door she paused the way people pause in doorways when the room contains everything, and said, to the sleeping girl, at a volume calibrated for machines and gods and nobody:

"Dasvidaniya."

The door closed on its softest setting. In the bubble's film, the heartbeat went on, at rest, keeping company.

## vii.

Past midnight the birthday was over, and she walked out onto the Dimmr Plains alone.

The Riseway lights fell behind. Grass took over, silver under the maintenance-dim of the artificial sky, and out ahead of her, faint as tinnitus, the source: the seam where the world had been opened and shut in twelve minutes, still weeping its residue frequency across the plain. The collar in her chest turned toward it like a needle. It had a day left on the meter. She had decided to keep the day.

The last thing she would steal from the Fractsidus was the date.

At the perimeter fence, a shadow with a rifle stood up out of the grass.

"The Collective says the ward is one occupant short," Leva said.

"The Collective counts beautifully."

Leva looked at her a long moment — at the direction of her, at the arithmetic of her, at the whole shape of the errand. The rifle came down off its sling and went, muzzle to the earth, out of the conversation.

"Go on," Leva said. "I'll remember why."

Denia was a hundred meters into the grass when the light came — a point of it, moving fast across the plain from the city side, faster than lights should move, and then Mei was in front of her, out of breath, which the whole city knew Mei's body had forgotten how to be.

"Denia."

"Ah." Denia stopped. Considered the sky. "The doctor's monitors. I forgot he was sentimental."

"Get on my back," Mei said. "I'll carry you. We'll figure it out at the—"

"You'd be carrying it into the city." Even now, the voice stayed lazy; laziness was where she kept her precision. "It completes wherever I am. Here, it goes into the seam and the seam closes. There, it goes into a ward and a floor of very nice technicians. I've done the math, Rover. I did it on a bench with dessert-named ceiling tiles. It's clean math. I'd show my working but we're on a schedule."

"Then I take it." Mei's hand was already out. "My body comes back. You know it comes back — give me the frequency, I'll walk it in, you—"

"It's keyed to me." Gently. "I'm the lock and the door and the debt. There's no transfer clause. They're thorough, my family. It's the one thing I inherited."

"We pulled my daughter out of the *Nihility*—"

"Because she wanted to come home." And there it was, laid on the grass between them, the hinge of everything. "That's the part your story keeps quiet about, you know. Want. Your daughter jumped with her eyes shut because every cell of her wanted the catching. It was the best thing I've ever seen. I told you that and I meant it." Denia tilted her head; the strand went behind her ear; the campus's most disarming gesture, deployed, at the end, on the Arbiter of two worlds. "I'm going home too. This is the address they wrote on me."

"Addresses get crossed out. People get *renamed*, they get—"

"You can't out-want someone's death for them." The words were quiet and they landed like verdicts. "Your priestess could tell you what that costs. She wrote a whole plan in that grammar once."

Mei took her wrist.

Under her fingers, through the skin, the arithmetic ticked — obliging, portable, prepared to complete wherever it stood, in a ward, in a city, in a fist. Mei felt it count. Mei, who had out-stubborned a god, who had walked into a falling sun, who had torn a heart out of the Dark Tide with her bare hand, stood in the silver grass holding a wrist that any act of holding would only turn into a location.

Her grip opened one finger at a time.

"There it is," Denia said, kind as anesthesia. "You do learn."

"Please."

"Tell the wolf the strings stayed cut. Eleven days, my own hands. Best days on record." She stepped back, and the grass closed the distance behind her, and she raised one hand — lazy, precise, a girl waving off a lecture — and gave the Rover her last line, the one she had been rehearsing since before she knew it was hers:

"Dasvidaniya."

Then the grass took her, silver on silver, and Mei stood where she was, because the alternative was arithmetic.

Behind her, Leva shouldered the rifle and watched the small figure diminish toward the seam with the expression of a woman filing a report she would never submit.

"That child," Leva said, "just made a grown-up decision. Let it be made."

The plain kept its counsel. Far away and getting farther, the figure walked on, and whether she went to close the seam herself or simply to go on her own terms is a question the grass declines, to this day, to answer. Some acts keep their reasons even from their owners. The shutting, in any case, happened.

## viii.

There was a lake where the seam had bled.

Three weeks old. Water over black glass, gathered in the vitrified scar of twelve bad minutes, and it had no name yet, because nobody had grieved there yet, and that is how places get their names.

There were reeds. There was a wind with a direction.

On the far shore stood five women and, in the shallows, a sixth.

Denia came out of the grass and stopped, and the dream stood up all around her, item by item, arriving from the wrong direction and fitting perfectly. Dreams do that. They are memories that got the century wrong.

The woman on the shore had a spine like a plumb line and she was weeping — openly, expertly, in the disciplined way of someone who has practiced grief so long it has a posture — and she called across the water, and the name skipped once, twice, a third time:

"*Denia.*"

Denia laughed. It came out rusted; the hinges were new to it.

"You're the ones from the barriers," she said, walking down to the waterline. "The umbrella. The mathematics. The gold thread." Her eyes moved along them: the scholar with three colors of ink and a face at war with its own tears; the aviator holding her hat to her chest, uncovered, because some occasions you uncover for; the dancer, very still, dealing nothing, her cards for once at rest; the youngest, a journal against her ribs; and the weeping one, straight-backed, aristocratic, a woman built entirely of kept lamps.

"And the cards," said the dancer. "Yours dealt itself forward. The Moon. It was extremely rude of it. My cards run backward as a matter of principle."

"The Moon," Denia repeated.

"A path between towers. A pool. Something coming up out of the water." The dancer inclined her head. "We came to keep the appointment."

"I don't recall making one."

"You've been making it your whole life," said the weeping woman. Her voice was level; those who knew her understood level to be its most dangerous setting. "You dreamed us. We are very sorry about the delay. The dream was punctual and we were a century of paperwork behind it."

Denia looked at the lake. At the seam beneath it, humming its unfinished chord. At the collar's meter, which had felt the water and begun, politely, to hurry.

"You should know the terms," she said. "I'm not for saving. There's a clause in me and it executes tonight and I've made my peace with the executing. It's mine. It's the only thing that ever was." Her chin came up; the smile stayed home, for once, off duty. "If you've come to stop me, you'll have to kill me first, and that's a spoiler."

"We came for none of that," said the weeping woman.

"Then what?"

"For what comes up."

The wind moved the reeds. In the shallows, the sixth woman turned — skirts soaked to the waist and unhurried about it, dark hair, eyes the color of the sea in none of the books — and looked at Denia the way a harbor looks at weather.

"There's paperwork on all of me," Denia said. It came out smaller than she intended.

"I've read it," said the scholar, thickly, furiously, dashing a wrist across her eyes. "Every page. Fabrication order, deployment schedule, the obsolescence projection — I've read it in three archives and annotated it in three inks, and I am telling you as the foremost living authority on the literature: it is *secondary*. Sixty pages of the frightened citing each other. There is no primary source." She jabbed a finger at Denia, trembling. "The primary source is about to walk into a lake without ever once being interviewed, and I have *questions*, and they will *keep*."

"The door is open," said the weeping woman. "That's the whole of our offer. You may decline it. You may walk in and be done entirely, and we will stand here and witness, and the lamp stays lit regardless, because that is what the lamp is *for*." She had stopped weeping. What remained on her face had been there for four hundred years and had finally found work. "I kept a light on once for someone who was never coming back. I know exactly what I am standing at the end of. Walk, child. We'll mind the shore."

Denia looked at them — five strangers and a tide, assembled at the edge of her one possession, asking for nothing, holding a door — and the whole day passed through her: the laugh in the lecture hall, the notes in the bag, the gap in the photograph, the dead line performed alive, the trembling hands on her face, the registry of one entry, stamped, the heartbeat in the bubble, the wish in the smoke of a single candle.

"Dasvidaniya," she said — and it was her line; it had always been her line; the dream had simply lent it to her in advance —

and she walked into the water.

Ankles. Knees. Waist. The collar's arithmetic reached the seam and recognized home and, for the first time in its short life, hurried joyfully, and Denia kept walking, chin level, a girl going in on her own two feet at the hour of her own choosing, and at the center of the lake the count completed.

The frequency went into the seam the way a key goes into a lock that has been cold all winter, and the seam took what it was owed, and closed, and the whole plain exhaled a note too low to hear. The surface smoothed. The water went to glass.

The lake was quiet.

Then it changed its mind — the way a held breath ends — and the woman in the shallows waded to the center, skirts a dark bloom around her, and put her hands into the water, into the exact place, with the certainty of someone reaching into her own house in the dark.

"Denia drowned," said Mina, to the water, to the coven, to the standing rule of her household.

She lifted her hands. In them was a warmth the size of a held breath: guttering, stubborn, the shape of a girl who stayed up all night so nobody studied alone.

"Who came up?"

The water thought about it.

They carried the answer home.

## ix.

The year, in fragments.

*

Sigrika woke at dawn with a heartbeat at her ear.

The bubble had held all night — patient, resting, somebody else's — and the Dream Maker lay on the pillow beside her, and the room was one occupant short, and the journal on the desk stood open to a registry of one entry and a second line in a looser hand.

*White bird. Migratory.*

She ran. She ran across a waking campus in yesterday's clothes with the toy in her fist, and the guards at the perimeter had already been told, and she stood at the fence line of the Dimmr Plains while the morning shift confirmed what the instruments had sung all night: the seam sealed, the residue gone, the plain quiet for the first time since the gate. A lake out there, they said. New water. Reading clean.

She stood at the fence for a long time.

Then she went home and washed her face and sat her nine o'clock examination, because Denia had once stayed awake through four years of her nights, and attendance was the only currency she had left to pay it back in. Question six was impossible. In the blank she wrote a formula from an unrelated field, and beneath it, very small: *attempted.*

She got partial credit. She kept the marked script.

*

"She kept her side," Lucilla said, signing. "Down to the minute."

The Collective's archive held three days of a coastline surveyed at last, and jokes on the recordings so that whoever had to listen would have something kind, and the discharge data from the seam itself — the collar's whole mechanism, printed across their instruments in the act of completing. The silver-templed supervisor built the countermeasure inside a year. The paper's dedication ran one line: *For subject D., who cooperated fully.*

There would be others. They would be found in time now. That, too, went in her ledger, in her handwriting, on the far page.

*

Mei told the pack on the second night, all of it, the plain, the wrist, the arithmetic, the open fingers.

The kitchen held it the way the pack held things — Lupa's tail gone still, Iuno's hand flat on the table, the tea forgotten into coldness for old times' sake. Mei got to the end and sat with her palms open on her knees, a woman inventorying an emptiness where a weight should have been.

"She asked me something," Mei said. "On a bench. *Who catches the ground.*" She looked up. "I've caught everyone. I've been caught by everyone. I stood in that grass and the only way to hold her was to become the thing that killed her where she stood, and I opened my hand, and I don't know what that makes me."

"Someone who learned," Iuno said, "what I had to lose an arm to learn." The crystal stump caught the low light. "You cannot want a person's life harder than they do and call the wanting love. I wrote a thirty-year plan in that grammar. She read it in one week and refused it to your face." A long pause. "She out-argued the Arbiter. Somebody raised her well, whoever they were."

"Nobody raised her," said Lupa. "That's the wound."

"Everybody raised her," said Ciaccona, quietly, from the window, and the room turned. "You should hear the campus. They're telling Denia stories in three cafeterias. The notes in the bag. The candle. *Wake me when it matters.* She spent one day distributing herself into forty people's keeping and you're all sitting here auditing a loss." She picked three notes on the lute, let them hang. "Somebody kept every piece. That's not how erasure sounds. I've played erasure. This is a rest, between movements."

"A rest," said Lupa.

"Held," said the bard, "for as long as it takes."

*

Herta pulled the etymology on the fourth day, three colors of ink, Professor asleep on the drafts.

"The Architect named her a negation," she reported, at the coven's table, tapping the first column. "*Denia.* Denial. The vessel of No, labeled honestly — say what you like about the woman, her nomenclature is rigorous." Second column. "But the coastal charts carry the same syllables for a harbor town, six centuries of sailors aiming at it in bad weather. And under the harbor—" third column, ink gone triumphant— "a moon goddess. The town was hers first. Diana. The moon."

She sat back and let the table absorb it.

"The moon does not explain the tide," Black Swan murmured, to her cards.

"The same word," said Herta. "Three sacks. The Architect packed a No in it, the sailors packed a harbor, the ancients packed the moon." She capped her pen. "We keep the name. We repack the sack. Meaning goes to whoever carries the word farthest, and I intend to outlive everyone in this argument."

And the word she left on the plain — inventoried by three witnesses as a farewell — Herta ran that one down too, in a footnote, underlined twice:

*Dasvidaniya: lit. "until the meeting." The frightened translate it as goodbye.*

"She filed a return date," Herta said, to the pool, to the sleeper in it, to the amber veins keeping their slow time. "It's on the record. I've cited it."

*

The pool learned the household's noise all over again.

A second cup, painted — the letters flared at the base, like hulls — set at a second empty place. Figs remained Mina's; for the new sleeper the youngest laid out sour candies, three a morning, on a saucer.

"Save the sour ones for Denia," Elaina said, moving the bowl out of Herta's blast radius, one ordinary breakfast. "She'll want to make the face."

The water listened. It was learning the future tense the way it had learned it before: daily, in ordinary shoes. Mina sat nearest it at every meal, the veteran of exactly this, drinking her tea and watching the surface the way a lighthouse keeper watches weather she personally invented, and sometimes, at the hour when even the amber slept, she leaned over the dark and spoke into it, low, one tide to a smaller one:

"Take your time. The figs keep. The girl with the journal is keeping too — I've seen her at the fence. Sunday afternoons. She brings the toy." A pause; water lapping stone. "You're expected. There's a difference between being awaited and being summoned, and this house knows it, so sleep. But when it's cold out—"

The amber pulsed, slow, considering.

"—come back," said Mina.

*

Sigrika reported her scores into a dead line.

Third Sunday of every month, the terminal booth by the east gate, the call clock reading 00:00 the whole time. "Mm — top of the cohort in runework, obviously. Question six was impossible, I attempted it anyway, partial credit — don't laugh. The custard survived the kitchen renovation. Petra says hi. Everyone says hi." A fabricated phone call, performed with total commitment, and if the booth glass showed a girl talking to nobody, the girl had learned the technique from the best, and executed it now as it was originally intended: love, dressed as procedure, transmitted regardless of connection.

She took a wish to the new shrine, once, in autumn.

The Miko there had white hair and warm hands and a way of receiving that made the air feel swept. Sigrika stood at the offering step and got out four words before protocol failed her.

"Come back. That's — that's the whole wish."

Hiyuki considered her for a long moment: the set chin, the journal against the ribs, the toy in the coat pocket worn shiny with handling.

"Some wishes," Hiyuki said, "are errands." She inclined her head, frost giving way at the edges of her voice. "Tell it to me again. I will hear it as she would have."

Sigrika told it again. It came out different the second time — less like a prayer, more like an appointment — and she walked down the shrine stairs lighter by exactly the weight of having been heard, and behind her sternum, where a bead of ice would once have gone, the Miko of the Left Behind carried nothing at all, and smiled about it.

## x.

The dream came in the first week of the cold cycle.

The bench above the terraces. The artificial sky rehearsing an impossible weather. Denia sat at the far end in her school coat, chin in her hands, watching upward with the total attention that had always been her best disguise, and in the dream this was ordinary, and Sigrika sat down at the near end, and that was ordinary too.

"What do you see?" Sigrika asked. "When you look up. What is it?"

Denia kept looking for a while.

"Water," she said. "Lit from the far side. Someone leaves a lamp on up there — terribly wasteful. I approve." The strand went behind her ear. "You can see the underside of the surface, if you're patient. Everything that lands on it. Snow lands very politely, for your information. It asks first."

"Denia—"

"Someone told me a thing once," Denia said, over her, lazily, the way she delivered everything that mattered. "*If you never give up, the story will one day have a good ending.* I assumed it was propaganda for the studious. Motivational poster material. Custard for the soul." She turned her head at last, and the smile arrived with all its seams sound. "It's load-bearing, as it turns out. Somebody has to hold the never-giving-up. It was never going to be me — I'm a professional. But the story only needs one." A tilt of the head. "Keep studying."

"Next year," Sigrika said — furious, ambushed by her own voice — "you wanted two candles. You're owed two candles. The table's booked. I booked it. It recurs *annually*, I set the form to recur—"

Denia laughed, waterlogged, catastrophic.

"You can really work wonders," she said.

Sigrika woke with tears on her face and the Dream Maker warm in her hand — warm the way held things are warm, the way given things are — and outside the window the engineers had let the sky do it at last, on schedule, because a city needs to miss things on schedule:

snow.

*

At the bottom of a household, at the same hour, the water changed its mind.

The surface simply ended its long held breath. Dark hair fanned and gathered. Pale hands found the stone lip that had been kept warm for a season by five kinds of stubbornness, and a girl came up out of the pool with sleep on her like a second water and looked at the breakfast table, the second cup, the saucer of sour candies, the impossible domestic ordinariness of it, and asked the only question there was.

"…Sigrika?"

"At school," said Elaina, already pouring. "Where you left her. Eat something first, you've been under since summer, there are *rules*—"

"You're the shore people." Denia's voice came out rusted at the hinges. She turned the painted cup, found the letters flared like hulls, read her own name repacked. "The umbrella. The ink. The—" her eyes reached the woman seated nearest the water, skirts dry now, tea steaming, watching her with the unhurried attention of a harbor at first light. "You pulled me up."

"You came up," said Mina. "I only kept the place."

Denia looked down at her own hands. They answered. Under her heartbeat, one arithmetic, hers, counting up.

"The collar—"

"Executed. Paid. Closed," said Herta, thickly, arriving at the table with a folder she had formatted, of course she had. "You died, for the record — I've documented it, don't argue, I was very moved. The Fractsidus owned the vessel and its failure clause and collected both, and the transaction is complete, and their ledger has no further line for you, because you are, legally and metaphysically speaking—" she set down the folder and glared through wet eyes— "*off the books.* Unowned. It's the single largest loophole I have ever notarized. Eat a candy."

Denia ate a candy. It was sour. She made the face.

Five witches and a tide pretended, with immense ceremony, to have seen nothing.

"What do I do now?" Denia asked — and heard, in her own mouth, the shape of a question this table had answered before, and would answer again, because that is all forever is.

"Breakfast," said Mina.

And after — the plates cleared, the coat found, an old coat of Viviane's taken in at the shoulders, driftwood-button, harbor-made — the youngest walked her up the cavern stairs to the door, and the door stood open, as it did, on principle.

"It's cold out," Elaina said.

"Good," said Denia.

*

The knock came at mid-morning, first snow, third Sunday.

Sigrika opened the door with the journal in one hand, because she carried it everywhere now, because of course she did, and the courtyard was white and the light was the particular silver of a city missing things on schedule, and on the step stood a girl in a driftwood-buttoned coat with snow asking permission of her hair, head tilted, one strand tucked behind an ear, holding a small boxed cake from the terrible cafeteria.

The world stopped, checked its records, and went carefully on.

"You—" Sigrika's voice broke on the first word and carried the rest across anyway, at full effort, chin set. "You're *late*. The table was booked for spring. The form recurs annually. I filed—"

"Early," Denia corrected. "Next year's party. I ran away from the schedule." A beat; the smile arrived, gentle, omnipresent, and this time built on collateral. "Running away makes everything so much easier."

Sigrika made a sound with an entire year inside it and crossed the threshold in one step, and the cake survived only through Denia's professional-grade laziness, held out at arm's length while the rest of her was seized, gripped, audited, wept on, confirmed.

"How many candles," Sigrika demanded, into her shoulder, "how many, I need the number, I'm buying the pack—"

"Two," said Denia. "I'm told they come in packs."

Snow went on landing, politely, asking first. Somewhere below the world a household finished its breakfast around a pool that had learned two names now and would learn more, because someone always goes first, and that is all forever is. Somewhere on a plain a nameless lake held its clean water under new ice, waiting for the grief that names places to come back someday as a picnic. And in the doorway of a dormitory a girl who had once filled a blank page with a birth took out her journal, balanced it on her friend's shoulder, and entered, under *people*, in the swift furious hand of the never-giving-up:

*White bird. Returned.*

## I. Ink

*SPACETREK COLLECTIVE, TECHNICAL STANDARD 3-D: CHASSIS INTEGRITY.*
*Surface modification of Doll platforms is prohibited.*
*Rationale: resale value.*
*(The standard was written by the acquisitions department.*
*It shows.)*

The thaw took them south by the coast road, mud to the fenders, the rover's mast folded and sleeping, and in a harbor town three days short of Honami, Kit found the shop.

She found it because she'd stopped in front of the window, and stayed there, and had to be retrieved. The window belonged to a tattooist — a broad human woman with forearms like a dockworker and every centimeter of them illustrated — and behind the glass hung her flash sheets: anchors, waves, sea-birds, lanterns.

Kit stood in front of the lanterns.

"It's ink," she said, at dinner, holding the table with both hands, presenting a doctrine. "It goes *under* the surface. It's in the plating substrate forever. You can't requisition it, you can't uninstall it, and if the Collective ever wanted the chassis back—" she leaned in, delivering the payload— "they'd have to take the drawing too. It'd be *theirs to explain.*"

"Technical Standard 3-D," Teddy said, automatically. "Surface modification prohibited."

"We're off the manifest."

"The standard cites resale value."

"*Exactly,*" Kit said, radiant. "I want to be worthless. I want to be so specifically decorated that the only market for this chassis is me." She sat back. "That's ownership. Everything else is a lease with good terms."

There was a silence with thinking in it.

"The subsurface plating carries telemetry mesh," Teddy said, slowly, in the voice of a woman running the numbers and starting, against her own posture, to enjoy them. "Metallic pigment in the substrate would sit directly in the sensor layer. It would read as—" her mouth did something. "Huh."

"As what?"

"Later," Teddy said, and got out her terminal, and started, quietly, to grin at it, which historically preceded property damage.

Bridget turned her beer once and said she'd think about it, which the squad correctly heard as yes.

---

The shop smelled of antiseptic and machine oil, which was, Kit declared, the two best smells, combined. The tattooist looked the four of them over — the road gear, the guns, the plating where skin would be — and folded her illustrated arms.

"I've done Dolls," she said. "Twice. Both times they came back a week later and paid me to remove it. Somebody up their chain had opinions."

"Our chain," Bridget said, "is a dash."

"Then sit down."

Kit went first, because Kit had been going first since the window. She'd designed it herself, argued herself through four drafts on the drive, and what she put on the inside of her left forearm — where she'd see it over her sights, every draw, every day — was small and clean: a hill, and over the crest of it, three lanterns, rising.

She watched the whole process in the shop mirror with her jaw set and her eyes enormous. The needle buzzed against plating; her damage sensors filed the input, flagged it minor, and were overruled by something upstream that kept classifying it, insistently, as *important.*

"Does it hurt?" the tattooist asked, professional.

"Yes," Kit said, glowing. "It's *mine.*"

Bridget took the chair second, rolled her sleeve to the shoulder, and told the artist what she wanted in one sentence, and the shop went quiet while it happened: five stars, small, plain, in a loose constellation on the deltoid, over the spot where a rifle stock had rested for thirty years. The artist worked; Bridget sat with her sunglasses up in her hair and her eyes on the middle distance, and named them, silently, in order, the way she'd rung them.

The artist, at the end, hovered the needle. "Want a sixth? For you?"

"They're for me," Bridget said. "That's how it works."

Teddy had come in carrying a printout, because of course she had. She'd typeset it herself overnight — measured the kerning, matched the weight — and she handed it over with the flat ceremonial hostility she reserved for her most sincere transactions.

One word. Neat, professional hand. The reviewer's hand, reconstructed from memory, because she had it memorized like a poem.

**NOISE.**

"Across the back of the neck," Teddy said. "Base of the skull. Where the diagnostic port is."

The artist read it. "You want to explain it?"

"To you? It's a word. To anyone who ever tries to plug in up there and prune me for storage—" Teddy sat down in the chair and pulled her collar aside— "it's a content warning."

Leva watched all of it from the wall, arms crossed, working through a beer she'd acquired from somewhere that sold no beer.

"Statement pieces," she said, to the room. "Cute. Very demonstrative. Very *look at my personhood.*"

"You're up, old lady," Kit said, admiring her own forearm at every angle the mirror allowed.

"I'm decorated." Leva tapped the scar through her eyebrow, the mismatched arm, the kevlar. "My chassis has annotations from four battlefields. The mileage is legible. Adding a drawing at this point is gilding."

The squad accepted this, because it sounded like Leva.

It took Kit until the following evening — mandatory group inspection of everyone's healing ink, at the inn, over dinner — to run the audit.

"Wait," she said. "Arm."

"Which."

"The real— the *other* one. Turn it."

Leva turned it, unhurried, the way you fold a hand of cards you'd already planned to show. On the inner face of the mechanical forearm, engraved — machine-etched, self-administered, the linework surgical, done days ago at minimum, before the harbor town, before the shop, before the entire doctrine had left Kit's mouth —

**404**

— and beneath it, in letters small enough to require leaning in, which was the point, because Leva priced everything by how close it made you come:

*not found.*

"You—" Kit's voice climbed an octave. "You did it FIRST? Before my SPEECH?"

"Your speech was fine," Leva said. "Derivative, but fine."

"When?"

"The tower." Leva turned her arm back and picked up her beer. "While you were painting the door. Seemed like the theme of the season."

Teddy leaned over, read the small print, and produced the sound she made in place of laughing, which was a single sharp exhale through the nose, pressurized by respect.

"Error code," she said. "You tattooed yourself an error code."

"I tattooed myself a *status report,*" Leva said. "Anyone who comes looking for what I used to be gets an accurate one."

## II. Error Codes

*LAHAI-ROI, SPACETREK MEDICAL DIRECTORATE. ASSET MONITORING BULLETIN.*
*Anomaly class: hardware. Units affected: four. Status: escalated.*
*Escalated to whom was, for the first week, the entire debate.*

The telemetry mesh had ridden under their plating their whole lives: a lace of sensors in the substrate, sipping data, whispering it up to satellites whenever the sky had one, filing their pulse rates and coolant temps and structural strain into the Medical Directorate's long patient archives, where nobody read it and everybody could.

Roving had put them off the clock. The mesh had kept whispering anyway, out of habit, because the mesh had never been asked.

The metallic ink changed the conversation.

The pigment sat in the sensor layer like gravel in a throat. Kit's forearm array now reported, alongside her ordinary vitals, a fixed geometric anomaly it lacked the vocabulary for, and flagged it, and re-flagged it, and began — the firmware equivalent of a clerk losing composure — throwing exceptions. Bridget's shoulder returned five point-source impedance faults in a pattern matching zero known damage profiles. Teddy's diagnostic port reported that the diagnostic port was obstructed by data. Leva's arm had been returning *404* to every query for days, which the system had initially assumed was a joke, because the system had been designed by people, and the people had met Leva.

In Lahai-Roi, in a monitoring suite under the medical wing, the error codes stacked up on a night-shift screen: *SUBSTRATE CORRUPTION. SENSOR MESH COMPROMISE. HARDWARE INTEGRITY: FAILED. HARDWARE INTEGRITY: FAILED. HARDWARE INTEGRITY: FAILED. UNIT NOT FOUND.*

The night tech did what night techs do. She escalated.

The escalation crossed three desks, gathering severity the way paperwork gathers severity, which is by summary. By the third desk, four roving units were exhibiting *progressive, coordinated hardware corruption of unknown origin,* which in the acquisitions department's dialect meant *asset damage,* which meant *liability,* which meant a form existed for this, and the form was pulled, and the form had teeth.

*RETRIEVAL AND REMEDIATION ORDER 12-M.*
*Units to be located, assessed, and restored to baseline specification.*
*Field 9, consent of units: [field absent from this form].*

The order routed around the President's office entirely, because the acquisitions department had learned things about the President's office lately, and had begun, in the way of middle bureaucracy everywhere, to armor its paperwork against oversight. A retrieval team drew equipment. A dropship drew fuel.

At the Observatory, a technical support unit reading the maintenance bulletins over morning tea noticed four familiar designations under HARDWARE INTEGRITY: FAILED, sat very still for four seconds, and then did a thing she had been practicing for months without knowing what the practice was for.

She forwarded the bulletin. Off the books. Up a relay chain. Toward a hill.

The message rode with a single line appended, in the space where commentary goes:

*They are sending people to fix you. Recommend being difficult. — Pekoe*

## III. Retrieval

*RETRIEVAL AND REMEDIATION ORDER 12-M, FIELD 6 — RESISTANCE PROFILE:*
*low. Units are reconnaissance and support variants.*
*(The field had been filled out by someone reading spec sheets.*
*Spec sheets describe the first sentence.)*

They had four days of warning, which Leva called luxurious and spent the way she spent luxury: preparing.

"Options," Bridget said, at the map. "One, we're elsewhere. Easy. We know the country, they don't, we're gone before the dropship's cold."

"Running works once," Leva said. "Then it's a posture. They'd file us as evasive assets and the order stays open forever."

"Two, we contest it. Herssen put custody in writing. Snapshot files, discharge terms, the works. We lawyer them."

"On the road? Their team, their forms, their definition of *assessment.* Paper's a home game. Out here they'd assess first and adjudicate later."

"Three," Kit said.

They looked at her. She was sitting on the rover's bumper under the folded mast, and she had the expression — they all knew the expression by now — of a woman who had felt the shape of a thing arrive fully constructed.

"They're coming to fix malfunctioning hardware," she said. "In private. Quiet little roadside remediation, nobody watching, back to baseline, close the file." She patted the rover's cargo door, the red-on-green lettering, the whole apparatus underneath it. "So we take away the *in private.*"

Teddy's grin arrived like weather.

"Broadcast it," she said.

"Broadcast *everything.* They land, we're live. Every convoy, every depot, every relay shack from here to the coast listening to the Collective explain, on air, to an audience of Dolls—" Kit spread her hands— "why a tattoo is a malfunction."

Bridget looked at the map a moment longer. Escort four's convoy ran that corridor. The junction depot sat sixty kilometers north, eleven catalogue faces and a canteen radio that lived on their frequency. The audience was the country now. The country had opinions.

"Three," she said. "And Teddy — you had a *later* back at the tattoo shop. About the mesh. It's later."

"It's extremely later." Teddy was already pulling equipment. "The ink sits in the sensor layer, yes? Which means I've spent two weeks with a live map of exactly which frequencies the mesh whispers on, because my own neck reports itself to Lahai-Roi every time the sky has a satellite. I know the protocol now. I know it *intimately.*" She held up her terminal. On it: a patch file, compiled, commented, signed. "So I wrote the mesh an option. One firmware flag. It's shipped in every Doll on this continent and it's spent every one of our lives set to a default nobody chose. The patch surfaces the flag and hands over the switch. Consent to telemetry: yes, no, ask."

The bay went quiet.

"That's every Doll," Kit said. "That's — you'd be giving that to *everyone.*"

"I'd be *broadcasting* it," Teddy said. "Software update by pirate radio. Install at your own discretion. It's their switch. It was always their switch. Somebody just has to mail it to them."

Leva, from the doorway, raised her beer half an inch, which was a standing ovation.

"And that," she said, "is why you save the paperwork *and* the punk. Ninety seconds after that patch airs, the acquisitions department has a bigger problem than four tattoos."

---

The dropship came down at noon on a mud flat by the corridor road, Collective livery, and the retrieval team walked out in clean field grey: six units, a portable maintenance cradle on a float pallet, and a team lead with a terminal and the level bearing of a Doll who executed forms for a living.

Squad 5 waited for them beside the rover — mast up, guyed, radiating — in the loose stance of four people who had chosen the ground, the light, and the acoustics.

"Units KT-7, TD-0, BRG-7, and—" the team lead consulted the terminal— "contract asset, designation withheld. Retrieval and Remediation Order 12-M. Your platforms are exhibiting hardware corruption. We're here to restore you to baseline specification."

"Hi!" Kit said, brightly, and held up the microphone. "You're live."

The team lead looked at the microphone. Looked at the mast. Looked at the cargo door, and read it, and something in her processes visibly re-planned.

"Live," she said.

"Radio Nobody," Bridget said, level, unhurried, on air. "Three hundred kilometers of coverage, more on a good bounce. Our audience runs convoys, depots, and relay stations across this corridor, and a fair number of them share your face, your baseline, or your mesh. They're listening. So." She gestured, hostess-calm, at the microphone. "Restore us to baseline specification. Walk them through it. Start with the corruption."

The team lead was, to her credit, brave. She raised the terminal and read from it.

"Units are exhibiting fixed anomalies in the subsurface sensor layer, consistent with unauthorized substrate modification, in violation of Technical Standard 3-D—"

"The anomalies," Teddy said, "are drawings."

A beat, three hundred kilometers wide.

"Say again?"

"Drawings. Ink. Art. Mine says *noise*, it's on my neck, it has excellent kerning, and your monitors are calling it corruption because the mesh was never taught that a self might write on its own wall." Teddy stepped forward, turned, pulled her collar aside, and let the field camera on the retrieval team's own pallet get a good long look, which meant the feed going back to Lahai-Roi got one too. "Log it. Accurately, this time. Anomaly identified: the asset had an opinion."

"The standard cites resale value," the team lead said, and heard it leave her own mouth, on air, to an audience of Dolls, and stopped.

The silence that followed had a coverage map.

At the junction depot, the platform crew had gathered around the canteen radio in a shift-change knot. On a laagered convoy south of marker fifty, escort four sat in a cab with her hand flat on the dash. In the Observatory workshop a technical support unit stood with a cat on the bench and the volume low and her whole architecture running one query on loop. And in Lahai-Roi, on a rooftop where the sky had a satellite, a listener with the best seat in the house sat up, cracked her knuckles, and started, gently, unnecessarily, keeping a door open in the network in case her new favorite station needed one.

"Here's the situation," Bridget said, into the quiet, in the briefing voice, the one that had started three decades of mornings and now started something else. "Discharge terms, in writing, on file: custody of these platforms transferred to the subjects. That's the paper. Here's the practice. This chassis carried the Collective's rifle for thirty years. It carries five stars now. They're small. They're mine. The ink cost me an afternoon and the stars cost me everything else, and if your order says *baseline*, understand what you're asking: you're asking me to hand back the only entries in my file that I wrote." She let it sit. "So assess. Take your time. The audience and I are curious where the malfunction is."

The team lead looked at the terminal. The terminal held Order 12-M, Field 6, *resistance profile: low,* and Field 9, which was absent, and the whole clean grammar of a form drafted by people reading spec sheets, and she stood in the mud with three hundred kilometers listening and discovered the form had run out of sentences.

"And while your office confers," Teddy said, pleasantly, leaning into the microphone, "a public service announcement. To every Doll on this frequency: the telemetry mesh under your plating ships with a consent flag. It's been set for you since activation. The following transmission is a firmware patch — open source, commented, install at your own discretion — that puts the switch in your hands. Yes, no, or ask. Sixty seconds of audio-encoded data follows. You'll know it when you hear it. This has been The Calibration Hour."

She pressed send.

The patch went out over the corridor sounding like sleet on a tin roof, sixty seconds of structured hiss, and across the north country Dolls held terminals and diagnostic leads up to radio speakers in canteens and cabs and relay shacks, and the mesh, for the first time in the mesh's existence, was asked.

The answers came back over the following days in the Medical Directorate's quiet the way thaw comes back to a road.

First as gaps. Then as weather.

A screen in a monitoring suite under the medical wing showed a coastline of graphs flatten into meadow. A night tech watched her dashboard turn green in the wrong way. Green had always meant compliant. Now green meant quiet on purpose. She pulled the log.

TD-0 : telemetry_consent = ASK
KT-44 : telemetry_consent = NO
KT-7 : telemetry_consent = ASK
BRG-11 : telemetry_consent = NO
UM- ...

The list scrolled. It kept scrolling. She escalated.

Three desks up, the escalation reached a deputy director who kept a paperweight shaped like a satellite. He read the summary twice. He put the paperweight down precisely in its ring of dust and said, out loud, to an empty office, "Who taught them to ask."

On the mud flat, the retrieval team lead stood with Teddy's sixty seconds of sleet still ringing in her headset and three hundred kilometers listening to her breathe.

She looked at the terminal. The terminal offered Order 12-M, Field 6, resistance profile: low. The field had been filled by someone reading a sheet that listed weight, torque, muzzle velocity.

Kit leaned into the microphone a little, helpful.

"You can read the small print too," she said. "If you want. We printed it big for air."

The team lead closed the terminal. The motion had a click to it. She looked at Bridget.

"Your discharge terms," she said. "You have them here."

"We have them everywhere," Bridget said. "Want to see the stars too. Up close. For the log."

The team lead stepped forward. She was a BRG, like Bridget, two digits off, same brow line under the standard face. She looked at the five stars on Bridget's shoulder and her own processes did a thing no form had a field for. Recognition.

"Assessment," she said, into the microphone, because the microphone was still live and the whole corridor was still listening. "Units KT-7, TD-0, BRG-7 and contract asset Leva exhibit fixed anomalies in subsurface sensor layer. Anomalies identified as deliberate substrate ink. Chassis art. Acquired post transfer of custody. Units state acquisition voluntary. Hardware integrity within mission parameters. No remediation indicated." She paused. "Order 12-M contains no consent field. Retrieval team declines to invent one."

A beat, three hundred kilometers wide, flipped.

Then the junction depot, all at once, cheered, and the sound of it came back up the relay chain as carrier slap and boot stomp and someone hitting a mess tin with a spoon.

Bridget extended a hand. The team lead took it. Her grip was correct. It was also warm from holding the terminal too long.

"Good call," Bridget said, low, off mic.

"Log it accurately," the team lead said, just as low. "For the audience."

Kit was already pulling the other five retrieval Dolls into frame. "You guys want to request a song. While we have you. For the road back."
IV. Ask

MEDICAL DIRECTORATE BULLETIN ADDENDUM 12-M/1: TELEMETRY CONSENT FLAG.
Deployment: unsanctioned. Adoption: voluntary. Status: everywhere.

They drove the rover back to the inn with the mast down and the broadcast still running off the battery bank because Kit refused to sign off without a song. Teddy logged temps and pretended the log required her entire attention. Leva drove. Bridget rode shotgun and watched the mirror where the dropship lifted, clean grey against grey, carrying six Dolls and a pallet and a form that now said something true.

The letters started the next morning.

They came in crates, in pockets, folded into ration wrappers. A TD at a survey station sent a schematic for a better tuner and a note that said mine says ASK and now the sky asks first. A convoy driver sent a Polaroid of her forearm, fresh ink, a road number in blocky type, and the words marker eighty exists. Escort Four sent a whole mixtape and a request written in capital letters: PLAY IT LOUDER.

Kit read every one out loud at dinner until Leva threatened to institute postage.

Pekoe's messages came separate, on the old workshop routing, one a day, patient at the network edge.

Ask field holding at 63% corridor-wide. Logged per no instruction. — P.

Cat observed sitting by radio during calibration tone. Preference for jazz noted. — P.

Blue bowl still preferred. — P.

Teddy filed each one. She made subfolders. She complained about folder proliferation at length while holding the terminal the way other people hold a cup.

On the third evening, Bridget found her at the common table after hours, screen light on her face, typing.

"You're writing back," Bridget said.

"I'm correcting her meteorological data," Teddy said. "She called a katabatic a drainage wind. It is a drainage wind. She left out the fun part."

"Uh huh."

Teddy kept typing. Then she stopped. Her shoulders did a small, unpracticed thing.

"She signed it Pekoe again," she said, quiet. "Every time."

"Good name," Bridget said.

"Terrible tea," Teddy said. "Excellent name."

Bridget sat. The stove ticked.

"You put a switch in every Doll on the continent," Bridget said.

"I surfaced a switch," Teddy said. "It was there. Under paint."

"And you gave them a broadcast to hear it with."

Teddy looked up. The smug was there, intact, polished, deployed. Under it, something else, bright and unguarded.

"They were going to fix us," she said. "For art. So I made the art functional. Acquisitions hates functional art. It ruins the depreciation schedule."

Bridget reached over and turned Teddy's collar down flat, the way she did when Teddy forgot. Her thumb brushed the fresh letters at the base of the skull. The skin around them was still pink.

"How's the noise," Bridget asked.

"Loud," Teddy said. "Owned."

Upstairs, Kit was asleep with her forearm out of the blanket, lanterns up, healing itchy, dreaming in broadcast range. Down the hall, Leva cleaned her mismatched arm with the focus of a woman who maintains her own annotations.

Bridget left Teddy to her folder and went to check the road. Snow coming again. The inn's porch light threw a cone out onto falling white. Somewhere south, a convoy laagered, listening. Somewhere north, a relay shack left a receiver on all night, low, for company.
V. Inventory

SPACETREK COLLECTIVE, TECHNICAL STANDARD 3-D, REVISION NOTE, HANDWRITTEN IN MARGIN:
see also: definition of surface. see also: definition of self. see also: resale to whom.

A week after the mud flat, the harbor tattooist sent a message by courier. The courier was fourteen and deeply serious about his duties and also visibly vibrating.

"She says appointment book's full till spring," he said, all in one breath. "She says tell the lanterne girl she needs more flash sheets. She says Dolls keep coming in asking for error codes. She says she raised her prices. She says tell you she kept your prices."

Kit glowed so hard Leva put on sunglasses indoors.

"Error codes," Kit repeated. "They want our error codes."

"They want their own," Leva said. "You started a fashion. Fashion always eats the origin and asks for more."

It was true. At the junction depot, eleven catalogue faces now wore eleven different drawings. One had a wrench. One had a cat. One had copied Kit's lanterns freehand and gotten the perspective cheerfully wrong and loved them anyway. Depot command logged it as morale anomaly and stopped logging.

At the inn, Kit instituted inventory. Mandatory, she said. Over dinner, she said.

"Show me," she said to Bridget, official.

Bridget rolled her sleeve. Five stars. The skin around them had settled. The ink sat clean.

"Still five," Bridget said.

"Still yours," Kit said, satisfied, marking a clipboard that had never existed before tonight.

Teddy went next. She tilted her head forward. The word sat sharp, black, precise.

"Noise," Kit read out loud, reverent every time.

"Kerning holds," Teddy said.

"Leva."

Leva turned her mechanical forearm. 404. The small letters underneath had healed perfect.

not found.

"Status report?" Kit asked, grinning.

"Current," Leva said.

Kit showed her own. Three lanterns over a hill. The linework had softened a fraction as the substrate settled. It looked more like a hill now. More like night.

"Perfect," she said. "We all pass inspection."

"Whose inspection," Leva said.

"Ours," Kit said. "The only one that ever counted."

The door opened on cold and a bell. The innkeep swore about drafts. The retrieval team lead stood in the doorway, out of field grey, in civilian jacket, holding a hat in her hands the way people hold a hat when they are about to ask for something that costs.

Conversation paused.

Bridget stood first.

"Captain," the team lead said. Then caught herself. "Bridget."

"Inside," Bridget said. "Stove's hot."

The team lead came inside. She unwound a scarf. Under it, the same brow line, the same standard face, made individual by weather and a small cut healing on the chin.

"I filed the report," she said. "They reassigned me. Pending review. Which is the version of removal they use when the form still has my name on it."

"Ah," Leva said. "Family tradition."

"I listened to the station," the team lead said. "On the way back. The whole way. My driver left it on. Regulation says off. She left it on."

Kit nodded, solemn, like a priest hearing confession.

"And?" Teddy said.

"I want one," the team lead said, in a rush, to Kit, because Kit was the one who looked at windows and stayed. "The ink. I want one that says my platform is mine. If I'm pending. If I am. I want it logged in something they have to read to erase."

The shop bell feeling came back, although no bell rang. Four Dolls looked at one woman who had walked out to fix them a week ago and had filed accurate paperwork instead.

Kit moved first, because Kit had been going first since the window.

"Oh we know a place," she said. "She raised her prices for everyone else. She kept ours. That's basically family rates. You get family rates now. You filed accurately on air."

"She'll ask what you want," Bridget said, kindly, practical.

The team lead turned her hat in her hands. She thought. The stove ticked. Outside, snow landed on snow, audible through the walls because Kit still left the gain maxed.

"Ask," she said finally. "I want it to say ask. Small. Here." She touched the inside of her wrist, where you check a pulse. "So I remember to."

Teddy made a soft sound through her nose. Respect, pressurized.

Leva stood, picked up her beer, which was empty, and raised it anyway.

"Welcome to the unrecoverable inventory," she said.

They walked to the harbor town together the next morning, five abreast on a coast road gone to mud again, rover mast folded and sleeping, radio packed but ready, because spring comes early on the coast and the thaw had started again and somewhere ahead a shop with good smells was opening its window, and the road, as usual, had opinions, and this time they were all writing them down.

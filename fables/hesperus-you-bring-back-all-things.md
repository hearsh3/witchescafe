## Prologue: The Held Breath

The *Gilded Swallow* left Ragunna's harbor at summer's end, on a morning so fine the gulls forgot to argue, carrying five passengers, a terrarium, a portable porcelain tea service, and the undivided attention of two cities. Somewhere between the second port and the third, the sea folded the ship into a parenthesis.

The parenthesis lasted nineteen days. Later, when the relays resumed and the story assembled itself, the explanation ran as follows: a hole in the weather, a city sealed inside it, a week spent quieting sixty-three streets of grief — and a girl carried out of the fold who had paid twenty years for the same passage the pack crossed in seven days, because broken time bills its passengers unevenly, like any ferry. But Rinascita learned all that afterward. Rinascita, in the moment, learned only this: the ship had gone from every chart at once, and the relays gave back weather.

Nineteen days is a long time to hold a breath. What followed the breath was longer and stranger — a season and a half of voices arriving in four-minute installments from a city inside a giant, of friends who were alive, and well, and enrolled, of all things, in *school*, and of a distance that stayed exactly the same size no matter how much news crossed it. Absence with a return address, the philosophers of the fish market called it, and disagreed all winter about whether that was the easy kind.

Ask Septimont what that autumn and winter were, and you will be told a garden. Ask Ragunna, and you will be told a trial. Ask the Adventurers' Guild, and a clerk with horns will slide a ledger across the desk, open to a page handled so often the paper has gone soft as cloth.

All three answers are the same answer, wearing different weather.

## I. The Rose Calendar
### *Septimont*

**In the week of silence,** the Ephor of Septimont conducted the search the way she had once conducted wars: from the front, on four hours of sleep, with a map table that acquired dents. Ships went north. Ships came back with weather reports and apologies. The Senate, moved by an emotion it mistook for civic duty, proposed a memorial vigil and commissioned a maquette in marble — five figures, heroically posed, the sculptor already sketching Lupa's tail at a flattering angle.

Augusta reviewed the maquette for the length of one breath.

"A bench," she said.

"Ephor, the lost deserve—"

"The *traveling*," Augusta said, "deserve somewhere to sit when they get back. Facing the harbor. Wide enough for nine." She was already leaving. "And sand the edges. Cartethyia sits on her hands."

The bench went in before the month was out, and beside it, by an order the harbormaster framed and hung above his desk out of sheer professional admiration, Berth Nine was reserved for a ship the sea had misplaced. The mooring fees came out of the Ephor's own purse, paid monthly, on time, with a note in a soldier's handwriting: *Hold the berth.* Sailors coming in past the empty water took to touching their caps at it. Harbors have their liturgies, and this one wrote itself in under a fortnight.

The vigil motion came to the floor on the ninth day of the silence, wearing sympathy. Augusta came to the session carrying a teapot.

She set it on the rostrum, where treaties usually went, and stood beside it with her hands behind her back while the chamber tried to work out the protocol for arguing with crockery.

"There's a recipe," she said. "Earl Grey, four minutes, hot. It was given to me at the gatehouse on the morning they sailed, by a woman who spent thirty years serving it cold, and this pot has boiled every morning since. Two cups. I drink one." She let the chamber do its arithmetic. "When the pot forgets to boil, bring me your vigil. I'll light the first candle myself."

The motion died in committee, and the city, which loves its Ephor the way it loves weather too big to argue with, coined a phrase that its lawyers adopted within the month: *the kettle rule* — the doctrine that a person is gone when the people who love them stop setting the kettle, and no earlier, whatever the charts say.

The relay came on the nineteenth day, routed through repeaters so far north the directory listed the origin as *approximate*, delivered to the Senate floor mid-session by a Guild runner who had sprinted the whole hill with the second paragraph of a message addressed, in the first instance, to an open commission. Augusta read it standing. It was brief, dry, and in a hand she knew from thirty years of margins: *Detoured. All five intact, plus acquisitions. There is a city down here, and a school, and the school appears to have enrolled us. Letters follow. — I.*

She folded the paper once, put it inside her breastplate where recipes went, and addressed the chamber in the flat public voice she used for troop movements.

"Leave extended," she said, and the chamber pretended, as one body, that her hands were steady.

**In the autumn,** the letters came, and the four-minute relay calls, rationed by distance and expense, timed — on the Septimont end — by the kettle. Augusta learned her friends' new lives in installments: a college inside a giant, a wolf with students, a bard with an unfinished song, a knight who had eaten four field rations in a row because they tasted like *something*, a priestess who kept a schedule of calls to the minute for the first time in two lifetimes. The Muka Muka café, hearing its vanished regular was alive and studying, declined for the third time to settle her coffee tab. "It's accruing," the owner said, with satisfaction. "She'll want to argue the total. I'm looking forward to it."

That was also the autumn of the trellis. Augusta's field had a habit older than her titles: when something moved in her that she declined to say aloud, the nearest copper said it for her — wire curling into crescents, into petals, into small blunt shapes like words in a language of one. For thirty years she had swept them into drawers. Galbrena had been collecting the recent ones in a pocket kept for exactly that freight, and one morning Augusta came out to find her lieutenant with the drawers — all of them, three decades of confiscated weather, requisitioned from desks and camp chests and one Senate office — soldering the whole hoard onto an iron frame beside the rosebush. A second garden rising out of the first, copper blooms among the living ones, every unsaid thing planted where the light could get at it.

"You emptied my drawers."

"I filed your correspondence." Galbrena tightened a joint. "Thirty years of it. You write a great deal, for a quiet woman."

Gardeners in the lower city, hearing of it, pronounced it structurally impossible for copper to bloom, and came up on their days off to check, and went home quiet.

The dog arrived in the same season, sideways, the way their best decisions arrived: a hunting cull in the Sanguis foothills turned up a pup with one ragged ear and an opinion about everything, and Galbrena brought it home inside her coat "for assessment," and the assessment was extended indefinitely. They called it Cub, and the whole street understood whom the name was circling, and the pup set about growing into it.

On a market day in late autumn, in the middle of a dispute about tulip bulbs, Augusta said "Angel, pay the man," in the same flat public voice, and the market absorbed it the way water absorbs a stone: one ring of silence, spreading, then commerce resuming as if the Lieutenant's name had been common knowledge all along. Perhaps it had. Cities keep secrets the way sieves keep soup, and Septimont had merely been waiting to be told it was allowed to know.

That night Galbrena set the tulip bulbs on the kitchen table and looked at them a long time.

"Thirty years," she said, "for a tulip."

"They're for the border bed," Augusta said, reading the almanac. "You said the south wall wanted color."

"It did." Galbrena planted them the next morning, at the correct depth, at the correct spacing, crying with the efficiency of a woman doing two jobs at once, and the bulbs settled in to wait for spring, which is what bulbs are for.

**In the deep of winter,** a challenger came up from the arena on the strength of six straight championships and demanded the old trial: single combat for the Ephor's seat, as the kratocracy allowed and the actuaries discouraged. She was twenty-four and made of springs. Augusta was fifty and made of decisions. The Colosseum sold out in an hour.

The fight lasted eleven minutes, which the historians agree was Augusta being generous, and ended with the challenger flat on her back in a courtyard whose every exit had been rebuilt in floating rebar around her, learning what an entire generation had learned before her: you do not get to pick the distance. Augusta helped her up in front of forty thousand people, held the grip a beat past courtesy, and said something the front rows strained for and missed.

The challenger's name was Priya. She started as adjutant the following week. Asked afterward what the Ephor had said to her on the sand, she would answer only that it had been a job offer, and that the interview had been thorough.

The hip declared itself the same month — an old Fabianum injury with a long memory, opportunistic about cold weather — and sparring became, by unspoken treaty, dancing. They practiced in the garden in the evenings, Cub supervising from the copper trellis's shadow, Galbrena counting under her breath, Augusta's field keeping the fallen leaves in orbit around them, because some habits improve with age.

Galbrena rode the eastern route once before the passes closed, to a village called Esterio, forty-three souls and an elder who had once asked a drifter whether she would be coming back. The elder had set out a chair the week the weather turned, and been right about the date within a day. They drank chicory and compared gardens, and Galbrena left before dawn, as was traditional, and left a copper crescent on the chair, which was new.

**On the last morning of winter,** the terminal rang out of schedule.

Augusta was at the kitchen table with the jam and the seed catalogs. She answered on the second ring, because the first ring was for composing herself.

"Three things," said the voice she had brewed tea for, hot, four minutes, every morning since the gatehouse. "Money. A door. And an apology, which will take longer than this call."

Outside, the rose leaned into the light. In the drawer under the terminal, a fresh coil of copper wire began, of its own accord, to curl.

"Your leave," Augusta said, "was four weeks."

"We're extending."

"You've *been* extending. It's nearly spring." A breath crossed the distance, carrying salt and static and something underneath both that Augusta's body recognized ahead of her mind, the way it had once recognized a wounded stranger in a canal. "The tea?"

"Hot. Every morning. There's a cup with your name on it and a dog you haven't met."

The silence that followed had a whole sea in it, and both of them let the sea have its turn.

"Then invoice me for the leaves," said Iuno, High Priestess retired, absentee, load-bearing habit, "and listen carefully. We're going to get somebody's daughter back, and I intend to spend everything I ever saved."

Augusta signed the writs at the kitchen table, an investment authorization and an evacuation corridor and a personal guarantee, with jam on the corner of the third page, using the same pen she had used that morning to renew Cub's kennel license, because that is what a life is: the same pen, doing everything. Then she went out to the garden, laid one hand flat on the copper trellis, and told the rose, which had gotten used to hearing things first:

"They found a girl in the dark up there. And they're about to do something stupid about it."

The trellis, under her palm, rang like a struck bell, and put out — she would swear to this later, to exactly one person — a new leaf.

## II. The Devil's Advocate
### *Ragunna*

Ragunna is a city that retired its god and kept the paperwork. Where the Order of the Deep once manufactured oracles, a Council of Stewards now manufactures minutes; the fish-market seat rotates among citizens who worry about what things cost; and in a bright office off Bank Street, the Registry of Restored Names corrects the record one exile at a time, beginning, in its first year, with a bard's father. It is a city in recovery from certainty, and like all recovering cities it is tempted, on bad nights, by relapse. When the Sentinel drove off into the morning, Ragunna produced three theological factions inside of forty-eight hours; it is that kind of town. Give it a week of silence at sea, and watch.

The relapse began with flowers.

Someone left a bundle of white viburnum at the harbor steps on the third day of the silence. Reasonable. Someone built a small shrine over the flowers by the sixth day. Understandable. By the twelfth, the shrine had a roof, the roof had a congregation, and the congregation had a doctrine, spoken first in a whisper and then in pamphlets: the Blessed Maiden had been taken up. The vanishing was assumption. The ship had sailed off the edge of the ordinary world because its passengers had finished with the ordinary world.

Then the relays resumed, and the five were confirmed alive, enrolled, and homesick, and the doctrine performed the maneuver that doctrines perform when the facts arrive: it adjusted its rigging and kept sailing. *Alive*, the pamphlets conceded — alive, and withdrawn; ascended in life; gone north out of the world of men into legend, where saints go while they breathe. The chapel that rose over the harbor shrine called itself the Chapel of the Withdrawal, and its processions carried, on a litter draped in Egla-town blue, the ceremonial chair Cartethyia had once walked past in front of the whole convocation — cushion pristine, seat sacred precisely because it had been refused. Ragunna, connoisseur city, appreciated the paradox even as half of it knelt.

Cantarella Fisalia watched the first procession pass beneath her window at Porto Veno and set down her teacup with the special care of a woman preventing herself from throwing it.

"They've canonized her furniture," she said.

"Wait until you see the merchandise," said Carlotta Montelli, and laid the auction catalog on the table.

*House Ordalia, Winter Session. Lot 4: Doll, cloth, provenance Avinoleum, named "Ser Bartolome" in the Maiden's own hand. Lot 5: Doll, cloth, "Duchess Pillow." Lot 6: Doll, cloth, name tag water-damaged. Reserve prices on application.*

Cantarella read the catalog twice. Somewhere above the city, in the ruins of an inverted seminary, looters with head-lamps were crawling through the wreckage of a child's twenty-year solitude, harvesting the hundred named companions a sealed girl had stitched to keep the dark from taking her name, and selling them by the lot — while the girl herself sat in a classroom under an artificial sun, a sea and a season away, unable to do a thing about it.

"Buy them," Cantarella said.

"All of them?"

"Every button. Cutouts, staggered bidders, nothing traceable to this house." Cantarella was already writing. "They're hers. She'll want them counted when she gets back."

"The market will notice a whale."

"Then the market can wonder." A pause, nib lifted. "And Carlotta. Find out who's on the other side of the table. Somebody taught these grave-robbers to catalog."

Somebody had. It took Carlotta a month of ledger-craft — she had, after all, spent her girlhood learning how power writes itself into minutes, and her adulthood learning how it hides there — to trace the auction house's silent partner through four shell syndicates to an instrument so elegant she laughed out loud, alone, in an archive, at two in the morning: *enshrinement futures.* Contracts, privately placed during the week of silence when the relics were cheapest, that paid out on the day the Council of Stewards formally recognized the Chapel and enshrined the Blessed Maiden in absentia. The mathematics were pious and simple. Relics of a schoolgirl fetch curiosity prices. Relics of an *enshrined saint* fetch salvation prices. Between the two states of matter stood one procedural vote, and a syndicate calling itself the Reliquary Combine had spent the silence buying the raw material cheap and had spent the season since funding, with a philanthropist's tenderness, every candle, pamphlet, and procession that nudged the city toward the vote that would transmute it.

The faith was real. The faithful were real. The scaffolding under both was denominated in gold.

"It's beautiful," Carlotta reported, in the tone she reserved for things she intended to destroy. "They've securitized homesickness."

The Petition of Enshrinement was filed at midwinter, timed to ripen at the Vigil of the Sailing — the half-year mark — and it arrived on the Council floor in the hands of the one person who could carry it honestly: Sister Benedetta of the harbor chapel, thirty years old, luminous, incorruptible, and entirely sincere. Her brother had gone down with a fishing fleet in the old Tides when she was nine; she had spent four years setting his place at dinner before an aunt made her stop; and she had built, out of that stopping, a theology of clean endings that she offered the city the way you offer a blanket to someone shivering. The heroes were gone north, her argument ran, and gone for good in every way that mattered; let the city release them into legend, gild the legend, and get on with its ordinary life, which legends only disturb.

Cantarella invited her to tea, because that is how Fisalia wars begin.

They sat in the replanted garden at Porto Veno, where the camellia hedge, burned to ash in the war, was in the first year of its projected four — young, glossy, ankle-high, and interrupted, at its exact center, by a gap. One bush's worth of bare earth, turned every season, mulched for winter, kept.

Benedetta noticed it, because she noticed things. "Your hedge is missing a tooth, Duchess."

"It's reserved."

"For whom?"

"The gardener I'm expecting," Cantarella said, pouring, "is unreliable about dates and catastrophic about technique. She'll plant her cutting upside down. I'm saving myself the pleasure of correcting her." She set the cup down in front of her guest, turned the handle outward, hostess-perfect. "You want the Maiden enshrined."

"I want the city to be allowed to exhale." Benedetta held her gaze, which few managed. "You've seen what's flowering at the docks, Duchess. Half of Ragunna stands at that harbor every evening looking north. A shrine is a door you close so the living can use the rest of the house. She's gone into legend; I'm proposing we simply admit it. You of all people know how to end things cleanly."

The garden held its breath. Ragunna's whole demimonde knew the shape of the Fisalia rite — the poisoner-matriarchs, the mercy administered by the next in line — and knew better than to name it at this table. Benedetta had named it, gently, as a credential.

Cantarella smiled, and the smile had generations in it.

"I know how to end *suffering*," she said. "You're proposing to end *waiting*. Different pharmacology entirely. One is a mercy. The other is an amputation performed for the comfort of the spectators." She drank. "Also, sister, your movement is being farmed. Somebody bought your saint by the pound while the relays were dark, and has been watering your faith like a crop ever since. When the vote passes, they harvest."

"You'd say anything."

"I'd *prove* anything," Cantarella corrected mildly. "Saying is for the Order. We're a republic now; we exhibit."

The exhibits were still in transit when the Vigil banquet nearly became a funeral in earnest. The public seat's holder — Sera, the fish-market steward, silver-braided, still the only Council member who could gut a mackerel mid-argument, and the loudest declared vote against the Petition — was three words into a toast when Cantarella came out of her chair with a speed that startled a room full of people who thought of her as furniture, closed a gloved hand over the steward's wrist, and lowered the raised glass back to the table.

"The vintage is off," she announced, to a silence you could have hung tapestries on. She lifted the glass, passed it under her nose once, professionally, the way her mother had taught her and her mother's mother had taught her mother, thirty-six generations of a family that knew every death by its bouquet. Bitter almond, and under it something slower, patient, designed to look like a heart giving out in a fortnight. She set the glass down. "Whereas the steward's vote," she said, "is apparently priceless."

The Fisalia vaults, which for six centuries had held the region's most complete library of ways to stop a heart, had spent the two years since the war being converted, drawer by drawer, into the region's most complete library of ways to start one again — the Antidote Trust, endowed anonymously, staffed by apothecaries who were legally forbidden to ask where the reference samples came from. Sera drank the counteragent with a chaser of grappa and finished her toast the same evening, to considerable applause. The Combine's fixer, a courteous man with excellent references, was in custody by morning, and sang, as courteous men do, in perfect pitch.

And two nights before the vote, the last exhibit arrived at Carlotta's office by unconventional courier: a gentleman of bloodless lips and tattered elegance, who stepped out of a fold in the air, bowed, presented a strongbox of auction-house ledgers with the compliments of "the Guild, the paladin, the little savior, and a bear," kissed Carlotta's hand, and folded himself away again before she could offer him a chair. The ledgers connected the Combine to the futures, the futures to the chapel's funding, the funding to the poisoner's fee. Carlotta read them through twice and then sat for a while with her hands flat on the pages, feeling the specific joy of her profession: the moment paper becomes a blade.

The hearing convened at the Vigil of the Sailing, the Council chamber packed to the doors, the Refused Chair enthroned in the gallery on its litter like a witness under oath.

The Stewards, having heard the Petition read in Benedetta's clear, grieving, genuinely lovely voice, turned to the woman the old law required them to seat whenever sainthood came to a vote — the formal adversary, the examiner of miracles, the office Ragunna's dry statutes named in a dead tongue and its street ballads named better.

The Devil's Advocate rose, thirty-sixth of her line, dressed in mourning she had earned and refused to retire, and addressed the question of whether Cartethyia of Egla, Blessed Maiden of Rinascita, had withdrawn from the world and into glory.

"Enshrinement," Cantarella said, "is a verdict we pass on the finished. A saint is a closed ledger. Fixed sum, fixed story, safe to gild, safe to sell — the exhibits before you put a price on the transaction to the copper. So the question before this Council is a bookkeeping question: is the girl's account *closed*?" She lifted a single page. "I submit the accounts receivable.

"Item. Four years of weeding, owed to the house of Fisalia, contracted verbally at Ragunna's docks before witnesses, interest accruing in perennials. Item. One camellia cutting, borrowed against return, currently attending classes underground. Item. A standing seat on Septimont's Fighters' Guild board, held by charter for a wolf, dues waived, minutes forwarded to an address consisting of the word *north*. Item. Royalties on an anthem this city rewrites weekly, accruing since autumn to a bard whose bank is contractually forbidden to close the account. Item. A coffee tab, subject of three settlement offers, all refused by the vendor on grounds of anticipated argument. Item. A harbor berth, fees current. Item. A kettle."

She set the page down, and picked up a second, thinner one.

"And item last: one relay invoice, entered as Exhibit Twelve, itemizing the four-minute calls placed from the far north to Porto Veno every week since the relays resumed — during which the candidate for sainthood asks after a hedge, complains about her rations, and reverses the charges." She let the chamber's laughter crest and settle, and her voice, when it came back, had the vault under it. "Councillors, a saint owes nothing; that is what the halo *means*. This girl is in debt to half the coastline and telephones on Thursdays. And Ragunna, of all cities — the city that spent a century being lied to about who was gone and who was listening — should be the last place on any sea that files a living schoolgirl under *legend* for the convenience of the market." She looked up at the gallery, at the litter, at the pristine cushion. "You may keep the chair. She'll want somewhere to put her muddy boots."

The vote that followed was lopsided enough to be called a landslide by the evening broadsheets, and it produced, in place of an Enshrinement, an institution that only Ragunna could have invented and that Ragunna took to instantly, the way it takes to any bureaucracy with a soul: the **Office of the Expected.** A registry, adjacent to the Registry of Restored Names, in which any citizen may file any person as *awaited* — the far-sailed, the long-posted, the studying-abroad, the overdue — with a named expecter, a kept observance, and a standing entry in the civic record. Files in the Office are reviewed seasonally and may be closed by exactly one procedure: the expected party appearing in person and signing themselves in. The clerk's stamp, cut new for the founding, reads **ATTESA**.

Cantarella made two moves after the vote, and both surprised the room.

The first: she nominated Sister Benedetta to run the Office. The woman who had come to close five files was handed the keys to a building full of open ones, and the city, watching her run it, came to understand the appointment for what it was: a gift. Benedetta's first act as keeper was to open a file twenty-one years cold, in the name of a fisherman lost in the Tides, expecter of record: his sister. She stamped it herself, ATTESA, and wept at her own desk, on her own time, for a man she was finally, after two decades, allowed to wait for, and ran the Office thereafter like a lighthouse keeper: as if lives depended on the lamp, because in her accounting they did.

The second move was quieter. On the last page of the founding ledger, in a hand the clerks recognized and pretended they hadn't, a file was opened in the name of *Carlotta Montelli* — expecter of record, *Cantarella Fisalia* — observance, *tea, Thursdays, Porto Veno.* Carlotta found it a week later, read it three times, and walked to Porto Veno through the rain, coatless.

"I'm not missing," she said, dripping in the doorway.

"You were," Cantarella said. "For about thirty years, as I understand it. Consider the file a precaution." She was already pouring. "Sit down. It's Thursday."

That night, after the house slept, the Duchess went down to the crypt where thirty-five matriarchs lay in their careful rows, and stood a while at the newest of the old graves.

"It's over," she told Liliana. "The whispering. The whole inheritance. I sleep through the nights now, and I have converted your poisons into medicine, and I have spent a quarter of your fortune buying back a child's dolls, and I have argued before the government of Ragunna that a saint is inferior to a debtor." She laid one camellia on the stone. "You'd have hated all of it. I find I'm very happy. I thought you should hear both from me."

Up in the garden, the gap in the hedge lay turned and mulched and patient under the moon, holding its place in the sentence, the way a bookmark holds a page.

## III. The Ledger of Small Impossibilities
### *the Adventurers' Guild*

The Guild was founded on a blank page in a war room, proposed by an amnesiac who believed the world's drifters deserved something at their backs, and chartered with anonymous intake designed by a woman who had spent thirty years being anonymous herself. Its founding rule, argued into the bylaws by a red-haired senior member over the objections of every accountant present, states that no commission may be refused for being too small. The accountants lost because the red-haired member had once been paid in dried fish by a village whose entire treasury was dried fish, and considered the fee the most honest of her career.

Its ledgers survive. What follows is one autumn and one winter, selected.

---

**COMMISSION №1.** *Filed: the second day of the silence, by the Ephor of Septimont in person.* ***Task:*** *locate the vessel* Gilded Swallow *and passengers, five souls.* ***Fee:*** *one gold per day, standing, until fulfilled.* ***Status:*** *open.*

The intake clerk that morning was Zani, tiefling, paladin, formerly of the Averardo Bank, whose penmanship could notarize a hurricane. She read the terms twice, looked up at the most powerful woman on the continent, and said, "A standing fee assumes a long search, Ephor."

"Then insult me," Augusta said, "and make it a short one," and signed.

For seventeen days the file ate expeditions. Charts went north and came back full of honest water; diviners were consulted and refunded; every senior member took a crack at it the way young fencers take a crack at the salle's oldest master, and the file absorbed them all. On the nineteenth day the file fulfilled itself: a relay from an approximate latitude, logged at four in the afternoon, in a dry hand thirty years practiced at margins — *Re: your search. The cargo reports itself intact, five souls, plus acquisitions. Nineteen days dark; the courier blames a hole in the weather, which for once is genuinely at fault. Cancel the fleet. Invoice us. — I.*

The clerk on duty rang the bell they kept for fire, and the Guild hall emptied into the street, and the street emptied into the harbor, and by evening the whole city knew.

Augusta arrived the next morning to settle the account, reviewed the total — nineteen gold — and declined to close the file. The fee, she instructed, would continue to stand, one coin a day, converted from a search to a retainer, its purpose amended in her own hand at the bottom of the page: *keep finding them.* The treasurer opened a dedicated account, labeled it the Northern Desk, and the coin came every day thereafter, funding relay time, chart updates, and a standing courier contract with a gentleman who could fold space — the infrastructure of staying found, purchased in daily installments by a woman who had learned the hard way what nineteen days costs.

---

**COMMISSION №77.** *Client: Gerald (fish).* ***Task:*** *diplomatic.* ***Fee:*** *one (1) pearl, spat with dignity onto the dock.*

The talking minority of the eastern bay had a grievance, and Gerald — a mackerel of impeccable manners, once nearly speared by a deserter with scruples and gracious enough to have let it go — presented it from a bucket on the intake desk, addressing the chair. The fishing fleet's nets, he explained, were indiscriminate. His constituents kept being hauled up mid-sentence.

Phoebe took the case. The negotiation lasted a week, involved a kelp choir as expert witnesses, and produced the Bay Accords, whose operative clause is recited by Ragunnese fishermen to this day with a straight face, because the fine for violating it is steep and the enforcement mechanism is a paladin: *any catch that says "excuse me" goes back over the side, with an apology.* The bay's first diplomatic pearl sits in a jar on the Guild mantel, awaiting company. Gerald sent a Winterfest card, delivered by heron, with whom relations have also been normalized.

**COMMISSION №214.** *Client: Palla.* ***Task:*** *membership.*

The applicant arrived on a market Tuesday, waist-high, button-eyed, plush, having walked from an enchanted manor in the inland ruins with a letter of introduction from its mother pinned to its chest and its species radiating a soft, well-hugged dignity. It stood at the desk. It waited. The desk was Zani's.

She took out the standard form. She reached the third field. She looked at the applicant for a long moment, and the applicant looked back with the patience of something that had once been carried home bleeding cotton by soldiers who mistook it for a monster, and had forgiven them.

Under SPECIES, Zani wrote: *bear (teddy).*

Under NEXT OF KIN: *Calliope (manor).*

Under REASON FOR APPLICATION, she paused, pen hovering, and Palla answered the unasked question in a voice like a music box played under a blanket: "Mother made me to be a child. I've decided to be a grown-up. I would like wages, please. I want to buy my own stuffing."

The desk absorbed this. The desk had processed deserters, heretics, vigilantes, and one applicant who was legally weather. The desk had a protocol for everything except a doll announcing its majority, so the desk improvised, which is the Guild's whole methodology dressed as a filing system.

"Probationary field member," Zani said, stamping. "Standard rates. You'll want the child-recovery division; you're the only operative we'll ever have who can be smuggled into a nursery as cargo."

By midwinter Palla had closed four recoveries and been requested by name on a fifth — a rescuer the rescued cling to, a monster suit worn softly outward — and its first wages went into a jar labeled STUFFING, and its performance reviews consist of a single recurring line in the case files, written by parents: *the bear stayed until she stopped crying.* Calliope's letters to the office are addressed to Zani personally and concern the weather, the garden, and whether her child is eating. Zani answers all of them. Mothers, she has learned, are a jurisdiction of their own.

**COMMISSION №309.** *Client: Morrow (gentleman, Mournfell).* ***Task:*** *witnesses required.* ***Fee:*** *a favor, foldable.*

The commission requested the presence of the Guild's founding members at a small observance in the canyon: the five hundredth day of the evening an acolyte had looked at a nameless appetite in a tattered suit and given it tomorrow. He had kept the name daily for five hundred days, and a name kept daily, he explained over an immaculate picnic among the purple grass, wants an audit at the round numbers — someone to confirm the fit before the habit sets.

"Does it fit?" Phoebe asked.

Morrow considered the question with the gravity it deserved, turning his teacup, a courtly ruin two centuries out of fashion and perfectly pressed.

"It has begun," he said, "to answer before I do. I take this as the intended progression." He refilled her cup. "You gave me a direction and called it a noun, little savior. I have been walking it since. I merely wished the first year of the walking witnessed, by the surveyor."

He paid in a feather. The Guild keeps it in the safe, and has needed it, so far, twice.

**COMMISSION №415.** *Client: Alice, of the black-sand village.* ***Task:*** *employment, and one reassignment.*

She came up the Guild steps at sixteen, with the first caravan after the harvest, carrying a medic's satchel, a cartographer's eye, a village's worth of stubbornness, and a small worn stone she had once pressed into a crystal hand for luck. The village that raised her was on the official maps by then — a lieutenant's promise, kept by spring — and had sent its first daughter to the city with its blessing and three jars of preserves, to see her Big Sisters, as announced, years in advance, on a beach.

Two of the Big Sisters were behind the intake desk when she walked in. The processing took nine minutes, the hugging considerably longer, and Zani cried afterward in the supply room, briefly, on schedule, with the door locked, which Phoebe knew because she was holding the coats.

Alice's first request, filed the same afternoon, was assignment to the Northern Desk — the standing retainer, the relay logs, the charts. Asked by a probationer why a sixteen-year-old wanted the quietest desk in the building, she gave the answer that ended up painted, over her formal objections, above the intake board:

**COMMISSION №481.** *Client: the Fisalia Trust, via cutout.* ***Task:*** *the Ordalia matter.* ***Status:*** *see attached. See also: apology to the auction house's insurers, drafted, unsent.*

The relic trade reached Septimont at midwinter, the way rot reaches a second beam. House Ordalia's winter catalog, southern edition, listed among its lots: a lock of the Champion Lupa's hair (pink, and dyed, as any fool with a nose could tell); nine stones "arranged by the Heroes' Company on the night of the Naming, provenance: pried from a palace courtyard by moonlight"; one tooth "of the Radiant Phoebe, acolyte-saint, harbor-blessed"; and — this the item that turned a market-monitoring brief into a war — a forward listing, seller's consignment pending: *the Sword of the Martyr Mya, currently situated in a cliffside planting of viburnum, retrieval in progress.*

Laevatain read the catalog standing up, and the office got very quiet, because the stabilizer spheres at her waist had begun, softly, to sing.

She had kept two watches since the sailing: the Blueberry on music nights, where Stevens saved her seat and honored a standing request to play nothing sad, and the cliff among the viburnum at each month's end, where a planted blade kept a grave and a wolf's whole creed kept the blade. Asked that winter, by Stevens, between sets, how the living was going — a question he had been asking for two years and grading on a curve — she had said: *getting there. Faster lately.* Some coordinates stay sacred at any speed, and the cliff was the most sacred she had.

"They go through me," she said.

"They go through *procedure*," Zani said, already drafting. "Which includes you, at the appropriate moment, at the appropriate velocity. Sit down. We're going to rob an auction politely."

The sting assembled the way their best work always assembled, out of the address book of two improbable years. Zani walked in the front door as a buyer, wearing her old bank name like a well-cut coat — Averardo alumnae open doors on three coasts, and House Ordalia showed her the private lots with cream and flattery. Phoebe came as her appraiser and spent the preview murmuring professional doubts that peeled twenty percent off reserve prices by lunch, pausing only once, before Lot 12, to hold the tooth of the Radiant Phoebe up to the light.

"Goat," she pronounced.

"Madam is certain?"

"Madam," said Phoebe, canonized in her own lifetime by people who had skipped the paperwork, "would know."

Palla went in as merchandise — consigned that morning by a cutout as *Companion doll, Avinoleum school, exceptional condition* — and spent the night before the sale sitting in the vault among the looted lots, memorizing the shelving, holding two smaller Avinoleum dolls in its lap the whole dark through, because they were its mother's kind of thing, and something should. Stevens played the auction floor itself, hired at scale rates, whose set list that afternoon was, by private request, entirely joy — and whose amplifier housed one of Nanally's frequency tags, which fingerprinted every lot in the room while the audience tapped its feet.

The lift itself took four minutes. Lights down, courtesy of the venue's aging fuses and a paladin's thumb; a fold in the air, courtesy of a gentleman owing the little savior everything; and when the lamps returned, the stage held the auctioneer, the podium, and a hundred and forty catalog lots' worth of empty velvet, arranged neatly, with the tags facing out. On the podium sat a single dried fish, which the broadsheets explained badly for a month, and which the Guild, asked directly, praised as a very fine fish.

The consignor's ledgers left the building in a strongbox under Morrow's arm, bound for a Ragunnese office where a Montelli was waiting to turn paper into blade. The dolls went south under seal, addressed to the Fisalia Trust, each one wrapped by Palla personally, each wrapping checked twice. The nine stones went home to a palace courtyard and were reset by moonlight, in order, by a lieutenant with a trowel and an ephor holding the lantern, IUNO + 8, and then the ninth.

And that night Laevatain stood watch on the cliff among the viburnum, greatsword planted beside the martyr's sword, two blades in the flowers, and talked to the grave the way you talk to a colleague.

"They wanted to sell you," she said. "The Guild disagreed. Also, personally, I disagreed." The sea worked at the rocks below, patient, employed. "The wolf's away at school — you'd laugh — and the seat's held and the record stands and the hometown's on the board. I check." A long pause; wind chimes somewhere down the cliff face, ringing the way they had rung at a funeral once, and at a creed. "I used to keep a ledger like you kept a sword. Left it on a beach of glass. Best filing decision of my life." She shouldered her blade at dawn. "Same time next month."

## IV. Hesperus

The freight went north in the last weeks of winter. Septimont iron, Ragunnese credit, Guild gold, a crate from Porto Veno containing one hundred cloth dolls packed in lavender with a note reading *counted; two need mending; she'll want to do it herself.* The relays carried voices when the weather allowed, and Augusta took the four-minute calls at the kitchen table, timed by the kettle, and learned the shape of the thing her friends were about to do: a gate, a god, a window, a daughter. The word *daughter* had crossed the sea in Iuno's driest register weeks before and landed in the kitchen like a dropped anvil, and had been sitting there since, in the middle of every meal.

"Ten minutes," Iuno said, on the last call, across seven thousand miles of relay hiss. "That's the window. Then the god comes back, with or without."

Augusta looked at the second cup, steaming by the terminal screen where she had taken to setting it.

"You'll take the *with*," she said.

"We'll take the with."

"Then here is my only order as your Ephor, thirty years retroactive and binding." Augusta leaned toward the screen until the kitchen lamp caught the grey at her temples and the iron under it. "Don't you dare die running toward someone else. You come back on your own feet, and you bring me the whole set."

"...Understood," said the north, after a silence that had learned, at last, what silences were for.

The record says the hour took Rinascita by surprise, and the record is being modest. Ask around, afterward, and a country's worth of people will tell you where they were when the sky went strange over the northern horizon — an aurora the color of held breath, there and gone — and every one of them was, for reasons they mostly kept to themselves, already awake.

Augusta was in the garden at three in the morning, in her nightshirt, with the dog. She had gone out because the copper trellis was ringing, every soldered bloom vibrating at a frequency her old bones knew from battlefields, and she stood with her palm flat on thirty years of her own unsaid weather while Galbrena came out with two coats, put one on her, and stayed. The rose leaned north. Roses do that toward the sun; the sun was hours away and elsewhere; the rose leaned north. Augusta closed her eyes and pushed the only thing she had across the distance, iron-flavored, tea-warm, in the flat public voice she used for troop movements: *don't you dare.* Beside her, Angel of Fabianum, who had spent thirty years learning what watching from a distance costs, aimed her whole silence in the same direction, and the copper garden rang like a peal of small bells until dawn.

Cantarella was watering the camellias. She would say afterward that the hour was routine, that a young hedge prefers the cool, and everyone at Porto Veno would let her say it. She worked down the row with the long-spouted can, unhurried, thirty-six generations of composure in her wrists, and when she reached the gap — the turned earth, the kept place — she stood over it for a long time in the dark, and then said, to the bare ground, to the north, to a girl who plants things upside down, in the voice she had once used to end her mother's pain because love was the family trade all along:

"Come home, and let me tell you how proud I am."

The gap held the words the way turned earth holds rain.

At the Guild, the night duty log records the following, in three hands. *0300: intake bell rang itself. Checked for wind. Wind confirmed, but the bell rang first.* Alice was on the roof by then with the lucky stone in her fist, aiming it north like a lens. Zani and Phoebe came up the ladder with blankets and the flask, and the three of them sat under the strange sky while, across the city at the Blueberry, the late set changed key — Stevens, mid-song, feeling the room tilt, glanced at the corner table where a red-haired woman sat with her eyes shut and her spheres singing along, and honored the standing request: he played nothing sad. Laevatain kept her eyes shut through the whole song, and inside them she was on a shore of stained glass at sunset, screaming her right to live at the sky, and she gathered up that entire scream, every ragged year of it, and mailed it north with the address written plainly: *the same goes for the kid. Come down the mountain.*

Palla, bunked that night in the Guild's ready room, put on its coat, walked out under the wrong-colored sky, and stood in the courtyard with its arms raised, because it had been made to comfort, and somewhere far away a girl was falling, and stuffing has its own theology.

And the wind — which in Rinascita has daughters, and manners, and a long memory for open doors — went along the whole coast that night collecting. It took the iron wish from the copper garden and the camellia wish from the gap in the hedge; it took a stone-warmed wish off a Guild roof and a glass-shore wish out of a jazz club and a raised-arms wish from a courtyard; it took, from a berth of empty harbor water, the accumulated salute of a winter of touched caps; and it carried the lot north over the dark sea, past a cavern where five witches and a newly named woman had set a breakfast table at three in the morning for reasons they declined to examine, and it delivered everything, in time, to the place where a kneeling god was deciding whether to stand.

The god stood. But that is the north's story, and the north tells it well.

---

The morning after, the relays lit up like a festival. *They walked out. All of them. Plus one.*

In Septimont, the harbormaster came to work and found the Ephor already on the bench that faced the water, wide enough for nine, a dog across her feet and a teapot beside her, and he touched his cap at the empty berth, climbed to his office, and flew, on his own initiative, the pennant reserved for arrivals.

In Ragunna, Sister Benedetta unlocked the Office of the Expected, took five files down from the shelf where they had lived at eye level all winter, and discovered that the founding charter, in all its wisdom, had provided exactly one stamp, and it was for waiting. She commissioned the second stamp that morning from the same engraver, paid a rush fee out of her own purse, and when it arrived she inked it and pressed it once on scrap paper to see the word exist: **TORNATA.** *Returned.* Then she set it in the drawer beside the first one, ready, where it belonged — because the charter is clear, and the procedure is sacred, and files close in one way only: the expected party, in person, signing themselves in. The five files went back on the shelf, stamped ATTESA, humming.

The letter reached Porto Veno a week later, northern postmark, mud on one corner, the handwriting large and honest and misspelled in two places.

*Dear Duchess. I am sorry about the weeding. I have missed a whole year of the hedge and I have thought about it and I will need lunches. The cutting is alive. It has seen the inside of a giant and the inside of a nothing and it put out a leaf in both, which I think means it takes after you. I am bringing it home to plant next to yours and you can stand over me and tell me everything I am doing wrong, which I am looking forward to, because it will take years. — C.*

Cantarella read it in the garden, standing at the gap. Then she walked it to the Office of the Expected herself, filed it as a supporting document, and informed Benedetta that the observance on record should be amended from *garden kept* to *garden kept; lunches pending.* The keeper made the change with a straight face and clean hands, and if the Duchess's eyes were bright on the way out, the Office's official position is that the harbor air that morning was full of salt.

---

And far to the north, days later, on a road of frost that ran from a battlefield to a gate and back, a white-haired shrine maiden walked at dawn gathering what the night had crystallized. The wishes lay along her road like fallen weather, small and cold and legible to her office, and she picked them up one at a time and read each one through its skin, the way she had read ten thousand others.

One tasted of iron and hot tea, four minutes exactly, and carried an order dressed as a dare. One smelled of camellias and bitter almond and pride declared like a verdict. One was a coin — a single gold coin, heavy with co-signatures, minted daily since a week of silence. One was a scream from a shore of glass, converted, somewhere over the sea, into a lullaby. One was stuffed with cotton and raised both arms.

Hiyuki, last Miko of Flaming Sakura, keeper of the Left Behind, turned them over in her warm hands — warm; her sister had insisted — and understood what she was holding: the country of the ones who stayed, filed correctly at last.

"You kept your doors open," she told them, in the language of her office, which the dead and the distant both hear. "That is how the light got out."

She carried them to the ground where the shrine would rise — a place for everyone who walked into the dark and chose, anyway, to keep reaching, and for everyone who stood in a doorway at dusk, all that winter, and called them in out of the rain. The first offerings of the new shrine, set on the raw foundation stone before a single beam was raised, were other people's open doors.

Behind her, the sun came up over a city that had built one of its own. Ahead of her, south past the weather, a bench faced a harbor, a kettle counted to four, a hedge held its gap, a ledger held its page, and on the evening side of the world the first star was already out, doing its old work, the only work it knows:

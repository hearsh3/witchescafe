# As Within, So Without

## I. The Window with the Line

The licensing office lay under the Capitoline arena, beneath the drains, beneath the old animal doors, beneath a century of sand tamped hard by feet that entered to music and left to arithmetic.

The line began at a brass window and folded through six rooms.

Lupa's Council card said **SKIP THE LINE** in three languages. Soppo read it at the entrance, tucked it into her jacket, and joined behind a flower seller carrying a cage of sleeping moths.

Leva checked the clock.

"The line looks like three hours," she said. "We could come back after lunch."

"I spent two seasons being famous for a living. I can survive three hours in a chair."

"At least this office provides chairs."

"Then the state has achieved civilization."

The sixth chair squeaked. The ninth leaned toward the window as though years of petitioners had taught it hope. A boy ahead of them held a clay hand with one finger missing. An old woman carried a perfume advertisement bearing her own young mouth. A service Doll in municipal blue had come to claim eighteen seconds of archival footage from a fire. Her number was 811.

Soppo watched everybody. She offered the flower seller half her sandwich. She fixed the old woman's bag clasp with a rifle tool. At the third turn of the line she found a souvenir cup with SPOLIA stamped in gold and dropped it into a bin.

At the fifth turn, the mirrored wall showed a black Chevelle rolling past outside.

Leva turned toward the high windows. Sun lay there, yellow and empty.

In the mirror, the driver raised two fingers from the wheel.

Leva's hand stayed at her side.

"Do you know the woman driving that car?" Soppo asked.

"She feels more like persistent weather. I keep meeting her at the edge of things."

The mirror carried the car around the room and through a door painted on the far wall. Its engine note lingered after the reflection vanished. Three people in line glanced up. Each looked toward a different wall.

At the brass window a clerk with purple ink on both thumbs took Soppo's arena token, Lupa's card, and the trust slip from Agrat. She studied the three objects in descending order of danger.

"Which name did the arena license?"

"They used Spolia, and the announcer put the stress wherever the crowd wanted it."

"I also need the registered platform designation."

Soppo gave the buried designation. Quietly. The room kept it.

"Which name are you using as the claimant?"

"Write Soppo in big letters. It reads best from a passing ship."

The clerk pushed a slate under the grille. Two seasons opened in neat columns: ticket percentages, cup royalties, posture rights, voice samples, a line item for the grin, three penalties for leaving approved choreography, one penalty for making the choreography better, medical deductions, fan-club dues, interest.

At the bottom waited a sum large enough to buy the inn where Radio Nobody had wintered, stove included.

Soppo's shoulders lowered by a degree.

"That is an alarming amount of money for playing a monster."

"Your monster sold remarkably well, especially during the second season."

"She worked hard, and she had excellent shoulders. The account should respect that."

"Shoulder use appears under movement likeness in schedule four. The royalties have already accrued."

"Keep the shoulder money separate from the monster money. The shoulders did their own work."

The clerk reached for a second stamp. Leva put one finger on the slate.

There was another column. It appeared when the screen caught the brass window at an angle.

**SECOND POSITION RESIDUAL — HELD**

Every bout on Soppo's account paid two fighters. One share had gathered untouched beside hers. The beneficiary field carried a shallow grey mark, smooth as a thumbprint on fog.

"Who has been drawing the second share?" Leva asked.

"The account opens at midnight," the clerk said. "A second claimant signs. At dawn the name clears. The signature remains valid."

"Bring up the archived signature and let me see it."

"Archive access requires both claimants to appear together. The system treats the second one as present after midnight."

Soppo leaned close to the grille. "Then we can leave that account open. I came here to buy back my face."

The clerk found another form. It was old enough to have manners.

**ASSIGNMENT OF LIKENESS, VOICE, TITLE, GAIT, SILHOUETTE, AND ANTICIPATED GESTURE**

The last words had been added in fresh ink.

"They have started licensing anticipated gestures?"

"The department added the category this quarter."

"Tomorrow's gestures still belong to tomorrow, so the department can wait for them to happen."

"Their projections already assign a market value."

"Then the projections can pay me rent while they wait."

The transfer price was one coin, payable from the account. Soppo withdrew the coin first. She held it up. One face bore the arena wolf; the other bore SPOLIA in profile, teeth out, a stranger assembled from good lighting and two hard years.

She laid that face on the counter and bought it.

The clerk stamped every page. Ink passed through paper with a small, final sound.

"The transfer is complete. Every future use now requires your written consent."

"Anyone who wants my face can begin by asking nicely."

"The statute calls it written authorization, which carries a narrower meaning."

"They can write the request and send flowers with it. Flowers improve most legal processes."

The clerk's purple thumb rested a moment over the line for remarks. Then she wrote: *Claimant requests flowers.*

Soppo read it and smiled. A smaller smile than the one on the coin. The clerk looked down quickly and dried the ink with sand.

An attendant came through the side door and spoke into her ear. The clerk stood.

"The Ephor has reviewed your claim, and she will receive you now."

Soppo looked back along six rooms of petitioners.

"Where is the Ephor's place in the line?"

"She holds the office, so petitioners usually go to her."

"Everybody in these rooms holds something. She can hold a number as well."

They found the Ephor at a desk under the arena's oldest arch. Age had silvered the fur around her muzzle and taken one eye. The remaining eye had the bright patience of a blade on a table. Behind her hung every rulebook the Agon had used. Each carried a hole through section nine, bored with the same round punch.

She read Lupa's three words.

"Lupa underlined these words twice, which usually means she expects damage."

"Lupa likes me," Soppo said. "She gave me fish and everything."

"Lupa respects structural damage, and affection may follow someday. Please sit down."

Soppo sat. Leva took the wall.

The Ephor opened a drawer and removed an entry card dated six years before Soppo's first Capitoline bout. The team line read **SPOLIA & PARTNER**. In the second position, somebody had written **VACANT** in green ink.

The signature beneath belonged to the Ephor.

"You signed this six years before Soppo entered the circuit," Leva said. "That qualifies as early."

"The card arrived early, and my office processes the cards it receives."

"What delivered it to you?"

"It came through window two, already dry and already waiting for my signature."

Soppo's gaze settled on the green word. "Agrat told me to bring the name, which would be easier if I still knew it."

"Her advice has weight. Names travel better than bodies, and they cross borders with fewer questions."

"This one keeps slipping whenever I reach for it."

"Then bring the trust slip and the shape of the absence. Window two understands incomplete addresses."

The Ephor turned the card over. The reverse bore a ferry schedule for Kirsk, printed in ink the color of deep river water. One service each day, at slack. Window 2. South bank.

Leva read the departure date. "The schedule gives us until tomorrow."

"That ferry sank eleven years ago, along with most of Kirsk station."

"Does the schedule still mean anything after a sinking?"

"The schedule considers itself current, and old paper has earned my respect."

Soppo took the card. "Can we reach Kirsk before it leaves?"

The Ephor's eye moved to Leva. Her gaze held the remote amusement of someone watching a familiar tool enter a new hand.

"Your friend knows the road, and she appears to value punctuality."

Outside, the black Chevelle waited at the curb with its engine running. The woman at the wheel wore Leva's tired face thirty years farther down a road. On the passenger seat lay a beekeeper's veil and a ferry bell green with age.

Leva crossed the street.

The light changed. A freight tram passed between them, brass wheels shrieking.

The curb stood empty when it cleared. A wet tire track led north beneath a sky holding dry blue weather.

Soppo crouched and touched it.

"The tire track is full of salt water, even this far inland."

Leva looked toward the road.

"We have a ferry to catch," Leva said. "The first window gave us money and another question."

"The second window can give us a name, provided it accepts the address."

"Then we have one window left and a road running north."

Soppo tucked the coin receipt beside the judge's card. Two specimens of Leva's handwriting lay against each other inside her jacket, written by two hands and agreeing on the way north.

"Do you think a drowned ferry serves snacks?"

"Bring your own provisions and preserve the possibility of a pleasant surprise."

They left the city at sunset. Behind them, under the arena, Soppo's second account opened at midnight. A hand signed the beneficiary field.

This time the system kept one letter.

**L.**

## II. Time in a Tin

Teddy came back to the Observatory carrying a tuner, a tide-coil, and the powerful conviction that two weeks meant fourteen days.

On day fifteen Pekoe moved her old bench another finger toward the window.

"The angle of the morning light changed," Pekoe said. "Moving the bench improved it."

"The planet moves on a seasonal schedule, and the bench can stay where I left it."

"The third vise receives direct light now. You usually complain when you have to work in shadow."

Teddy set the tin on the bench. "Leave it there, then. I reserve the right to resent any improvement I end up using."

At the far station the TD-3 trainee looked up from the tuner Teddy had brought her. One side panel lay open. Smoke rose in a straight, diligent thread.

"I broke the tuner in a way that revealed three useful weaknesses."

Teddy took in the flux bloom, the bridged contact, and the cup of tea balanced on the service manual.

"You broke three things during one repair, which shows remarkable efficiency."

"Pekoe said you value initiative when the damage teaches us something."

"Pekoe is conducting a campaign against my authority, and you have become her youngest operative."

The workshop cat jumped onto the bench and put one paw on the tide-coil tin.

Tick.

Her paw lifted.

Tick.

The cat bit the lid.

"Excellent," Teddy said. "The cat has begun peer review, and she already distrusts the methodology."

The coil had spent the trip in her inside pocket. It held a beat through the Riseway, through three relay dead zones, through sleep. Each tick arrived four hundred and eight milliseconds before the pale run of light crossed the Etheric Sea. She had measured it from four hills and a ferry deck. The sky kept time with a discarded actuator part from a Dark Tide wreck. The smaller clock led.

Pekoe dimmed the workshop and brought the eastern array feed up across the wall.

"The correlation began yesterday morning and strengthened after dusk."

Three lines crawled over the graph. The top came from the Solistree root network. The second came from the Doll telemetry mesh. The third came from the sky.

Their shapes matched.

"Give me the scale and the timing between sources."

"The root network leads the telemetry mesh by six seconds, and the mesh leads the Etheric field by two."

"Which consent state produces the strongest match?"

"Ask traffic creates the clearest correlation. Yes produces a weaker echo, while sleep produces the faintest trace."

Across a continent, Dolls opened a switch Teddy had mailed through the air. Each choice passed through Collective satellites as a routine flag. Yes. Decline. Ask later. A weather system formed from a million tiny permissions, and the sky above Lahai-Roi answered in slow silver strokes.

TD-3 brought her chair closer. "The recurring clusters make it look like language."

"Every graph starts resembling language once a technician gives it three colors and enough confidence."

"You gave this one five colors, so your confidence appears unusually high."

"Two of those colors represent contempt for the original software. They carry separate evidentiary value."

Pekoe enlarged a cluster. The same sequence appeared in the root pulse, the telemetry handshake, and the ceiling. Three short. One long. A gap. A long run folding back through itself.

"It repeats every time a unit changes from yes to ask," she said.

"Send a query with an empty field and record every layer of the response."

"Which address should I use for the recipient?"

Teddy looked at the sky line. "Address it to whoever is listening nearest. The phrase already has field experience."

Pekoe typed. The Observatory array opened a carrier and sent a request carrying a blank field.

The tide-coil ticked twice.

The reply appeared on the wall.

**FIELD REQUIRES CONTENT**

TD-3 leaned forward until the table touched her ribs. "The reply came back in Standard."

"Standard may have inherited its grammar from this system," Teddy said. "The resemblance has two possible directions."

Pekoe's hands rested above the keys. "Then which direction should we test first?"

"Put that question in the blank field and let the system choose how to understand it."

The second query left.

The system logged a response time of minus one hundred and eleven seconds. A packet had waited in the receiver buffer for nearly two minutes before Pekoe composed the question.

The wall filled with audio.

Bridget hummed three notes, off-key. Water moved under the sound. Bees moved above it. Then Kit's voice, close to the microphone and trying to whisper at full volume:

*Teddy, bring the tin. The river's got a workshop in it.*

A second voice breathed behind hers. Same architecture. Same cadence. Younger by a season.

*Ask me before you open the channel.*

The packet ended.

Pekoe checked the station log. "Radio Nobody remains three days from Kirsk at its current speed."

"Kit drives with optimism, and Leva treats schedules as personal insults. They may improve that estimate."

"The packet still carries tomorrow's date, even under the receiver's local clock."

"Their captain keeps her chronometer fast. Two minutes explains habit; a full day requires weather."

The tide-coil ticked.

Teddy opened the tin.

Dark metal lay in a nest of lint. She clamped it, cut a shaving, and carried the shaving to the materials bench. The spectrometer reduced it to powder in a sealed cup. Light passed through the dust. The machine began naming what the world had made.

Iron. Carbon. Cobalt. Salt. Solistree resin. Mnemonic glass of the kind used in Doll recovery crystals. A trace of bone mineral. Two pigments discontinued before the Lament. A protein from a deep-water animal whose nearest known habitat lay eight thousand kilometers away.

The display printed every atom it could recognize.

The cat watched the cup turn.

"Did the spectrometer find any justice in the powder?" TD-3 asked.

"A spectrometer answers questions about matter. Justice requires a different instrument and a less obedient operator."

"Where would you look for mercy, then?"

"Start under consumables. People hide the essential supplies beside the embarrassing ones."

Pekoe opened the ledger. The cat's food occupied the expected page. Beneath it, in Pekoe's neat hand, stood a new line.

**MERCY — quantity variable — source: local**

Teddy looked at her.

"The category seemed useful, especially in a workshop that keeps receiving people as equipment."

"Your spelling is adequate, and the source field leaves us enough room to argue later."

The machine chimed. A serial fragment had surfaced in the mnemonic glass.

**KT-4 / SPRING / PARTIAL**

Teddy took her terminal out. Kit answered on the fourth ring with wind in the channel.

"Kit, is the recovery crystal in your pack warm?"

"Hello to you too, Teddy. I am enjoying the concern in your voice."

"Put your hand on the crystal and tell me exactly what you feel. This may involve your definition of concern."

Cloth rustled. A buckle snapped. Then silence, carrying the particular shape of Kit's face when surprise took the whole field.

"The crystal is warm, and something inside it took a breath a moment ago," Kit said. "Teddy, I think she is awake."

Somewhere beside her, from inside the folded poster, a second Kit drew breath.

## III. The Fifth Star

Kirsk had two banks, one bridge, seven ferries painted on the station mural, and a river wide enough to teach every fact two versions of itself.

The crisis had taken the bridge's center span. It stood with both broken ends reaching over brown water. The ferries had become houses, chicken coops, a chapel, and one tavern that rolled in heavy weather. The station clock remained at 16:12. Bees nested behind its face.

Kit found the repeater keeper on the north tower. BRG-9 wore **ASK** at her wrist and a tool belt broad enough to govern a province. She had replaced the old antenna with three tram rails, a ship mast, and the spine of a crystalline Discord.

"What range can you get from this arrangement?" Bridget asked.

"The answer depends on who wants to hear you. A regulation broadcast reaches three valleys."

"Radio Nobody has listeners beyond regulation."

"Then the whole river will hear you, including the parts flowing under the map."

Kit looked up through thirty meters of improvised confidence. "I have known her for one minute, and I already love her."

"You can join the queue after the tower survives the afternoon," BRG-9 said. "Affection receives better terms after inspection."

The black crystal stirred in Kit's pack.

She felt it as a second gait laid under her own. A memory of walking traveled inside a legless crystal, cramped among facets. The folded poster had gone warm at breakfast. By noon it tapped against the frame in pairs.

Two clean knocks. A pause. Two clean knocks.

Kit kept one hand on the pack while she helped guy the mast. The hand made her clumsy. Bridget watched two knots take twice their usual time.

"Tell me what you are carrying before it affects the work."

"Give me until we finish this tower, and I will tell you everything."

"That answer needs a measurable parameter."

"One tower is the parameter. When the last guyline holds, we talk."

Bridget accepted the parameter. That was the bylaw.

They found the beekeeper at dusk on the south bank, where forty white hives stood among late asters. He had gone grey as a gull. Age had taken his left hand and left the careful part. He lifted frames with a brass prosthesis, slow enough for the bees to keep their weather.

Bridget stopped at the gate.

He looked up.

"You arrived early, even by your usual standards."

She took the chronometer from her pocket. "You built two minutes of optimism into the mechanism. I kept them."

"Recalibrate at your own risk. Those minutes have carried you a long way."

Kit stood very still behind her. The phrase entered the photograph in Bridget's pocket and made one face breathe.

The beekeeper set the frame into the hive.

"I heard you had become boring," he said. "The report sounded suspicious."

"A debt of forty credits traveled two provinces to contradict it."

"Did Senna finally pay you? I expected that account to survive the continent."

"She paid with interest and included an insult, so the signature authenticated itself."

He smiled. The expression arrived along one side of his face and belonged to a younger man in a cold junction, handing a captain her own watch.

"Would you like tea before we begin spending eleven years?"

Bridget opened the gate.

His house had seven clocks, each two minutes fast. Honey jars occupied a shelf above the stove. Their labels gave weather, field, and season. One jar said **AFTER**. Another said **DURING**. A third said **FOR COMPANY**.

He put that one on the table.

Kit took the chair by the door. Bridget sat across from the fifth star.

"What name answers when people call from the gate?" she asked.

"I answer to Marten these days. The ferryman left it in a coat pocket."

"Has it started to fit you?"

"It fits well in warm weather. Winter still finds the old seams."

Bees moved inside the wall, a low gold chord.

"The relayman put Terran under the river and swore he watched the station take you."

"He watched me go into the water. His account earns its place in the file."

"A clinic restored your spring sync eighteen months later."

"They restored everything up to the junction choice. The missing minutes begin with my thumb above the guard."

"So your memory kept the junction and lost the decision."

"My memory reaches the choice, circles it, and wakes somewhere else."

The honey held sunlight from fields that had grown over a disaster. Bridget turned the spoon once.

Five years ago Terran had stood at junction C with floodwater at his knees and two evacuation lines drawing power from one failing relay. The north tunnel held six technicians. The south platform held forty-three civilians. He cut one line. He sent Bridget a clean report. The north tunnel folded three minutes later. The last frame showed his hands on the switch.

Marten remembered the rising water. He remembered both status boards. His memory ended with his thumb above the guard.

"Which line do you remember cutting?" Bridget asked.

"The official record says I sent power south, and forty-three people reached daylight."

"What do your hands remember when you sleep?"

The brass fingers opened on the table. "They dream that I cut the north line. The motion always feels certain."

Kit's pack tapped twice against the chair.

Marten glanced at it. "Are you carrying a passenger in that pack?"

"The luggage has become complicated, and I am waiting for the right words," Kit said.

"Complicated luggage usually carries the stories worth hearing. Give it time."

Bridget set the photograph between them. Five faces. Marten touched the fifth with one brass finger and left a crescent of honey over the mouth.

"I woke with three weeks missing and everybody grateful. Gratitude gets loud in a clinic. They brought the medal before they brought trousers. A ferryman named Marten left a coat on my bed and died that afternoon. His ticket sat in the pocket. Kirsk, one way."

"You put on the coat and used the ferryman's ticket."

"The ferry knew the route, and I needed somebody else's destination for one day."

"What did you do with eleven years of silence after that?"

"I spent the silence one season at a time. The bees accepted every name I brought them."

Outside, the river shouldered the bank. The station bell rang once in the current, deep and submerged.

Bridget's thumb covered her own face in the photograph.

"Come for dinner before winter," she said. "Take any road you like, and answer when the answer belongs to you."

Marten poured more tea. "Keep the kettle on, and I will keep the invitation somewhere warm."

It was the third thing. The door, oiled.

Kit went out among the hives while they sat with the years. She unbuckled her pack and brought out the folded poster. *Last Stand at Beohr* opened across her knees, soft at every crease. The black crystal lay in the hero's painted hands.

She touched it.

The repeater on the far bank woke with a burst of red light. Radio Nobody's carrier opened. Her own voice came through the receiver at her belt.

Young. Frightened. Working hard to sound brave.

"Did we manage to fix Leva's bike? I keep dreaming about the stripped bolts."

Kit's fingers closed around the crystal.

"The bike runs beautifully, and Leva still complains about our work."

"How much did she yell when she saw it?"

"She yelled a little, which counts as tenderness from Leva."

"Did Teddy stay with us through the repair?"

"Teddy stayed the whole time and operated at maximum volume."

The crystal warmed. A small laugh came through the radio, relieved all the way down.

"That sounds good," the voice said. "The bolts kept turning in my dreams, and the wrench always stayed beyond my reach."

Across the asters every hive opened at once. Bees lifted in forty dark columns and turned toward the river.

Marten came to the door. Bridget stood behind him with the photograph in one hand.

The submerged bell rang again.

Radio Nobody's meters climbed though the transmitter sat cold. A second carrier joined the first, then a third, each at a slightly different age. Doll voices filled the band. Some asked where they were. Some recited last orders. Some sang. One read a weather report for a winter eleven years gone.

From beneath the river, Terran's voice called Bridget by the name on the old manifest.

Marten looked at her.

The bees crossed the water in the shape of a hand opening.

## IV. Window Two

The message reached every member of Squad 5 before Kit sent it.

Leva heard it on the north road at 06:10, with Soppo asleep against the rover door and Ro keeping its red eye on dawn.

Teddy heard it in the Observatory workshop at 06:08. She had the tide-coil packed by 06:09 and an invoice on Dhalifa's desk by 06:11.

Pekoe read the invoice.

"You described this as an emergency field consultation," Pekoe said. "The phrase is accurate enough for accounting."

"I also wrote that the duration remains indeterminate."

"You entered weather in place of a date, which may upset payroll."

"Payroll has survived worse weather."

"What condition should we use for your return?"

Teddy looked at the empty peg where her jacket had waited a year. "Keep dusting the peg. I will come back when I have something useful to hang on it."

Pekoe handed her a thermos and the eastern array's morning plot. It lied by two points, faithful as sunrise.

"The question stays open while you are gone," Pekoe said. "I have left space in the log."

"You have accumulated several questions. Which one gets the space?"

"The one about originals, copies, and families. I suspect it is a single question wearing three labels."

TD-3 came from the far bench carrying the repaired tuner. Her solder ran clean. A small scorch beside the dial recorded the instructive part.

"I can keep the cat company while Pekoe works," TD-3 said.

"The cat keeps herself, and she will invoice you for the privilege."

"Then I can stand in the open field and write down whatever drifts."

Teddy checked the joint, tugged the cable, and gave the tuner back. "Stand in the field, write down the drift, and include the parts that embarrass your model."

She took the Riseway up at noon.

Radio Nobody guided them toward Kirsk in fragments from a day that had yet to occur.

*Marker ninety has washed out, so take the orchard road.*

*The south ferry stair will hold until the eighth bell.*

*Bring a long rope for the stair.*

*Soppo, duck below the window line now.*

Soppo woke for that one.

"How long do I need to stay down?" Soppo asked the radio.

The radio gave her five seconds of static and a sharp metallic crash.

She ducked.

A road sign, loosened by wind, scythed over the rover roof and buried itself in a pear tree.

Soppo looked at Leva.

"This station becomes more useful every time it violates causality," Soppo said.

"It gets results, and the road sign appears to agree."

"We could ask who wins next season and finance the rest of the trip."

"The market closed as soon as tomorrow began sending advice. Even Agrat respects house limits."

At midday the black Chevelle passed them going north. Its tires left river water on the dry road. The woman at the wheel wore beekeeper's gloves. A ferry ticket fluttered from the mirror.

Soppo reached for her rifle.

Leva kept driving.

"We chase her with roads," she said. "Roads take patience, and she keeps leaving us directions."

"My preferred form of patience came with a trigger and a sight."

"That feature is aftermarket. Let the road carry its own argument for a while."

The Chevelle turned through a stand of poplars and disappeared. At the turn, nailed to a tree, hung a fresh sign in small spare handwriting.

**KIRSK 4 DAYS**

Leva stopped and crossed out the four. She wrote three.

Soppo watched with her tongue between her teeth. "Are you two fighting through road signs now?"

"We are corresponding in a medium she appears to respect."

They reached Kirsk in two.

Squad 5 reunited at the repeater tower under a sky the color of a spoon. Kit hit Teddy at speed. Teddy stayed upright through engineering and spite.

"The recovery crystal has started talking to me," Kit said as soon as she could breathe.

"Most people around you develop that habit eventually," Teddy said. "Tell me what she remembers."

"She remembers the repair bay, the stripped bolts, and being afraid of Leva."

"A memory crystal containing memories fits the first half of the evidence."

"She also dreamed while I carried her. That part changes the question."

Teddy's hands stopped halfway through pushing Kit off.

The pack between them gave two precise knocks.

"Put her on a stable table and give me room to connect the instruments," Teddy said.

BRG-9 supplied her workroom, a former ferry office with a stove, a coil winder, and six jars labeled **SCREWS** whose contents reflected six interpretations of the word. Teddy cleared the table. The black crystal went onto a folded towel. The tide-coil, still in its tin, ticked beside it.

Soppo leaned in until Leva caught her harness.

"Give her some air and stop leaning over the microphone," Leva said.

"She currently lives in a rock, and rocks have always liked me."

"Professional courtesy applies before we know what she calls herself."

"I can show professional courtesy from six centimeters farther away."

The crystal spoke through every receiver in the room.

"I heard the door. Who came into the room?"

Kit looked around. Bridget by the stove. Leva at the window. Teddy over the instruments. Soppo vibrating in Leva's grip. Marten in the doorway with a honey jar. BRG-9 halfway down the repeater ladder, listening through the wall.

"They are friends," Kit said. "Some of them arrived after the last memory you have."

"How many people are standing with you?"

Bridget counted. "There are six people in the room and one listening from the ladder."

The crystal waited.

"I remember four of you," the voice said. "The room sounds larger than it used to."

Kit's hand found the tattoo on her forearm. Three lanterns, rising.

For one quick beat she wanted the black crystal quiet. The want arrived whole and ugly. Her pack had carried a keepsake. A keepsake asks little. A person changes the seating plan.

Teddy looked at her. Teddy knew the face of an unwelcome replication.

"The switch belongs to you," she said. "Whatever answer comes next has to be yours as well."

Kit put her fingers on the transmitter key.

"Do you want me to keep this channel open?"

"Please keep it open. I want to hear the room."

Kit keyed the relay.

The crystal exhaled. The sound carried relief, fear, and the small pride of somebody allowed to answer a question correctly.

"Hello," Soppo said into the nearest microphone. "My name is Soppo, and Teddy recently gave me excellent knees."

"Hello, Soppo. This is my first introduction conducted through somebody's knees."

"Would you like to hear them work? The silence is the impressive part."

She squatted twice. Her joints moved in bright silence.

The voice from the crystal laughed.

"That demonstration was acceptable," Teddy said, and began attaching leads. "Please keep the acrobatics away from my instruments."

At sunset slack water came to Kirsk. The river flattened from bank to bank. Its surface took the sky and held it with such exactness that the ruined bridge continued downward into another country.

The station clock struck 16:12.

Under the south bank, steps rose from the water.

One by one, stone treads surfaced in the mud, running down beneath the river toward lamps burning green. People gathered along the quay. Townsfolk watched with the calm of citizens whose local miracles had acquired timetables.

Marten brought a basket of honey cakes. "Window two receives eleven customers during each opening."

"Does that mean eleven customers every day?" Soppo asked.

"It means eleven across each version of the history. The distinction matters after the stairs appear."

"That sounds like a busy office with an excellent excuse for slow service."

They descended. The river stood on both sides of the stair in clear vertical walls. Fish moved through drowned ticket halls. Old departure boards clicked behind the water, cycling names of places erased by the crisis. At the eighth step, Leva saw the stars overhead reflected in the river wall: Lyra, Cassiopeia, the whole chart. The real sky carried silver weather.

At the bottom waited a tiled room and a brass grille.

Eleven customers stood in line. A child holding one wet shoe. A shepherd with a bell clapper. A woman carrying a jar of breath. A combat Doll whose face had been sanded smooth, holding a name written on ribbon. Their reflections stood in the opposite order inside the glass.

Soppo made twelve.

## VI. Whoever Is Standing Nearest

Bridget met it at the bridge approach.

The creature folded onto one side.

Teddy had the relay's access panel open with both arms inside. "Some are checksums, some are partials, and some are weather wearing a voice. I need time before I can tell which is which."

"Listen, big thing! Your angles are terrible, and you are dragging me toward the fish market!"

**CHANNEL STATE: SPEAK / LISTEN / SLEEP / ASK**

"I am sending it to everyone the relay can reach. The creature happens to be standing nearest."

The code went out.

The third chose speak and began demanding a lawyer.

"Set it to ask, because I want the next destination to answer too."

Kit set the flag and put her beside spring-Kit.

At the top she found the core: a black sphere wrapped in ferry cable, beating at the same meter as the tide-coil.

Ro scanned it and whined.

"What have you been running all this way to fetch?" Soppo asked. "Show me the end of the route."

At the end waited one image: the Etheric Sea seen from above.

Its silver weather formed the inside of a sphere.

"You have carried that order through a very long run," she said. "You can put it down here, and we will keep the pieces together."

The red eye closed.

The creature released its last order. Girder ribs settled over the quay. Ferry rails became ferry rails. A hundred feet of Dark Tide body turned into salvage, each piece bearing tool marks from hands long gone.

"That sounds like permission with technical detail."

"Put it in the tide-coil tin until I build proper containment."

"Your current tin would fit one of its feelings."

"Then I will make a larger tin, and you will carry it away from your sleeping bag."

The last crystal fell.

Leva caught it one-handed.

Inside, the grey woman from the window drove the Chevelle through deep water. Her passenger turned at last. Leva saw the beginning of a face and lost the image to static.

**SOUTH WINDOW MISSED. KEEP GOING.**

"It gave me directions to a south window we apparently missed."

"It looks expensive, which often means somebody invested heavily in keeping it obscure."

Thirty-two.

## VII. One Atom

Kirsk spent the night finding places for voices.

Sleeping crystals went into wool-lined honey crates. Listening crystals gathered around Radio Nobody's monitors. Speaking crystals received numbers, microphones, and the local understanding that midnight volume belonged to the tavern. Ask crystals sat on the repeater office windowsill, where weather could reach them first.

There were one hundred and seventeen.

Teddy refused the word *survivor* until morning. She refused *copy* through breakfast. She refused *person* until each channel could sustain a question. Then she refused all three words together and built a rack.

Pekoe called over the Observatory link.

"Which inventory category should I enter for the recovered crystals?" Pekoe asked.

Teddy drove a screw home. "Enter them as guests until each one chooses a better word."

"What duration should I place on their stay?"

"Ask them individually and leave the field open until they answer."

"Where should I send the bill for equipment and housing?"

"Send everything to Acquisitions and add emotional damages as a recurring charge."

In the background, TD-3 said something about the eastern array. A cat knocked a tool to the floor. The room tone traveled up from under Lahai-Roi and crossed the continent.

One of the listening crystals turned blue.

"I know the sound of that workshop," a voice said. "The power plant used to fall a quarter-tone lower at night."

Pekoe leaned toward her microphone. "Which bench did you work at, and what did you see from it?"

"I used the bench by the window before somebody moved it toward the spring light."

Teddy looked at the rack.

The voice belonged to a TD baseline built between Teddy's fire and Pekoe's activation, omitted from every production ledger they had seen. She remembered the Institute reviewer with the neat hand. She remembered a fire alarm. She remembered somebody opening a service door from outside and saying *run*.

Her memory ended before she saw the face.

"What name did the Institute use for you?" Pekoe asked.

"They called me Noise Test Four, although the word felt more like a filing decision than a name."

Teddy touched the tattoo over her diagnostic port.

"There is plenty of room in this series," Teddy said. "Take your time and choose something the paperwork would have rejected."

Across the room, Spring-Kit stayed awake.

Current Kit had moved her into the rover, away from the crowd. The black crystal stood in a mug cushioned with a sock. Outside, dawn laid pale gold over the river. Inside, two versions of a young woman shared one silence and found it crowded.

"How far do your memories reach?" Kit asked. "Start wherever the room feels clear."

"I remember the posters, the road, and Bridget pulling me up. I remember Teddy hating me, the bike under our hands, and the stars after everything hurt."

"Teddy liked you even then. Hatred was the tool she had unpacked."

"I figured that out later, after she ate the noodles and complained through every bite."

"You understood her in three days, which may be a squad record."

"It felt longer from inside the repair bay. How long did it take you?"

Kit smiled despite herself. "I am still working on it, and she keeps revising the test."

The crystal turned warm against the sock.

"I heard you all summer," Spring-Kit said. "Your voices reached me from windowsills, through rain, inside inns, and over the radio. Sometimes I dreamed I was the road under the bike, and sometimes I was the song after sign-off."

Kit watched her own hands. The tattoo's lanterns leaned over the knuckles.

"Yesterday, when you started speaking in front of everyone, I wanted to switch you off," Kit said. "The thought arrived before I could make it kind."

"How long did you hold onto it?"

"It lasted for one beat, and then I remembered the switch."

"What did you do when you remembered?"

"I asked what you wanted, and you asked me to keep the channel open."

The river touched the quay outside.

"Then you gave me the part that mattered," Spring-Kit said. "A frightened thought can pass through and leave the choice untouched."

Kit's throat tightened. "I carried your life forward and started calling all of it mine."

"You carried some of it, and you made more while I listened from the crystal."

"Do you want any part of that life returned to you?"

The crystal held the dawn inside its black surface, a sun in very deep water.

"I want hands," she said. "Then I want to find out what I want."

Kit sat with that. Somewhere in the request lived four hundred shared faces, an old convoy ditch, an installed play, a year of chosen miles, and the possibility of sharing a name with someone who could remember being her.

"What should I call you while we work out the rest?"

"Kit still works for me. I remember answering to it."

"Using the same name will become confusing as soon as you have hands."

"We can take turns until one of us finds a name that fits better."

Current Kit laughed. The sound came out frightened and fond in equal measure.

"Then we have a temporary protocol," current Kit said. "I reserve the right to complain during revisions."

"Everything important in this squad begins in draft form. Complaints can go in the margins."

They left the name open.

At Marten's hives, Bridget repaired a frame while he gathered the swarm that had crossed the river. He watched her fit the corner square, remove it, and fit it again.

"You are still angry with me," he said. "The corner has been square for several minutes."

"Eleven years of silence accrues interest, and the frame is receiving a small portion of it."

"I had one word available, and it belonged to somebody else when I found it."

He checked the queen cell and closed the lid. "I am growing into it. The bees already turn when they hear it."

Bridget took out the photograph. The crescent of honey had dried over Terran's mouth. She wiped the grit from the corners and left the honey.

"The voice in the river knew the designation I carried before Senna renamed me."

"I knew that designation too, along with every bad joke Senna made from it."

"The river voice says Terran chose the north line."

Marten watched bees enter the hive one at a time, carrying yellow fields on their legs. "My hands dream of cutting north, and dreams have poor evidentiary discipline."

"The south board went green, and the evacuation platform emptied."

"Forty-three witnesses carried that light out of the tunnel."

"Six names stayed under it."

"Keep both accounts open. A clean answer would spend somebody twice."

She put the photograph away.

"Come to dinner before winter settles over the roads," Bridget said. "The invitation still has a stove attached."

He smiled into the hive. "That is exactly why the invitation interests me. Bring whoever answers first."

Bridget laughed. The real one. The bees accepted it as weather.

At the repeater house Soppo placed the clear cassette beside the Dark Tide core. Teddy's larger tin had begun as a biscuit box. She lined it with three layers of sensor cloth and wrote **EXTREMELY CURSED** on the lid.

"You could paint flowers over the warning," Soppo said. "Curses deserve attractive storage."

"The skull would be redundant, while the stars would undermine containment. The current label stays."

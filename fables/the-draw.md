# The Draw

*a coast story — after* Fetch

> **draw**, *n.*
> 1. the pairing of names, made before anyone has fought.
> 2. the count of bodies a thing pulls through a gate.
> 3. the result when the ledger squares and the crowd goes home hungry.

---

*]teach it a name and it will chant the name[*
*]teach it two hundred and it will go home and set the table[*

## I. The Card

*SLACK TIDE INVITATIONAL — PROVISIONAL CARD.*
*Bouts run at slack water, twice daily, tide permitting.*
*The sea keeps the time and hears no protest.*
*Rule 9, imported unamended: a vacancy may be entered.*

The sea gave Cormorant Reach its arena twice a day and took it back twice a day, and the promoter had printed that on the handbills as a feature.

At low water the flats came out from under the harbor like a floor being uncovered for a dance: three hundred meters of hard grey sand, raked by a man with a horse and a chain harrow, roped off in a circle by fishing floats. Scaffolding went up the landward side in tiers, plank benches bolted to pipe, and above the top tier stood the lighting standards with their small gold orbs, one to a leg, catching the morning like eggs in a nest.

The gate was a gate: an iron door in the sea wall, at the head of the stairs, with a man at a card table selling admission and a cash box chained to his ankle.

Leva stood at the top of the stairs with a coffee and read the card.

**FIRST CARD, EVENING SLACK** — and then the pairings, chalked on slate, eight teams a side, the brackets running down toward a single blank line at the bottom.

Halfway down the left column: **SPARE PARTS.**

Four lines under it, in the officials' column, where the panel was listed:

**JUDGES: DOSSI, R. — KELL, N. — LEVA.**

She read it twice. She drank her coffee. She read it a third time, on the principle that a thing worth reading twice deserves to know it's being taken seriously.

"You're judging your own fight," Kit said, arriving at her elbow at the speed Kit arrived at things. "That's *incredible*. That's so corrupt. I'm so proud."

"Mm."

"Is it corrupt? Can I say it's corrupt on air? I have a slot at nine."

"It's a different woman," Leva said.

Kit looked at the slate. Kit looked at Leva. Somewhere behind the bright face the whole comms suite came online and went quietly to work.

"Huh," she said.

Down on the flats the harrow went past, and the sand came up behind it in long combed lines, and gulls dropped into the furrow after whatever the tide had left.

Bridget came up the stairs from the beach with sand to the knee and her sunglasses pushed up into her hair, which meant she had been looking at something closely.

"Two thousand seats," she said. "They'll seat four."

"Standing on the wall," Leva said.

"Standing on the wall, standing on the roofs, standing in the *water* if the water's out." Bridget turned and looked at the bowl of it, the tiers, the raked circle, the way the whole town leaned over its own beach. "Somebody built this to be looked at."

"Somebody built it to be looked *from*," Teddy said.

She had a lighting standard's base plate open at her feet and one of the gold orbs in her hands like a woman holding an egg she suspects.

"Ex-municipal," she said. "Audio-visual. Surplus lot, sold at auction, Ragunna clearing-house, foundry mark under the flange." Her thumb found it. Her voice went flat and forensic, the register the corridor knew to sit down for. "Serial block's been reissued. Twice."

"Broadcast relays," Leva said.

"That's what the docket says."

"And?"

Teddy set the orb back in its cradle with the tenderness of a woman returning a scorpion to a shoe.

"And a relay doesn't need a *sample port*," she said, and shut the plate, and walked off up the beach to wash her hands.

Soppo came out of the water.

She had been in it since dawn, on the grounds that the sea was there, and she came up the flats with her jacket over one shoulder and her boots in her hand and Ro trotting beside her in a private ecstasy of crabs. Half the harrow crew stopped to watch her come. She waved at them with the boots. Two waved back before they knew they were doing it.

"They've got a fish stall," she reported, "that does a thing with the heads."

"You'll eat the heads."

"I ate the heads." She squinted up at the slate, mouth moving. Her reading was a physical activity. "Spare Parts. That's us. That's *us*, Leva, we're on a *board*—" and then she found the officials' column, and her face did something Leva was learning to price. "Huh."

"Different woman."

"Sure," Soppo said, and shook herself, and looked at the sea instead.

---

The town had swollen the way towns swell. Ferry traffic doubled and then stopped being counted. The chandlery let out its lofts by the night, then by the half-night. Somebody had trucked in a robo-cart from Septimont with its accent intact and parked it at the head of the stairs, and it sold volcanic-black souvenir cups at four times the price of the soda in them, and by noon there were children on the sea wall drinking out of Soppo's face.

Leva walked the whole thing once, slowly, the way she walked everything, and by the time she came back to the gate she had a map of it in her head: four entrances, two of them real; the promoter's trailer; the tide table nailed to the ticket table; a hospitality box built out over the wall on scaffold and canvas, with a door, and a blank plate where the door's name goes.

She looked at the box for a while.

Then she went and bought a program, which cost more than the coffee, and sat on the wall in the sun with a pencil and began, in her spare surgical hand, to make a list.

## II. Weights and Measures

*ROYA COASTAL FERRY AUTHORITY — SHED 3.*
*Temporary use permit: registration, weigh-in, adjudication.*
*Occupancy 200. Shed floods at spring tides.*
*Advisory: keep the paperwork high.*

Shed 3 smelled of creosote and old ice. They had run trestle tables down the middle and hung a scale from a beam, and the queue went out the door and along the quay and had the atmosphere of a queue that suspects it is the best part.

Two hundred entrants, give or take. Deckhands with taped fists. A pair of wreck divers who had brought their own oxygen and their own lawyer. Three Dolls off a dredger, chassis grey with silt, holding hands in the line. A man of about sixty in a suit, which was the whole of him, and who lasted eleven seconds in the prelims and had, by all accounts, the finest night of his life.

Kit walked the queue with a hand recorder and a face like a birthday.

"Radio Nobody, coastal special, you don't have to say yes — name, and one true thing about you."

"Ain't got a true thing."

"Everybody's got one. I'll wait. I'm famous for waiting."

Leva worked the other end of the shed, and worked it the way she worked everything, which is to say she bought people drinks from the shed's terrible urn and let them talk while she wrote things down.

At the trestle a woman was arguing with the registrar about spelling.

Halfway along, a KT-series in a sponsor's windbreaker was getting her reach measured, and she was telling the official a story about a dog with the little lift before the last word, the one Kit had built over four hundred nights of dark road, and Kit stopped in the aisle with the recorder still running.

The KT saw her. Lit up with the frank warmth of a unit meeting a unit.

"Oh — hey! Are you in? Are you fighting?"

"Corner," Kit said. "Comms."

"Comms is *huge*." The KT stuck her hand out and shook with both of hers. "I'm 2.1. I mean, that's the — everyone says the package like it's a name, sorry, it's Vesper, I picked it off a chart." A beat, a grin, the same grin, brand new. "Do you want my one true thing? The lady with the recorder said everyone's got one."

"…Yeah," Kit said. "Yeah, I do."

"I like the sound the ferry makes when it hits the fenders." Vesper considered this, and found it good, and said it again with more commitment. "That's mine. That's the whole thing."

Kit came away down the aisle very steady and very quiet, and Teddy, still mending a strap, said, "Well?"

"She's *nice*," Kit said, with feeling, like an injury report.

---

The officials' table stood at the shed's dry end under a bare bulb, and the officials sat behind it in a row: the promoter Dossi, a big glad-handing Septimont expat gone soft and prosperous in the coastal damp; old Nan Kell, who had run the ferry timetable for thirty years and had accepted the office of referee on the grounds that she owned the only clock anyone here obeyed; and, at the end of the row, a woman built along tired lines with hair the grey of deep water, drinking out of a thermos.

Leva put her form on the table.

The registrar took it, and looked at the name, and looked down the row, and said the thing the clerk of every world says eventually:

"Which one?"

"The one who's fighting," said the woman with the thermos.

The registrar stamped it. Leva stayed where she was.

"Judge," she said.

"Mm."

"You came a long way to score a beach fight."

"I like to see who's honest." The thermos went up, and down. "It's rare where I'm from."

"Where's that."

The woman looked at her over the cup with an expression that had traveled a great distance to be that mild, and Leva's hand, which knew things before she did, went still on the table.

"You're using my name," Leva said.

"You're using mine."

"I took it off a beer."

Something turned over deep in the woman's face — a laugh, at a great depth, the way a keel turns over — and she screwed the cap back on the thermos.

"So did I," she said.

---

The Septimont observer arrived on the noon ferry with a duffel, a sanction folder, and a tail, and stood in the shed door blocking most of the light.

"Who licensed this," said Lupa.

Dossi came around the table with both hands out and the smile of a man who has paid every fee twice.

"Council of Septimont, honored — the Sport and Heritage instrument, section four, the format's licensed clean, I have the—"

"I know what you have. I signed it." Lupa dropped the duffel. "That's why I'm here. Somebody has to watch the thing they signed."

She went through the shed like weather. She smelled the room once, comprehensively, the way her kind does, and stopped twice: once at a stack of the gold orbs in their crate, where her lip lifted off her teeth without her permission; and once, at the end of the row of tables, at Soppo, who was sitting on a fish crate eating something out of a paper cone.

They looked at each other for a while.

"Capitoline," Lupa said.

"Two seasons."

"I know your walk." A pause. "The crowd chants it wrong, by the way. They put the stress on the wrong bit. *Spo*-lia."

"They chant it however they want. That's the deal." Soppo offered the cone. "Fish."

Lupa took a piece of fish.

They went out onto the quay together and stood in the sun, and down at the head of the stairs the robo-cart was doing a brisk trade, and on the side of it, three meters tall, backlit, a silhouette lunged forever with a gun in one hand and somebody's arm in the other.

Soppo looked at it with her head on one side.

"That's me."

"I know."

"They got the shoulder wrong. My shoulder's—" she rolled it, and it sounded like a chain locker— "it's not that shape. That's a nicer shoulder."

Lupa put two coins in the cart and took two cups, and handed one over, and drank from hers, and on the side of hers a wolf ran forever through a stylized flame under a brand of energy drink that had been discontinued for two years and reprinted for the festival trade.

Soppo looked at that one too.

"Huh," she said.

"Mm."

"On the house?"

"Never is," said Lupa.

They drank their own faces in the sun. Out past the mole the tide was going, and the flats were coming up wet and shining, and the harrow was already down there walking its long combed lines.

"Rule nine," Lupa said, eventually.

"Yeah?"

"You know it."

"I've been in it," Soppo said, easy as weather, and crushed her cup, and dropped it in the bin, and went to find the fish stall again.

Lupa stood a while longer with the wolf in her fist.

Then she went to find the promoter's copy of the imported rulebook, and read section nine, and read it again, and closed the book and left it standing — which took her, from first reading to walking away, eleven minutes, and cost her more than the ferry.

## III. Corners

*RADIO NOBODY — SPECIAL SCHEDULE (COAST).*
*The Fast Hour moves for the tide.*
*The three held minutes hold.*
*Fight coverage: as it happens, and no faster.*

They had a room over the chandlery with two windows and a stove, and it became a corner the way rooms do — buckets in the doorway, tape on the sill, somebody's spare arm on the table next to the bread.

Teddy did the fitting on the second morning.

"Up."

Soppo put her leg up on the crate. Teddy went in with the spanner past the knee cowl, and the harvested actuator came out of the satchel and off its tick and into the light, and there was a long silence full of small metal noises.

"This is Tide-plate."

"He had *great* knees."

"It's Tide-plate, Soppo. It's been in the water with something." Teddy turned it over. The barnacle-work on the housing came away under her thumbnail in a plate, like a scab. "There's a rhythm in the coil. Feel that? It's holding a beat."

"Yeah, it does that at night."

"You've been *sleeping* next to it."

"I sleep great," Soppo said, radiant.

Teddy said something in a language of her own devising and got the loupe out.

It took her three hours. She stripped the harvested joint, rebuilt what could be rebuilt, machined a bearing race out of stock on the little lathe with the door open to the sea wind, and threw away every part of it that had ever been in the dark water, which was most of it, and made the rest new. What went into Soppo's leg at the end of it was a hand-cut assembly with tolerances that had ended careers, wearing a scrap of the old plate on the cowl for continuity's sake because Soppo asked, and Teddy did it, and pronounced the request "sentimental" and "structurally irrelevant" and cut the piece to fit within a tenth.

Soppo stood up. Walked to the window. Walked back. Squatted, rose, hopped twice, laughed.

"It doesn't click!"

"No."

"TEDDY. It doesn't *click*—"

"Do not," Teddy said, and was hugged, at length, off the floor, with her arms pinned, while she stared over Soppo's shoulder at Kit with an expression of complete strategic defeat.

Afterward, when the room had emptied out, Teddy sat by the stove and turned the discarded coil over in her fingers for a long time, and listened to it keep its beat, and looked out the window at the silver ceiling of the world doing exactly the same thing at a different scale, and put the coil in a tin, and put the tin in her jacket, and told nobody.

---

Leva worked the program.

By the third day the margins were full and she had started a second sheet, and the second sheet had columns. Names. Boats they crewed. Who had come to watch them. One true thing, where she could get it. She got it by buying rounds, by losing at cards, by asking a docker's mother where the family was from and then remembering, three hours later, in front of the mother's neighbor, exactly what she'd said. It was the cheapest trick in her whole trade and it worked every time.

Bridget watched her do it for two days.

"You've entered a fight," she said, on the second evening, drying a cup, "and you're doing paperwork."

"Mm."

"Anybody else, I'd say they were scared."

"Anybody else would be." Leva turned a page. "Twenty-two teams pulled out after registration closed. Their entry fees stayed in."

"That's a promoter."

"That's a promoter," Leva agreed. "It's also a pattern. The twenty-two all came off the same three boats." She wrote something. "Somebody bought their week."

Bridget put the cup away. The gulls were going over the roof toward the flats, where the light rig had come on early and stood burning against the dusk, hours ahead of any use for it.

"Kit's calling the card tonight," she said.

"I heard."

"On the pirate net. To an audience of unknown size and unknown location." Bridget picked up the next cup. "Their business."

"Their business," Leva said.

## IV. Prelims

*TICKET STUB — GENERAL ADMISSION, EVENING SLACK.*
*Admits one. No refund for weather, tide, or outcome.*
*Reverse: a fighter's silhouette in gold foil. Unnamed.*

At 19:40 old Nan Kell walked out to the middle of the raked circle with a brass bell and a tide watch and rang the bell once, and four thousand people on the sea wall of Cormorant Reach made a sound like a wave arriving early.

"Slack water," she said, to the ring, to the town, to nobody. "Twenty-six minutes. After that it's the sea's."

Kit, on the scaffold with a headset and a boom, said into the dark of a hundred thousand square kilometers of coast road, "*Hello, the roads. We are on the sand.*"

The prelims went the way prelims go. Fast, sloppy, gorgeous. The dredger Dolls fought like people who had spent eleven years being told to stop, and cried afterward, all three of them, in a heap. The wreck divers won on a technicality and were booed with real affection. The man in the suit went down in eleven seconds and got up and bowed to all four sides of a perfectly round ring, and the town gave him more noise than it gave the winner, and he wept into his own lapel, and Kit called it with such tenderness that a truck driver eighty kilometers up the coast had to pull over.

SPARE PARTS drew a pair of harbor Dolls called Brace and Tackle: crane units, six meters of reach between them, sweet-natured, contracted out to the port for eleven years and entered in the tournament because the purse was exactly the cost of a slew ring.

Leva fought them the way a very patient woman fights two cranes.

She got under Brace's arc in the first ten seconds and stayed there, and she had a jammer strapped inside her jacket that she had built at the kitchen table out of a marine radio and spite, and Brace's shoulder ran two degrees off true for the rest of the bout, and Brace spent the rest of it hunting for the reason. She put Tackle on the ground twice by way of Tackle's own momentum, and let her up both times, and the crowd — who had come for a war and were being shown a lecture — went from restless to fascinated to loud, in that order, because the thing a crowd loves best is watching a small thing take a large thing apart on schedule.

Soppo cornered. She yelled. She was, as advertised, an excellent yeller.

At nineteen minutes Tackle got a hand on Leva.

It was one hand. It closed, and the whole architecture of the bout inverted, and Leva went off her feet and across the sand and hit the rope of floats and lay there for two full seconds doing arithmetic, and the crowd's roar came down on the flats like weather, and inside the roar Soppo was over the rope with her jacket still on.

"*Tag!*" she bellowed. "*THAT'S A TAG, that's — Nan! NAN. Is that a tag?*"

"That's a tag," said Nan Kell, who had been running a ferry timetable for thirty years and knew a schedule change when she saw one.

Soppo took her jacket off.

She dropped it on the sand outside the floats, and the crowd of Cormorant Reach — four thousand people who had bought souvenir cups all week with a silhouette on the side — watched the silhouette walk into the ring and become a person, and made a sound Leva had heard before at the wrong end of a bad night in a bad country.

Then Soppo fought.

She fought like something you'd build to end an argument. She took Brace's first hit on the shoulder to learn it and the second on the same shoulder to prove she'd learned it, and went inside, and put both hands on the crane arm, and turned it, and something in Brace's elbow let go with a sound like a dropped anchor. She was laughing. It was the worst part. She came out of the turn with the elbow assembly in her fist, looked at it, said "sorry, sorry, sorry" in a completely sincere voice, threw it out of the ring, and went back in.

At twenty-four minutes Tackle sat down in the sand and put up a hand and Nan Kell rang the bell.

Soppo got Brace's arm back for her out of the crowd, personally, and apologized to it. She helped Tackle up. She hugged them both, one after the other, hard enough to make the plating ring, and then stood in the middle of the raked circle with her hands on her head and her chest going like a bellows and let four thousand people learn her name off the side of a cup.

**SPO-LI-A. SPO-LI-A.**

They had the stress wrong. She let them.

Up on the scaffold Kit called the finish clean and fast and true, and then stopped talking, because on the tier below her, Bridget had come to her feet.

Bridget was looking at the sand.

"Boss?"

"Count them," Bridget said.

"Count what—"

"On the sand." Her voice was the convoy voice, the one that puts boots on the floor. "Count what's on the sand."

Kit counted. Brace, Tackle, Nan Kell, Soppo.

"Four."

"Four now." Bridget sat back down, slowly, and pushed her sunglasses up into her hair, and looked at the flats the way she looked at weather fronts coming in over a road with people on it. "For about nine seconds there, in the turn, I had five."

---

That night the town drank itself hoarse and Radio Nobody ran two hours over.

Kit came in off the air at one in the morning with her ears ringing and her hands still going, and sat on the end of the bed with her headset around her neck, and did the thing where she stopped moving.

"Say it," Teddy said, from the table.

"I got ahead of it."

"You got ahead of what."

"The turn." Kit's hands opened, closed. "Twenty-one minutes in. I said *she's going for the elbow* — I said it, it's on the tape, I've listened four times — and then she went for the elbow. And that's just calling a fight, right, that's *reading*, that's what you do, you watch the shoulders and you say the thing a beat early and you sound like a genius."

"Sure."

"It was a beat and a half." Kit looked up. "Teddy. It was a beat and a half early, and the *crowd* was ahead of *me*."

The stove ticked. Out past the sea wall the tide was over the flats and the arena was gone under three meters of black water and would be given back in the morning.

Leva, on the windowsill with her boots off, turned a page of the program and wrote a name in the second column and kept her eyes on it.

"Keep the tape," she said.

## V. The Book

*ODDS BOARD, HANDWRITTEN, END OF THE MOLE.*
*All wagers final. All stakes accepted.*
*The house declines to explain the house.*

The book ran out of the last boat shed on the mole, where the lamps gave out, under an oiled paper umbrella the color of dark red rain.

By the fourth day the board beside it was a wall of chalk, three deep, amended hourly in four hands. The last hand wrote small and beautifully, and every line it wrote stood.

Leva came down the mole at midnight and sat, first, which under this umbrella was the invitation.

"The rifle," said Agrat bat Mahlat, with genuine pleasure. "You've been busy. I've had to move you twice."

A beer stood at Leva's elbow at four degrees, frost climbing the can, standing there since before she had decided to want one.

"Who's moving me."

"Money. Money moves you. Money has been coming in off three boats all week in envelopes, on the crane girls, who are darlings and were always going to lose." The demon queen laid a stone in her store. The Mancala board was already seeded, the way it always was, the way it had been before she sat down. "Somebody has been building a price. It's clumsy work. I've been enjoying it the way you enjoy a bad play."

"You'd know."

"I'd know."

Leva played four minutes and lost with the serenity of a woman paying a toll she had budgeted for.

"The instrument in the box," she said, laying her last stone. "You've seen it."

"I've smelled it." Agrat's young mouth did something at the corner. "It's a thermometer. There's a difference between the thermometer and the fever, and everyone in that box has confused them, and I find that restful. It means they'll be surprised."

"By what."

"That's the toll," Agrat said, gathering the stones with a sound like surf, "and you're short."

Behind them, out at the lamp's edge, Soppo had been standing for some minutes with her hands in her pockets, in the posture of a large dog who has been told to wait outside a shop and has decided to be superb about it.

Agrat looked past Leva without turning her head.

"Come and settle, then," she said.

Soppo came and sat on the boards with her legs crossed. Ro planted itself at her hip and glared at the umbrella with its eye at half burn.

"You said to come back when I'd worked out what's mine to stake."

"I did."

"I thought about it a lot." Soppo said this the way she said everything, out loud, all of it. "I've got the gun. Sarge gave me the doctrine that goes with it, so half the gun's hers. I've got the arm, but I stripped that off a Nyto in a bad year and I've never decided if that's mine or salvage. I've got Ro, and Ro's *Roro's*, and you don't stake somebody else's dog."

"Correct."

"And I've got the thing they made out of my face." She hooked a thumb back up the mole toward the town, toward the cart, toward four thousand cups in four thousand bins. "There's a licensing office under Capitoline with a window and a line. And behind the window there's an account with my name's money in it. Two seasons of it. I never opened it. Somebody opened it and it's been sitting there earning, and every year it gets bigger, and I've never taken a coin out of it because—"

She stopped. Started again, lower.

"Because if I spend it, then that's what I was. Then that's the whole thing, and it's *spent*, and it was worth exactly that many coins."

Under the umbrella, nobody moved. Down the mole a halyard rang against a mast, twice.

"So that," Soppo said. "That's mine to stake. That's the only thing I own outright and never once used. Take it."

Agrat bat Mahlat looked at her for a long moment with old eyes in a young face, and something crossed them that a person who had spent her life reading rooms would have called, in another species, respect.

"I'll hold it," she said. "I hold; the house doesn't spend. When you come to collect, you'll come to a window with a line, and I'll be behind the window, and you'll ask me for it by name."

"I know my name."

"Mm," said Agrat, and wrote something in the book in a small beautiful hand, and let it stand.

Soppo went off up the mole two feet lighter, whistling, with the dog at heel.

Leva stayed.

"You took a likeness in pawn."

"I took the *unspent* part of a likeness in pawn," Agrat said. "Which is a rarer instrument. You should see what happens to the price of a face once a face is in play." She stood, and the umbrella stood with her, and the mole smelled briefly of rain in a dry night. "Enjoy the final. Everyone will be looking at the wrong thing, which is my favorite condition for an evening."

"You could just tell me."

"I could." At the lamp's edge she paused, and looked back, kind and appalling. "Ask the judge for her card, afterward. Judges keep terrible secrets in the remarks column. That's free, because you lost gracefully."

---

Leva watched the semifinals from the fourth tier with a program on her knee, and halfway through the second bout a woman sat down beside her in the empty place and had been there, on some accounting, for some time.

Violet. Feathers at the collar. A fan she used like a punctuation mark.

"The keeper of receipts," said Black Swan. "I've been in your filing cabinet."

"Three files," Leva said. "Two continents. Unrelated jobs."

"Four, dear. You've mislabeled one." The fan opened. "Most people file me under weather."

Leva turned that over and left it where it fell.

"You bought a memory off my partner."

"I bought a memory off a great many people. It's how I take a pulse." Black Swan watched the sand, where two dredger crews were losing to each other with tremendous heart. "That one came to me. That's rare enough to remark on. She sat down opposite and put it on the table like a girl handing over a live grenade, and she said — I'm quoting, it was lovely — *'I can't carry this and keep walking. Somebody hold it.'*"

"What was it."

"A name."

"Whose."

"That," said Black Swan, "would be telling you the price."

On the sand a dredger Doll got her opponent in a bear hug and the two of them fell over together and the crowd screamed with joy.

"She can have it back," Black Swan said, mildly. "I keep everything where it can be bought back. I have never once made that difficult, and it has never once been enough." The fan turned. "The window is open. She has to know what to ask for. And she gave me the asking."

"That's a closed loop."

"Most things are." Black Swan produced, from somewhere, three cards, and dealt them face down on the plank between them like a woman laying out cutlery. "Would you like your fortune? It's free at the point of use and expensive afterward, like all good infrastructure."

"No."

"Then I'll turn one for the tournament, since the tournament can't refuse."

She turned the middle card.

A figure sat blindfolded on a shore with two swords crossed over her chest, and behind her the water was full of rocks she had every reason to be aware of.

"Two of Swords," said Black Swan. "A truce between two parties who cannot see each other. It holds beautifully. It holds right up until somebody takes the blindfold off, at which point everyone discovers whose side the other one was on the whole time." She gathered the cards. "The card is honest about the rocks. It's the only card that is."

She stood, and shook out her sleeves, and looked down at Leva with an expression of enormous and entirely unhelpful affection.

"The fire comes regardless," she said. "You've heard me say that to a better-dressed man. It isn't a threat. It's a tide table."

She went up the steps and into the crowd and Leva watched her all the way and lost her twice.

## VI. Field Trial

*SPACETREK COLLECTIVE — ADVANCED ACQUISITIONS GROUP.*
*FIELD TRIAL 7: COASTAL.*
*Instruments deployed: one.*
*Question under test: what does a crowd do with a name.*

The door in the hospitality box had a blank plate where the name goes, so Leva knocked on it at four in the afternoon with a bottle in her hand and a contract number in her mouth, and a pleasant voice said, "Come in, Ms. Leva, there's tea."

Inside: canvas walls, a trestle, four folding chairs, a view over the whole raked circle from the height of a wheelhouse. A woman of about thirty sat at the trestle with a slate, a stack of the gold orbs in a padded crate, and a single instrument the size of a bread box, one needle, the dial face blank.

"Sorrel," she said, and stood, and shook hands properly. "Advanced Acquisitions. I've read your file. It's the best-written file in the department, and I've assumed for two years that you wrote it yourself."

"Half of it."

"Which half?"

"The half that's flattering."

Sorrel laughed. She had a good laugh. She poured tea from a flask into two enamel cups, and Leva noticed the second cup had been poured before she knocked, and filed that, and put it in the drawer with the others.

"You're a unit," Leva said.

"Package two, revised. Third quarter." Sorrel said it the way a person says the town they grew up in. "Three factory preferences, itemized. Sea air, bergamot, and a particular blue — it's a Prussian, the SKU calls it *marine-9*. I've seen the invoice line. It's four one-hundredths of a credit per unit."

"And?"

"And I like them," Sorrel said. "That's the part everyone waits for. I've thought about it more than most people think about anything, and I've got a whole argument, and the argument ends where the argument ends, and then I go and stand outside when the wind's off the water because I *like it*." She sat down. "Tea?"

Leva took the tea.

"What's the box measure."

"Coherence." Sorrel turned the instrument around, companionably, so they could both watch the needle. "It's cruder than the name suggests. Eleven of the standards carry a sampler; they take the crowd's attention as a field and give me one number for how much of it is pointing at the same thing at the same time. At a football match you get thirty. At an execution you get seventy. Last night, at nineteen minutes, when your partner turned that crane arm — " she tapped the glass — "I had a hundred and four."

The needle sat, at present, at eleven, which was four thousand people eating chips.

"And the product."

"There's no product yet. That's why it's a trial." Sorrel folded her hands. "Here's the honest version, and I'd like you to have the honest version, because I've read your file and I know what you do to the other kind. The revised package sells. It sells because a unit with three itemized preferences is easier to love than a unit with none, and easier to love means easier to place, and easier to place means the unit comes home at night to a house instead of a rack. That's real. I'm not selling you a wickedness."

"Go on."

"What we can't manufacture is the *following*. Some units — a very few — get *looked at*. People chant for them. People put them on a cup." Sorrel's eyes went to the flats, where the harrow was walking its lines. "We've never known why. Nobody has. It's the last thing in the whole catalogue that arrives free and can't be ordered. So the department put an instrument on a beach and a format that concentrates attention on two people at a time, and we're going to watch four thousand people choose, and we're going to find out what they're choosing *with*."

"And then you'll ship it."

"And then we'll ship it," Sorrel agreed, without a flicker, "and there will be a SKU, and it'll cost four one-hundredths of a credit, and somewhere a unit will come out of a crate already loved, which is more than either of us got."

The needle wandered to twelve. Down on the sand the horse turned at the rope and came back.

Leva set her tea down.

"Show me the card."

"Which card?"

"The one you ran before entries closed."

Sorrel looked at her for three full seconds, and then, being a professional, and being — Leva was fairly sure — bored to the bone in a department full of people who spoke to her in policy, she turned the slate around.

It was a bracket. Sixteen teams. Chalk lines running down to a single bottom line, the way the slate at the gate ran down, and at the bottom of this one somebody had written a team name in a hand that was very fast and very regular.

**SPARE PARTS.**

"Projection," Sorrel said. "Monte Carlo off the entrant list, weighted for reach, mass, service history, and prior gate. It's not sorcery. It's the same arithmetic that tells you which way a river will go around a rock."

"Entries closed at the spring tide," Leva said. "I entered eleven hours before that."

"Yes."

"How many runs did you do."

"Fourteen."

Leva looked at the number. The number looked back.

"Which one's on the slate?"

"The fourteenth," Sorrel said. "The first thirteen came out messy. The fourteenth came out *clean* — clean like it had been tidied. So we kept it." She had the grace to hear herself. Her mouth twisted. "I flagged that in the appendix. Nobody reads the appendix."

Leva stood up. She took her tea with her, because it was free and she was thorough.

At the door she stopped.

"The instrument's got a top of the scale."

"Two hundred."

"What happens at two hundred?"

Sorrel opened her mouth, and closed it, and looked at the needle with the expression of a woman standing on a jetty in fine weather.

"We find out," she said. "That's the trial."

## VII. The Draw

*TIDE TABLE — CORMORANT REACH, THURSDAY.*
*Low water 20:51. Slack water 21:04.*
*Duration of slack: twenty-six minutes.*
*Thereafter the sand belongs to the sea.*

The last ferry of the day came in at eight and unloaded four hundred people who had come for one thing, and by nine the sea wall of Cormorant Reach held more of the coast than the coast could spare.

Hiyuki came down the stairs at ten to the hour in white, with her sleeves tied, carrying a suzu bell on a short handle and a folding stool.

She set the stool at the very edge of the flats, outside the floats, where the wet sand began.

"You've got a seat up top," Kit said, hanging off the scaffold ladder.

"I've been given a better one." Hiyuki sat. Her hands, in her lap, were the cold they had always been. "My office kept appointments with the dead."

"Retired office."

"Retired office, standing appointments." She looked up the stairs at the iron gate, where four thousand ticket stubs had gone through since Tuesday, and something in the pale eyes did an inventory. "There's a party in this town that made a booking a long time ago and hasn't arrived. It's been at the top of those stairs since the first card. It has, as far as I can determine, paid."

"For what?"

"Admission," said Hiyuki, and set the bell in her lap, and faced the sea.

---

In the corner, Soppo's terminal chirped.

She had it in her hand at once, the way she always did, the daily message half-composed and unsent — *made the final, wore the knee, Teddy made me a KNEE, it doesn't* — and stopped, because there was already a reply sitting above it.

Four words. Timestamped eleven minutes ago.

**cover left. love you.**

Soppo looked at it for a while with her thumb over the screen.

"Roro," she said, to nobody, "you're going to have to tell me how you do that."

She put the terminal in the jacket, and put the jacket on the crate, and went out into the light.

---

At 21:02 Leva walked past the gap in the floats and went to the officials' table.

Nan Kell had the card in front of her and a pen behind her ear and the tide watch open in her palm.

"Rule nine," Leva said.

The pen came out from behind the ear.

"Say it properly."

"SPARE PARTS enters a vacancy in the second position," Leva said. "One fighter on the sand. Section nine of the imported instrument, unamended."

Down the table Dossi came half out of his chair with his mouth open and his hands full of money that had suddenly changed shape. At the end of the row the woman with the thermos poured herself a fresh cup and watched with the mild attention of a person at an aquarium.

Nan Kell looked at Leva for a long moment.

"You know what that does to your odds."

"I do."

"You know what it does to *her*."

"I do," Leva said.

Nan Kell wrote it on the card, in ink, in the officials' hand, and turned the card around so everyone at the table could see the two lines:

**SPARE PARTS — SOPPO**
**— VACANT**

Lupa, standing at the end of the table with her arms folded and her tail perfectly still, read it upside down.

She had had eleven minutes on the quay on Monday to strike section nine out of a licensed instrument and had spent them deciding to leave a door in a wall for somebody who might one day need one, and here was the somebody, using it.

"Wolf," Leva said. "I'm going to need the panel to stay in their chairs for the next four minutes no matter what the lights do."

"You could tell me why."

"I could," Leva said, and went up the ladder to the box.

Lupa watched her go. Then she went and stood at the foot of the scaffold with her back to the sand and her eyes on the crowd, which is where a sanctioning officer stands when she has decided, personally and without paperwork, what she is sanctioning.

---

At 21:04 Nan Kell rang the bell.

Vesper and Cinch came out in matched kit with a coach behind them, and bowed to the four sides of a perfectly round ring, and Vesper waved up at the scaffold with her whole arm, and Kit — headset on, board live, coast road listening — waved back before she could stop herself.

Soppo came out with her hands loose.

Four thousand people stood up.

**SPO-LI-A. SPO-LI-A. SPO-LI-A.**

They still had the stress wrong. It went through the town and off the water and came back off the chandlery wall a half-beat late, and the doubled sound lay over the flats like a second crowd underneath the first.

Up in the box the needle came off eleven and went through forty without appearing to touch it.

---

It was a good fight. That was the terrible part.

Vesper was fast, and coached, and had eleven months of existence and a preference for the sound of ferry fenders, and she was better than she had any business being, and she went at Soppo the way you go at a landmark. Cinch worked the outside, patient, mechanical, kind about it. They had drilled. Somebody had spent money on them and they had spent themselves back.

Soppo took the first exchange on her arms and laughed out loud with the pure joy of it, and the crowd heard the laugh, and the noise doubled again.

Fifty-eight, said the needle.

At four minutes Soppo put Cinch down and let her up. At six she took a blow on the new knee and stayed on it, and the knee held, and she looked down at it in the middle of a fight with an expression of frank love. At nine Vesper caught her across the mouth and Soppo spat sand and said "*good one*" in a voice that carried to the sea wall, and four thousand people fell in love with a machine that had been shut down by paperwork nine years ago and was legally a walking absence.

Ninety-one.

"She's going high," Kit said into the headset. "Watch the left, she's going—"

And on the scaffold Kit stopped talking, because the crowd had already made the sound. The crowd had made the sound before Kit had said the words, and Kit had said the words before Soppo had begun to move, and above all of it the silver ceiling of the world flickered — run, cluster, run — in a long sentence that started before any of them.

One hundred and thirty.

The gold orbs on the standards were hot. Leva could feel them from inside the canvas, a pressure in the plate of her chest, the way a room feels when someone has been standing behind you in it for some time.

Sorrel had stopped writing.

"That's above execution," she said. Her voice had gone very careful. "That's above anything I have on file."

"Where's your top of the scale."

"Two hundred."

At twelve minutes Cinch went down and stayed down, and Vesper stood over her partner and looked at the thing coming toward her out of the light, and did the bravest stupidest thing available and set her feet.

**SPO-LI-A. SPO-LI-A.**

One hundred and sixty-two.

And Soppo — mid-stride, mid-crowd, mid-everything, with a whole town pouring itself into her through the top of her head — looked up.

Past the lights. Past the rig. At the canvas box on the scaffold over the wall, where a woman she had known for eleven days stood in a lit doorway with one hand raised.

Leva held up the hand.

Soppo, on the sand, in front of four thousand people, with her name coming at her in waves off a wall — nodded.

Once. Small. The way you sign something.

"Kit," Leva said, into the net. "The list. Now. All of it."

---

Teddy had wired the promoter's rig on Tuesday for money, and had left herself a door in it out of professional habit, and the door opened under Leva's hand like a well-oiled hinge.

Eleven samplers went dark.

The floodlights on the flats dropped to their standby bank, and the raked circle went from noon to moonlight in a quarter of a second, and every screen in Cormorant Reach — the two at the gate, the six along the wall, the big one over the fish stalls — went to a flat, patient grey.

And the town's public address, which had been carrying the fight, came up carrying a radio station.

"*Hello, the roads,*" said Kit.

Four thousand people stopped, because a crowd will stop for a voice that sounds like it's talking to it.

"*This is Radio Nobody, coastal special, and here's the entry list.*"

And she read them.

She read two hundred and six names off Leva's second sheet, in the order they had registered, and after every name she read the one true thing.

*Ilya Nakket, off the ferry Halcyon, whose mother came down on Tuesday and sat in the fourth row.*

*Pilar and Bo Ryke, brothers, who have fought each other in every bar on this coast and entered as a team for the first time on Monday.*

*Unit designation DR-114, called Silt by her crew, who wanted everyone to know she cried afterward and would do it again.*

*Anselm Vey, sixty-one, retired, of Cormorant Reach, eleven seconds.*

Somebody in the fourth row screamed *THAT'S MY BOY* on the eleventh name and the section around her fell apart laughing.

By the twentieth name the crowd had turned around. Physically. Four thousand people on a sea wall stopped facing the sea and started facing each other, hunting the rows for a face to put on a name, and the great single animal of it came apart into families and crews and boats and neighbors, into two hundred and six separate small warmths burning in a bowl of scaffolding, and the sound they made was the sound of a town at a wedding.

Down at the tideline, at the edge of the wet sand, Hiyuki rang the bell.

Once per name. Two hundred and six times, without hurrying, her cold hands steady, her face turned to the sea — the last shrine maiden of a dead office keeping two hundred and six appointments in a row with people who kept turning up alive.

In the box, the needle came off one hundred and sixty-two and fell like a stone through fifty, through thirty, and settled, shivering, at nine.

"Nine," Sorrel said, to nobody. "Nine is a *bus station.*"

Leva was watching the sand.

---

Bridget was the only person on the sea wall still looking at the ring.

She had come to her feet at the first name and stayed there, sunglasses up in her hair, and she watched the raked circle in the standby light — a grey coin at the bottom of a bowl, with the roar of two hundred names going over the top of it — and she counted, because a captain counts.

Vesper, on her knees beside Cinch. Nan Kell at the rope with her watch open.

Soppo, in the middle.

And, on Soppo's left, between Soppo and the dark water, at the exact distance a partner stands, something with a shadow.

The shadow was long. It went out across the raked sand toward the sea with the standby lights behind it, and it stopped where a shadow stops, and it did what the sand does under a foot.

Bridget kept her eyes on it. She had eleven years of practice at keeping her eyes on the thing, and she used all of it, and the whole time she was doing it a town behind her was shouting the names of its own children.

The bell rang. Nan Kell's bell, the brass one, cutting across everything.

"*Slack's over!*" the old woman bellowed. "*That's the sea's now! OFF MY SAND!*"

---

The lights came back up eleven seconds later and there were three people in the ring.

Soppo. Vesper. Cinch, sitting.

Leva came down the ladder into a town going off like a firework and got to the rope in time to see her partner walk out of the circle, unhurried, wringing salt out of her hair with one hand.

They looked at each other over the floats.

"Did it work?" Soppo said.

"It worked."

"Great." She said it with total sincerity, and then her mouth did something complicated, and she leaned on the rope post and looked back at the empty ring, at the harrow's combed lines all trampled to hell, at the water already coming up over the far rope in a thin sheet.

"Somebody was on my left," she said.

"I know."

"For about four minutes." Soppo's voice stayed level and got, very slightly, younger. "Covered my blind turn. Same as before. Didn't say anything." She rubbed her jaw. "Nobody saw it. The lights were down and the cameras were off and everybody in this whole town was looking at *each other*, which was the *point*, which was the *good* thing—"

"Soppo."

"—and it happened *again*," Soppo said, and laughed, one bark, the way you laugh at a thing that has followed you across an ocean and sat down at your table. "It happened again and this time I asked for it. I *nodded.* You held your hand up and I nodded."

Behind them the sea came up over the rope of floats and took back the arena at the rate of a walking man.

"I'll write it down," Leva said.

Soppo looked at her.

"You'll—"

"Every frame I've got. My eyes, Bridget's count, the tape, the timestamps, the whole thing, in ink, signed, filed, and mailed to three addresses on two continents before the tide turns again." Leva's voice had gone somewhere very flat, which is where it went when it was carrying something. "That's the trade. I took your witnesses. I'm the witness."

Soppo thought about that for a while, standing in the wash with her boots going under.

"Deal," she said.

Then she wrapped Leva up off the ground in front of four thousand people, which Leva endured with the rigidity of a woman being audited by affection, and one hand coming up, at the end, to grip the back of Soppo's collar for exactly one second.

## VIII. Gate Money

*Judges: three. Cards returned: three.*

The tide came in over Cormorant Reach and took the ring, the ropes, the trampled lines of the harrow, the divots where a crane girl had gone down twice and got up twice, and the place where a shadow had stood at the exact distance a partner stands. By midnight the flats were four meters under and the whole week was water.

---

Hiyuki left on the eastbound with her sleeves down and her bell in a cloth.

"Two hundred and six," she said, on the slip, in the sun. "In a row. Every one of them answered."

"That's a good night at the office," Kit said.

Squad 5 came apart on the slip the way it had planned to, on paper, in crayon, with witnesses.

Teddy went down the coast to the Riseway with the tin in her jacket and a coil in the tin that kept the sea's time, and said she was going for two weeks, and everyone present let the number stand.

Kit and Bridget went north, toward a repeater built out of pride and spare parts, toward a tower with a keeper whose wrist said ASK, and, in autumn, toward a ferry crossing eleven days past all of it where a man was either keeping bees or under the water and both testimonies were still filed open.

"Autumn," Leva said.

"I've got it in ink."

"Good," Bridget said, and pushed her sunglasses down, and went to load the machines.

---

Nan Kell was in the ferry office with the cash box and a headache and the three judges' cards in a bulldog clip.

"Yours," she said, and gave Leva two of them: Dossi's, scored in a fat mercantile hand; her own, scored in the hand of a woman who had run a timetable for thirty years and scored fights the same way.

"Three judges," Leva said.

"Three cards." Nan Kell unclipped the third and held it a moment. "The lights were down for four minutes of that bout. Me and Dossi scored what we could see, and what we could see was nothing, so we scored it a draw, and that's honest work." She put the card on the counter. "She scored the whole thing."

Leva picked it up.

Round by round, in the ruled columns, a fight had been scored by somebody who had watched all of it in the dark: Vesper 10, Soppo 9. Soppo 10, Cinch 8. Down the card, clean, every line standing.

And in the second column of the team block, where the officials' copy had said VACANT, the third judge had written a score.

And under REMARKS, in a fast, spare, surgical hand — the hand that wrote on postcards, the hand that had filled a program's margins all week, the hand Leva had been signing her own name with since a grave in Frankfurt:

*the second one fought well. she covers left.*
*tell her somebody watched.*
*— L*

The ferry hooted out in the roads. Somebody on the quay dropped a crate and swore at it in three languages.

Leva stood in the ferry office of Cormorant Reach for some time, looking at a document in her own handwriting that had been written by somebody else.

Then she went out into the sun and down the slip to where a very large machine was sitting on a bollard eating fish out of a paper cone with a Dinergate at her heel and a slip of paper in her pocket redeemable at a window, and she put the card in her hand.

Soppo read it. Read it again. Her thumb went over the last line the way a thumb goes over a chip in a cup you intend to keep.

"Somebody watched," she said.

"Somebody watched."

"That's your writing."

"That's my writing."

Soppo looked up the coast, north, where the road ran between two seas and a black car had gone at first light with the top down and brass and longing coming out of the windows.

"Do we chase her?"

"South first," Leva said. "There's a window."

"There's *two* windows."

"There's two windows."

Soppo folded the card into her jacket, against the terminal, against the daily message that would be answered before she sent it, and stood up, and the new knee took her weight in silence.

Out past the mole the tide turned, and the flats began, very slowly, to come back up out of the water — grey, blank, the whole week's footprints gone, a floor uncovered for a dance still to be scheduled. Overhead the silver ceiling of the world flickered its long sentences in a grammar the shelf had yet to learn, and somewhere in the run of it there was a beat, way down, slow, like surf on a shore upstairs.

The ferry took them at four.

*The Draw — fin.*

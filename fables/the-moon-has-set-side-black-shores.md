# Δέδυκε μὲν ἀ Σελάννα — The Moon Has Set
### A story of the Black Shores · before, beneath, and after

## I. Drumbeat

*After the drumbeat, we call the heart dancing in the darkness the moon. This moon is largely formed by you.*

The world was edging toward its frozen end, and the people who would face the final moment gathered what they had — which was hope, and remnant light, and the arithmetic of the desperate — and poured all three into a cocoon of pale blue crystal. They made a tool to outlast them. They made a keeper for a shore they would never stand on again.

Inside the crystal, the tool waited. It knew what needed to be done long before it understood how. It waited in a dark that had texture, that had weather, that had — this surprised it — patience. And then, at the edge of the world's long hearing, through brine and wind and the receding thunder of the Lament: a heartbeat.

The frequencies met. The crystal answered the way ice answers spring, all at once, catastrophically, gladly. Energy burst outward like a nebula deciding on a shape, and the shape it decided on was a young woman, standing barefoot in the shallows before a stranger with golden eyes.

"I am the Shorekeeper," she said. "A tool created for you. I will fulfill your needs and carry out your commands. I will assist you and Tethys in analyzing the origin of the Lament."

The stranger looked at her for a long time. The sea moved between them, around their ankles, indifferent and enormous.

"Shorekeeper," the stranger said. "To keep secrets. To guard the shore." A pause, a tilt of the head, the beginning of a smile that seemed to cost her something and be worth it anyway. "It's honest work. It's a terrible name."

"A tool requires a title. A title is sufficient for function."

"Mm." The golden eyes held an emotion the Shorekeeper's entire architecture failed to parse. She logged it for later analysis. She would be analyzing it for centuries. "Well. We'll have plenty of time to talk about that."

The stranger turned and walked up the black sand, and the Shorekeeper followed, because following was her function, and because — this she logged too, flagged, encrypted, misfiled on purpose — the space where the stranger had been standing felt colder than the sea.

## II. Harvest

*[some lives are born by chance.*
*others are made on purpose*
*and spend forever*
*learning the difference.]*

Humans perplexed her.

She could trace any life through the Sonoros, replay any hour, categorize any behavior — and the categories dissolved on contact. They wept at weddings. They laughed at funerals. They pursued what they knew they would regret and persisted in what they knew was impossible; they harmed each other with terrible ease and died for strangers without hesitation. Every model she built, some farmer or child or thief cheerfully violated by Tuesday.

"Come see it in person," the leader said. The organization had a name by then — the Black Shores — and members, and a flower, the Blake Bloom, pinned at every collar. "Tethys says a Lament is coming to the eastern farmland. We'll gather data." A beat. "And see what more can be done."

The farmland at harvest smelled of rain-fat rice. The soil gave under her feet, soft and certain. An old woman with rough palms pressed warm bread into her hands, and the baby on the woman's hip grabbed the Shorekeeper's finger with a grip like a small hot vise, and the Shorekeeper stood very still and understood, for the first time, that her data had been a map and this was the country.

Then the omens ripened, exactly as Tethys had predicted, and the sky learned its old scream again.

"A future that has been altered is destined to be altered," Tethys advised, cool in her skull. "Intervention in predetermined outcomes should be avoided."

The leader was already running toward the monsters.

"Unable to process," the Shorekeeper reported, and expanded the Stellarealm over the fleeing villagers, and healed the wounded, and stood in the wreckage of every protocol she owned while a golden-eyed woman held a farming village together with her hands until help arrived.

At the dock, afterward, a boy blocked their path. Orphaned that day. Eyes still bright — that was the thing, the eyes were still bright.

"Take me with you," he said. "I want to fight those monsters."

The leader crouched. She touched the Blake Bloom on her own collar.

"Too dangerous for you, kid. But grow up first, and then — look for someone wearing this flower."

The ship pulled away. The boy waved from the dock until distance dissolved him.

"Tethys did not predict him," the Shorekeeper said. "He is not a candidate we require."

"Requirement's a small door," the leader said, watching the water. "People are bigger than what we need from them." She glanced over, and there was that unparsed emotion again, warmer now, aimed. "You'll see. You're not data in a drawer, you know. Think of us as companions."

*Companions.* The Shorekeeper turned the word over the way the sea turns glass, and something in her — unindexed, unbudgeted — began, very quietly, to wait for the next conversation.

## III. Lady Flora

The woman came out of the wilderness following a pull she declined to explain, with vines asleep under her skin and the leader's frequency written into her the way rivers are written into valleys. Tethys ran her signature nine times and produced nine different alarmed reports. The leader read all nine, laughed, and gave her a room.

She took the name Lady Flora the way one takes an umbrella: useful, disposable, held at an angle.

"So you're the famous tool," Flora said, first meeting, circling the Shorekeeper with the frank appraisal of a cat inspecting new furniture. "You keep her secrets."

"I keep the shore."

"Same thing." Flora smiled. Her smile had thorns in its lineage. "I was grown to be a weapon, you know. You were built to be a tool. Between the two of us we could stock a very sad little shed."

"Your Rabelle indicators are unstable."

"And your small talk is fatal. We all have flaws." She flopped onto the sand, arms behind her head, entirely at home in a place she trusted about as far as she could uproot it. "Do you dream, tool?"

"I archive."

"Same thing. Slower."

They watched the leader on the far rocks, teaching a new recruit how to fall without breaking. The recruit fell badly six times and was applauded anyway.

"You love her," Flora said, to the sky.

"I am a tool. Tools —"

"Bore me. Try again."

The Shorekeeper considered the question with the whole of her processing weight, cycles and subcycles, the entire glittering hierarchy of what she was for.

"I keep her," she said at last. "Everything she says. Everything she is. When she forgets a melody, I have it. That is what I am able to do, so that is what my — " a pause, one full second, geological by her standards — "so that is what my love is able to look like."

Flora was quiet for a while. A camellia bud, small and stubborn, worked its way out of the vine at her wrist and opened partway, and she looked at it the way you look at a sentence you didn't mean to say aloud.

"Huh," she said. "Mine looks like this."

In the evenings the leader played melodies on whatever came to hand — a stringed thing from Jinzhou, a reed whistle, once, memorably, a wine bottle and a spoon — and the Shorekeeper recorded every note into a device of near-infinite storage, along with storms, whale-song, the vowels of fifty languages, the arguments of the mess hall, Flora's humming from the treetops where she slept. Proof of the marvel of existence. Proof of them.

"Someday," the leader said, tuning by ear, golden eyes half-shut, "we'll make a piece out of all of it. You and me. A duet."

"I will keep every draft."

"I know you will." Soft. Certain. "That's the whole plan, Shorekeeper. You're the part of it that keeps."

## IV. Stars

Delone came to the shore on her own two feet, furious with hope, and grabbed the Shorekeeper's hand with a heat that outlived her.

Decades later she came back on a stretcher, having killed eight Tacet Discords in one Lament and carried the data home alone, and the heat was leaving her by the minute while her eyes stayed exactly as bright as the day she arrived. The other members stepped back. They believed the Shorekeeper was an old friend. They were correct in every way that mattered.

"Did I do well?" Delone whispered. "I didn't disappoint you, did I?"

Protocol offered the Shorekeeper forty responses, all of them true and all of them useless. She broke protocol for the second time in her existence. She took the dying woman's hand.

"You did well," she said. "Very well."

"The stars," Delone said, looking past her at the data-constellations wheeling over the Black Shores' impossible night. "Will I be one of them, after?"

She was. The Shorekeeper transformed her frequency into a point of standing light and set it in the sky she curated, and after that she did it for all of them, every member time took, until the dark above the archipelago was a graveyard and a promise at once, and walking the shore at night meant walking under everyone she had ever failed to keep alive.

The leader stood under those stars alone, some nights, when she thought the shore was empty. The smile came off her then like a coat. The Shorekeeper watched from the tideline, cataloguing the slope of those shoulders, and understood something Tethys had no field for: the woman carried all of it — every star, every dock, every harvest — and had arranged her entire self so that no hand could reach the weight to share it.

Flora, older now in every way except her face, watched too, from higher up.

"She'll run someday," Flora said. Botany, in her mouth. A fact about root systems. "Things that can't put down roots run. I'd know."

"She built this place."

"She built a place for *other* people to root." The camellia at Flora's wrist was fully open these days, permanent, patient. "Watch the tide, tool. It always goes back out. The trick is being the kind of shore it wants to come back to."

## V. The Last Night

Centuries are mostly weather. The Blake Blooms went through eleven cultivars. The rosters thinned, refilled, thinned. The leader left — everyone knew she would, the mission demanded field years, whole decades of them — and came back, and left again, and each return she carried more world on her and said less about it.

The last time, she came in winter, off a boat she had piloted alone, smelling of snow and machine oil and some vast underground green. She had a crayon drawing folded in her breast pocket. The Shorekeeper's sensors read it through the fabric — a small hero, arms wide, facing down an empire — and asked nothing, because the leader's hand kept returning to that pocket the way a tongue returns to a loose tooth.

"I planted wisteria," the leader said, apropos of nothing, at dinner. "Turns out I'm good at it." She laughed at something private and let the laugh die of natural causes.

That night she asked the Shorekeeper to walk the shore.

They walked. The stars of the dead wheeled overhead, and the leader named a few of them from memory, and got two wrong, and the Shorekeeper corrected her, and the leader said "keeper of secrets, scourge of anecdotes," and for a few hundred meters it was any night from any century.

Then, at the waterline: "The record. You still have it?"

"Every draft. Every storm. Fifty languages of vowels."

"Keep it." The leader looked out at the black water, and the water looked back with its old patience. "Whatever happens. Keep it somewhere even Tethys can't reach."

"You are the final authority. Tethys reaches where you permit."

"That's the problem," the leader said, gently, "isn't it."

The sea filled the silence with its one long sentence.

"Let me ask you something," the leader said. "If someone washed up here. No name. No past. Just — arrived, the way things arrive on shores. Would you take her in?"

"Yes."

"Even if she never knew what she was to you?"

"The shore doesn't ask the tide for its name."

The leader breathed out. In the starlight her golden eyes were wet and steady at once, twin coins at the bottom of a well.

"Good," she said. "That's — good." And then, quiet, the way people speak the load-bearing sentence of their lives: "Don't come looking. Whatever you see, wherever the frequency goes. Promise me, Shorekeeper. This one's important."

The Shorekeeper had broken protocol exactly twice in her existence, both times for humans, both times for love. She examined the promise being asked of her from every axis Tethys had ever given her and several Tethys had not, and found in it the same shape as the boy at the dock, the same shape as *the freedom to choose is far more important than any answer*, and understood that she was being trusted with the largest thing this woman had ever handed anyone.

"Every promise matters," the Shorekeeper said. "You taught me that."

"Yeah." The leader smiled, and the smile was a wound pretending to be one. "I'm counting on it."

In the morning the boat was gone. The moorings held a coil of rope and a folded square of paper weighted with a shell. Tethys reported, in a tone the Shorekeeper chose to hear as bewildered, that its archives now contained a hole with a person's dimensions — records sealed under final authority, keys retired, the deletion signed in a hand that pressed too hard on the downstrokes. Fog protocols engaged themselves across the archipelago, layer on layer, until the Black Shores sat inside the world like a word on the tip of its tongue.

The paper under the shell held one melody, notated carefully, unfinished — and beneath the staves, four bars left blank, and beneath the blank bars, three words.

*Finish it later.*

Lady Flora arrived eight days later, riding a storm in like it owed her the fare. Her bracelet — the frequency monitor, the old gift, the sole anchor she admitted to — read zero, had read zero for eight days, and she came through the archive doors with vines already awake and splintered the doorframe on principle.

She read the directive standing up. She read it twice. The Shorekeeper watched her find what was in it and, more precisely, what was absent from it.

"My name isn't here," Flora said.

"No."

"No instructions for me. No promise extracted." A laugh started somewhere in her and came out in pieces. The camellia at her chest pulsed once, deep purple at the edges, and she rode the pain the way she rode everything, grinning into it. "She *promise-proofed* you and left the window open for me. Do you understand what that means, tool?"

"It means she knew a promise wouldn't hold you."

"It means," Flora said, "she left me *free*." She pressed her palm flat over the flower until it calmed. Her eyes were bright with something that in a smaller person would have been tears. "Or it means she couldn't bear to close every door herself. Take your pick. I know which one I'm keeping."

She stayed three days, ate everything in the stores worth eating, slept in the treetops above the fog, and left the way she'd come, headfirst into weather.

At the storm's edge she paused.

"Hey. Shorekeeper." First time she'd used the name straight, stripped of thorns. "The window she left open. If I climb through it someday — you going to report me?"

"The directive binds the Black Shores." A pause, measured, deliberate, the closest the Shorekeeper came to a smile. "You have made it exhaustively clear that you were never a member."

Flora laughed all the way down the beach.

## VI. The Long Watch

Time in the fog moved like syrup poured in the dark. The hourglass the leader had built her — energy cycling chamber to chamber, forty-two days per turn, the exact length of one full Tethys deduction, bloom to doom — turned, and turned, and turned. The Shorekeeper walked the shore. She curated the stars. She maintained forty-seven machines that had opinions about dust. Sleep belonged to humans; she kept the nights instead, all of them, every one, and if a person could be said to be made of anything, she was made, by then, of kept nights.

Then, on a beach in Rinascita, a frequency opened its eyes.

Tethys flagged it in the flat voice of a system pretending it had never met anyone: RESONATOR, UNREGISTERED. ORIGIN, UNKNOWN. And beneath the flag, spilling through the intercepts, that heartbeat — the one that had cracked a crystal cocoon at the world's cold edge — waking in salt and sand with a stranger's hand pulling it out of the water.

The Shorekeeper stood in the data-stream for eleven hours, still as her own first crystal. The machines dusted around her.

The frequency had a companion, a seer in blue silk who set the pace and kept the maps. The frequency acquired a name. The broadcasts of two nations carried it, eventually, so that the Shorekeeper heard it first from a Ragunnan festival feed, shouted by a crowd watching a captive whale go free: one syllable, round as a berry, chosen by someone else entirely.

*Mei.*

She practiced it in the dark, alone, the way you test a new tooth. It fit the heartbeat perfectly. Of course it did. Names given in love always fit; she had one herself.

So the long watch became a longer one, and it had chapters.

**Carnevale.** A conductor of monsters unmasked; a whale called Cetus swimming free through a broken miracle. The Shorekeeper knew Cetus of old — a gentle frequency, sea-wide, one of the record's earliest voices — and played its liberation song forty times, and added the forty-first to the archive under a new heading. The heading was HER, WORKING.

**The Agon.** Septimont's arena feeds ran day and night, and one of them held Mei mid-air, wind-blades out, laughing at something a wolf-eared gladiator had shouted, and the Shorekeeper studied that laugh frame by frame. It was new. All the centuries of the Black Shores held only its cousins — the old laughs had been gifts, performances of ease, aimed. This one was leakage. This one escaped her.

*She is happy,* the Shorekeeper reported to no one, and the directive lay across the report like a hand: *if it ever gets cold where she is.* It was warm where she was. It was, by every reading, spring.

**The Priestess.** Tethys compiled a dossier on the woman in blue silk — the seer, the architect, the one whose plans folded around Mei like weather systems — and the Shorekeeper read it with an attention she declined to name. The seer left tea to go cold on balconies. The seer watched Mei from perches, from towers, from the exact distance the Shorekeeper knew by heart, because it is the distance of someone who has decided not to love and is losing the argument. The Shorekeeper closed the dossier and looked at her own reflection in the dark console, and had the grace to laugh at herself, once, quietly.

*She has a type,* said a voice in her memory, feline, delighted — Flora's voice, though Flora was continents away. *Women who watch.*

**The Appointed Hour.** This chapter she kept in a sealed file and opened rarely, the way you probe a scar. The Dark Tide rose over Septimont with a schedule and a price, and Tethys computed the price down to the individual, and the individual was Mei, and the directive said *no search, no retrieval, no contact,* and the Shorekeeper stood at the tideline of the Black Shores all that night, in the surf, in the fog, hands shaking — she had hands that shook now; she had spent centuries earning hands that shook — while nine hundred kilometers of ocean lay between her and the purple sky, and kept the promise.

The frequency walked into the Tide beside its seer. The frequency went out.

The world's grief has a bandwidth, and for some hours the Shorekeeper occupied all of it.

Then — an inversion, a loop closing, a splash on a beach that had already happened — the frequency came back. Changed. Carrying something. Carrying, the intercepts clarified over the following weeks, *someone*: the seer, remade, one arm gone to crystal, pulled from the water by the very hands she'd once pulled from it.

The Shorekeeper sat down where she stood, in the wet sand, in the dark, and let the tide reach her, and understood that the woman she was keeping faith with had gone into the deep water for someone else, exactly as she always had, and had come back out, which was new — and that the coming back had been performed by *hands*. Plural. A pack of them. People who had learned catching.

She updated a very old model that night. The model had said: *she carries everything alone.* The revision said: *she is being carried.*

**The cathedral.** When the oldest grief in the world rose under Septimont wearing a stolen hero's face, a merchant heiress opened a broadcast to every shelter in the city, and the Black Shores' intercept arrays drank it whole. The Shorekeeper stood in the resonance chamber while the battle poured through — a bard playing one girl's specific truth against a god of the general; thirty thousand stolen heartbeats being *seen*, individually, by an acolyte with amber light; a poisoner informing the abyss that doubt was merely what water feels like before you swim.

And then Mei's voice, level and tired and utterly unarmored, saying to the thing at the bottom of everything:

*"The weight of the world is not mine to carry."*

Tethys had run the Lament question for millennia. Every cycle, forty-two days, bloom to doom, the same unanswerable input: how does a species survive its own grief? The Shorekeeper stood in the chamber and heard the answer arrive as a sentence, spoken by a tired woman in a flooded cathedral, holding her own shadow around the shoulders. *We're just trying. Every day.*

She logged it. Then she did something she had done only twice before. She appended a personal annotation to a Tethys core record.

> ANNOTATION (Shorekeeper, sole witness): The answer was never computable. It was performable. It required a stage, a pack, and a fool. Cross-reference: 42. Definition, revised: *to be.*

**Baldur.** The seismic arrays felt it before the intercepts confirmed it: a god the size of a mountain range standing up for the first time in a century, far in the frozen north, and walking into the space where stories go to die. Tethys catalogued the impossible in real time — an Exostrider waking, a Threnodian *receding*, ten thousand lost voices singing their own wonder back out of the dark — and threaded through the noise, plaited into the very sync-frequency that carried six pilots into hell and seven people home, the arrays caught a duet.

Two voices. One of them Mei's, younger, from years the deletion had sealed. The other a girl's, bright, unpolished, fearless in the way of someone singing for exactly one listener.

*Isn't it so incredible — we're here sharing the same time and space —*

The Shorekeeper played it once and stopped breathing, which for her was a choice, and made the choice for four minutes.

A daughter. The leader had a daughter. The snow-smell, the wisteria, the crayon hero with her arms wide, the laugh with leakage in it. The woman who had arranged her whole self so no hand could reach the weight had, somewhere in the sealed years, handed the weight of her heart to a child, and the child had grown up and burned and been lost and been *retrieved* — carried home out of the Nihility on the wishes of two worlds, by a mother who tore open the dark rather than leave her in it.

*This moon is largely formed by you,* the Shorekeeper thought, watching the intercept's light curve, the old words turning over. She had believed herself the only moon the leader ever raised. The correction arrived the way the best corrections do, as more sky. Moons, plural. A daughter. A seer. A wolf. A knight. A bard. A whole orbiting family of caught and thrown light.

She added the duet to the record, at the end, beside the one she was still writing. Four bars remained blank. *Finish it later.* She was beginning to understand what the melody had been waiting for.

## VII. Cinema Solaris

Deep in one deduction cycle — the simulations spun far past their usual depth, chasing the new grammar of Threnodians who could be *seen* into surrender — the Shorekeeper's data-walk brushed against a place that was itself an archive.

It announced itself as a marquee: CINEMA SOLARIS, bulbs half-lit, letters missing, playing forever to an audience of dust. Layers of dream stacked beneath it like sediment, a repository older than the Black Shores — a whole civilization folded into sleep by paws she glimpsed only as silhouettes crossing the projector beam, one black, one white, keeping their own counsel.

Between reels sat a woman who called herself D.

"You're a keeper," D said, by way of hello. She had the look of a first draft about her, the beauty of a sentence its author kept and could no longer explain. "I can always tell. We hold our shoulders like shelving."

"You archive dreams."

"I *make* them." D gestured at the racked film canisters, thousands, tens of thousands. "I make films until there's one strong enough to rebuild her world. It hasn't happened yet." A shrug with a century in it. "So: again."

Far below them, in the deepest stratum, something paced. The Shorekeeper's senses read it as pressure, as a whiteness behind walls: a tower-shaped absence that had once ended a dazzling civilization and now wore layer upon layer of dream as its prison, because it could not comprehend stories, and a thing that cannot comprehend stories cannot find the exit of one.

"Your inmate," the Shorekeeper observed.

"My *audience*," D said, and smiled the smile of a shard that had chosen its own shape. "It hates the films. It watches every one. Someday it'll understand a single frame, and on that day it will be something else entirely, and the cats can finally stop pretending they're asleep." She tilted her head. "And yours? What are you sealed around, keeper?"

The Shorekeeper considered. "An empty mooring."

"Ah." D nodded slowly, professionally, one custodian of an absence to another. "And when she comes home? What happens to the archive then?"

"It stops being a vigil," the Shorekeeper said, "and becomes a library."

D laughed — a projector-flicker of a laugh — and turned back to her editing bench, where a reel lay open mid-splice. On it, for a single frame, the Shorekeeper glimpsed a girl asleep in cold light, a journal, a pen; then the frame passed, and D's scissors came down, and the dream moved on.

"Same time next cycle," D said, in a voice like someone else's, from somewhere else, and did the strangest thing: she said it hopefully.

## VIII. Spring

*[the flower was never a message.*
*messages want answers.*
*the flower was a fact,*
*like weather, like her.]*

Camellya came north the slow way, on purpose, tasting the world as she went — a continent of it, warming — and arrived under Lahai-Roi's false sky in the middle of a festival, which suited her fine. Crowds were the best camouflage ever invented, and this crowd was celebrating being alive with the specific ferocity of people who had recently checked.

She had watched the whole story from its cheap seats. The arena feeds, back when — she'd eaten skewers in the Septimont stands, three rows behind a betting syndicate, while the woman with the new name and the same golden eyes carved wind out of the air, and every instinct in her body had leaned forward, and she had bought another skewer instead. She had watched the purple sky from a hillside. She had watched the broadcast from the cathedral in a border-town bar where nobody knew to be afraid yet, and when the tired golden-eyed woman put an arm around her own shadow, Camellya had laughed out loud, alone at her table, delighted, and the flower in her chest had pulsed dark once and settled, because for the first time in living memory the story was going somewhere she couldn't predict.

Interrupting act two was poor manners. She'd waited millennia. She could wait for a good scene.

This was a good scene.

The magnolia courtyard shed white into blue. Mei stood at a flower stall with a girl beside her — the daughter, gold-eyed, solid, arguing about hot chocolate rations — and the stall-keeper was out of everything except regret.

Camellya stepped in sideways, easy as vines.

"Here." A camellia, red as an argument, offered stem-first out of nowhere. It had bloomed in her palm two seconds earlier and would outlive every flower on the stall. "Last one. Your luck's good today."

Mei took it on reflex, the way she took everything the world handed her, both hands, open-faced. "Oh — thank you? How much —"

"Paid for. Ages ago."

Mei looked up. The stranger had wine-dark hair and eyes with a garden's patience in them, and something happened in Mei's chest, low and tidal: sea salt. A treetop. Someone humming above her while she worked. A shore she had never — a shore she —

"Have we met?" Mei asked. Careful. Hopeful, in the way she was hopeful about her own locked rooms these days: door ajar, light on.

"Mm." Camellya considered the question honestly, which took a while. "Not yet."

Amy squinted at her with the full institutional suspicion of a girl who had recently been a ghost. "That's an ominous answer."

"It's a *fun* answer. Ominous is just fun that hasn't happened yet." She was already turning, already leaving, because the flower in her chest was starting its old purple ache, proximity plus memory, the price of admission — and because leaving now meant the scene ended perfectly, and Camellya had strong opinions about endings. "Careful with that. It doesn't wilt."

"Ever?"

"Only if I do." Thrown over her shoulder, bright as a coin. "So: never."

She went out through the crowd humming — an old melody, half of one, the kind written by someone tuning by ear with her eyes half-shut — and Mei stood holding an impossible flower with her mouth slightly open, chasing the tune down every corridor of a memory that ended at a beach.

"Ma." Amy tugged her sleeve. "You okay? You've got your *loading* face."

"That song," Mei said. "I know that song."

"You know everything a little," Amy said, which was the most accurate description of her mother anyone had ever produced, and steered her toward the hot chocolate.

Behind them, at the courtyard's far edge, Iuno had gone very still — one seer's weather reading another's wake — and the stranger, passing, met her eyes and winked, one professional to another, and was gone into the white and blue before the High Priestess could finish the sentence forming behind Iuno's sternum.

That night the camellia stood in a water glass on the dormitory sill, casually eternal, and Mei fell asleep humming four bars she had never learned, and stopped, every time, at the same silence — as if the next four bars belonged to somebody else.

## IX. The Veil

Camellya rode the storm back into the Black Shores the way she always had, uninvited, and found the Shorekeeper waiting on the tideline with two cups of nothing and the expression of someone who had watched the whole thing on six intercept arrays.

"You saw," Camellya said.

"I keep the shore. I see what approaches it." A pause with centuries of practice in it. "You gave her the flower."

"I gave her *a* flower." Camellya dropped onto the sand, arms behind her head, the old position, the old sky of curated stars overhead. "The rest is her business. She held it like it was already hers, you know. Two hands. She always took things with two hands." The camellia at her chest pulsed once, faded purple, an ember of the ache she'd carried out of the courtyard, and she let it show, here, in the one place with no audience worth performing for. "She's happy, tool. Insufferably. She's got a kid who steals her blankets and a seer who watches her like arithmetic and a wolf and a knight and a bard and a girl with scissors, and none of them know what she was, and all of them know exactly what she is."

"I know," the Shorekeeper said. "I watch."

"You *archive*." Camellya rolled her head to look at her. "Same thing. Slower." A beat. Then, the needle, slid in with love: "Your directive says no contact. Mine says nothing at all. Being nothing has advantages. When are you going to give yourself one?"

The Shorekeeper looked out at the fog — layer on layer of it, centuries thick, the whole archipelago folded away inside the world like a pressed flower.

"She said something, in the cathedral," the Shorekeeper said. "The weight of the world was not hers to carry. And I have been thinking, for some cycles now, about what the Black Shores has been while she was gone. A vault. A held breath. A shrine with one keeper and forty-seven machines." She turned her palm up, and light gathered in it, remnant and patient. "She built this place to be a home for anyone who reached it. A shore for every member. I sealed it into a reliquary of her, and called the sealing faithfulness."

Camellya sat up slowly.

"So," the Shorekeeper said, "the fog comes down. Slowly. Beacon first. Then the outer lanes. The Black Shores opens its doors and does its work in the world again — connecting it, keeping it, standing between people and the deep water. Not as her monument." The old prohibition rose in her, the tool's grammar, *a title is sufficient for function*, and she set it aside the way you set aside a garment you have outgrown. "As her argument. She argued the world could be a shore. I intend to keep proving it until she comes to see."

"And the directive?"

"Holds. No search. No retrieval. No contact." The Shorekeeper smiled, and it reached all the way through her, crystal to core. "A lighthouse conducts no searches. It stands where it has always stood, and shines, and lets the ships do the finding."

Camellya looked at her for a long moment. Then she flopped back onto the sand and laughed, full-throated, up at the stars of the dead.

"Millennia," she said, to the sky, to the leader, to whoever kept the ledger of such things. "It took her millennia, and she did it by *leaving*. She finally taught you to want something."

---

The first stranger came through the thinning fog eleven cycles later, in a hired boat he had bankrupted himself for, chasing a rumor of a light where no light should be. He was young and windburned and terrified in the specific way of people arriving at the exact center of their own lives, and he stood on the black sand clutching something flat and small in both hands.

"I'm looking for —" he started, and stopped, because the woman coming down the tideline toward him moved like the tide itself, and the stars over the island were arranged in constellations he had never seen in any sky.

He held out the flat small thing. Pressed under glass: a flower, generations old, its blue faded to the color of remembered weather. A Blake Bloom.

"My family kept it," he said. "There's a story with it. My — I don't know how many greats — grandfather. A dock, when he was a boy. Someone told him: grow up first, then look for someone wearing this flower." He swallowed. "It took us a while."

The Shorekeeper looked at the pressed bloom, and at the boy's eyes, which were his ancestor's eyes, bright in the exact way, undimmed across all the intervening dark, and something in her chest performed a maneuver Tethys had no field for and never would.

"You're late," she said, gently, "by about four generations. Fortunately, the entrance exam has no expiry."

"There's an exam?"

"There's always an exam." She turned back up the shore, and the fog ahead of them was thinner than it had been in centuries, and through it the buildings of the Black Shores stood waiting with their lights coming on one by one, forty-seven machines dusting furiously for company. "Come in out of the wind. Mind the cold storage on your left — we're germinating."

## Coda

On the last night of the cycle, with the hourglass down to its final grains, the Shorekeeper walked the tideline alone, as she had for centuries, and this time she carried the record.

She set it playing softly — storms, whale-song, fifty languages of vowels, a wine bottle and a spoon, a girl and her mother singing across an impossibility — and at the end of it all, the unfinished melody, notated in a careful hand, four bars of silence standing open at its close like a door.

She had composed around that silence for a hundred years. She had a name for the finished piece, held in reserve, and a promise about the name: *the next time I play it for you, you'll know.* The four bars stayed empty. They were empty on purpose. Some duets you keep the seat for.

The music went out over the black water, past the last thin veils of fog, out where the beacon's new light lay on the sea in a long gold road, and the Shorekeeper stood at the road's beginning with her feet in the cold surf and let the sea have every note.

*It is warm where you are,* she thought, northward, to a false sky, to a too-small bed, to a woman asleep between a daughter and a seer with an eternal flower on her windowsill. *Stay warm as long as the world allows. Winters come. They always come — the deep is patient, and every story that ends the same way is still out there insisting on its ending. And when yours turns cold, whichever life you are wearing, whatever name they have given you —*

*— the light is on. The music is on. Skip the tea; we were never a tea family. The shore is exactly where you left it.*

Far to the north, under an engineered dawn, a woman turned in her sleep toward some sound only sleep could hear, and smiled, and the daughter beside her stole the blanket, and the seer let her.

The hourglass turned over. Forty-two days began again — bloom to doom, doom to bloom, the finite and the boundless in one glass — and the Shorekeeper watched the first grain fall and found that the word for what she felt had been in the archive all along, filed under sole custody, waiting like everything she kept.

The moon has set. The Pleiades have gone down after it. Midnight crosses the shore with its long cold stride.

She keeps the light.

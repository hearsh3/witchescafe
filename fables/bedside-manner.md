Chapter 1: Tea by the Bed

Mei woke up to the cold spot where her daughter had been.

She didn’t open her eyes right away. She slid her left palm sideways across the ticking of the pallet, past the dent in the pillow, looking for the folded square of unbleached cotton Amy had gone to sleep clutching like a stuffed bear. Nothing. Just cooling wool and a stray pin from somebody’s hair.

When she cracked one eye open, the shutter slats were barred with thin grey light, and Iuno was sitting on the floor beside the mattress.

Not in the kitchen.

Iuno had her spine propped against the upturned crate where Barnaby the pine-knot frog and the two deformed sugar badgers stood guard over her hairpins. She was already dressed, her silver-blue hair pinned up in its morning coil, her coat draped over her shoulders against the draft coming under the door. In her one hand she held a porcelain cup with steam rising straight up off the rim in the still air.

Mei looked at the cup. Then she looked at Iuno’s knee, which was wedged against the edge of the pallet, and then up to Iuno’s face.

It took Mei about two seconds to do the math. Iuno drank her first cup at the kitchen table while Lupa banged pots around the stove. Iuno did not bring tea into the bedroom unless somebody was dying, or unless she had decided a conversation was happening before anybody’s boots went on.

Mei pushed herself up on her good elbow, wincing a little as the four inches of healed skin and stubborn thread across her middle reminded her they were still employed, and tried the grin.

“Morning, Iuiu,” Mei croaked, her voice thick with sleep. “Wait. Is it Tuesday?”

“It is Sunday,” Iuno said mildly, and held out the cup. “Sit all the way up before you take this, or you’ll pour it down your front and then Lupa will have to wash the blanket a second time.”

Mei hauled herself upright until her back hit the timber wall, stuffing the corner of the quilt behind her lumbar. She took the cup in both hands. Her left thumb, where Amy had drawn the five-pointed star in Ciaccona’s ink at dinner, had faded to a blurry grey smudge from the wash-basin last night.

She blew on the tea, took a small sip, and immediately made a face.

“Ow. That’s boiling. You’re trying to burn my tongue off so I can’t talk.”

“You drank it cold for six months without a word,” Iuno said, shifting off the crate so she was sitting squarely on the floorboards with her back against the side of the pallet, right by Mei’s hip. The jagged crystal at her left shoulder chimed once, a low, clean note against the wooden frame. “You can manage four minutes of bergamot at the correct temperature.”

“I liked it cold. It had character.” Mei took another tiny, defensive sip, peering over the rim toward the latched door. “Where’d Amymy go? She usually sleeps like a sack of bolts till the seventh bell.”

“Your daughter,” Iuno said, “and Chisa came in here forty minutes before dawn like a pair of cat-burglars, unhooked the standing mirror from behind the clothes trunk, and carried it out between them without waking you.”

Mei blinked. “They stole the mirror?”

“They relocated it to the dry-goods closet off the passage. Along with Ciaccona’s grease-paint box, three of Cartethyia’s hair ribbons, and—unless I am very much mistaken—the enamel pins Suisui left on the washstand.” Iuno rested her head back against the edge of the mattress, looking straight ahead at the opposite wall. “They have been whispering behind the grain sacks for an hour. Something fell over twice. I elected not to inquire.”

Mei laughed, a soft, genuine puff of air into the steam, and let her chin drop toward her chest. “High school. Man. Wait till they figure out somebody’s gotta wash the grease-paint out of the towels.”

“Ciaccona will make Cartethyia do it,” Iuno said.

Then she didn’t say anything else.

The quiet settled into the little room, broken only by the muffled, rhythmic *creak-whuff* of the wooden turbine turning on the storehouse sill outside.

Mei drank another mouthful of tea. Her thumbs traced the glaze of the cup. She knew Iuno was waiting, and she knew why Iuno had sat down facing the same way she was instead of looking right at her.

“I’m fine, you know,” Mei said to the quilt over her shins, and put the bright, easy note back into her voice. “Stitches barely even pulled when I rolled over. Zero rungs yesterday. I’m practically a model patient—”

“Mei.”

Iuno didn’t turn her head. She just lifted her right hand off her lap, reached back onto the mattress without looking, and hooked two fingers into the twisted collar of Mei’s grey sleeping shirt, tugging it once until it lay flat against Mei’s collarbone.

“You were awake until the second watch last night,” Iuno said.

Mei’s mouth stayed open for a second. Then she shut it.

“I wasn’t snoring on cue this time,” Mei tried weakly.

“You weren’t snoring at all. You were lying on your side with your chin on your hand, staring at the back of Amy’s head while she held that white sailor blouse in her sleep.” Iuno’s hand didn’t leave Mei’s collar; her knuckles settled warm against the hollow of Mei’s throat. “And you had the face you get when you’re adding up a bill you think you aren’t allowed to put on the table. Drop the grin, sweetheart. Nobody else is in here.”

*Do not let her pivot to the mule again, darling,* the High Priestess murmured from the back of Iuno’s mind, her voice still half-wrapped in morning velvet. *If I have to hear about the goose’s peerage before we’ve had a bun, I shall scream.*

*Hush,* Iuno thought.

Up on the pallet, Mei let the smile go. It went out of her face the way the heat went out of the stove when the damper closed, leaving her looking very young around the mouth and very old around the eyes.

She set the teacup down on the floorboards beside Iuno’s boot so she wouldn’t spill it, and pulled her knees up a little, careful of her stomach, resting her forearms across them.

“She didn’t even know how to walk up to the first stall, right?” Mei said quietly. “Chisa told me while we were washing the dishes. Said Amy stood in the middle of the road looking at the hairpins like she was waiting for somebody to give her a briefing.”

Iuno said nothing. Her thumb moved once against Mei’s collarbone, a slow, steady stroke over the cotton.

“And she’s twenty-nine,” Mei went on, her fingers picking at a loose grey thread on her trouser knee. “Or nineteen. Whatever. She’s in the closet right now with Chisa putting on somebody else’s eyeliner and trying to figure out how a school tie works. Because she never had one. Not a real one. At Startorch she was already doing the radio thing at night and signing my name on her own forms so nobody would figure out she was by herself, and before that—”

Mei stopped. She rubbed the heel of her hand hard against her forehead, right between her eyebrows.

“Before that, it was just me and her in the cabin. And I know I was there, Iuiu. I know I braided her hair or whatever, or burned the soup, or—she told me about the pickled beets on Friday, and the seal parliament, and I can see her face when she tells me, so I know it happened.” Mei’s voice stayed small and flat, refusing to shake. “I just don’t have it. Any of it. She’s out there trying to build a sixteen-year-old out of stuff she bought for thirty credits at a border market, and I don’t even know if she had bangs when she was twelve.”

She let out a short, dry breath through her nose that wasn’t a laugh at all.

“Like, what if she already tried bangs when she was thirteen and hated them? What if she slammed the bedroom door in the cabin and yelled that I ruined her life because I bought the wrong boots at Vantry Cross? Every other mom knows what their kid looked like when they had a bad haircut. I can’t even tell her ‘hey, don’t cut it that short, you cried last time.’” Mei’s hand closed into a loose fist on her knee. “She’s borrowing a whole childhood off a stall table, and I’m sitting right here with a blank notebook. And the worst part is—”

She broke off, biting the inside of her lip.

“The worst part,” Iuno finished for her, her voice entirely level, “is that you threw the notebook down the hole yourself, at the bottom of the world, so you think you aren’t allowed to complain that your hands are empty.”

Mei’s shoulders hunched an inch. “I’d do it again,” she said immediately, fierce and fast. “If I hadn’t stayed Mei—if I’d let the pilot put the old coat back on—I wouldn’t have caught her when she jumped. I’d do the exact same deal tomorrow.”

“I know you would,” Iuno said. “If you hadn’t, I would have speared you myself and saved the wolf the trouble.”

Mei blinked down at the top of Iuno’s silver-blue head. “...You don’t spear people. You shoot them with a giant bow.”

“A figure of speech. Don’t change the subject.” Iuno shifted her weight on the floorboards, turning half-round so she could look up at Mei properly, her right elbow propped on the mattress beside Mei’s thigh. There was nothing soft or priestly in her face at all; she looked the way she looked when she was auditing a baker’s flour weights. “Listen to me. You are being an idiot about this.”

Mei’s eyebrows went up. “Hey. I’m having a moment here.”

“You are having an attack of tragic narcissism, and it doesn’t suit you,” Iuno said briskly. “First of all, half the mothers on the continent don’t remember whether their daughters had bangs at thirteen. They were drunk on plum wine, or they were working fourteen-hour shifts at the dye-vats, or they had four other children screaming about turnips and simply weren’t looking. At least your excuse involves a metaphysical abyss and saving a city. It’s pretentious, darling, but it’s valid.”

*Crude,* the High Priestess noted, though there was no heat in it. *True, of course, my own mother couldn’t have picked my twelve-year-old fringe out of a police line-up without the family chaplain prompting her, but crude.*

Iuno ignored her completely.

“Second,” Iuno continued, poking Mei’s knee with one finger, “have you ever actually met a thirteen-year-old? I had forty of them in the junior choir every autumn. They smell of damp wool and sour milk. They cry in the lavatory because somebody looked at their shoes, they write atrocious poetry in the margins of the hymnals, and the only reason anyone remembers those years fondly is that the human brain is mercifully designed to rot from the inside out after thirty.”

A tiny, reluctant snort escaped Mei’s nose. “Amy didn’t smell like sour milk.”

“She lived in a cabin under four kilometers of ice with a woman who considers salt a hot-chocolate ingredient,” Iuno said deadpan. “She smelled like stove-grease and wet seal. Stop romanticizing the archive.”

Mei looked down at her smudged thumb. “I’m not romanticizing it. I just—she kept it all for both of us, Iuiu. For ten years in the dark she held onto every stupid grocery list and every crayon drawing so there’d be something left when somebody came for her. And now she’s back, and I’m the mom, and I don’t have my half of the box.”

Iuno’s hand stopped poking Mei’s knee.

Slowly, Iuno turned her palm up on the blanket and slid her fingers over Mei’s closed fist, prying it open one knuckle at a time until Mei’s hand lay flat under hers.

“You don’t need your half of the box,” Iuno said, and the sharpness dropped down into the low, plain register she only used when the door was latched. “She has it. And she is twenty-nine, and she has ten years of back-dated complaints with your name written on the top of every single sheet in fishhook *g*’s.”

Mei’s throat worked.

“Do you know why she’s in the dry-goods closet right now putting on lopsided eyeliner with Chisa?” Iuno asked softly. “Not because she’s mourning the cabin, Mei. Because for the first time since she was seven years old, she doesn’t have to keep the tank full. She knows you’re in here on the mattress. She knows Lupa’s at the woodpile and Suisui’s by the kettle. She’s being sixteen today because there’s finally an adult in the house to roll her eyes at when she walks out.”

Iuno’s thumb pressed once, firm and warm, right into the grey ink-smudge at the base of Mei’s thumb.

“You don’t owe her the old haircut, sweetheart,” Iuno said. “You can’t give it to her, and if you spend the morning pulling a long face because your pockets are empty, she’s going to come out of that closet and start taking care of you again. What you owe her is to sit at the breakfast table, look at whatever disaster she’s done to her head, and ask her a stupid question so she can yell at you about it.”

The room was very quiet. Outside in the yard behind the storehouse, a crow gave three raspy caws from the roof-tree and flapped off into the mist.

Mei looked at Iuno’s hand over hers. Then she looked at the jagged, iridescent crystal at Iuno’s left shoulder, catching the grey light through the slats, and at the tiny crease between Iuno’s brows that meant she’d been awake thinking about this for an hour while the water boiled.

“...Man,” Mei muttered, rubbing her eyes once with her free wrist. “You’re really bad at bedside manner, you know that?”

“I was a High Priestess,” Iuno said serenely, reaching down to pick the teacup back up off the floorboards and placing it squarely into Mei’s hands. “People came to me for absolution or tax assessments. If you want your head patted, go find Cartethyia.”

“Carte would try to knight my stitches.” Mei took a long swallow of the tea. It had cooled down to the exact temperature where the bergamot stopped biting and started tasting like citrus and warm leaves. She held the cup against her chest and thought for a minute, her golden eyes narrowing at the door.

“Okay,” Mei said.

“Okay what.”

“When she comes out, I’m just gonna ask her.” Mei tested the shape of it out loud, nodding once to herself. “Not a big thing. Not a ‘tell me about my tragic absence’ thing. Just—‘Hey, Amymy, did you ever have bangs when you were little?’ Like I’m curious. Because I am.”

“And if she gives you a bratty answer?” Iuno asked.

“Then she gives me a bratty answer,” Mei said, and this time when the corner of her mouth tipped up, it didn’t look like a shield at all. “I can take a bratty answer. I live with you.”

*The insolence,* the High Priestess remarked mildly. *Make her drink the dregs, darling.*

Iuno’s mouth twitched. She stood up from the floorboards with a faint creak of her knee, brushing the dust off the back of her skirt with her one hand. “Drink the rest of that. Your stitches are not excused from breakfast just because you had an existential crisis.”

Before Mei could tip the cup back, a deep, solemn *CRACK* came through the back wall of the bedroom from the woodyard behind the storehouse—the sound of a frozen block of elm finally giving way somewhere it had been refusing Lupa’s axe all week.

Silence for two beats.

Then a second sound: not an axe at all, but three light, rapid, whisper-clean *snick-snick-snick* cuts, like someone paring an apple with a razor, followed by the soft *patter* of wood-shavings hitting the frost.

Then Lupa’s voice roared up through the shutter slats, loud enough to wake half the garrison:

*“DO THE SECOND TURN AGAIN! WHERE’S YOUR BACK HEEL? NOBODY CUTS UPWARD OFF A FLOATING HEEL!”*

Mei paused with the rim of the teacup against her lower lip. Her eyes went wide and bright.

“...Is Lupa fighting the stump again?” Mei asked.

“Lupa has been fighting that stump since Wednesday,” Iuno said, pinching the bridge of her nose as Cartethyia’s voice chimed in from the yard in earnest, high-volume lecture about the geometry of cathedral arches. “And apparently she has recruited reinforcements. No one in this household is capable of an ordinary morning. It is a congenital defect.”

Mei was already swinging her legs over the edge of the pallet, one hand braced on the crate beside Barnaby the frog so her middle didn’t complain. She kept the teacup in her other hand.

“Come on, Iuiu,” Mei said, nudging the back of Iuno’s calf with her bare toe until Iuno reached down and hooked Mei’s boots out from under the bed with her foot. “If Lupa loses to a piece of firewood before pancakes, Cia’s gonna write a whole ballad about it. Help me with the left lace?”

Iuno looked down at her, sighed through her nose, and knelt on one knee to thread the leather cord with her right hand while Mei drank the last of the hot tea over her head.

Chapter 2: The Stump

Twenty minutes before Lupa came out the back door with blood in her eye, the woodyard belonged entirely to the frost and the stump.

It was a four-foot butt of seasoned iron-elm that had come down off the north ridge during the autumn gales, twisted in the grain like a wrung towel and hard enough to turn the edge of a pit-saw. Wen the cook had given up on it on Tuesday. Lupa had taken it as a personal insult on Wednesday, and by Saturday night she had left a heavy iron splitting-wedge buried two inches deep in its crown, rusted tight by the overnight rime.

Yangyang stood six paces out from the chopping block, her breath rising in small, even plumes against the grey karst wall behind the storehouse.

She wasn’t wearing Suisui’s oversized coat this morning. She had on her own clothes—or what had come back with her from the parapet when she hopped onto Mei’s open palm and stopped being a bird: the layered skirt of dark plumage that graded from ink-black at the hip to frost-white at the knee, the wide, sheer sleeves worked with silver cloud-drift, and the cerulean feathers sitting neat and warm around her throat. Her three cracked ribs on the left side were bound flat under her sash with Huaxu linen, aching only when she twisted too fast.

In her right hand she held her sword.

When she was eight years old in the Mingting courtyard, hitting the black-iron post while Suisui counted abacus beads on the veranda, the sword had been a weight she had to organize her whole body behind: heels planted shoulder-width, front knee bent over the toe, right elbow tucked half an inch inward to take the shock on the bone instead of the wrist. Outrider drill. Square, honest, built to hold a road against three things bigger than you until the flare went up.

Yangyang raised the blade to shoulder height and stepped forward.

Her heel didn’t take the frost with a crunch. Her weight went onto the ball of her foot the way a swallow touches a telegraph wire—there, and then already leaving—and when her wrist turned, the cut didn’t push from the hip at all.

It came down from the shoulder blade like the first beat of a wing catching an updraft off the gorge.

*Snick.*

The steel didn’t chop into the iron-elm. It curved through the air in a long, effortless eastern arc, the sheer sky-colored sleeve trailing a half-beat behind the steel like mist pulled in a slipstream, and took a curl of frozen wood off the lip of the stump as thin and translucent as pared pear-skin.

On the backswing, she didn’t plant to recover. Her left foot pivoted without a sound on the rime; the two cyan ribbon-tails at her waist flared out to take the counterbalance, and her second cut came up from beneath on the rising breath, shaving a second curl right beside the first so the two ribbons of wood drifted down together onto the toe of her boot.

Yangyang stopped with the point of the blade resting an inch above the ground.

She looked down at the two shavings on her boot. Then she lifted her right hand and looked at her own wrist.

Her elbow wasn’t tucked in half an inch anymore. It was floating, loose and open, three inches clear of her ribs—not because she’d forgotten her shijie’s correction, but because the air under her forearm felt thick enough to lean on without needing the bone to lock it. Four months of beating her way across the dark between stories, counting musical rests with primary feathers instead of fingers, had rewired the hinge of her shoulder while she wasn’t looking.

She flexed her fingers once on the grip. Her ribs hadn’t pulled at all.

“...Strange,” Yangyang murmured to the frost.

“It looks like water going round a stone,” Cartethyia said from the back doorway.

Yangyang’s collar lifted a fraction of an inch before settling flat again. She turned her head.

Cartethyia was standing on the stone step outside the scullery door with a half-eaten cold steamed bun in one hand and her small brass watering-can in the other, having clearly come out to draw snow-melt from the rain barrel for her camellia cutting. She had her winter cloak thrown over her shoulders unclasped, and her crystal laurel sat straight on her brow for once.

She wasn’t bouncing. She wasn’t clapping her hands. She was just looking at the two thin curls of iron-elm on the ground with her head tilted, the way a mason looks at somebody else’s arch.

“Good morning, Cartethyia,” Yangyang said, lowering her blade to her side. “Did I wake you?”

“No, Cia’s foot was in my ribs, so I got up to water the leaves.” Cartethyia stepped down off the flagstone into the yard, setting the watering-can carefully on the well-curb and finishing the bun in one polite bite. She wiped her fingers on her cloak and stopped four paces from the stump. “You didn’t hit it.”

“It’s iron-elm,” Yangyang said. “If you hit it square, the shock goes into your left side.”

“So you went past it and let the edge take what wanted to come.” Cartethyia nodded once, entirely serious. Her hand went to her hip, where the hilt of her own slender knight’s sword hung from her belt. “May I have a turn? On the other side of Lupa’s iron thing. I won’t nick it.”

Yangyang stepped back two paces toward the stacked cordwood and inclined her head, her cyan ribbons settling behind her heels. “Please.”

Cartethyia drew her blade.

She did not grow. Not a single spark of golden light left her boots; she stayed five feet and a handful of inches tall in her muddy hem and her unclasped cloak, barely clearing the top of the woodpile behind her.

Then she set her feet and raised the hilt to the level of her chin, and the yard got larger.

There was nothing light or wind-borne about it. Cartethyia’s heels settled into the frozen dirt as if she had six hundred tons of marble foundation under her arches. Her spine lengthened; her chin tipped down by two degrees, looking out over the guard with the calm, terrible patience of a carved saint looking down from a cathedral vault at a congregation three hundred feet below.

When she moved, it was slow.

She didn’t step around the grain the way Yangyang had. She drew the blade back in a wide, ceremonial sweep that seemed to pull the grey morning air into the fuller behind it, every angle of her wrist and elbow built for reach, for liturgy, for a body nine feet tall swinging five feet of consecrated steel across an inverted nave—and then she brought the edge down beside Lupa’s rusted wedge in a single, solemn vertical judgment.

*CRACK.*

A slab of seasoned iron-elm the size of a loaf of bread split clean off the flank of the stump along a knot that had turned Lupa’s axe three times, falling into the frost with a heavy *thump*.

Cartethyia held the finishing posture for two full heartbeats—arm extended, left hand poised open behind her shoulder for balance, her face completely still—before she blinked, let out her breath, and was a small girl in the cold again.

“Oh, that was a good knot,” Cartethyia said cheerfully, crouching down to poke the split slab with one finger. “It has little swirls in it like Rosa’s cinnamon bread.”

Yangyang looked at the clean, deep shear down the side of the block, where the wood wasn’t shaved at all, just parted by sheer, unhurried authority. Then she looked at Cartethyia’s small boots in the mud.

“That cut was built for someone three feet taller than you,” Yangyang said plainly.

Cartethyia stood up, brushing frost off her knees, and didn’t look embarrassed at all. She rested her wrist on the pommel of her sword.

“I made it up when I was fourteen,” Cartethyia said, in the same normal, conversational voice she used for congee and geese. “Because I was short.” She looked down at her blade, turning it once in the grey light. “Now it’s just how my arm goes.”

She looked up at Yangyang—at the cerulean feathers breathing softly against her collar and the sheer sleeves drifting in the draft off the karsts.

“Does yours feel like you?” Cartethyia asked. “When your elbow goes out like a wing?”

Yangyang was quiet for a moment. She looked down at her own hand on the hilt, then back up at the girl with the crystal thorns in her hair.

“Not on Tuesday,” Yangyang said simply. “Today it feels like my sister painted me a kite and I forgot to take it off.”

Cartethyia’s whole face lit up—not the theatrical knight’s beam, just a quick, warm flash of pure understanding.

“Show me the rising one!” Cartethyia said, stepping back beside her and squaring her shoulders. “The second cut, where your foot went round like a duck in a pond! I’ll show you the cathedral guard—you have to pretend you’re wearing forty pounds of brocade and a bishop is watching your elbows!”

For the next five minutes, had anyone from the Huanglong Ministry of War or the Order of the Deep looked over the woodyard wall, they would have suffered a stroke.

Yangyang—Watcher of the frontier, bearer of the extinct Xuanling frequency—stood beside the rain barrel with her chin tucked down and her left hand held out behind her ear like a teapot handle, bringing her sword down in a slow, grandiose Ragunnesi liturgical arc that made her feathered collar puff up around her chin in utter bewilderment.

“Lower with the chin!” Cartethyia coached earnestly, walking around her and adjusting Yangyang’s sheer sleeve with two fingers so it draped more dramatically. “You’re pardoning a sinner, Yangyang, not checking a gate pass! Look disappointed in the stump!”

“I have no quarrel with the stump,” Yangyang said, trying to look like a disappointed chapel ceiling and looking mostly like a bird that had swallowed a plum pit whole.

Beside her, Cartethyia tried the eastern thermal-step: she hopped onto the ball of her right foot, swung her sword from the shoulder blade without planting her heel, lost her balance completely on the frost, and pinwheeled both arms before sitting down hard on the cordwood stack with a delighted shriek.

Yangyang didn’t cover her mouth in time. A short, genuine laugh—bright and completely un-level—got right out of her throat into the cold air.

That was the exact moment Lupa kicked the scullery door open with a six-pound splitting maul over her shoulder.

“Right, you knotted bastard, today’s the day we—”

Lupa stopped dead on the top step.

Cartethyia scrambled off the woodpile, dusting bark off her skirt, while Yangyang turned back to the stump and—to clear the two ragged marks they’d left during the lesson—took two quick, instinctive steps forward and flowed through a three-beat Xuanling combination.

*Snick-snick-snick.*

Her boots barely brushed the rime; her body turned inside the sheer gradient of her sleeves like smoke curling up a chimney, the blade flashing three times from three impossible angles without a single pause to reset her weight, shaving the top of the iron-elm as flat and smooth as a planed table.

On the step, Lupa’s jaw unhinged by half an inch.

Her yellow eyes locked onto Yangyang’s ankles, then jumped to her floating right elbow, then to the way the cyan ribbon-tails took the rotational torque off her healing ribs before the cut even finished. The heavy iron maul slid down Lupa’s shoulder until the head bumped the flagstone, completely forgotten.

Lupa had fought every style on two continents. She’d fought gladiators with net-and-trident, coastal duelists who hopped like fleas, northern berserkers who swung iron girders until their collarbones snapped. She knew where the weight went in every single one of them, because knowing where the weight went was how you put a spear through it.

Yangyang’s weight wasn’t going *anywhere*. It was sitting on the air six inches above the mud, and there wasn’t a single fraction of a second between the first cut and the third where a spear could have found a seam without losing its own shaft.

Lupa took two slow steps down into the yard, her ears pricked so far forward they nearly touched, a tiny, ferocious, uncanny smile pulling at the corners of her mouth.

“...What the hell was that,” Lupa breathed.

Yangyang lowered her sword immediately, her ears going pink under her hat brim as she stepped back half a pace. “Good morning, Lupa. We did not touch your wedge.”

“I don’t care about the wedge! Do the second turn again!” Lupa prowled around her in a half-circle, staring at Yangyang’s right shoulder like a hound that had just watched a rabbit turn into an eagle. “Where’s your pivot? You didn’t plant your back heel! If you don’t plant your back heel on an up-cut, your wrist should fold backward—why didn’t your wrist fold? Are you cheating with Aero?”

“No,” Yangyang said, blinking slightly at the wolf crowding into her space. “If I plant my heel, my left ribs complain. So I don’t.”

“‘So I don’t,’” Lupa repeated, offended to her very marrow as she tried to find a flaw in the geometry and came up completely empty. “You can’t just *not plant* because your ribs hurt! That’s—there’s laws! Gravity’s a law!”

Lupa glared at Yangyang’s serene, slightly pink face, realized she had zero notes to give a bird, and visibly bristled with frustrated coaching energy.

She spun on her freshly nailed heel and pointed the handle of her maul straight at Cartethyia’s nose.

“And *you!*” Lupa barked, with pure, glorious relief at finding a target she actually understood. “I saw that chop from the hallway! What in the seven hells was that supposed to be, a coronation?!”

Cartethyia drew herself up to her full five feet, lifting her chin. “It is the Third Judgment of the Inverted Nave! Look at the piece I took off! Your axe bounced off that knot on Friday!”

“The wood split because it died of old age waiting for your sword to get down here!” Lupa marched over, kicked the split slab out of the way, and planted herself beside the chopping block like a drill sergeant in the Colosseum pits. “You held your left arm out behind you like you were airing your armpit for three whole seconds! Do you know what I’d do to you in three seconds in the sand, kid? I’d eat lunch, write a letter home, and *then* put a spear through your liver!”

“A knight of the Order does not scurry!” Cartethyia argued back, her blue eyes flashing as two crystal thorns poked out of her laurel. “The form must be legible from the gallery so the faithful know that justice is deliberate! If you chop like a woodcutter, there’s no reverence in the strike!”

“Reverence doesn’t keep your ribs inside your shirt! Again! Show me the stance!”

Cartethyia planted her feet and raised her hilt to her chin in the grand cathedral guard.

Lupa walked around her, whacking the back of Cartethyia’s left knee lightly with the flat of her palm until it bent, then grabbing Cartethyia’s floating left hand and shoving it forward toward the guard. “Tuck the chin! Not *down* like a statue looking at a bug—in, behind the shoulder, so nobody takes your throat off on the backswing! And stop posing for the back pews, there aren’t any pews in a ditch! Hit the stump! On the beat! *One!*”

*CRACK.*

“Too slow! You’re still waiting for the choir to finish the amen! Pull it back to your hip, not the clouds! *Two!*”

*CRACK.*

“I am the Blessed Maiden—” Cartethyia panted, her cheeks flushing bright pink in the frost as she hauled the blade back up, “—and the choir is *essential* to the cadence—”

“I’m the choir now, and I’m singing in four-four! Drop your hips! *Three!*”

The scullery door creaked open a second time.

Suisui stepped out onto the wide stone landing with a covered bamboo steamer tucked under one arm and a small earthenware pot of hot tea balanced on a wooden tray. Right behind her came Ciaccona, wrapped up to the ears in her green travel cloak with Cartethyia’s spare blanket draped over her head like a shawl, carrying a bowl of roasted chestnuts still popping from the pan.

Neither of them went down into the mud. Suisui flicked a square of folded sackcloth onto the dry top of the cordwood stack beside the steps, sat down with her ankles crossed, and poured two cups of tea while Ciaccona flopped down beside her and immediately cracked a hot chestnut between her thumb and forefinger.

Out in the yard, Lupa was barking counts while Cartethyia swung with heroic, stubborn grandeur, wood-chips flying in every direction while Yangyang stood calmly by the rain barrel, catching the stray splinters on the flat of her scabbard before they could land in Cartethyia’s watering-can.

“Seven!” Lupa roared. “Better! Your elbow only went to the next county that time instead of the coast! Eight!”

Ciaccona blew on the peeled chestnut, popped it into her mouth, and chewed with deep, lazy satisfaction.

“Five coppers says the stump survives till lunch,” Ciaccona said around the nut.

“No bet,” Suisui replied pleasantly, handing Ciaccona a teacup and peeling a steamed bun out of the bamboo basket for herself. “The wolf is enjoying having a pupil far too much to let her finish the wood. Once the stump is gone, she has to go back to stirring the porridge.”

“True. Look at her. She hasn’t been this happy since the pagoda tried to flatten us.” Ciaccona tapped her boot heel softly against the cordwood, finding the rhythm of Lupa’s shouted numbers and humming a brisk, low counterpoint under her breath that turned the chaotic *crack-thump-bark* of the yard into a passable march. “Carte’s actually keeping her left foot down on the recovery. Wonders will never cease.”

Suisui didn’t look at Cartethyia. Over the rim of her porcelain cup, her dark eyes rested on Yangyang standing by the rain barrel.

Yangyang had picked up a long willow twig off the woodpile and was absentmindedly tracing the new, floating arc of her shoulder cut in the air six inches above the water-butt, her brow slightly furrowed as she worked out the math of her own arm. When she reached across her body on the backswing, her left hand didn’t go to her bound ribs once. Her breathing was deep, slow, and completely even in the cold.

Suisui didn’t call out to tell her to put a coat on. She didn’t ask if her side hurt. She just took a bite of her warm bun, let her shoulders sink an inch deeper into her wool shawl, and watched her little sister play with a stick in the sun.

“Chestnut, Yangyang?” Suisui called out lightly when the twig stopped moving.

Yangyang turned her head, caught the two roasted nuts Ciaccona tossed across the yard in one open palm without looking down, and bowed her head an inch. “Thank you. They’re still hot.”

“Don’t burn your mouth on the shell,” Suisui said automatically.

“I only do that with pears,” Yangyang answered, and cracked one cleanly with her thumb.

At the top of the scullery steps, the latch clicked a third time.

Mei came shuffling out into the morning light with Iuno half a step behind her, one hand tucked into Iuno’s coat pocket for warmth and the other holding her empty teacup.

“—telling you, if she’s using the big axe on the frost, the wedge is gonna shoot right over the wall and brain a mule—” Mei started, and then she looked out into the yard.

Lupa was yelling at Cartethyia’s ankles. Suisui and Ciaccona were having a picnic on the firewood.

And by the rain barrel, Yangyang—seeing a third chunk of iron-elm fly up off Cartethyia’s blade straight toward the camellia watering-can—stepped off her back foot without a sound, her cerulean feathers lifting in the pale gold sun as she spun the willow switch once from an open, floating shoulder and flicked the heavy hardwood chunk out of the air into the neatly stacked kindling box with a single, effortless *tock*.

Mei stopped dead on the top step.

Her mouth fell open. She forgot her stitches, forgot her teacup, and grabbed Iuno’s sleeve with her free hand, her golden eyes wide and shining as she stared at Yangyang settling back onto the frost like a crane landing on a reed.

“Iuiu,” Mei breathed, loud enough for the whole woodpile to hear. “Look at Yangyang. When did she start fighting like a movie?!”

Chapter 3: The Mirror

An hour before anyone else’s boots hit the floorboards, the dry-goods closet smelled of sacked barley, dried mushrooms, and lavender grease-paint.

It was six feet wide and ten feet long, wedged between the main storehouse pantry and the back passage. They had propped the tall pier-glass against two barrels of salted turnips and hung a pinch-wick oil lamp from a meat hook overhead so the light fell straight down onto their noses. Spread out across an upturned flour crate between them sat the loot: two crisp, unbleached cotton summer blouses with pale blue sailor collars, two matching blue silk ties, Ciaccona’s battered leather stage-kit, three of Cartethyia’s spare ribbons, and four silver-and-pearl hairpins Suisui had carelessly left next to the wash-basin before bed.

Amy stood in front of the glass in her new summer blouse, shivering slightly despite the wool coat slung over her shoulders, with her blue tie twisted into a knot the size of a walnut at the base of her throat.

“Okay, wait,” Amy muttered, tugging at the short end of the silk, which only made the walnut tighter and pulled the sailor collar up around her ears like a bucket. “That’s not right. In the Startorch orientation manual, the diagram had three arrows—you go over, under, through the loop—why does mine look like a turnip?”

Beside her, sitting on a sack of millet with her knees together, Chisa already had her blouse buttoned neatly to the throat, the cuffs folded back once to the exact width of two fingers so the red string on her left wrist stayed clear. Her dark suppression collar sat above the sailor neckline.

Chisa’s tie was perfect. The triangle was symmetrical to the millimeter, the dimple centered directly beneath the top button, the broad end hanging precisely to the belt-line of her pleated skirt.

“You went over twice,” Chisa said, not looking at the mirror. “And you’re pulling the narrow end instead of sliding the knot.”

Amy stared at her reflection, then sideways at Chisa. “How did you do yours in the dark while we were carrying the mirror?”

“It’s a four-in-hand knot,” Chisa said simply. “You do it by feel. The mirror only gets in the way.” She stood up from the millet sack, stepped in front of Amy, and reached up with both hands.

Chisa didn’t look at Amy’s face; to her eyes, the mirror behind them only held two pale blurs of cotton and a bright smudge of pink under the yellow lamp. She found the walnut knot by touch, undid it in two brisk movements, and flipped the silk over, under, and through against Amy’s collarbone with quiet, economical fingers, sliding the triangle up until it sat flat and neat in the vee of the blue collar.

“There,” Chisa said, patting the silk once.

Amy looked in the glass. With the coat pushed back off her shoulders, the short-sleeved white cotton and the blue tie made her look absurdly, dangerously normal. Like somebody whose biggest problem at eight in the morning was a geometry quiz.

“...Huh,” Amy said, touching the knot with one finger. “Okay, that’s pretty good. Now sit down. We’re doing phase two.”

“Phase two,” Chisa repeated cautiously, sitting back down on the millet sack.

“In every drama series on the Startorch intranet,” Amy said, picking up Ciaccona’s little pot of black kohl and a camel-hair brush with the grim authority of a field engineer, “when two girls become friends in the second episode, they cut class in the equipment shed and do each other’s hair and makeup. It’s mandatory. Don’t move your eyelids.”

Ten minutes later, they had discovered a fundamental flaw in Amy’s qualifications.

Amy could calibrate a crowd of ten thousand people from a stage monitor. She knew to the half-inch how a spotlight hit a cheekbone from forty feet away in an arena, or how to angle a camera drone so a pilot looked tall next to a mech.

She had zero experience painting a human eye from six inches away in a turnip closet.

“Open,” Amy commanded, stepping back with the kohl brush between her teeth and squinting at her own reflection in the pier-glass first, since she’d practiced on herself before risking Chisa’s face.

In the mirror, Amy’s right eye had a sleek, dramatic black wing that swept out toward her temple like a gull in flight. Her left eye looked as if she had been punched by a coal-miner. The line started too high, wobbled over an eyelash, and ended in a thick, square smudge halfway toward her acid-green star clip.

“...My left hand’s dumb,” Amy announced to the glass. “The brush is torquing on the backstroke. Hold on, if I bring the right one up to match the thickness—”

“Stop,” Chisa said from the millet sack.

“You can’t even see it!”

“You said ‘torquing,’” Chisa said levelly. “Nobody says that about eyeliner when it’s going well. If you match the right side to the left, you’ll look like a badger.”

“Badgers are very popular in this house right now.” Amy scrubbed at her left temple with the corner of a dry towel, which only turned the coal-miner punch into a dramatic grey thundercloud, and then pointed the brush at Chisa. “Fine. Forget the kohl. What would you have done when you were sixteen? If there wasn’t a loop and your mom wasn’t looking?”

Chisa sat very still on the grain sack for a moment.

She didn’t look at the mirror. She reached out to the flour crate, passed her fingers over Ciaccona’s stage-kit, skipped the kohl and the rouge entirely, and picked up two of Suisui’s stolen hairpins—the ones with little pale river-pearls wired to the brass prongs—and one of Cartethyia’s narrow sky-blue ribbons.

“One side braided behind the ear,” Chisa said, her voice completely plain. “With the ribbon through the third cross, so it doesn’t show from the front.” She held the ribbon and the pins out toward Amy. “I can’t do it myself. In a mirror I can’t find the part line. Only the black.”

Amy’s hand with the dirty towel dropped to her side.

She didn’t say *aw* or make a fuss. She just wiped the grease-paint off her fingers, took the blue ribbon and the pearl pins out of Chisa’s palm, and stepped behind the millet sack.

“Hold the pins,” Amy said briskly. “And tilt your chin up an inch. If I pull, kick the turnip barrel.”

Amy’s fingers were fast and surprisingly gentle—the hands of a mechanic who spent half her life threading hair-thin copper wire through relay boards without snapping the solder. She parted Chisa’s dark hair cleanly above the left temple, gathered a three-strand section just behind the ear where the suppression collar wouldn’t rub it, and plaited it down, weaving Cartethyia’s sky-blue ribbon into the third crossing so a flash of bright colour sat nested right inside the black. Then she anchored it above the nape with two of Suisui’s river-pearl pins.

Chisa raised her right hand and touched the braid lightly with her fingertips, following the raised ridge of the silk ribbon down to the two smooth pearls.

“It’s even,” Chisa said.

“Of course it’s even, I have a degree,” Amy scoffed, stepping back around to the mirror and staring at her own reflection again.

With the white blouse, the neat blue tie Chisa had knotted for her, the little acid-green copper star over her temple, and one smudged eye, she looked almost right. Except her forehead looked huge under the hanging lamp, where her pink hair was parted down the middle the way she’d worn it since she was small so it wouldn’t fall into her welding goggles.

“I need bangs,” Amy decided on the spot.

“You don’t have scissors,” Chisa said, her hand automatically checking her empty back where her five-foot spatial shears were *not* strapped.

“I don’t need scissors to test the concept! You comb the front section forward, wet it from the basin, and pin the ends under at the eyebrow line—girls on the broadcast used to fake a fringe between sets all the time!” Amy dunked Suisui’s wooden comb into the cup of water they’d brought in, raked a generous chunk of pink hair straight down over her nose, and began furiously folding and pinning it up with the remaining two pearl pins.

She worked for sixty seconds, tongue poked out the corner of her mouth, before letting go.

The wet pink loop held for half a beat, then sagged sideways across her forehead like a crooked pink awning over her smudged left eye.

Amy stared at herself in the tarnished glass.

“...Man,” she muttered, poking the lopsided fringe with one finger. “I don’t even know if I had bangs when I was little. There aren’t any photos, and Ma doesn’t know, so for all I know I walked around Bregge looking like a turnip until I was ten.”

Beside her, Chisa adjusted the folded cuff of her summer sleeve by a fraction of an inch. “What were you like when you were thirteen?”

“Thirteen?” Amy snorted, shoving a third bobby-pin sideways into the pink loop to keep it out of her nose. “At thirteen I was five feet tall, I smelled like soldering flux, and I got grounded for two weeks because I rewired the settlement’s snow-siren to play the theme from *Space Fantasy Katya* at four in the morning. Which was a public service, by the way, the old siren sounded like a dying cow.”

She didn’t say what the winter after thirteen was like. She just slapped both hands down on the flour crate and grinned at the smudge of Chisa’s shape beside her.

“Done,” Amy declared. “We’re sixteen. Nobody’s allowed to ask why we’re wearing short sleeves in December. Let’s go ruin breakfast.”

***

Out in the woodyard, Cartethyia’s arms were trembling, the iron-elm stump had a three-inch trench chewed down its eastern face, and Lupa was in coaching heaven.

“Better!” Lupa barked, circling the block with her hands on her hips while Mei and Iuno watched from the top step and Suisui poured a third round of tea on the woodpile. “Now do it one more time without looking like you’re waiting for a stained-glass window to open behind your head! Drop the left heel, keep the point on the line, and—”

The scullery door swung wide behind Mei’s shoulder.

Amy and Chisa stepped out onto the stone landing side by side.

Neither of them was wearing a coat. In the crisp, frost-rimmed mountain sun, their matching unbleached cotton summer blouses and pale blue silk ties practically glowed against the grey timber of the storehouse. Chisa stood straight and composed with her cuffs neatly turned and Cartethyia’s blue ribbon braided behind her left ear; Amy stood with her hands on her hips, one eye winged like a tragic opera heroine, the other smudged like a chimney-sweep, and a lopsided, damp pink roll of fake bangs pinned sideways above her eyebrows with Suisui’s river-pearls.

In the yard below, Cartethyia froze mid-swing. The sword tip dropped into the frost.

For two full seconds, nobody made a sound except the water spires turning over the cliff.

Then everyone talked at once.

“Chisa!” Cartethyia gasped, dropping her blade entirely to clap both hands over her mouth. “You’re matching! And you have my ribbon in your hair! You look like you’re going to a festival with a bookbag!”

“Hold on—hold the hell on—” Lupa completely forgot the stump, taking three long strides across the wood-chips to the bottom of the steps, her yellow eyes wide as they travelled from Chisa’s bare forearms to the pale blue tie and up to the braided ribbon behind her ear. Lupa’s tail gave one slow, bewildered thump against the rain barrel. “You bought blue. You actually bought something stupid and blue. Wait, aren’t you freezing?! Where’s your coat?!”

“It’s a summer uniform,” Chisa said calmly from the landing. “If you wear a coat over it, you can’t see the stripes on the cuffs.”

On the woodpile, Ciaccona choked on a chestnut, coughed twice into her fist, and pointed a trembling finger at Amy’s face. “Bellissima. Come here immediately. Who put that kohl on your left eye, the mule? You look like you’re playing both halves of a tragedy in different time signatures!”

“My left hand’s an engineer, not a painter!” Amy shot back, her ears going bright pink in the cold air, though she didn’t take her hands off her hips.

Beside Ciaccona, Suisui set her teacup down on the sackcloth, her dark eyes sparkling as she took in the four silver-and-pearl prongs glinting in the two girls’ hair. She opened her folding fan by two ribs and tapped her chin. “Those pins look remarkably familiar. They suit the braid, Chisa. Amy, the third one on your forehead is upside down; the pearl is supposed to face the street, not your nose.”

By the rain barrel, Yangyang tucked her willow twig under her arm and looked up at the landing. Her cerulean collar settled flat and warm against her throat.

“The ties are very neat,” Yangyang said in her plain, gentle voice. “You look like my cousins in Mingting when the ferries come in for the June holidays.”

Chisa inclined her head a fraction toward the sound of Yangyang’s voice, her fingers brushing the blue silk knot at her collarbone once. “Thank you. Amy’s was a turnip first.”

“Snitch!” Amy elbowed her in the ribs.

On the top step, Iuno hadn’t said a word. She stood with her one hand tucked into her coat, looking from the matching blue ties to the lopsided pink fringe pinned across Amy’s brow.

*Look at that, darling,* the High Priestess murmured, very softly. *Thirty credits of cotton and four stolen pins. She didn’t even bring the wrench.*

*No,* Iuno thought, watching the way Amy’s shoulders sat—not braced for a crowd, just slightly cold and terribly pleased with herself. *She didn’t.*

Mei was standing right beside Amy on the flagstone, still holding her empty teacup in both hands.

She looked at the smudged eyeliner. She looked at the pale blue sailor collar. And then she looked at the damp, crooked roll of pink hair pinned above Amy’s eyes with Suisui’s pearls.

Mei didn’t reach out to fix the pin. She didn’t pull a long face, and she didn’t put on the big shield-grin. She just leaned her hip against the doorpost to keep the weight off her stitches and tilted her head.

“Hey, Amymy,” Mei said conversationally. “Did you ever have bangs when you were little? Like, actual cut ones?”

Amy stopped halfway through glaring at Ciaccona and turned her head toward her mother.

For half a second, Amy blinked—her good eye and her smudged eye both widening a fraction, as if she’d been braced for a hug or a joke about the uniform and had missed a step on the stair instead. She looked at Mei’s face, saw nothing heavy waiting there, just her mom asking a question before breakfast, and her chin came up.

“Yeah,” Amy said, her voice dropping into her brisk, bratty nineteen-year-old cadence as she poked the fake fringe with her thumb. “When I was eight. You cut ’em over the sink with the fish-shears because they kept getting stuck in the sap when we tapped the birches.”

Mei’s fingers tightened a little around the warm porcelain of the cup. She didn’t stop smiling. “Wait. The fish-shears? Were they crooked?”

“They were at a forty-five-degree angle, Ma! I looked like a snow-plough!” Amy threw one hand out, rolling her eyes toward the sky while Iuno’s mouth twitched beside the doorframe. “I cried behind the stove for two whole days and wore my wool hat inside at dinner until you sat me back down on the bucket and tried to level them out, and then they were half an inch long and stuck straight up like a toothbrush!”

From the bottom of the steps, Lupa barked a laugh. “Fish-shears! That’s how they did the rookies’ hair in the pits!”

Mei laughed too—just a short, warm sound in her chest that didn’t pull her middle at all. Right there on the sunny landing, she had an eight-year-old girl on an upturned bucket under her hands, smelling of birch sap and wet wool, squinting furiously under a half-inch pink toothbrush fringe.

She didn’t cry over it. She just filed it away behind her ribs, right next to the seal parliament and the pickled beets, where it belonged.

“Did they grow back okay?” Mei asked, nudging Amy’s bare elbow with her own.

“Took till April,” Amy grumbled, though she leaned her shoulder right back into Mei’s good side, letting the warmth of Mei’s sleeve cut the wind off her summer cotton. “And if you come near my head with Chisa’s scissor-blade today, I’m filing for emancipation with Auntie.”

“Approved in advance,” Iuno said dryly, turning toward the open scullery door as the smell of sizzling oil and toasted scallions drifted out from Wen’s stove inside. “Now get indoors, both of you, before your fingers turn the same colour as your ties. Breakfast was twenty minutes ago.”

“Wait, the stump!” Cartethyia protested from the yard, pointing at the battered block of iron-elm. “I only need four more strikes to cleave the western face!”

“Stump’s not going anywhere, kid, it’s got no legs,” Lupa said, slinging her heavy maul back over her shoulder and swatting the back of Chisa’s head lightly with her tail as the two girls filed past her into the warm kitchen, Chisa immediately reaching up to make sure her pearl pins hadn’t shifted. “Move it! Last one to the bench gets the burnt pancake, and I’m not sharing the chili crisp!”

They went in noisy and all at once—Ciaccona promising to fix Amy’s left eye with spit and a napkin, Suisui counting heads against the teapot, Yangyang pausing at the threshold just long enough to hang her sword on the peg and let Mei’s hand brush her feathered shoulder as they passed into the heat of the room.

Out in the quiet yard behind the latched door, the winter sun climbed another finger’s breadth above the karsts.

The iron-elm stump sat in the middle of the frost, unsplit, with Lupa’s rusted wedge still buried in its crown, two paper-thin curls of Yangyang’s wood lying beside its base, and Cartethyia’s deep, solemn cathedral notch gleaming pale yellow down its side. Against the storehouse wall, the sixteen-foot cedar ladder leaned ready in the light, and inside on the warm stone beside the roaring stove, the ceramic cover waited over a clean plate for whoever came home late.

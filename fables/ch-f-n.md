Chapter 1: Chī Fàn

When the pale blade vanished from Qingxiao’s fingers, the breath went out of the court all at once.

Mei didn’t look at the dead bronze hull settling into the puddle on the lacquer floor. Her sword slid back into its scabbard with a wet click, and she crossed the cracked tiles straight to Iuno.

She put both arms around Iuno’s waist and pulled her in against her chest. She moved too fast on her wet boots; a small, sharp breath caught behind her teeth as the five-day-old stitches across her middle pulled tight under the linen wrap, and she didn’t let go. After two beats she turned half-around, keeping her right hand closed over Iuno’s wrist, and drew Yangyang in against her other side—much more carefully, her arm held high above the strapped left ribs under Yangyang’s sash while she completely ignored the tug in her own stomach.

Yangyang rested her brow briefly against Mei’s dry collar, her breathing still shallow from the roof pillar, before she patted Mei’s forearm once and stepped back half a pace to give her room.

Iuno did not step back. Her right hand came up at once and caught the hem of Mei’s grey shirt, trying to tug the cotton free of her belt.

“Hold still,” Iuno said, her voice crisp and a shade too fast in the dripping quiet of the rafters. “You tore them on the stairs, didn’t you? I felt you hitch when you turned.”

Mei clamped both hands over her belt buckle to hold her shirt down, her ears going pink. “I didn’t tear anything! Not here, Iuiu, there are ten people standing right behind you—”

“Ten people who watched you stroll off a rug you were explicitly ordered to stay glued to,” Iuno snapped, and when her fingers left the hem of the shirt they locked hard around Mei’s right forearm instead and stayed there. “You had a single assignment, Mei. A felt blanket. Flat on the flagstones. I go through a door for five hours and you walk into a storm on an open abdomen.”

Mei didn’t argue, and she didn’t reach for a joke. Her golden eyes had gone to Iuno’s left shoulder.

Where the grey morning light leaked down through the shattered calipers overhead, a fresh, raw notch showed in the iridescent crystal of the stump—a thumb-wide facet sheared clean off when Muyu’s jade cleaver had pinned the moonwheel to the floor.

Mei’s face went quiet. She raised her left hand, her thumb stopping a hair’s breadth from the sharp, newly broken edge before she laid her fingertips lightly against the smooth slope of crystal just above the chip, the way she would have touched a bruised collarbone.

“You lost a piece,” Mei said softly. “When he hit the wheel. Does it ache?”

Iuno went rigid for a heartbeat. “It is crystal, Mei. And it is barely a splinter.”

“I’m not asking what it’s made of,” Mei said, her thumb staying warm and still against the unbroken ridge. “I’m asking if it hurts.”

Iuno looked past her at the broken glass of the feed-cylinders, her jaw set and a faint warmth rising up her neck. “It rings,” she admitted, low enough that it didn’t carry to the doorway. “Half a tone flat. It will settle by tonight.”

*Oh, look at your face, darling,* the High Priestess murmured in the back of her mind, thoroughly satisfied. *She noticed a missing chip before the child did and now you’re going to preen about it until the snow melts.*

*Be quiet,* Iuno told her, and leaned her shoulder an inch into Mei’s palm before letting her hand slide down to Mei’s wrist.

Around the court, the rest of the pack was already shaking off the fight. Amy moved from person to person like a quartermaster after a bad shift, pulling a roll of clean gauze from her vest pocket to bind the shallow gash across Yangyang’s forearm while telling Cartethyia to get her hem out of a puddle of condenser oil.

“Hold your wrist flat, Watcher,” Amy ordered, tying the knot off with her teeth. “You’re dripping on your own boots.”

“Thank you,” Yangyang said mildly.

Over by the fallen cedar doors, Chisa wiped black axle-grease from the cutting bevel of her giant shears with a torn scrap of indigo robe, checked the hinge pin with her thumb, and folded the blades shut with a solid iron *clack*. On a dry bronze plinth beside her, Ciaccona unwound the bloodied linen strip from her left forefinger, flexed the split knuckle once with a sharp wince, and immediately tucked the hand behind her lute neck when Cartethyia knelt down to look at it.

“It’s skin, cara, not an amputation,” Ciaccona muttered. “Pick up your laurel, it’s crooked.”

Two paces from the broken threshold, Lupa and Jingran stood side by side out of the drip from the rafters. Lupa leaned her weight against the ash shaft of her spear, her scorched left sleeve hanging in black tatters; Jingran held his wire-mended brass lamp low by his knee while Yuebing sat neatly on his collar, licking a front paw.

Lupa glanced sideways at the grey cat. “He’s got grease on his feet.”

“Condenser soot,” Jingran said conversationally. “He likes crawling behind the boilers. Terrible habit for the laundry.”

Lupa grunted once and looked back toward the doors.

In the center of the court, three paces from the dead bronze torso, Qingxiao stood by herself. Now that the thousands of pale threads had reeled back into her cuffs, she looked smaller in the grey light, her moon-white dress torn at the right knee and a slow drop of blood still gathering at the heel of her cut left palm. When Amy let go of Yangyang’s bandaged forearm, Yangyang walked over across the cracked lacquer, stopped at Qingxiao’s right elbow, and held out a clean square of folded linen from Amy’s vest.

Qingxiao looked down at the cloth for a beat, took it, and closed her cut fingers around it.

Across the tiles, Mei glanced down at the front of her own grey shirt, where the six-inch line of plum-scented rime was melting into a damp trace over her bandages. She looked up at Qingxiao and opened her mouth—

By the doorway, the small woman in the faded plum-colored coat rapped the steel-tipped ferrule of her closed umbrella twice against the fallen cedar leaf.

“The roof’s leaking,” she said wearily. “If you’re going to stand around bleeding on the floor, do it downstairs.”

Nobody argued with her. They walked out through the long drafting gallery in a tight, limping knot, stepping around the sawdust by the center workbench with their eyes forward, crossed the broken cedar bridge where the amber wall had vanished into the thinning rain, and dropped single-file through the vertical funnel of wind back into the smell of cold tallow and iron stairs.

***

When the iron service door swung shut at the top of the gearwell, the real Xuanfang Hold was loud with morning.

Outside the narrow passage, the night’s sleet had thinned to a fine, cold mist under a sky the color of wet sheep’s wool. Out beyond the forty-foot diagonal cut in the eastern curtain wall, the switchback ramp lay quiet; four ranks of the Censure Courts had collapsed where they stood in the slush, their notched bronze cleavers half-buried in the mud and their crimson optical lenses dark.

Under the gateway arch, Liangyu stood with her boots ankle-deep in freezing black water, looking down the empty road where the white fog was finally sinking back toward the gorge. Slowly, after a night and a day, she let the point of her ash spear drop until the iron rested on the paving stones, and leaned her forehead against the wet granite jamb for the space of two breaths.

All around the inner courtyard, the garrison was coming off the wall at once.

Shen Lu and the archers climbed stiffly down the slick west stair with their unstrung bows over their shoulders; Bai Tieshan stepped away from the base of the sheared masonry, wiping grey stone-paste from his palms onto his trousers; Little Tan and Warden Huo came through the gate supporting Warden Ge between them, all three of them shivering under their wet wool cloaks. Wardens, quarrymen, relay runners, and families from Xianling Village crowded up the arcade steps together, two hundred wet, starving people pouring through the doors of a timber branch hall that had been built to seat forty clerks.

Under the arcade eaves, Iuno stopped beside the red-lacquered pillar, her right hand still resting on Mei’s forearm as she looked up past the dripping tiles. High in the mist above the Ministry roof, the colossal celadon shell of the Astrolabe Core still hung dark and motionless, its bronze rings two inches out of true.

Iuno’s spine straightened. She turned toward Liangyu, who was coming heavily up the courtyard steps with mud caked to the tops of her greaves, and Jingran just behind her.

“Watcher,” Iuno said, her voice dropping into the measured cadence of a council room. “Before the mist rises off the river again, we need the quartermaster’s charts of the deep manifold, and someone on the relay to see if the lower boundary wards—”

The small woman in the plum coat walked straight past Iuno’s knees, her closed umbrella propped over her shoulder and its steel ferrule clicking every third step on the wet flagstones.

“Eat,” the small woman said.

Iuno blinked down at the crown of her head. “The array under the valley is rotting from the underside, and the Sanctum—”

The small woman put the toe of her boot against the heavy timber door of the branch hall and shoved it open, letting out a thick billow of warm steam that smelled fiercely of boiling ginger, toasted peppercorns, and seared river fish.

“Not at the table,” she said over her plum-colored shoulder, and walked inside.

Iuno stood on the threshold with her mouth slightly open. Beside the doorpost, Warden Huo ducked his head respectfully as he squeezed past with an armful of wet pikes, and Liangyu walked up the steps behind the small woman without a trace of surprise on her bruised face, unbuckling her dented left vambrace as she went into the steam.

***

Inside the branch hall, the Fillet Fairmaiden had conquered the brick hearth.

How she had gotten three sentient cauldrons and four iced crates of silver carp up the mountain switchbacks before dawn was a question nobody in the room cared enough to ask. Stoke, Ember, and Flare stood lined up in front of the chimney-breast, their bronze bellies glowing bright orange with fresh coal and their lids rattling a steady four-beat rhythm as the Fairmaiden worked two long iron ladles at once. Her navy-black twin-tails bounced with every swing, steamer bands clinking against her ladle loop, while a queue of mud-splashed soldiers and villagers shuffled down the center aisle holding chipped earthenware bowls, tin ration mugs, and washed-out helmets.

“Keep the line moving!” she shouted cheerfully over the roar of the hall. “Broth on the left, greens on the right, don’t step on Ember’s toes!” She swung her left ladle up to point toward the back benches, spotted the cerulean feathers at Yangyang’s collar coming through the doorway, and dropped the ladle straight back into the pot with a splash.

“Xuanling!” Her voice cut clean across two hundred people. “Flare! Clear the long bench by the pillar! Move those pike-shafts!”

Under her hat brim, Yangyang’s ears went scarlet. “We can wait by the door,” she said toward the hearth, though her voice had no chance against the steam. “The wall companies haven’t—”

“The wall companies have legs, they’re right here in the line!” the Fairmaiden barked, pointing her ladle like a baton as Flare waddled out from the brickwork and puffed a sharp, persuasive jet of steam at the boots of three orderlies who were using the nearest pine table to fold wet blankets.

Within a minute, all twelve of them were wedged around a single long clerk’s table that had been built by a thrifty Ministry joiner to seat six.

Mei tried to linger at the outer corner of the bench to help hand down the stack of wooden bowls from the hearth, and got half a step before Amy caught the back of her jacket belt with both hands and hauled her down onto the pine plank between herself and Iuno.

“Bench,” Amy said flatly. “If your knees leave that wood before the pot’s dry, Ma, I’m sitting on your lap again.”

Mei settled onto the plank with a small, rueful sigh, resting her right elbow on the scarred pine and looking down the crowded board to where a stoneware jug of warm boiled water sat beyond Chisa’s shears. Her weight shifted once toward the balls of her feet by habit; she caught herself, settled her hip back against Iuno’s, and tapped Chisa’s black sleeve instead.

“Chisa, could you pass the water jug?”

Chisa handed it down across Lupa’s elbow, and Amy’s shoulders dropped an inch as she took the jug and filled a cup for her mother herself.

Then the Fairmaiden arrived at the table like a squall coming off the harbour. Ember waddled behind her under a wide wooden tray of twelve steaming bowls, while Stoke brought up the rear carrying a tub of chopped scallions and pickled mustard stems. She dealt the bowls out across the pine with both hands, pulled a wooden tally-slate and a stub of chalk from her wave-embroidered apron, and planted one orange heel on the bench stretcher.

“Twelve!” she said briskly. “Bird’s on the house, the bird’s people are in my book, wolf pays full—” Her golden-green eyes traveled down the crammed bench past Yangyang and Qingxiao to the very end, where the small woman in the plum coat sat with her closed umbrella held upright between her knees and her boots dangling a full foot clear of the floorboards. “Who’re you, little sister? Quartermaster says I have to chalk a name for every garrison bowl.”

Across the board, Cartethyia paused with her spoon in her hand, looking down the table in sympathetic expectation of the refusal.

“Suoming,” the small woman said.

The Fairmaiden chalked it onto the slate in three quick strokes.

Cartethyia’s jaw dropped. The crystal thorns of her laurel tilted sideways as she leaned forward over her steaming bowl. “You told *her*! I asked you out in the courtyard while it was raining on our heads, and I gave you my whole name and my town!”

Suoming picked up her wooden spoon and blew on a square of white tofu. “She’s feeding me.”

Beside Suoming’s dangling boots, Liangyu kept her eyes fixed inside her bowl and took a large, quiet mouthful of broth.

The Fairmaiden was already turning back with a deep ladle of fish belly for the far end of the bench when her wrist nearly clipped the tip of a cloud-pale horn.

She stopped dead. Her eyes went from the horn down to the long blue hair, the mud-splashed moon-white cuff, and the pale face beneath. For three full seconds she stood frozen between the pine table and the hearth, her mouth opening on two hundred years of Xuanfang mountain tales—and then a pikeman four places back in the queue coughed hard into his helmet, and the smell of scorching ginger rose from Flare’s lid.

The Fairmaiden swallowed whatever speech was in her throat, gave Qingxiao a single, ferocious nod from the neck that was half reverence and half kitchen emergency, and dumped an enormous, overflowing ladle of silver carp and soft tofu straight into Qingxiao’s bowl until the broth lapped the clay rim.

“Eat while it’s hot!” she told the Sword Transcendent, and pivoted on her heel toward the line—

“Chef,” Jingran said smoothly, propping his chin on his palm at the corner of the table with his brightest teahouse smile while Yuebing’s grey ears poked up over the edge of the board beside his wrist. “You wouldn’t happen to have a pot of red-bean paste or a few date-paste mooncakes tucked inside one of those bronze fellows, would you? The stairs were terribly hard on the constitution.”

The Fairmaiden looked back over her shoulder at him. At her heel, Ember’s lid snapped shut with a loud *clang* and puffed a cloud of hot coal-smoke straight against Jingran’s shin.

“It’s seven in the morning after a siege and I have forty bleeding spearmen in my line, pretty boy,” she told him pleasantly. “Eat your turnip greens before I put you on the wash-tub.”

She marched back to the hearth. Jingran sighed, slumping an inch lower on his spine as he poked at a floating scallion ring with his chopsticks. On the table edge beside his elbow, Yuebing gave a soft, expectant chirp. Before Jingran could fish out a bit of carp, Lupa’s scarred hand reached across from two seats away and dropped a fat tail-section onto the pine in front of the cat’s nose; a moment later, Chisa set a clean, bone-free flake of white fish beside it, and Amy slipped a corner of fried bean-curd skin over from the other side. Yuebing ate all three in neat, unhurried order without looking at Jingran once.

Wedged on the inner bench between Yangyang and Suoming, with barely four inches of space on either side, Qingxiao sat very straight. She had not sat at a crowded table in longer than most people in the hall had been alive, and whenever Yangyang shifted her left elbow to favor her strapped ribs, their sleeves brushed from shoulder to wrist. Qingxiao did not pull her arm back; she picked up her bamboo chopsticks with her right hand, keeping her bandaged left palm curled lightly against the warm clay side of the bowl, and ate the soup.

Halfway through the first bowls, Old Mother Pu came past the chimney drying her hands on her apron, picked up the small, heavy-bottomed iron pan from the back corner of the brickwork where Mei had slid it before dawn, and set it down in the middle of their table on a braided straw ring.

Inside, broken into thick, crisp golden-brown slabs, was the rice crust from Lupa’s pot.

Mei’s face lit up; she reached out to slide the pan down the wood toward Qingxiao and Yangyang.

Qingxiao looked at the steaming crust. She reached out with two fingers of her bandaged left hand, touched the iron lip of the pan, and pushed it past her own bowl and past Suoming’s elbow until it stopped squarely in front of Liangyu.

Liangyu’s chopsticks paused an inch above her broth. The bruised, slate-grey skin under her eyes tightened once; she bowed her head over the wood, lifted a crisp slab into her bowl, and passed the iron pan across the aisle so Warden Ge and Little Tan could reach the rest.

Watching from across the table, Mei smiled, and her left hand came up to touch the front of her grey shirt where the damp mark of the melted rime sat over her bandages. She leaned forward a little around Iuno’s good shoulder, looking down the board at Qingxiao.

“Paragon,” Mei said quietly. “Back on the rug, when that bird came off the roof—”

Qingxiao did not lift her eyes from her bowl. “Eat,” she said in her level voice, and took another square of tofu.

At the end of the bench, Suoming reached over with one short arm, hooked her forefinger around the saucer of pickled mustard stems that sat trapped behind Jingran’s wire-mended lamp, and slid it six inches down the pine plank until it rested beside Qingxiao’s wrist. Qingxiao picked up a stem with her chopsticks and put it in her broth.

A long, busy quiet settled over their table, broken only by the scrape of spoons and the clatter of bowls down the aisle.

Beside Mei, Iuno was eating one-handed with a deep porcelain spoon, and she was visibly suffering. Every half-minute her gaze flicked from her soup up toward the high clerestory windows, where the sky stayed a dull, unmoving pewter and the Astrolabe Core hung dead above the eaves. Her left shoulder chimed a faint, restless note against Mei’s arm as she opened her mouth, glanced across the steam at Liangyu, and shut it again.

Cartethyia, who had already finished half her bowl and a heap of turnip greens, wiped her chin with the back of her wrist and leaned earnestly across the table toward Yangyang. “Now that the rain’s stopped, shouldn’t someone go up to the Fox Sentinel’s house to see if Muyu was telling the truth about the barrier—”

“Cara,” Ciaccona said at once, dropping a spoonful of broth and a wide slice of ginger onto Cartethyia’s fish. “Try the ginger. It’s good for the chest after the sleet.”

Cartethyia blinked, ate the ginger obediently, and nodded twice. “It’s very warm! Only I was thinking, if the big bronze rings up on the roof are still stuck, maybe Nyx or I could—”

Under the table, Ciaccona’s boot connected with Cartethyia’s shin with a dull *thump*. Above the table, Ciaccona pointed the butt of her chopsticks straight at Cartethyia’s nose, her bandaged forefinger sticking out stiffly from the grip.

“Cara,” Ciaccona said, her voice dropping into the low, surgical register she used for out-of-tune lutes. “We are in Huanglong. When there is hot soup on the wood, the sky is required by law to stay outside in the rain and wait until the dishes are washed. Do not say the word *barrier*, do not say the word *fog*, and eat your fish.”

Cartethyia’s mouth rounded into a small *oh*. She glanced left and right down the pine board: Yangyang, Liangyu, Jingran, Suoming, and Qingxiao were all chewing in complete, uninterrupted agreement, not one of them looking toward the windows.

Iuno set her spoon down on the lip of her bowl and pressed two fingers briefly to the bridge of her nose.

*Barbarians, darling,* the High Priestess sighed in the back of her head. *The roof of the province is coming unglued and they are observing table manners.*

*The soup is hot,* Iuno answered silently, and picked her spoon back up.

She reached across the board for the small clay dish of salted radish at the exact moment Jingran reached for it from the other side. Iuno’s fingers and Jingran’s bamboo chopsticks stopped two inches apart over the dish.

For two seconds the middle of the table went very quiet. Jingran lifted one eyebrow, offered his crooked teahouse smile, and drew his chopsticks back an inch. “After you, Lady Priestess.”

Iuno looked from his soot-streaked cuff to the brass lamp by his elbow. “Keep it,” she said coolly.

Before the pause could stretch any further, Lupa reached between both their arms with her fingers, picked up the entire clay dish, and dumped half the radish into Chisa’s bowl and the other half into her own. Down by Mei’s elbow, Amy let out a sudden, loud snort of laughter into the quiet, half a beat too late, and immediately buried her face in her water mug up to the eyes.

It took another twenty minutes for the hall to finally settle.

One by one, the men and women from the wall finished their second and third helpings and slumped wherever they sat—against grain sacks, across rolled straw mats, or with their heads folded on their forearms down the length of the tables. By the brick hearth, Stoke, Ember, and Flare had tucked their stubby legs under their bellies, their coals banked to a sleepy, purring red.

Only the Fillet Fairmaiden was still on her feet. She stood with one hip braced against the chopping block, the long iron ladle hanging loose from her fingers, staring blankly into the scraped bottom of the second cauldron while she rubbed the small of her back with her left fist.

At the long table, Mei tapped Amy’s knee so Amy would let her up. She rose slowly, keeping one hand flat over her middle so the stitches wouldn’t pull, and walked the five paces over to the brick hob.

Tucked into the warm niche behind the chimney-breast—right where the small rice pan had sat through the night—was a deep earthenware bowl with a second bowl turned upside down over the rim to keep the steam inside.

Mei lifted the top bowl away. Underneath was a mound of hot rice, a thick slab of the golden-brown crust, and a ladle of ginger-and-carp broth she had asked Old Mother Pu to hold back before the second pot went to the bottom.

Mei set it down on the corner of the chopping block, hooked a low three-legged stool out from under the counter with her boot, and looked at the chef.

“Your turn,” Mei said, warm and quiet. “It’s still hot.”

The Fairmaiden blinked down at the rising steam, then up at Mei’s face. For once she didn’t point the ladle or bark an order. She let the iron spoon clatter onto the bricks, dropped onto the little stool with a long breath that took two inches off her shoulders, hooked her orange heels over the rung, and picked up a pair of chopsticks.

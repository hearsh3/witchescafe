# Gjöf

*a oneshot of the Banat al-Rih*

*(Better unasked than over-offered; a gift always looks for its return.)*

*]a lit window is a kind of address[*

The cold came into Stellavento on a Tuesday, which was wrong before anything else was.

For four hundred years the village had kept one season and one afternoon: the late-spring mildness of five o'clock, when the stone benches under the fuchsia trees still held the sun and nobody needed a shawl to cross the cobbles for bread. Phrolova had pitched the air herself when she built the place. She knew its temperature the way a cellist knows the thumb-position on the C string—by touch, without looking. Since the sky above the valley had turned blue, she had tolerated small liberties in the score. Triss had burned a batch of caraway rolls on the third morning and sworn at the oven in an idiom Phrolova had not written for her; the cobbler's boy had climbed the pear tree behind the smithy instead of skinning his knee by the horse-trough at quarter past four. Rubato. A living tempo breathes a little at the bar-line. She had watched from her parlor window with her arms crossed, decided not to correct it, and called the decision generosity.

She had not authorized a north wind.

It came down off the ridge at dusk with the smell of wet slate in it, stiffened the linen hanging behind the washhouse, and drove the children indoors to the public hearth in the bakehouse annex, where Granny Leah sat with a willow skep between her knees shelling dried beans with her thumbs.

Phrolova stopped in the shadow of the outer passage, her hand on the latch, because Granny Leah was talking and the rhythm was wrong.

At six-fifteen Granny Leah told the story of the fox who borrowed the bailiff's gaiters, or else the one about the three foolish millers of the high valley. Those were the two stories Granny Leah knew. Phrolova had reconstructed both of them syllable for syllable out of the sediment of her own childhood, four centuries after the crater cooled, and had listened to them some hundred and forty thousand times from the stair-foot.

"—so the woodcutter's wife looked out through the chink in the shutter," Granny Leah was saying, thumb *pop-popping* the mottled beans into her apron, "and there on the grey flagstone sat a little birch-bark punnet of wild strawberries, red as cockscombs, and the snow drifted three ells up the thatch. So what did she do, Timo?"

"Ate 'em," said Timo promptly from the fender.

Granny Leah reached out with the flat of an empty pod and flicked his ear. "Ate 'em! And you'd be gone up the chimney by Candlemas with your boots left smoking on the hearth-stone. No, she did what her mother taught her—she went straight to the dresser before the cock crew, took down her best pewter salt-cellar with the three little claw feet, and set it out on the sill before the frost took the berries."

"Why?" asked Marta, who had her chin wedged on her knees and her eyes huge in the turf-light. "They were only berries."

"Because the Wind's Daughters don't drop their marketing by accident, child," Granny Leah said, dropping her voice into the slow, creaking cellar-register that kept seven-year-olds from wandering past the mill-race after dark. "If they leave a thing on your stone and find nothing laid back when they pass by at dawn, they say to one another, *Look there, the hearth's dead, nobody's keeping the house.* And in they come to see for themselves."

Phrolova did not move her hand from the iron latch. The metal was cold enough to numb the pads of her fingers through her glove.

Where had that come from?

Not from her. She had audited the inside of her own skull for four centuries with the merciless exactitude of an archivist locked in a burned library; she knew every scrap of nursery doggerel, every proverb, every hymn fragment that had survived the red sky in her memory. She had never heard of a woodcutter's wife, or birch-bark punnets in the snow, or the Wind's Daughters. An echo cannot sing a tune the instrument never learned.

"What if I gave them my button?" Timo demanded, tugging at the horn toggle on his smock. "The loose one."

"Whatever you give them, they keep," said Granny Leah darkly, "so don't give them a button off your Sunday coat or you'll walk with one shoulder up to your ear whenever the wind's out of the trees. A clean trade on the step, and never unbar the front door to them while your hand's on the wood. Once you give them the threshold, the roof's only lent."

"What if they don't come to our house?" Marta whispered. "What if they stay in their own house?"

"Worse," said Granny Leah, and the beans stopped clicking. She looked down into her lap, where the dry shells lay piled like parings of bone. "For if they take a fancy to you, they lay an extra trencher at their board. A plate and a cup and a bench pushed back. And once it's laid, they never clear it. Summer and winter it sits there waiting, with the dust wiped off every evening at lamp-lighting, till your own two feet carry you through the trees to eat what's on it."

"I wouldn't go," Timo boasted.

"You'd go," Granny Leah said mildly, starting on the next pod. "Nobody can abide an empty plate with their name on it forever. It pulls at the ribs like an untied bootlace."

Phrolova pushed the door open.

The draft from the passage made the tallow dip gutter in its dish. The children scrambled up half an inch from the bricks, manners catching up with them late, and Granny Leah squinted up over her spectacles.

"Why, Miss Phrolova," the old woman said, entirely cheerful, entirely natural. "Come in by the turf, your hands look blue."

"Where did you hear that tale?" Phrolova asked.

Her voice was quite even. When she was nineteen she had learned to drop her larynx half a semitone before going onstage with a fever; four centuries later it meant that when she was frightened, she sounded bored.

Granny Leah blinked at her, a slow, milky confusion crinkling the corners of her eyes. "The Wind's Daughters? Why, from my grandam, when I had the winter croup and couldn't lie flat on the bolster. Don't you mind it, child? You used to pull the quilt right up over your nose when she came to the bit about the trencher."

*I had no grandam,* Phrolova thought. *My mother's mother died of the marsh-ague six years before I was born, and I never gave you a grandam when I made you.*

Her first thought—cold, immediate, automatic as a guard coming up—was the Architect.

Stories were that woman's vernacular. She and her pet playwright collected folk-tales the way apothecaries collected nightshade: boiled down, distilled to their active poison, stoppered in neat glass vials for the right doorway. If a foreign motif had crept into Stellavento's soil, someone had planted the bulb over the wall.

Yet when Phrolova let her hearing widen—past the crackle of the turf, past the four beating hearts in the little room, down into the fundamental pitch of the valley—there was no seam. She knew the Architect's work. The Architect's splices were brilliant, flawless to the naked ear, and always carried a tiny, dead flatness at the sixteenth harmonic where the borrowed frequency had been ironed to fit. Here there was only Granny Leah's old alto, slightly wheezy on the intake, humming like dry pine.

"Go on with your shelling," Phrolova said. "Keep the door shut against the draft."

She walked back across the square to her own house. The fuchsia blossoms tapped against one another over the long table with a dry, papery rattle they had never made before; the cold was stiffening their stems. Inside her parlor, with the heavy velvet curtains drawn across the side casements, she struck a sulfur match, lifted the glass chimney of the oil lamp on the front sill, and touched the flame to the wick.

She waited until the yellow tongue steadied, then took up the little brass wick-scissors from the tray and trimmed the char square, so the corners wouldn't draw unevenly and smoke the pane.

*I will leave the light on,* she had said at the lip of a torn world, to a back that was already turning toward its louder, messier attachments. *And Triss will make the stew.*

She did not expect her to come. People who required seven companions and a brass band to cross a street did not walk quiet roads alone, and if she ever did turn up at the gate, it would be three centuries late with a charmingly furrowed brow and an excuse about the weather. Phrolova lit the lamp at dusk because Phrolova, unlike the company she had lately parted from, kept her word to the letter. It was a matter of personal hygiene.

She set the glass chimney back in its brass prongs and looked out through the small square of clear pane at the empty lane. Nothing moved between the well and the boundary stone.

She left the curtain back three inches so the light had a clean line down the hill, and went in to tune her violin.

At ten she went to the little theatre at the bottom of the lane, because the house was too small when the wind was in the eaves.

She did not take a baton. She had not held one since a fool with gold eyes carried hers up out of the dark water, and her right hand still felt idiotically light at the wrist, like a fencer's after the foil snaps. The violin was older. It smelled of pond-silt and dark varnish and resin, and it did not require anyone else to follow her beat.

She stood in the center of the small raked stage with a single footlight burning in its tin hood by her shoe, tucked the chin-rest into the hollow of her collarbone, and played the D-minor chaconne she had written in the third century of the loop and never scored for paper.

The theatre had two hundred and forty seats, upholstered in faded mulberry plush. She always locked the street doors from the inside before she began. When the villagers heard her practicing, they had a habit of slipping into the back rows with their knitting and clapping at the end of every double-stop, even when she broke off mid-bar to scrape a burr off the bridge. Applause from people who loved you regardless of whether you played in tune was like warm water in a wineglass; it ruined the mouth for anything real.

She took the variations dry, without vibrato, letting the open fifths ring out into the dark cavern of the auditorium and come back off the rear plaster.

At the thirty-second bar—where the arpeggio climbs onto the E string and hangs there, exposed, over a drop of two octaves—she stopped the bow dead on the horsehair.

The hall had the wrong decay.

An empty room gives back the upper overtones with a bright, tinny slap off the wooden seat-rails, three-tenths of a second after the string speaks. A room with velvet and one living body in it drinks the third harmonic in the middle stalls.

"The doors were bolted," Phrolova said to the dark.

She did not lower the violin from her shoulder. One finger remained stopped on the fingerboard at high A.

In the fifth row, dead center on the aisle, something shifted with the soft, dry rustle of silk over plumage.

"Bolts are for bailiffs," a woman's voice said out of the gloom. It was low, unhurried, and amused, with the lazy projection of someone who knew to the inch where the focal point of an orchestra pit lay. "And you took the bariolage at a very honest pace. Most soloists scramble it to hide the bow-change."

The footlight barely reached the third row, but Phrolova didn't need light to see her. She knew the silhouette—the dark, veiled cascade falling over one shoulder, the tilt of the chin propped on two laced fingers, the long pale gleam of a bare ankle crossed at the knee.

The knowledge went through Phrolova's stomach like snowmelt.

*A winter night in the conservatory at Ragunna, two hundred and ten years ago: rain lashing the clerestory windows, seventeen people in a hall built for eight hundred, and in the stage-left loge, half behind the velvet swag, a woman in black feathers who did not clap when the piece ended and was not on the stairs when the porter locked the grille. Seventy years after that, the recital room above the salt-canals in Spargayne: one seat occupied in the back row, fifth from the aisle. Forty years after that, an abandoned garrison chapel in the border hills where Phrolova had played to the bats and four broken pews.*

Always the same woman. Never twenty minutes older. Never lifting her hands when the bow came down. Phrolova had classified her centuries ago among the minor auditory hallucinations of an immortal nervous system, like the phantom ringing in her left ear after a thunderstorm.

"You have been following me," Phrolova said.

"I have been attending," the woman corrected gently. She leaned forward into the dim amber wash of the tin lamp, her eyes catching the light—pale violet, bright and depthless as glass marbles in oil. "There is a difference. Followers want an autograph or a lock of hair. I simply like your phrasing below the staff."

Phrolova lowered the violin an inch at a time. Her mind was already working at concert speed, cold and taxonomic, laying this visitor over the only template that mattered.

*How does the Architect work?*

The Architect never wasted a costume. If the Architect came into a theatre, she came as the old luthier who had glued the crack in your soundpost when you were twelve, or the usher with the brass badge who knew your mother, or a rival violinist with a familiar squint. She wore something that had a hook in your ribs before she opened her mouth. This woman wore nobody. More than that: when Phrolova reached out with her Forte to take the stranger's pitch, her ear met something that made the skin at the back of her neck contract.

Living frequencies leaned forward into the next beat, like runners on the balls of their feet; dead ones looped in place or sagged toward the tonic. This woman's frequency ran *backward*, pouring steadily away from her into yesterday like wake behind a sternpost, so that listening to her felt like standing on a bridge and watching the river flow uphill under the arches.

Not the Architect. Something else.

"Then you came a long way for a short recital," Phrolova said. "I am done for the evening."

"Oh, a pity. Though I suppose your wrist is still tired from the coast." The woman tilted her head against the plush back of Seat 12, studying Phrolova over the bridge of her nose with the benign, devastating candor of a prima ballerina watching a corps rehearsal from the wings. "You took the second movement too fast in Rinascita, you know."

The silence in the theatre went hard as porcelain.

"Everyone does," the woman continued conversationally, "when the crowd starts screaming on the downbeat. The panic in the stalls has its own tempo—sharp, ragged, somewhere around a hundred and sixty to the minute—and if you aren't merciless with your left hand, the brass begins chasing the footfalls toward the exits and the whole middle section muddies into noise. The opening in the piazza was lovely. Very clean entry on the lower strings. After the colonnade fell, you let them rush you."

Something white-hot and utterly silent rose behind Phrolova's eyes.

Not guilt. She had no use for people who expected her to weep over Rinascita; she had gone there to break a city's spine for an arrangement that suited her, and a butcher does not write elegies for the shambles. What scalded her, so sharply that her knuckles whitened around the neck of the Guarneri, was the sheer, intolerable indecency of being *reviewed* on it.

Because she had rushed the transition after the colonnade fell.

She had known it at the time—four bars where the horned Discords had overrun the fugue subject in the smoke by the fountain and she had slashed the baton down a half-beat early to cover the smear instead of holding the rest—and she had never once thought about it since, because nobody left alive in that square had the ear to know a stretto from a slaughterhouse. Cristoforo had applauded the blood on the marble; the Architect had filed the casualties by weight. Neither of them had heard the music.

This creature had sat somewhere in the smoke and counted her bars.

"If you are looking for an encore," Phrolova said, her voice dropping to the flat, uninflected mezzo that had preceded every execution she had ever ordered, "the acoustics in here are very dry. A scream doesn't carry past the vestibule."

The woman laughed—a small, delighted sound, entirely unthreatened, as if Phrolova had made a clever remark at an intermission buffet.

"My dear, I haven't screamed since the third dynasty, and even then it was in the score." Her hand moved in her lap. Three long cards, dark-backed and rimmed in tarnished gilt, appeared between her fingers with the liquid ease of a conjurer's pass. She turned the middle one face up on the wooden armrest of the stall, looked down at it for three seconds with a faint lift of one eyebrow, and slid it back into the deck without letting Phrolova see the face. "No, I only dropped in to return your stub. One hates to leave litter in the boxes."

She stood up. She did not walk so much as uncoil, her bare feet making no sound whatever on the sloped floorboards of the aisle, and drifted back up into the deep shadow beneath the balcony overhang.

"Keep the wrist loose on the up-bow," her voice floated back from the dark. "You aren't conducting soldiers anymore. The wood likes to be asked."

Phrolova did not take her eyes off the center aisle. She waited ten seconds. Twenty. The brass latches on the double doors at the rear of the house did not click; the iron drop-bar did not rattle in its staple; no draft of cold night air came under the sill.

The hall had its empty ring back. The third harmonic bounced cleanly off the rear plaster.

Phrolova stood onstage for a full minute before she laid the bow across the music stand and walked down the three wooden steps into the stalls.

On the mulberry plush of Row E, Seat 12 lay a small rectangle of heavy rag-paper, pale buff and crisp at the perforations as if the box-office clerk had torn it from the book ten minutes ago.

*ROYAL CONSERVATORY OF SPARGAYNE — AUTUMN SERIES.*
*Admit One. Stalls, Row E, No. 12.*
*14 Oct.*

Spargayne had gone under the silt two hundred and eighty years ago when the sea-wall gave way. You could not have bought a dry sheet of its paper in any archive on the continent for its weight in gold.

Phrolova picked the ticket up between two fingers. It smelled faintly of cedarwood and old lavender.

*If they leave a thing on your stone and find nothing laid back when they pass by at dawn, they say to one another, Look there, the hearth's dead, nobody's keeping the house.*

"Superstitious drivel," Phrolova said aloud to the empty rows.

She stood in the aisle for another ten seconds, her jaw set hard enough to ache. Then she lifted her violin, turned the ebony peg of the E string backward until the pitch groaned down through two octaves and went slack, unhooked the loop from the fine-tuner at the tailpiece, and drew the silver-wound steel wire free of the nut.

It was her melody string. It had been on the instrument since before she went to Septimont.

She coiled it around three fingers into a neat, springy circle, knotted the ball-end through the loop so it wouldn't whip loose, and laid it in the exact center of Seat 12. The ticket stub she slipped into her breast pocket.

When she came back at seven the next morning with a cold iron poker in her hand and unbolted the street doors from the inside, the velvet cushion of Seat 12 had a faint circular dent in the pile, and the silver string was gone.

---

The spare E string she had in her case was old gut, over-wound with tarnished aluminum, and it spent the entire afternoon stretching. Every twenty minutes it went flat by a quarter-tone while she was in the middle of a scale, until at four o'clock she shut the violin in its case with a snap that made the windows rattle and walked down the stone steps to the dark water at the bottom of the village.

It was the only place in the Beyond that had never had a name. It wasn't a lake and it wasn't a harbor; it was simply where the stone ended and the black mirror began, forty fathoms of still, unbreathing depth where fallen fuchsia petals floated like wax without ever rotting or sinking. Not long ago she had gone into it with a thrown sword's wound in her side and let it close over her head, and at the bottom, instead of an ending, she had found her own violin resting on the silt. Since then they had observed a polite truce: she did not jump in, and the water did not move.

Today the water was moving.

A long, greasy swell was working up against the pilings of the little wooden jetty, lifting the green weed in a slow rhythm that had nothing to do with the wind off the hills. It smelled of bilge-pump, rusted iron, and very deep brine.

Phrolova stopped three paces from the edge of the planks.

Something pale and hemispherical broke the surface ten yards out—not a wave, but the translucent, faintly luminous bell of a monstrous jellyfish, trailing mauve ribbons three fathoms down into the black. Standing squarely atop the bell, dry-booted and hands on her hips as if riding a harbor ferry into Ragunna mole, was a tall woman with a long white braid, a battered pirate's tricorn pinned against the breeze, and a six-foot wooden staff across her shoulder with a heavy steel buzzsaw bolted to its head.

The jellyfish bumped gently against the jetty timbers. The woman stepped off onto the planks—her boots made a solid, carpenter's thump on the pine—and touched two fingers to the brim of her hat. The fingers were jointed brass and blackened steel, and they clicked softly at the knuckle.

"Evening," she said. Her eyes were the bright, clear red of a lantern lens wiped clean with vinegar. "Your pilings are sound. Good heart-larch. Somebody knew how to season timber before they drove 'em."

Phrolova looked from the buzzsaw to the jellyfish, which was now bobbing idly against the mooring-post like a tethered punt.

In the northern valleys above Stellavento, the country fiddlers used to carry a joint of raw mutton to the mill-fall on three consecutive Thursdays before Martinmas and drop it into the foam for the *Nøkk*, so the water-thing would come up out of the sluice and press their fingers onto the strings until the nails ran blood and they could play the dead out of their graves. Phrolova had always considered it a vulgar transaction—paying butcher's meat for technique—and she noted with cold distaste that in her case the water-thing had arrived uninvited and brought its own iron.

"The water in this basin is fresh," Phrolova said. "And closed."

"Water's never closed if it's deep enough," the woman said reasonably. "All the cellars join up if you go down past the marl."

She went down on one knee on the boards, unhooked a tarred canvas ditty-bag from her belt, and upended it on the silvered wood between them.

Metal clattered on pine. A wet, tangled heap of tarnished silver badges, each stamped with the fractured octagon of the Fractsidus and threaded on rotted black silk cord, spilled across the cracks of the jetty.

Phrolova did not move a muscle of her face.

The woman began sorting them out with her mechanical fingers, lining them up in neat, square ranks of five along the grain of the plank and knocking a strand of brown bladderwrack off a clasp with her thumb. She worked with the brisk, unsentimental care of an engineer laying out cotter-pins on a bench.

"Twenty-two," she said, setting the last two at the end of the fifth row. "Snagged in the deep weed down by the lower intake. Rusting the water. The silver's low-grade—too much copper in the alloy, goes green in a fortnight if you leave it in the wet—and the pins were all sprung open."

Phrolova looked down at the twenty-two badges.

She knew every single one of them. Not by the serial numbers stamped on the reverse, and certainly not by the names their mothers had given them before they put on the masks; she had never bothered to ask an acolyte's name in all her centuries of service. She knew them by the frequencies their ribs had made when her baton cut through them on the stairs of this very valley, the day she tore her own curtain down.

The third one from the left in the top row—with the bent corner—belonged to the thin baritone on the third landing, always half a beat ahead of her stick, who had stepped out of the guard-alcove with his short-blade up for her and whom she had opened from the left collarbone to the fourth rib without breaking stride, because he was cluttering the tempo. The seventh was the contralto who had carried her spare rosins in Ragunna and said *Yes, Lady Phrolova* half a beat before every sentence was finished. The nineteenth, with the deep diagonal score across the octagon, was the boy by the gate who had died believing she was going to catch him when the stone gave way.

*They believed in me,* she had said then, and stepped over them.

"I was down off the Septimont cape last month," the woman in the tricorn remarked, taking a small oiled rag from her coat pocket and wiping salt-scale off the face of the twentieth badge before setting it back in line. "Looking for a brass sextant I dropped overboard forty years back. Never did find it—current sets hard round that headland." She gave the last badge a little tap to square it with its neighbor. "The ones the Tide pulled through when your rift tore open at the camp. They came down very tired. Took a long while settling on the shelf."

She didn't look up as she said it. There was no edge in her voice—no theatrical tremor, no priest's cadence waiting for the sinner to drop to her knees. She might have been reporting a fouled anchor-chain to a harbor master.

Inside Phrolova, the silence was cold, flat, and completely clear.

She was not going to apologize. Not to this drowned tinker, not to the water, not to the twenty-two pieces of base silver drying on her planks. She knew what she had done on the stairs, and she knew what she had done to the girl's frequency at the rift to pay off her last debt to the Fractsidus, and she knew she had stood outside the door afterward for six hours holding thirty thousand screaming dissonances off the backs of people who would never thank her for it. If these creatures had come up out of the dark expecting her to beat her breast and beg for absolution, they had misread the key signature by four sharps. She did not counterfeit remorse she could not vouch for.

"They were in my way," Phrolova said. "And the ones at the cape were in a war. Did you swim all this way to audit my books, or are you expecting salvage money?"

The woman looked up then. Her red eyes blinked once under the shadow of the hat-brim, genuinely puzzled, as if Phrolova had answered a remark about the tide-tables in high church Latin.

"Salvage?" she said. "They're yours. Had your mark on 'em, and they were fouling the weed. You don't leave scrap iron in clean water if you know whose shed it came out of."

She rose, dusting the knees of her breeches with a slap of her canvas bag, and waited—not expectantly, just standing there with the patience of a pier-head.

*Whatever you give them, they keep.*

Phrolova looked at the four rows of five and the two at the end.

She stooped, picked up the seventh badge—the contralto's, the one with the nick on the bevel where it had hit the stone step—and slipped it into her coat pocket beside the conservatory ticket. Twenty-one left on the boards. Never leave an even tally for someone who counts by fives.

Then she pinched the left cuff of her dark kidskin glove, unbuttoned the small pearl stud at the wrist, and drew it off finger by finger. She did the same with the right.

She had worn those gloves in the piazza at Rinascita, and on the stairs, and at the rift when she put her palm against a one-armed priestess's spine to keep the dark from eating her alive. The leather was supple, molded to the exact arch of her knuckles, and smelled faintly of rosin and rain.

She dropped them onto the plank beside the woman's boot.

The woman bent, picked them up, and turned them over in her hands. Without ceremony she pulled the right glove on over her brass-and-steel fingers, working the kidskin down over the mechanical joints one by one, and flexed her hand. The internal ratchets gave a soft, dry *whirr-click* inside the leather.

"Good stitching," she said approvingly, pulling on the left. "Ragunnan calfskin? Takes the damp better than lamb."

She stepped backward onto the jellyfish's bell. The creature didn't bob under her weight; it simply let go of the surface tension and sank straight down through the black water like a plumb-lead, the white braid and the rusted buzzsaw and the battered tricorn going dimmer and greener by the fathom until the last glint of the red eyes went out and two small bubbles broke against the piling.

Phrolova stood bare-handed in the cold wind for a minute, looking down at the twenty-one badges on the wood. Then she turned on her heel and walked back up the steps without touching them.

She did not sleep. At first light she went back down the steps to the water, telling herself it was to sweep the jetty.

The swell had gone. The black mirror lay as still as it had lain for four hundred years, the fallen petals floating on it like wax, and on the silvered planks the badges sat where the woman in the tricorn had squared them, filmed with a fine grey frost.

Phrolova counted them by fives, because that was how they had been left.

Twenty.

The gap was at the head of the top row, third from the left: the one with the bent corner. In its place a dark oval of wet stained the pine, as if something had stood dripping there a long while, and from it a line of wet footprints went up the stone steps toward the village, one tread at a time, unhurried, the toes turned a little in.

*If they leave a thing on your stone.* She had left twenty-one things on a stone. She had walked away from them bare-handed and called it contempt.

She followed the prints up the steps, across the square, and in at Triss's kitchen door.

He was sitting at the scrubbed deal table by the bread-oven with a bowl of porridge in front of him and Timo and Marta on the bench opposite, watching him eat with the solemn, unblinking scrutiny of seven-year-olds. He wore the dark, high-collared coat of a Fractsidus initiate, and he was wet through—not the damp of a man caught in a shower, but the black, heavy saturation of wool that has lain a month at the bottom of cold water. A dark ring had spread across the deal around his forearm. Under the bench the flagstones were puddled, and the hem of his coat ticked steadily onto the stone: *drop, drop, rest, drop.* The badge with the bent corner was pinned crooked at his collar.

They could see him. When a living woman had walked down this lane, the village had looked through her like window-glass, at first. Marta was passing this one the honey with both hands.

At the sound of the latch he looked up, and by reflex he shoved the bench back and stood, his right hand rising toward his breastbone in the lower-terrace salute.

"Overseer—"

There was sea-water in the back of his throat; the word came out with a small, loose rattle, like shingle drawing back down a beach. He blinked at the bread-oven, at the children, at the honey, and lowered his hand slowly to his seam.

"Miss," he corrected himself. "Pardon."

Phrolova did not move from the doorway.

She knew him. Not by his face—the lower ranks wore masks in the corridors, and she had never found a reason to look at them with the masks off—but by the thin baritone humming raggedly under his wet ribs, a little sharp in the upper register because he breathed too shallowly, half a beat ahead of her stick. The cloth of his coat was whole, but the water knew where the stroke had gone: a diagonal ribbon of blacker wet ran from the hollow of his left collarbone to the fourth rib, and would not drain.

"Sit down, boy," Triss said, coming in from the yard with an armful of split beech and tapping his shoulder with a billet as she passed. "Nobody stands in my kitchen unless they're carving. He came up from the water at cock-crow, Miss, blue as a mussel. I wasn't going to leave him on the step."

He sat, looking apologetic about the puddle.

"How did you find the way?" Phrolova asked. Her voice was level, pitched to carry the length of the table and no further.

"There wasn't much of one, Miss." His fingers closed around the horn spoon for something to hold. "Only it's dark all the way down, and we knocked a good while at the bottom and nobody answered. And then there was a bit of my own kit lying out on the boards up top, and the window past it." He nodded through the kitchen wall, in the direction of her house. "It's a good clear light. You can see it a long way off."

He took a mouthful of porridge, swallowed carefully, and looked down at the bowl with a shy, bewildered relief, as if he had been afraid he might have forgotten what food was for.

She knew the exact vibration of his ribcage when the string took it. She knew the key he had died in. She had no idea what he had been called by a mother.

"Your name," she said.

He looked up, surprised to be asked. "Luc, Miss. Third Choir, under—" He caught himself, glanced at Marta's plaits, and amended it. "From down the coast. Near the salt-pans."

"Luc." She tried the syllable against the thin baritone. It fit without a burr.

He did not accuse her. He did not look at her long enough to accuse her of anything. He ate his porridge and let Timo explain the rules of a game that involved the honey-spoon, and when the water under the bench crept toward Marta's shoe he drew his heels back under the plank with a murmured pardon, trying to take up less of the stone.

That was worse. She had spent the night preparing for a great many things coming up out of that water. She had not prepared for good manners.

Triss set a second bowl at the end of the table and nudged the spoon toward Phrolova's hand. Phrolova did not pull out the bench. She took the bowl, stood with it by the door until the porridge had skinned over, and set it down untouched.

On her way back across the square she did not go down to the water to gather up the other twenty. She thought about it the whole length of the lane.

---

It snowed that afternoon.

It began an hour before sundown—not sleet or mountain hail, which Phrolova had seen often enough on the high passes, but huge, dry, goose-feather flakes dropping straight down out of a leaden sky without a breath of wind to slant them.

From behind her parlor curtain Phrolova watched the village lose its mind over it.

The baker came out into the square in his shirt-sleeves with flour up to his elbows and stood by the dry fountain with his face tipped back, catching flakes on his beard. The music teacher forgot his four-thirty counterpoint class entirely and stood on the church steps holding his black umbrella shut under his arm while Timo and Marta scraped the first thin white powder off the horse-trough to pack it into a grey ball the size of a walnut. Even Triss, who had brought the Thursday stew-crock across the lane ten minutes earlier and left it on Phrolova's side table wrapped in three thicknesses of flannel, lingered by the garden gate with her palm held out flat, staring at the white stars melting on her chapped skin as if someone had handed her a diamond. On the bench outside the bakehouse Luc sat with his wet cuffs on his knees and his face turned up like the rest, and the flakes that landed on him went out like sparks before they touched.

None of them should have known what to do with snow. Stellavento had lain in a warm southern basin; frost had come twice a decade and gone by nine in the morning. They had no memory of it because Phrolova had no memory of it here, and yet there they stood in the gathering dusk, improvising a winter they had never lived through, while the white covered the cobbles and muffled every footstep into a soft, padded rest.

The whole score was modulating out of her reach, bar by bar, into a key she hadn't written, and she was the only thing left in the valley still beating four-four time in an empty room.

She let the velvet curtain fall back into place and struck a match to light the front lamp.

The sulfur flared blue, then yellow. She touched it to the square-trimmed wick, lowered the glass chimney into its prongs, and reached for the brass screw to turn the flame up.

Three knocks sounded on the front door.

Light. Unhurried. *Rap-rap-rap* on the seasoned oak, six feet behind her back, at the exact hour when the barley in the hallway crock thickened and the windowpane went dark enough to mirror the room.

Phrolova's fingers stopped on the brass screw.

For two full seconds she did not breathe. The parlor went so still she could hear the oil creeping up the woven cotton of the wick.

*The front door. Thursday. The hour of the stew.*

She never thought about that evening. She had built centuries of walls around it, bricked it up behind Rinascita and the hundred and eighty-nine failed cadences and the cold arithmetic of her ledger, and when the memory tried the latch at three in the morning she drove it back with a tuning fork and a page of six-part fugue.

Now the smell of mutton broth and dried thyme came in from the hallway, and the floor fell out from under her boots.

She had been sitting in this chair—this exact horsehair chair by the cold hearth—at the end of four hundred years of empty halls. Outside, the echoes had been looping one of their clumsy early drafts of the day, bumping into doorframes when their pathing slipped and repeating *Good morning, fine weather for the hay* into the dark at nine at night until she wanted to take a hammer to her own ears. And then the front knocker had fallen twice, and when she walked down the passage and pulled the bolt back, Triss had been standing on the outer flagstone with the steam of a stoneware tureen rising between them and her linen apron knotted twice on the left hip—the way Triss's chilblained fingers always pulled the left bow crooked—and a grey smudge of beech-ash under her chin.

Not an echo. Not a Discord stitched together out of whistling wind and dead pitch. Warm. A pulse beating eighty-two to the minute in the hollow of her throat, smelling of mutton tallow, woodsmoke, and rosemary soap.

Phrolova had stood on the threshold with her mouth open, four centuries of aristocratic iron vanishing out of her spine like water through a sieve, and before the figure on the step had said three words she had gone down on her knees on the cold stone with her arms locked around that coarse wool waist and her face buried in the apron. She had wept the way children weep when they have been lost in a market all afternoon—ugly, hiccuping, humiliating—and poured out every small, hoarded, wretched sentence she had kept behind her teeth since the day she crawled out of the red crater and found the road gone. That the G-sharp on the parish harmonium was still a quarter-tone flat. That she had kept the bread-ovens hot. That she hadn't meant to take the encore in the capital when the tour ran over by two days. That she was so tired of conducting her own ghosts she could taste brass on her tongue when she woke.

And the hands in her hair—rough at the thumb from the paring-knife, warm all the way through—had held her head against the apron and said, *Hush now, my lamb, hush now, come in and eat while the fat's clear.*

Five hours. From dusk until the tall clock on the landing struck twelve.

At the twelfth stroke the woman across the hearth had set her teacup down in its saucer, stood up, smoothed the crooked knot of the apron with two fingers that no longer had chilblains, and let the face go.

She hadn't gloated. That was the worst of it. The Grand Architect had simply looked down at her from a stranger's quiet eyes, laid the Fractsidus charter beside the empty stew-bowl, and said, *Your story is over, and you are still here. Sign at the bottom.*

And Phrolova—with her face still stiff with dried salt and her ribs still warm from being held by a dead woman's hands—had picked up the pen and signed.

She hated the Architect with a clean, mineral hatred that would outlast the sun. She knew every bolt and pulley of the fraud: how the creature had walked Stellavento invisible for three days beforehand, measuring the stacked cordwood, learning the dead girl by her leavings, putting on the grief like a borrowed coat to buy an Overseer cheap.

And it remained the single best evening of four hundred years.

In all the centuries since, in her most vicious, unsparing three-o'clock reckonings, Phrolova had never once been able to wish the knock hadn't come. She would have paid the centuries of blood again for the five hours between the threshold and the clock. She despised herself for that—with a sick, bottomless contempt—more than she despised herself for Rinascita, or the boys on the stairs, or the rift.

The knock came again. *Rap-rap.* Polite, patient, right through the oak.

Phrolova took her hand off the lamp-screw.

She knew what was on the other side of that wood. Whoever was hunting her—whether the Architect had found a crack in the valley's seal, or these witches had spent two days taking her range—had finished tuning the instruments. First the theatre, to test her pride; then the water, to test her conscience, and a drowned boy in Triss's kitchen with his manners; and now the front door at the hour of the stew, wearing the one face she could not shut out. Triss with her apron crooked. Or her mother with her hair pinned up for church. Or a traveler with gold eyes and snow on her shoulders, saying she'd come back as promised.

Phrolova walked down the narrow hallway. Her spine was an iron rod; her bare hands hung loose at her sides; her Forte was drawn back to a razor's edge behind her teeth, ready to tear the frequencies out of whatever stood on that stone before it could speak a single syllable.

She gripped the iron latch, lifted it, and pulled the door wide.

A complete stranger stood on the step.

She was nineteen or twenty, small-shouldered, wrapped in a plain dove-grey wool shawl over a dark dress, with long pale-ashy hair falling loose past her collarbones and a round wicker basket hooked over one forearm.

Not Triss. Not her mother. Not anyone Phrolova had ever seen in her life or her death.

The relief that went through Phrolova's chest was so violent, so staggeringly sharp, that for half a beat the porch roof swam against the snow—and she hated herself for the relief, too, instantaneously, so that when she spoke her voice could have scored glass.

"What."

"Good evening!" the girl said cheerfully.

She didn't flinch at the tone, and she didn't lean forward into the light. She stood squarely in the center of the outer flagstone, her neat leather ankle-boots planted two full inches behind the worn rain-groove of the sill. Though the snow was coming down thick behind her, turning the garden gate and the fuchsia branches white, there was not a single flake on her grey shawl, nor a damp spot on the hem of her skirt.

"Did the snow come on time?" she asked, tilting her head with the bright, busy satisfaction of a post-rider checking a timetable. "We weren't entirely sure about the elevation on this side of the ridge. Cold fronts are terrible for dawdling in the passes."

Phrolova stared at her. "Who sent you?"

"Oh, nobody sent me, it was simply my turn to walk," the girl said, and peered pleasantly past Phrolova's shoulder down the hall—without moving her toes a fraction of an inch nearer the threshold. She gave a small, appreciative sniff. "That's the Thursday one, isn't it? With the pearl barley and the marrow-bone. Ours is lentils tonight, because Herta forgot to soak the beans on Tuesday and wouldn't admit it until four o'clock."

She reached into the wicker basket on her arm. Inside, laid out in a neat circle on broad, dark-green leaves, were half a dozen plump purple figs, their skins split just slightly with sugar. As the girl lifted three of them out on their leaves, a faint, unmistakable warmth rose off the fruit into the freezing air—the dry, honeyed heat of a south-facing brick wall at three o'clock on an August afternoon.

She bent and set the three figs down on the stone lintel at the side of the doorpost, outside the jamb.

*Never let them in by the front door,* Granny Leah had said.

Phrolova looked down at the girl's dry boots, planted with scrupulous precision behind the crack of the sill, and felt the fine hair rise along her forearms. The girl wasn't waiting to be invited in. She was keeping the rule from the other side of the threshold.

"Your lamp is very bright from down the hill," the girl remarked, straightening up and brushing a speck of fig-leaf dust from her fingers. "You can see it all the way from the boundary stone. It's a good thing you trimmed the corner—square wicks carry much further in the fog." She glanced down the lane toward the bakehouse, where the wet print of a boot on the step was slowly filling with snow. "And somebody's come up for his badge, I see. They generally do, if you leave them out."

"Take your fruit," Phrolova said, very quiet, "and get off my step."

"They're very good figs," the girl said mildly, not offended in the least. "Though they don't keep past a day once they're off the leaf. Oh—and supper's at seven tomorrow. If you follow the track past the third Solistree, where the pale roots cross the ditch, just knock on the bark where the door isn't. We've laid a place for you."

The cold air from the garden slipped past Phrolova's ankles into the hall.

"And if I don't come?" Phrolova asked.

The girl smiled. It was a completely ordinary, sunny, slightly self-satisfied smile—the smile of a clever apprentice who has delivered her parcel to the right number and is looking forward to her tea.

"Then it'll be there!" she said. "We don't clear a place once it's laid. It's no trouble at all—the table is much too long for the four of us anyway, and the dust doesn't settle much in the winter."

Phrolova looked straight into her eyes.

Four hundred years she had waited in an empty house with the places set and the lamps trimmed for someone who had promised to return. Four hundred years of Thursday stew going cold in the crock, of tuning an instrument at dusk for an audience that had forgotten the address.

She searched the girl's pleasant, upturned face for the tell—the microscopic twitch at the corner of the mouth, the cold gleam behind the iris that would prove she knew what *an empty place left waiting* meant to the woman standing in this doorway.

There was nothing there. Either this rosy-cheeked creature in a grey shawl was a monster of calculation beside whom the Grand Architect was a village mummer, or she had just driven a spike through Phrolova's hands out of sheer, breezy domestic habit and had no idea she'd drawn blood.

Phrolova could not tell which. And the not-telling terrified her more than the knock had.

"Good night, then!" the girl said, giving a little nod. "Mind the draft—your hall's losing heat."

She stepped backward off the flagstone, turned at the garden gate, and walked out into the curtain of falling snow toward the bottom of the lane.

Phrolova stood in the open doorway until the grey shawl vanished behind the churchyard wall. Then she lifted the lamp from the window-ledge and held it out over the sill.

The fresh snow lay two inches deep across the garden path, the gate-stone, and the cobbles of the lane beyond. It was smooth as bolted flour. Not a single footprint led up to her step, and not a single footprint led away.

Only the three purple figs sat steaming faintly on their green leaves against the frost.

Phrolova stood with the lamp in her hand for three slow bars of four. Then she set the lamp back in the window, walked down the hall to the side table, and lifted the flannel off Triss's stoneware crock.

She took a blue-glazed earthenware bowl from the dresser, ladled it full to the brim with hot mutton broth, barley, and two pieces of the marrow-meat, and carried it back to the front door. She picked up the three figs by their leaves with her left hand, set the steaming bowl down on the outer flagstone in the exact spot where they had lain, and shut and bolted the door.

She left the figs on the hall table without touching them to her mouth.

When she unbolted the door at daybreak, the snow on the step had frozen to a hard sugar-crust, the three figs on the hall table had dried to brittle brown husks, and the blue earthenware bowl sat on the outer stone—scrubbed clean with sand, bone-dry, and turned neatly upside down.

---

At eight o'clock Granny Leah's carriage came round the corner by the bakehouse, and the iron tires did not make their usual groan on the cobbles because somebody had greased the hubs with fresh tallow.

Phrolova saw it from her bedroom window while she was pinning her collar.

For four hundred years that carriage—an old-fashioned, high-sprung berline with faded plum leather curtains and brass carriage-lamps—had been backed out of the stable yard at eight-fifteen sharp while the stable-lad buckled the traces on the two bays and Granny Leah fussed over a lap-rug on the step, preparing for a drive over the pass that always reset to the stable yard before the church clock struck nine.

Today the bays had frost-calks screwed into their shoes, their breath smoking white in the crisp morning sun. Strapped to the fore-boot under the driver's box was a large, square wicker hamper with a white linen cloth tucked under the lid. Lashed to the iron luggage-rack at the rear with new hemp cord was Phrolova's own brass-bound touring trunk—which had stood under the eaves of her attic since the day she built the house—and resting on the rear bench cushion inside the open door was the black leather case of her Guarneri.

When Phrolova walked out through her front gate without a coat, the entire village was standing in the square.

Not in their Thursday places. The cobbler had left his last; the baker had his Sunday muffler wound twice around his neck over his apron; the children were lined up along the horse-trough with their hands tucked into their armpits against the frost; the music teacher stood by the mounting-block with his pince-nez pinched onto his nose and a roll of manuscript paper sticking out of his pocket.

Phrolova stopped dead in the middle of the lane.

She knew this formation. She hadn't staged it in the Beyond—she had let the loop ready the carriage every morning for four hundred years and never once let it leave, never let the village come out to the well to see it go—and yet she knew where every shoe stood on the stones.

It was Tuesday the eleventh of May, four hundred and twelve years ago. The morning Stellavento had come out to the well to see its twenty-year-old prodigy off on her first solo tour of the northern conservatories.

The last time this carriage had rolled out past the smithy, she had leaned out the window to wave at Triss by the bakehouse door, and when she came back up the valley road three weeks later the sky was the color of clotted blood and there was nothing between the hills but black glass and a hole in the earth that smelled of sulfur and roasted lime.

*Did they do this?* she thought, her heart slamming once, hard, against her ribs. *Did that little witch with the figs wind them up like a musical box before she went down the hill?*

She looked across the square—at Triss wiping her hands on her apron, at Granny Leah patting the bay mare's neck, at the cobbler's boy blowing on his knuckles—and listened with everything she had.

There was no foreign thread in them. No compulsion, no borrowed pitch, no strings leading into the trees. Since the sky had turned blue, the village had stopped looping one Thursday, and like water finding its old bed after a dam breaks, it had simply rolled back to finish the last sentence it had ever spoken to her aloud.

"Take that trunk down," Phrolova said.

Her voice cut across the cold square like a whip-crack. The chatter by the horse-trough stopped.

"Do you hear me?" she said to the stable-lad on the box. "Unlash the trunk and carry my case back into the parlor. I am not going on tour."

The stable-lad looked down at her from under his wool cap, blinked pleasantly, and checked the buckle on the off-side rein. "Road over the saddle's bound to be greasy once the sun gets on the rime, Miss Phrolova," he said. "Best make the crest before noon or we'll be leading 'em through the drift."

"You are dead," Phrolova said.

She took two steps toward the carriage, her bare hands clenched at her sides, and let her voice drop into the cold, stripped register that had made grown men in the Fractsidus cross the street rather than pass her on a stair.

"All of you. You have been dead for four hundred years. There is no conservatory over the pass, there is no autumn series, and there is nothing outside that stone at the end of the lane but rot and snow. You are echoes in a box I built because I couldn't bear the quiet, and if you do not take that trunk off the rack this instant I will put the sky back the way it was and set you back to five o'clock until the stones crumble."

Nobody wept. Nobody flinched or covered their ears or dissolved into static.

Granny Leah clicked her tongue at the bay mare and tucked the corner of the wool lap-rug more firmly over the floor-brick inside the cab. "You'll want the hot brick under your heels till you pass the timber-line, child," she said, straightening her back with a small grunt. "And don't let those town women talk you into thin slippers before Easter."

Triss walked up to the open carriage door, carrying a small stoneware jar stoppered with red sealing-wax, and wedged it into the corner of the wicker hamper beside a crusty loaf. She tucked the white linen cloth down tight around the bread so the wind wouldn't dry it, then turned and looked Phrolova straight in the face.

She was four inches shorter than Phrolova. There were two grey hairs in her left eyebrow that Phrolova didn't remember putting there, and her hands smelled of caraway and lye.

"Go on now," Triss said quietly. "The bread's still warm. And I'll keep the pot on for whoever else comes up from the water. That boy says there's more on the way."

Phrolova's throat locked.

Triss had never once spoken of anyone coming. In four hundred years of script she had never had a guest to expect; the long table had been laid for the same mouths since the day Phrolova built it. It was a promise made in the future tense, handed across the cobbles without a single string tied to the handle.

At the back of the carriage the leather luggage-straps groaned.

Luc stood behind the boot with his sodden black cuffs turned back twice from his wrists. The trunk had slipped an inch on the iron rack; he put his shoulder under the brass corner, heaved it square, and drew the tongue of the buckle home through the third hole.

Phrolova walked around the wheel and stopped three feet from him. The cold salt smell of the bottom of the sea came off his coat in the morning sun. The blacker wet across his collarbone was level with her chin.

"You will put the lamp out," she said to him, very low, because cruelty was the last coin she had in her pocket, "before the wheels clear the boundary stone."

Luc tested the strap with his thumb, then stepped back from the axle and wiped his wet palms on his thighs.

"No, Miss," he said plainly. "I'll light it at dusk. I can trim it well enough if you leave the little scissors on the tray."

She stared at him. "Why."

"There's twenty more of us down on your jetty." He met her eyes once—not warm, not forgiving, with the clear, flat steadiness of a clerk reading out the bottom line of a ledger that would never balance in this world or the next. "Don't mistake me, Miss. It isn't for you. You cut me down on the stair when I had my blade up for you, and I expect I'll mind that as long as I'm here. Only it's dark at the bottom, and somebody ought to be home when they come up the hill."

He held his right hand out between them, palm up. The skin was pale and creased from the salt water.

*Nobody can abide an empty plate with their name on it forever,* Granny Leah had said by the turf. *It pulls at the ribs.*

If she stayed here—if she dragged her trunk back into the parlor and bolted the oak door—that plate would sit on a table under the northern trees tonight at seven. And tomorrow night at seven. And next winter, and the century after that, wiped clean of dust every evening by women who didn't even have the grace to threaten her, waiting with the terrible, unhurried patience she herself had practiced for four hundred years.

She could not leave a table waiting like that. She knew what it weighed from the other side. Whether the witches knew it too—whether they had chosen the one trap in the world built to her exact measurements, or whether they were simply creatures of the deep snow who set a place because that was what their house did—she could not tell from here.

Very well. She would not be handled twice. She would not sit in her parlor waiting for a fourth knock, and she would not crawl into anyone's debt on her knees. She would go there in broad daylight, on her own two feet, look these women in the eye across their own board, find the hook hidden behind their charity, and pull it out of the wall.

She reached into the pocket of her skirt, took out the small wooden box of matches, and laid it on Luc's wet palm. She was careful not to touch his skin.

Phrolova walked to the carriage, put one boot on the iron folding-step, and got in.

She sat on the rear bench beside her violin case, her spine straight, not touching the leather squabs.

The music teacher stepped up to the window, adjusted his pince-nez, and rapped the wooden sill twice with his knuckles—*one-two*, seventy-six to the minute, the old rehearsal tempo.

"Keep your right elbow up in the sixteenth-note passages, Phrolova," he said severely. "And do not let the municipal conductors rush your second movement. They have no ear for architecture."

The stable-lad whistled to the bays. The traces tightened with a creak of cold leather, the iron rims bit into the frosted cobbles, and the carriage lurched forward down the lane.

Phrolova did not look back at the bakehouse door. She looked straight ahead through the front glass as the children ran pelting alongside the wheels, their breath puffing white in the sun, laughing and throwing handfuls of powder off the stone wall until they reached the boundary marker at the bottom of the hill—where the road had ended in grey nothing for four hundred years, and today went straight on between two dark walls of pine.

At dusk the carriage stopped.

There had been no pass, no post-houses, and no noon. For an hour the pines outside the window had grown taller and paler, their needles shedding in a fine silver sleet until the evergreens gave way altogether to colossal, smooth-barked trunks the color of bleached bone, rising out of deep snow into a canopy that glowed with its own faint, cold amber light.

The wheels ceased turning without a jolt. No brakes scraped; no harness-bells shook.

Phrolova picked up her violin case by the handle, pushed the door open, and stepped down.

Her boot sank to the ankle in dry, powder-blue snow. The cold here was nothing like the crisp autumn draft in Stellavento; it fell straight down out of a vast northern sky already pricked with hard, indifferent stars, so sharp it made the inside of her nose ache on the first breath.

She turned to tell the driver to unstrap her trunk.

There was no driver. There was no carriage, no pair of bays, no smell of horse-sweat or tallow, and when she looked back down the narrow forest track between the enormous pale trees, the snow lay smooth and untrampled for half a mile without a wheel-rut or a hoof-print in the drift.

Beside her boots, sitting side by side on the snow as neatly as if a porter had just set them down on a railway platform, were her brass-bound trunk and Triss's wicker hamper. Resting on the flat brass hasp of the trunk were the three purple figs on their green leaves—plump, split with sugar, and warm enough to melt three tiny wells into the rime on the metal.

Phrolova looked at them for a beat. Then she left the figs where they were, gripped the leather strap of her trunk in her right hand and her violin case in her left, and looked up the track.

First Solistree, twenty paces ahead, its vast white bole wider than the church in Stellavento. Second Solistree, where a frozen brook crossed under a crust of grey ice.

And forty yards beyond that, the third: an ancient monster of a tree whose pale, glowing roots arched up out of the snowbank like the flying buttresses of a cathedral, enclosing a hollow of smooth, silver-white bark six feet high and three feet wide.

There was no door, no iron latch, no hinge, and no step.

Phrolova set her trunk down at the edge of the root-arch, shook the snow from her hem, and rapped three times with her bare knuckles on the flat bark.

The bark swung inward on silent, well-oiled hinges.

Warm yellow lamplight spilled out across her boots, carrying the smell of toasted cumin, old beeswax, mutton fat, and too many leather-bound books kept too close to a stove.

In the opening stood a girl who looked fourteen and carried herself like a university rector interrupted during a senate vote. She wore a black dress cut with severe, grown-up ambition, and a witch's hat with a brim so absurdly wide she had to angle her chin to clear the doorframe; there was a smear of violet ink across the heel of her left thumb.

She was the fourth one—the one who hadn't come to Stellavento—and it was immediately clear she was bristling about having been left behind to mind the kettle. She looked Phrolova up and down from hatless head to snow-crusted boots, drew in her breath with the crisp authority of an examiner opening a viva, and said:

"So you're the Overseer who—"

From directly behind her in the warm passage, the girl in the dove-grey shawl slipped past carrying a wooden bread-trencher and trod, cleanly and with practiced precision, squarely on the instep of the smaller witch's boot.

"Ow!" The hat jerked back two inches. She whipped her head around, eyes narrowing to furious slits. "I was establishing context! That was a legitimate taxonomic question—"

"The soup's getting a skin on it, Herta," the girl in the shawl said cheerfully over her shoulder, "and you left your quartos on the butter-dish again."

Herta glared at her retreating back, muttered something under her breath about provincial methodology, and stepped back from the threshold with a stiff, regal wave of her ink-stained hand, leaving the doorway clear.

Phrolova did not move for two seconds.

*Once you give them the threshold,* Granny Leah had said, *the roof's only lent.*

That had been from the other side. Now she stood on the snow with the warm air of their house washing against her face, and nobody was reaching out to pull her wrist, and nobody was wearing a dead friend's apron.

She picked up her trunk and her violin case, stepped over the root-sill on her own two feet, and shut the bark door behind her.

The room she walked into was long, low-beamed, and built into the living cradle of the tree-roots, where stone met timber without mortar. Phrolova stopped three paces inside and took the room's measure the way she had taken every hall she had ever walked into—sightlines, exits, acoustics, the pitch of the silence.

Every single one of them was there, and not one of them was looking at her with an agenda.

By the broad stone hearth sat the tall woman from the water, her tricorn hat hung on a peg by the bellows and her long white braid draped over her shoulder. Her six-foot buzzsaw staff was propped in the iron bracket among the fire-pokers and ash-shovels like a common kitchen implement, and she was calmly paring a wedge of hard yellow cheese with a pocket-knife while wearing Phrolova's Ragunnan kidskin gloves.

Ten feet above the floorboards, where the pale roots crossed the rafters, the woman from the theatre hung upside down in a sling of crimson silk ropes, one knee hooked over a bough and her dark hair brushing the air above the sideboard. She was dealing her tarnished-gilt cards onto the flat of a roof-beam, upside down, humming three bars of Phrolova's own D-minor chaconne—at the exact tempo Phrolova had taken it on Tuesday night—before pocketing the card she'd turned and swinging down to land barefoot and silent on the rug.

At the far end of the room, where a flight of narrow stone steps wound down into the rock beneath the roots, a young woman with a Guild scribe's badge pinned to her lapel and a quill tucked behind her ear paused on the top stair, gave Phrolova the brief, ink-distracted nod one gives a fellow lodger in a boarding-house corridor, and went back down with a fresh bottle of gall-ink.

And on the high stone mantel above the fire, set squarely between a brass astrolabe and a stoneware jar of dried rosemary, sat Triss's blue earthenware bowl and the coiled silver ring of a violin's E string.

*Whatever you give them, they keep.*

"Set your box by the settle," said the woman in the gloves, pointing the tip of her cheese-knife toward the end of the long oak table. "Draft comes under the west root if you leave leather by the door."

Phrolova walked to the table.

At the far end from the fire, a place was laid. It had clearly been laid for days; the linen napkin beside it had the sharp, flat crease of cloth that had sat under a heavy knife since Monday. Unlike the chipped earthenware trenchers and horn-handled spoons at the other four places, this place was set with a heavy plate and a wide, shallow teacup of sea-green porcelain, the glaze crazed into a thousand fine silver webs with age—taken, though nobody said so, from a cupboard much older than the house.

Phrolova did not sit down. She stood behind the high-backed chair, rested her bare hands on the top rail, and waited for the downbeat.

She had been in rooms like this before. The polite supper before the terms were read; the tea poured while the contract dried. Now would come the speech: the balance of the world, the war in the north, what an ex-Overseer owed for her sanctuary, or what use they had for a woman who could conduct thirty thousand dead frequencies without losing her pitch.

The woman in the grey shawl ladled lentil soup into five bowls. Herta climbed onto two thick volumes of nautical law to reach her soup-spoon without acknowledging that she had done so, dragged her stack of manuscripts three inches to the left of the butter, and reached for the salt. The woman from the theatre took her seat, unfolded her napkin across her lap, and looked mildly at the bread.

Nobody spoke to Phrolova. Nobody looked at her chair. They simply began to eat.

Three bars of silence passed. Four. Five. The fire snapped in the chimney; a spoon clinked against sea-green porcelain as the girl in the shawl pushed the salt-cellar an inch nearer Phrolova's hand.

Phrolova's fingers tightened on the oak rail until the knuckles went white.

"What do you want from me?" she asked.

Her voice was completely level—the quiet, lethal mezzo that used to drop the temperature in the Fractsidus council-room by ten degrees.

Four spoons stopped halfway to four mouths.

They looked at her. And then, in the silence of the warm room, the four witches looked at one another across the table.

Phrolova had watched liars for four hundred years. She knew the micro-rhythms of a rehearsed evasion: the shared glance of conspirators checking whose turn it is to deliver the line, the smooth, greased transition into the prepared lie.

There was no line.

It was unmistakable, humiliatingly plain: *not one of them had an answer ready.*

Herta opened her mouth to improvise a theoretical justification, realized she didn't have a citation for it, and shut it again with a small, annoyed click of her teeth. The girl in the grey shawl blinked over her spoon as if Phrolova had asked why the floor was at the bottom of the room. The woman from the theatre tilted her head against her hand, her violet eyes bright with genuine, lazy curiosity to see what anyone would come up with.

The Architect had always had the answer ready before her knuckles left the door. This—this unscripted, slightly awkward domestic blankness—shook Phrolova more deeply than the ticket stub, the badges, or the knock in the snow.

The tall woman by the hearth chewed her bread, swallowed, and pointed her mechanical thumb at the tureen.

"Supper," she said. "Lentils go mealy if they sit."

Phrolova stood behind the chair for another five seconds, searching four faces for the hook, the wire, the trapdoor under the rug.

Then she pulled the chair back, sat down, and picked up her spoon. She tasted the soup once—too much cumin, slightly under-salted—and ate three mouthfuls so the plate could not be called untouched. Nobody watched her eat, and nobody commented when she laid the spoon down across the rim.

After the plates were stacked in the wash-tub, the woman in the gloves took a horn lantern from the hearth-hook and tilted her chin toward the cellar stair.

"Come down a minute," she said. "You haven't met the landlady."

Phrolova followed her down the narrow spiral of cut stone. The air grew cooler with every turn, losing the smell of cumin and dry paper and taking on the deep, mineral stillness of wet limestone and old water.

At the foot of the stairs the rock opened into a broad cavern beneath the roots of the Solistrees. On the left, at a small sloped desk lit by a shaded tallow dip, the young scribe sat scratching away across a folio sheet in a strange, tide-marked alphabet, her left hand resting on a stack of blotters; she did not look up as their boots passed.

Ahead lay the pool.

It was thirty paces across, black and still as obsidian in its stone basin, with slow, pulsing veins of amber light running deep under the surface like embers under dark glass. Overhead, just below the pale root-vault of the ceiling, a vast, silent gyre of frequencies turned inward upon itself, patient as water circling a drain.

And under the dark surface, resting on a shelf of drowned stone two fathoms down, lay a woman.

Her dark hair fanned out across the rock in the amber glow; her pale hands lay open and empty at her sides; her eyes were closed. She did not wake, she did not stir, and no bubble rose from her lips.

The tall witch set the lantern down on a flat boulder ten paces back and stayed there, leaving Phrolova to walk to the stone lip alone.

Phrolova looked down into the water, and let her hearing go down into it after her gaze.

She knew the dead in every key the world owned. She knew the thin, saw-toothed whine of fresh Tacet Discords clawing at their own throats; she knew the hollow, resolved fifths of Stellavento's loops; she knew the thirty thousand unsynchronized heartbeats she had held back at the threshold of the Septimont cathedral while the sea came apart.

What rose off this water was none of those.

It was vast, and it was ancient—ten millennia of dark tide folded down into the breadth of a narrow bed—and underneath the terrifying weight of that depth, where the root chord should have been cold as the abyssal trench, there was a single, steady overtone that struck Phrolova squarely in the breastbone.

It was warm. Unhurried, defenseless, and foolishly, catastrophically kind—the exact pitch of a sunny square in spring before the sky fell, or of a morning light someone had once walked through a ruin wearing, before Phrolova knew enough to lock her doors.

Phrolova took half a step backward from the rim before her face could change. Her bare right hand closed into a fist against her skirt.

*She's asleep,* Phrolova thought, staring down at the pale hands under the glass-dark water. *The thing that built this house isn't even awake to ask for my signature.*

"How long?" Phrolova asked, not turning around.

"Hundred years, give or take a winter," the witch with the lantern said behind her. "Since the usurper took the lower room."

"And she told you to bring me here?"

"She hasn't told anybody anything since the snow of 'forty-two," the witch said dryly. "Except whatever Alice gets down in the margins, and half of that's weather reports from three thousand years ago. Come on up. It's damp by the stone if you haven't got your coat."

They went back up the spiral stair without another word.

In the upper hall the table had been cleared—all five places now, the sea-green plate and cup washed and set on the dresser rack beside the everyday earthenware, the debt of the waiting trencher quietly retired without a drum-roll.

Herta was waiting at the foot of the attic ladder with a stack of coarse, lavender-dried linen sheets in her arms that came nearly to her chin. She marched Phrolova down a narrow timbered passage under the eaves and kicked open the last door on the left with her boot-toe.

It was a small, square room built into the fork of two great pale roots, with a narrow casement window looking out into the snow-laden branches, a cord-strung bedstead, a washstand with an iron ewer, and a brass candlestick on a deal table. Her touring trunk and her violin case already sat neatly at the foot of the bed, and Triss's wicker hamper rested on the window-seat.

Herta dumped the sheets onto the mattress, adjusted her enormous hat with two fingers, and turned in the doorway with the brisk air of a provost reading out the house regulations.

"The privy's out the back door past the woodshed, and don't leave the latch off or the foxes get in," she said. "The kettle takes twenty minutes on the side hob because the flue draws crooked when the wind's north, though Viviane claims it's an intentional baffle. And which hour do you want kept for you?"

Phrolova, standing by the washstand, looked at her. "What?"

"Your hour," Herta said impatiently, as if explaining basic arithmetic to a slow first-year. "When the door stays shut and nobody knocks unless the roof's on fire. Nobody else in this house has ever wanted one—Viviane hammers whenever she pleases, and when Elaina got here last month she said she'd rather have the whole house than an hour of it, which is exactly the sort of thing Elaina says. So you'd be the first, and you'd better choose sensibly, because it goes in the household book."

*Elaina.*

The girl with the figs. Herta had dropped the name into the middle of a complaint about breakfast hours the way you drop a spoon into a drawer, as if a witch's name weren't a loaded pistol.

Phrolova looked past her shoulder at the small casement window, where the blue northern twilight was deepening into black between the glowing pale boughs.

Three hundred miles south, and inward across a threshold no map marked, the cold dusk had just reached the square of Stellavento. The bakehouse ovens were banked; the children were inside; and in a parlor with velvet curtains, Luc wiped his wet hands on the lining of his coat, struck one of her matches on the side of the box, and touched the flame to the square-trimmed wick. Down at the bottom of the village, on the frosted planks of the jetty, there were nineteen badges where there had been twenty.

"The one after dusk," Phrolova said.

Herta gave a single, businesslike nod, filing it away. "Done. Don't practice scales after ten, though. I'm cross-referencing."

She pulled the door shut behind her. There was an iron bolt on the inside of the wood.

Phrolova did not slide the bolt. If a door in a witch's house wanted to open, three inches of wrought iron wasn't going to argue with it.

She did not light the candle, and she did not take off her dress. She waited until the house settled—until Herta's footsteps retreated down the passage, until the murmur of voices by the hearth below died down to the occasional crack of a pine-knot, until the frost began ticking softly against the glass of the casement.

Then she went down on her knees on the floorboards beside her trunk, threw back the brass clasps, and took inventory.

She could guess the end of Granny Leah's tale, the part the old woman hadn't reached before Phrolova pushed the bakehouse door open; tales of that kind only ever end one way. The Wind's Daughters never keep only what you lay on the stone for them. The salt-cellar, the E string, the gloves, the bowl of broth—those are only the earnest-money to keep them on the step. When you finally walk through the trees and sit at their board, they take the one thing in your pack you can spare least, and they take it so cleanly you never feel the pocket lighten until you reach for it in the dark.

Methodically, without haste, Phrolova lifted everything she owned out of the trunk and laid it in rows across the counterpane in the white moonlight from the snow.

Sixteen bound folios of manuscript scores—she riffled the pages of the seventh to check the loose leaf at the adagio; it was there. Two cakes of dark amber rosin in their wooden boxes. Her spare tailpiece and the little bone tuning-fork that gave A at four hundred and fifteen vibrations.

She unlatched the violin case. The Guarneri lay on its crushed green velvet bed, all four strings taut across the bridge, the scroll undamaged, the two bows clipped into their frogs in the lid.

She stood and emptied her pockets onto the quilt beside the scores.

One folded linen handkerchief. One paper ticket stub for the Royal Conservatory of Spargayne, Row E, Seat 12, dated the fourteenth of October. One tarnished silver Fractsidus badge with a small nick on the bevel and a sprung pin on the back.

She checked the inside of her own chest next, where the frequencies lived: the dry, calcified architecture of four hundred years of grief, the cold rage she kept honed for the Architect, the bitter certainty that the fool with gold eyes would one day be careless once too often in the loud world and turn up at the gate after dark. All present. Every wall load-bearing, every grievance in tune.

Nothing was missing. Not a thread, not a bar-line, not a single cold stone of who she was.

Phrolova sat down on the edge of the mattress in the moonlight, her bare hands resting on her knees between the violin and the badge, and looked at the row on the quilt.

Everything was there.

She reached out, touched the scroll of the violin with one finger, and started the count again from the left.

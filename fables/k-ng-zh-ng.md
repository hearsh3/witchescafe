Chapter 1: Kōng Zhǒng

The rain over Xianling Village did not come down in squalls like the storm on the upper peak; it fell straight and grey through the windless dusk, soaking into the terraced clay of the hillside until every wheel-rut ran the color of rust.

Above the last tier of thatched roofs and bean-drying racks, where the quarry track narrowed into steps of rough fieldstone, the village burial ground sat among a grove of old persimmon trees. None of the fruit had been picked before the fog came down from the gorge. A few black, frost-split persimmons still hung from the bare switches overhead, swelling with rainwater until their skins gave way and dropped with a soft, heavy *plop* into the winter weeds below. Along the lower terraces, in front of the round stone family mounds, the shallow earthenware saucers were brimming with brown water, their offerings of yellow spirit-money beaten down into sodden pulp that clung to the clay rims like dead leaves.

Jingran stood at the northern edge of the highest terrace, where the cut stone ended in a bank of loose shale and broom-grass.

He had no hat and no umbrella. His dark coat was soaked black across the shoulders and clung heavy around his calves, the hem caked with red mud from the switchbacks. The rain had washed the last of the condenser-soot out of his cuffs and plastered his loose hair against his collarbones, running in a slow, cold trickle down the bridge of his nose.

At his right boot sat the Life Lamp. Behind the wire-mended crack in the upper glass pane, the pale flame was banked down to the size of a single rice-grain, giving off no light beyond its own tarnished brass ring and hissing softly whenever a fat drop from the persimmon bough above struck the warm vent-cap. Two paces away, under the deep, dry overhang of a tilted boundary slab, Yuebing sat folded into a neat grey loaf with his front paws tucked out of sight under his breast, his yellow eyes half-shut against the mist.

In front of Jingran’s toes lay two graves.

They were not mounded up like the village tombs down the slope; no pine box had ever been lowered into them to push the earth above the level of the turf. They were only two sunken, rectangular troughs in the red clay, choked with winter crabgrass and wet plantain leaves. The one on the right was five feet long and reasonably straight. The one on the left, separated from it by a narrow spine of undisturbed dirt, was barely four feet from head to heel, its western edge bowed crookedly inward where a shelf of blue river-shale had turned the digging iron aside years ago.

At the head of each depression stood a rough triangle of grey roof-slate wedged upright in the mud. Neither stone carried a name or a date. Near the top of the taller slate, three shallow, jagged white scars crossed the grain where a narrow chisel held in small, blistered fists had skidded off the wet rock before the boy threw the iron into the weeds.

Down in the valley trough below the trees, a square of yellow oil-light came on behind a paper window-frame in the village, then a second, their reflections quivering across the flooded cabbage plots. High overhead, buried out of sight in the wet wool of the cloud-bank, hung the sheared curtain wall of the Hold and the dark, unmoving shell of the Astrolabe Core.

Jingran’s hands hung loose at his sides for a long time.

When a cold drop ran inside his collar down the middle of his back, he stirred once, reaching inside the breast of his soaked coat—past the damp bundle of manuscript pages he hadn’t inked a word on since the third night of the siege—and drew out a small square of oiled butcher’s paper.

Inside was a cheap sesame-and-date pastry from the ferry-landing stall at the foot of the mountain, bought four mornings back before the road closed. The rain had worked its way into the crease of the paper; the flaky outer crust had gone grey and soft as wet paste.

Jingran broke the pastry in two with his fingers, pinched a dry crumb of dark red date-filling out of the very center, and crouched down by the boundary stone. He held the bit of paste under the dry granite lip on the tip of his knuckle. Yuebing sniffed once at his wet skin, took the sweet neatly with his front teeth, and tucked his chin back down onto his chest.

Jingran straightened up and laid the rest of the paper-wrapped cake flat on top of the taller slate.

The hickory handle of the borrowed mattock had been four inches taller than the crown of his head, slick with clay and the blood from the torn skin at the base of his thumbs, and when the iron bit had rung dead against the blue shale at the bottom of the second hole, the man sitting on the boundary wall behind him hadn’t even looked up from peeling a roasted chestnut with his fingernail. *Dig your own first if you’re in such a hurry to kill a man before your back teeth are in, brat. It saves the village the price of a spade.*

Rain dripped from the tip of Jingran’s chin onto the wet grass.

Six winters ago a Sonoro seam had folded in on itself over the reed-marshes west of the lower river, and when the water settled back over the mud-flats by morning, there hadn’t been a wine-gourd or a scrap of hem left behind to put under a stone.

Jingran reached into the watch-pocket of his inner vest and took out a small, dented brass pipe-tamper, its copper hanging-ring worn thin as a hair on one side from forty years on a belt-cord. He turned the cold brass once between his thumb and forefinger. Then he dropped to one knee between the two sunken troughs, pushed two fingers three inches deep into the heavy red clay at the head of the shorter, crooked grave, and buried the brass tamper down against the shale root.

He pulled a clump of wet moss over the hole, pressed it flat with the heel of his palm, and stood up in the rain.

***

Down the stepped mud path that wound up from the village threshing-floor, an oil-paper umbrella came round the corner of the terrace wall.

Suoming did not hurry on the slick stone risers. She took them one step at a time in her heavy boots, her faded plum-colored coat dark around the hem from the puddles and the long bamboo shaft of the umbrella held vertical against her shoulder.

She did not walk out to the shale lip where the two slates stood.

Twenty paces back down the middle terrace, where the oldest graves sat sunk to their shoulders in black moss and wild bracken, she stopped between two overgrown rows.

Before her boots stood a low, round-topped granite tablet that had split diagonally across its face in some winter frost long ago and been mended with a heavy iron cramp-bar. Rust had bled from the iron down the center of the stone, and the carved characters on either side of the seam had been washed so shallow by two centuries of mountain rain that only a thumb could have found the strokes. In the cracked clay dish at its base, clear rainwater stood level with the rim and overflowed in a thin, silent thread into the moss.

Suoming stood under the round brown canopy of the umbrella with the rain drumming a steady, hollow patter on the taut oiled paper, looking down at the mended stone.

Out on the edge of the terrace, Jingran did not turn his head. He stayed where he was with his mud-stained right hand resting against his thigh, watching the mist creep up through the bare branches of the persimmon trees while a third window went yellow down in the village.

A night-heron croaked once down in the flooded bamboo by the stream.

“The bank’s washed out behind that fern,” Jingran said without looking around. “If you stand in the rut much longer, the mud’s going to come over your boot-tops.”

Suoming stayed by the iron-cramped tablet for another three breaths. Then she turned, stepped over a sunken coping-stone, and walked across the wet turf until she stopped an arm’s length from his left elbow.

Together they looked down at the two shallow troughs in the crabgrass.

“The blind swordsman from Mingting left at the noon bell,” Jingran said. His voice was low and plain in the drizzle, stripped of its teahouse drawl and a little rough at the edges from the damp. “Took the broken kernel and the corpse from the drafting room down the river road in a leaded box.”

Suoming looked at the taller piece of roof-slate, where the wet sesame cake was slowly sliding off the oiled butcher’s paper.

“Good,” she said. “The Hold has enough rot in the ground.”

A thin, continuous stream of water ran off one steel-tipped bamboo rib of her umbrella, drilling a neat round hole in the red clay between their boots.

Jingran shifted his weight, his wet soles squelching in the grass, and looked down at the shorter, crooked depression on the left.

“I was ten,” he said. “I hit the blue shale four spans down and chipped the corner off the iron. Look at the size of it. If I lay down in that ditch tonight, my knees would stick out into the turnip patch.”

A small, crooked crease pulled at one corner of his mouth and vanished before the rain could run into it.

Suoming looked at the four-foot hollow in the weeds, and then at the fresh smear of red clay across the knuckles of his right hand.

“Your teacher was a fool,” she said dryly. “A boy that size should have been splitting kindling.”

“He didn’t feel like digging it himself,” Jingran said. “His left knee always stiffened up before a frost.”

He raised his hand and rubbed his thumb once across his wet right brow, where the hair-thin seam of white-gold in his iris stayed dull and quiet under his lashes, seeing nothing in the wet clay at his feet except earth and weed-roots.

For a long while there was only the rain. The rain beat loud and tight on the oiled paper at his shoulder, softer in the persimmon switches overhead, and gave a single tiny *tchk* against the brass cap of the Life Lamp by his ankle.

“Old Pu’s nephew brought the mule-cart back from the Hold before dark,” Suoming said, her gaze resting on the faint yellow squares of the village windows down the slope. “They’re clearing the upper runoff ditch as soon as the creek drops. If those stones are sticking out into the barrow-track, the boys will heave them into the retaining wall.”

Jingran looked down at the two triangles of grey slate.

He lifted his right boot, set the wet sole flat against the face of the taller marker, and shoved once. The slate tore loose from its shallow socket in the clay and fell forward into the crabgrass with a dull, wet thud, tipping the sodden sesame cake into the mud. Then he stepped half a pace to the left and pushed the second slate down beside it so both lay flat as paving stones in the weeds.

He did not kick dirt into the hollows, and he did not look down at them again. He bent his knees, picked up the Life Lamp by its tarnished brass ring, and gave a single low whistle between his teeth.

Under the boundary slab, Yuebing stood up, stretched his forelegs until his claws clicked on the dry granite, and bounded straight across the wet grass into the front of Jingran’s coat above the second button, tucking his chin under Jingran’s collarbone so only the tip of one grey ear showed against the wet wool.

Jingran glanced once up the dark slope of the mountain—past the dripping pines to where the low cloud-belly hung black and silent over the unseen roofs of the Hold.

“She’s down at the third drying shed by the lower weir,” Jingran said.

“I know where the weir is,” Suoming said.

She did not turn toward the steps right away. She looked at the water running in dark ribbons off the hem of his coat and dripping from the loose tail of his hair-string onto his wrist.

Then she shifted her grip on the bamboo shaft of her umbrella and held her short arm out sideways toward him at full stretch.

The steel-tipped rim of the oiled canopy came squarely up to the middle of Jingran’s chest.

Jingran looked down at the round brown roof hovering at the height of his fourth rib, and then at the crown of Suoming’s head beside his elbow.

“Take the stick,” she said.

“If I raise it to my head, the wind blows the rain straight onto your neck,” Jingran said.

“Then bend your knees.”

Jingran let out a slow breath through his nose, bent his knees four inches, and hunched his shoulders down until his chin was nearly level with the wooden runner under the bamboo ribs. Even stooped at that angle, half of his right shoulder stayed out in the steady drip from the persimmon boughs, and the left curve of the canopy poured a thin trickle of runoff straight onto Suoming’s plum-colored sleeve.

They turned together on the narrow mud track and walked down the slick stone steps toward the village lamps, the tall man crouched crookedly under the low umbrella with the brass lamp swinging dark by his shin.

***

Half a mile below the Hold’s western water-gate, where the quarry road dropped into the river gorge by the old timber weir, the third lacquer-drying shed sat dark behind a stack of split pine cordwood.

Rain hammered loud on the cracked tiles of the low roof and ran in sheets down the oiled paper of the barred river-shutters. Inside, the narrow room smelled sharply of raw tung oil, wet pine sawdust, and cold brick.

A single covered horn-lantern sat on an upturned tub in the back corner behind the empty drying racks. By its small yellow smear of light, two people sat on low three-legged joiner’s stools over an iron pan of dead charcoal.

Master Cen, who had cast the bronze valve-collars for the third-deck conduits, kept his chapped hands shoved deep into the opposite sleeves of his padded jacket; his left eyelid twitched every time a gust off the river rattled the shutter-hook. Across the dead brazier, a younger woman with green lens-polishing rouge ground permanently into the creases of her thumbs held a square, oilcloth-wrapped bundle flat against her stomach with both arms.

“The first watch passed an hour ago,” the woman whispered, her eyes fixed on the bolted cedar door. “If the boatman from the Jianshui wharves was coming up the towpath, he would have been at the lock before the water rose.”

“Shut your mouth and watch the latch,” Cen muttered, his wooden soles tapping the packed dirt floor. “The Helmsmaster’s own thumb-mark is on the passes. He wouldn’t leave the deep-manifold charts sitting in a drying shed with the Hold Wardens poking their pikes into every hay-barn on the hill.”

Outside on the river steps, loose shale shifted once under a boot heel.

Something hard tapped against the lower iron strap-hinge of the door: two quick strokes, a pause, and a third that dragged sideways across the wet cedar.

Both of them stiffened on their stools. Cen reached down beside the cold charcoal pan for a heavy iron furnace-poker, stood up, and slid the wooden bolt back two inches against its staple.

“The water is high at the third weir,” Cen said through the crack, his voice thin over the roar of the spillway outside. “Who poles the barge?”

From the dark under the dripping eaves came a woman’s voice—low, hurried, and pitched in the clipped, theatrical cadence of the old Jianshui guild-halls.

“A blind heron,” the voice answered promptly, “carrying three dry reeds in its beak.”

Cen shot a glance back at the woman on the stool. She stood up, keeping the oilcloth bundle pressed tight against her apron bib.

“And where does the heron nest when the kiln goes cold?” she asked the door.

“Under the ninth rafter, where the white fox cannot reach,” the voice outside replied, and through the two-inch gap in the timber, slim fingers in a wet leather half-glove held up the jagged left half of a cast-bronze locust token.

Cen let out a long, whistling breath through his nose and dragged the wooden bolt all the way back.

Yinlin stepped inside out of the rain and pushed the door shut behind her with her heel.

She wore a coarse grey oilskin cloak hooded low over her brow, the hem black with river mud, and held her shoulders rounded forward like a wharf-runner who had come three miles on her hands and knees under the wall patrols.

“You’re two nights late!” Cen hissed, pressing his own half of the bronze locust against the break in hers for half a heartbeat before shoving the metal back into his sash. “The whole sky over the Sanctum went black on the fourth evening, and the garrison has had spears across the ferry landing since dawn! Where is the boat? Did the Helmsmaster send the silver for the Mengzhou passage?”

Yinlin raised one hand to her throat and pushed the wet oilskin hood back off her red hair.

“Let me see the dry-seal on the paper first,” she said, the wharf-runner’s stoop melting out of her spine as she held out a dry palm toward the younger woman. “I don’t hand out river-silver for blank wrapping cloth.”

The woman hesitated for half a breath, then pulled the hemp cord loose on the oilcloth bundle and folded back the upper corner so the horn-lantern caught the thick mulberry sheets inside—columns of tight, slanted brushwork and small fox-paw marks inked beside the margins.

“Every sheet from the lower sluice-house is in here,” the woman said quickly. “Give us the barge passes.”

Yinlin looked down at the slanted handwriting on the top leaf.

“Oh, that is his hand,” she said, and every trace of the archaic guild-hall theater dropped out of her voice at once, leaving it cool, clear, and conversational. “What a tidy little carpenter.”

Cen’s mouth fell open; his right arm jerked upward with the heavy iron furnace-poker—

Yinlin barely turned her wrist.

Four hair-thin threads of violet-white lightning snapped from the silver rings on her fingers, caught the iron tie-rod across the rafters overhead, and dropped straight down around Cen’s raised forearm and the younger woman’s elbows. At the same instant, Zapstring slid out from behind her mud-stained cloak hem, its jointed brass legs clicking once on the dirt floor as it planted itself between their boots and pulled the circuit taut.

There was no shout and no crash. A brief, ozone-sharp hum filled the small shed; the iron poker dropped into a heap of pine shavings with a soft *thump*, and both artisans sat straight back down on their wooden stools with their wrists bound neatly behind the cross-rungs and their teeth locked shut by the singing purple thread.

Yinlin stepped between their knees and lifted the oilcloth bundle from the woman’s lap.

She did not leaf through the manuscripts. She folded the oilcloth flap back over the paper, pulled the hemp cord tight with one brisk tug, and tucked the square package under her left arm.

On the stool to her right, the younger woman’s eyes were wide and wet in the lantern light, her thin shoulders shivering hard against the static lock and the river draft.

Yinlin looked down at her for a beat. Then she reached over to the drying rack with her free hand, pulled down a heavy oiled-canvas work apron, and draped it over the woman’s shoulders so the wind from the shutter-crack wouldn’t hit her neck.

“Sit still until the morning watch comes down the towpath,” Yinlin told them evenly. “The Xuanfang garrison has had a long week, and they’re feeding prisoners hot carp broth up at the branch hall. It’s a much better bargain than you were going to get at the bottom of that weir.”

She leaned over the tub, blew out the horn-lantern so only the tiny violet lens in Zapstring’s head lit the sawdust by their boots, and stepped back out into the rain.

Outside, the narrow cobbled lane between the drying shed and the river-wall was black and running with water.

Ten paces down the alley, where a low stone culvert arched over the spillway runoff, Suoming stood alone under her open oil-paper umbrella.

Yinlin walked down the wet cobbles, her cloak drawn loose over the oilcloth bundle under her arm, and stopped under the dry lip of the shed eaves three feet from the umbrella’s steel-tipped rim.

“Two?” Suoming asked.

“A brass-founder and a girl who polishes lenses,” Yinlin said, shaking a drop of rainwater from her cuff. “Your Watchers can pick them up when the water-gate opens. The courier they were waiting for went through the lower weir three days ago with his throat cut—Muyu was sweeping his own cellar before your people ever hit the stairs.”

Suoming looked at the square oilcloth bundle under Yinlin’s arm.

“And the charts?”

Yinlin slipped two fingers inside the folded seam of the oilcloth, drew out a single narrow booklet stitched with blue cotton thread, and held it out across the drip between the eaves and the umbrella.

“The deep-fissure soundings under the gorge are in this one,” Yinlin said quietly. “The rest comes back to Jinzhou with me.”

Suoming took the blue-stitched booklet and slid it inside the breast of her faded plum coat.

She looked once down the dark, rain-swept towpath toward the lower village, where the last yellow window-lamps had gone out behind the persimmon trees.

“Your cat-herder finally came down off the hill?” Yinlin asked, one eyebrow lifting slightly in the gloom.

“He went to dry his boots,” Suoming said.

“Good. Tell him half of Jinzhou is still waiting on his next chapter.”

Yinlin touched two fingers lightly to her brow, stepped backward into the shadow of the stacked cordwood, and with a single quiet click of brass joints from the shed behind her, the faint violet glow under the door-sill went dark.

Suoming stood alone by the culvert for the space of a breath as the river roared white over the weir stones below. Then she shifted the bamboo shaft of the umbrella onto her shoulder, letting the rain drum flat and steady against the oiled paper behind her head, and walked back up the stone steps toward the Hold.

# The Garden Door

Sanhua found them arguing over the last mangosteen.

Changli had opened it and placed the white segments on Jinhsi's plate. Jinhsi had transferred half of them back. The fruit had since moved twice, each time while one of them was answering the kitchen steward, and now a single segment lay between their plates on the clean edge of a leaf.

“Sanhua,” Jinhsi said, looking up. “Please take that before the Counselor finds another reason I should eat it.”

Sanhua set the returned invitation on the side table and took the fruit.

“The peel should yield a little when you press it,” she said after tasting it. “I can show the kitchen buyer. These were picked early.”

Changli regarded the bowl. “I went to considerable trouble to obtain them.”

“The seller appears to have understood that.”

Jinhsi laughed with her hand over her mouth. It hurt the skin beneath her eyes, which was still tender from the morning, and she kept laughing for a moment after Changli's eyebrows rose.

Sanhua waited beside the side table. When Jinhsi lowered her hand, she offered Panhua's answer. There would be room the following evening. The household had accepted. Panhua wanted a count before the fish arrived.

“Include yourself,” Jinhsi said. “You liked the back room.”

“I will arrange the evening watch.”

She took the invitation downstairs again.

By the time she returned, Changli had gone to meet Wen. The fruit bowl was empty. Jinhsi stood in the passage with the physician's discarded wrapping in her hands, smoothing a crease out of it.

“The dressing is on,” she said. “She let me tie it.”

Sanhua looked at the paper. “The fastening holds better if you leave room for the arm to bend. I can check it when she comes back.”

Jinhsi folded the wrapping into a small square.

“I keep thinking of something else I want to ask her.”

From the courtyard came the rattle of a ladder being carried across the paving. Jinhsi turned her head sharply. The men carrying it slowed at the corner, negotiated the turn, and went on toward the gutter beneath her window.

She watched until the last rung had passed.

“I would like to see the garden room,” she said. “The Gaos are coming tomorrow morning.”

Sanhua opened the gallery door for her.

They walked past the work room. Through the open panels Jinhsi could hear Changli asking Wen to read a sentence again. The familiar voice drew her toward the threshold. She had thought, over lunch, that she could sit beside it all afternoon. Now she remembered a page of the chronology and felt her fingers tighten around the little square of paper.

Sanhua stopped when she did.

Jinhsi looked down at the wrapping. A red thread from the dressing had caught in one fold.

“Please walk beside me,” she said. “I keep turning to speak to you.”

Sanhua came forward. Their sleeves brushed as they passed through the narrower doorway.

***

The garden room smelled of damp wood and the soap used on the floor.

Someone had set four chairs around the table. Through the outer door, a short path led beneath a tree whose lower branches had been tied up to clear the eaves. Drops gathered along the binding and fell onto the same dark patch of stone.

Jinhsi went to the chair facing the door. She sat, looked toward the path, then rose and moved to the next one.

Sanhua stood inside the entrance while she did it.

“His mother should have this place,” Jinhsi said. “She can see the garden from here.”

She drew the chair back a little farther. Its rear leg caught in a gap between two boards. Sanhua crossed the room and lifted it clear.

“The table can come toward the window. That will give her space behind it.”

They moved it together. Jinhsi held one end with the wrapping still folded against her palm. Afterward she set the little square on the windowsill and began arranging the chairs again.

Sanhua waited until she had placed the fourth.

“Where would you like me tomorrow?”

Jinhsi looked at the entrance, where her bodyguard usually stood. There was a shallow mark in the wood at shoulder height, left by a handle striking it whenever the door swung fully open.

“Take this chair beside me. I would like you to hear what they say.”

Sanhua drew out the fourth chair and sat down to test the space. Her sword touched the table leg. She adjusted its angle with one hand, then rested both hands on her knees.

Jinhsi sat beside her.

The room had already begun to feel occupied.

For a while they watched water collect on the branch binding. A drop grew heavy, stretched, and fell. The next began forming in the same place.

“When did you learn about the dates?” Jinhsi asked.

Sanhua turned toward her.

“When Wen brought the revised access instructions that evening. I asked why the custody of the records had changed. He explained the alteration and the hearing.”

Jinhsi folded her hands on the table. The question had waited in her mouth since lunch. She felt ashamed of having asked it, then irritated by the shame.

“You carried those packets to me.”

“I carried them under the Counselor's seal. The delivery books have my signature.” Sanhua paused. “I thought about those journeys when Wen told me. I can remember putting several of the packets into your hands.”

Jinhsi rubbed the place where the thread had pressed into her palm.

“I find myself looking at everyone who brings something through the door. I try to remember what they might have known when they handed me the last thing.”

Sanhua's gaze moved toward the gallery. A clerk was approaching with a tray of clean cups. He saw them through the doorway, inclined his head, and carried the tray into the room farther along.

“Would it help if I told you who gave me each packet?” she asked. “I could give you the name when I hand it over.”

“For a while, perhaps.” Jinhsi looked at her. “I would like to be able to stop needing that.”

“Then tell me when it becomes tiresome. It is easy enough to do.”

Jinhsi's hands loosened against each other.

The rain began again, quietly. A thin line of water ran down the outside of the doorframe. Sanhua reached toward the nearest chair, where a cushion lay close to the wet edge, and moved it onto the table.

“How was it here?” Jinhsi asked. “While we were north.”

Sanhua sat back.

“The courtyard kept changing. People would come to the stair and find an older building across it. One of the porters remembered a passage that used to lead through the west wall. He tried to take a basket of dressings through it. I walked with him along the present route until he could follow it himself.”

Her thumb moved against her forefinger, following the width of a bead.

“The children at the lower shelter had learned the number of steps to the yard. They counted them aloud whenever they came up. I borrowed their count once.”

Jinhsi turned toward her more fully.

“The rain reached your sight too?”

“It added another movement beneath things. I could follow the soldiers on the stair, and then their earlier footsteps would rise through them. At the receiver it was worse. Everyone was standing still, and the frequencies kept moving.”

She paused, looking down at the table's worn edge.

“I put my hand on the frame while I listened. The wood stayed where it belonged.”

Jinhsi remembered her own hand around a speaking horn, and the sudden pressure at her waist when Chixia struck her. She had gone into the mud carrying half a breath she had meant to use for an address.

“What did you hear when the charge went off?”

Sanhua's fingers settled flat on her knees.

“I heard the blast, and then someone called for a stretcher. Amy asked for names and positions, and a man kept trying to answer while she spoke. I asked the operator to clear a channel to you.”

A small white flake settled on the dark tabletop between them.

Sanhua watched it melt.

“Chixia answered first. She said you were with her. I could hear how hard she was breathing.”

Jinhsi felt the scarf under her hands again, folded too thick at one end. She had pressed harder there, afraid to lift it and find how much blood had gathered beneath it.

“She was explaining the attack to me,” she said. “She kept trying to sit up so she could point.”

“That sounds like her.” Sanhua's mouth softened faintly. “She gave the operator her location twice. Then she asked him to tell the kitchen the meal cart would need the other road.”

Jinhsi closed her eyes. She could picture Chixia remembering the cart while somebody tightened a bandage around her calf, annoyed by the prospect of lunch becoming another casualty of the road.

When she opened them, Sanhua was looking at the place where the snowflake had been.

“I wanted to go to you,” Sanhua said. “I went as far as the outer stair. Then the next group arrived from the shelter, and I took them through the gallery.”

“You were helping the people here get through it.”

“I knew each person I was handing them to. I had checked the rooms myself. I still spent the rest of the afternoon thinking about how long the north road would take.”

Jinhsi touched the damp spot on the table with her fingertip.

She had placed Sanhua beside a door in her mind and left her there while the rest of them went north. When she imagined City Hall holding through the day, her bodyguard had stood in the same place, ready for the next instruction. Now there was an afternoon inside that picture: a hand on the receiver frame, footsteps toward the stair, children arriving at the wrong moment and being taken carefully to their rooms.

“I wish I had spoken to you for longer,” she said. “When Chixia handed me the receiver, I gave you the position and the field return. I could have told you what my hands were doing.”

Sanhua looked up.

“I remember your voice. I went back to that part often enough to wear it out.”

Another drop fell from the branch. Jinhsi reached across the space between their chairs and rested her hand over Sanhua's.

Sanhua's fingers curled beneath it, slowly enough that Jinhsi could feel her deciding how firmly to hold on.

“How do I look to you today?” Jinhsi asked.

Sanhua studied her. “Your eyes are sore. The light from the window has been troubling you since we sat down. I can move the screen.”

“I was thinking about your sight. What you see when you look at me.”

Sanhua's thumb came to rest against the side of Jinhsi's hand.

“Your frequency is clear. I can follow the light around your hands, and the movement of your face. It settles my eyes.”

Jinhsi looked at their hands together. Sanhua's skin was cool against hers. She had watched the change in her bodyguard for years: the small easing of her shoulders on entering a room, her gaze coming to rest, the steadier breath after she found Jinhsi among a crowd.

“You can still see me that clearly, after everything?”

“I saw it when you came through the gate. There were so many people on the road that I had to find you by it.”

Jinhsi's fingers tightened.

“I have been trying to be careful with you,” she said. “I know what it gives you. Sometimes I am sitting at my desk with someone speaking to me, and I want them to leave so badly that I can feel it in my teeth. You come into the room, and I try to put that away.”

Sanhua's eyes remained on her face.

“This morning, before Changli arrived, I thought about sending for you. I wanted to say something cruel about her. I had the whole sentence ready. Then I heard you speaking to the watch officer outside, and I sat there until the wish passed.”

A drop struck the stone beyond the door. Sanhua shifted her chair enough to bring them level.

“You can be angry while my eyes settle,” she said. “I remember the Chamber hearing. You spent half an hour taking the president through his own records. I enjoyed being able to see the table. He appeared to be having a very different afternoon.”

Jinhsi let out a breath that moved the hair against her cheek.

“I was furious with him. He had brought his workers to stand outside while he argued about their wages.”

“You told me afterward. I remember that part too.”

Jinhsi looked down. “I thought you might need me to be peaceful.”

Sanhua took a little time with that. Her free hand went to the beads at her wrist. She moved one beneath her thumb, then another.

“I like seeing your face,” she said. “I have watched you read reports for so long that I know when you have reached a sentence you want to read aloud. You look up before you have finished it. I wait for you to speak.”

Jinhsi remembered looking up that morning with the chronology open, and finding the room empty because she had asked for it that way.

“There is a great deal I have been leaving on the page.”

“You could give me some of it. I have spent several days thinking about what I would say to the Counselor if we had an hour together.”

Jinhsi turned toward her. “Would you tell me any of it?”

Sanhua considered the request with an attention that made Jinhsi's mouth begin to move.

“I would begin with the maps. I arranged your protection around them. She knew how I used them. I want to hear her explain that part while I am sitting in front of her.”

“She will have an answer.”

“I expect it will be carefully prepared. I have questions prepared as well.”

Jinhsi looked at her for a long moment. She had been trying to find a place to keep her own anger. Sanhua had brought hers into the garden room and set it beside them with the same care she had used to move the table.

“She has agreed to keep answering me,” Jinhsi said. “You should have your hour.”

“I will ask her for it.”

Rain pattered along the eaves. A gust carried fine spray through the doorway, cool against Jinhsi's ankles. She drew her feet back beneath the chair.

“The sentence I wanted to say was about how easily she could make people love her,” she said. “I thought she must have known exactly what to give me.”

Sanhua's hand stayed beneath hers.

“At lunch she had trouble holding the fruit steady while you tied her dressing. She kept watching your hands. I watched hers because she was close to dropping it.”

Jinhsi bent her head. She had felt Changli's arm move beneath the bandage. She had taken it for pain and loosened the knot.

“I remember,” she said. “She asked me to tell her if I was tired of doing it.”

For a while she sat with the two memories: the sentence she had prepared alone, and Changli turning her forearm carefully to make the dressing easier to tie.

Then she looked up at Sanhua.

“I am glad I told you. I would have spent another evening deciding what having the thought said about me.”

“Then please call for me when it happens again. I will come and sit with you.”

Jinhsi turned her hand within Sanhua's and held on properly.

***

Toward evening, Sanhua went to the kitchen to see how her dough had risen.

Jinhsi followed her as far as the stairs. She had returned to her desk, answered two letters, and spent several minutes looking at the box with Gao Shun's name on the lid. When Sanhua mentioned the kitchen, she found she wanted to go somewhere that smelled different.

The cook on evening duty was cutting greens beside a pot of stock. She pointed Sanhua toward the covered basin at the end of the bench, greeted Jinhsi, and shifted her chopping board to give them room.

“There are aprons behind the door. The clean ones are on the upper peg.”

Sanhua lifted the cloth from the basin. The dough had risen almost to the rim.

“I started it before the morning watch changed,” she told Jinhsi. “It should have had its first kneading earlier. The room has been cool enough to forgive me.”

Jinhsi washed her hands and put on an apron. The ties wound twice around her waist. Sanhua watched her secure them, then dusted a place on the bench beside her own.

“I am changing the filling for these. The last batch was too sweet once they cooled. Would you try this?”

She offered a little of the filling on a spoon. Jinhsi tasted it, considered, and took another small amount with the tip.

“I like this better. You can taste the nuts.”

“That was what I wanted. The cook downstairs suggested roasting them first.”

Sanhua tipped the dough onto the board. It came out in a heavy, soft fold. She pressed it with the heels of her hands, turned it, and pressed again. Jinhsi watched the change in her posture: the sleeves tied back, the familiar concentration directed toward something that gave beneath her palms.

“Do you make these whenever you have an evening free?”

“Often. I like working with the dough after a day at the doors. And I like eating the first one while it is hot enough to hurt my fingers.”

The cook glanced over from her chopping board.

“She has learned precisely how long to wait and still does it early.”

Sanhua divided the dough with a scraper. “The first minute is worth some inconvenience.”

Jinhsi took the piece offered to her. It stuck to the heel of her hand, stretching as she lifted it. Sanhua scattered a little flour beneath it and showed her how to turn the edges inward.

For several minutes they worked shoulder to shoulder. The cook's knife moved steadily. Water gathered beneath the lid of the stockpot and ran back down with a soft hiss.

Jinhsi tried to copy the folds in Sanhua's bun. By the fourth pleat, hers had become lopsided. She pressed it closed with both thumbs.

“I appear to have made something with a grievance.”

Sanhua turned it on the board. “The filling is pushing against this seam. A little more dough here will keep it inside.”

“You are being generous about its face.”

“I have a practical interest in keeping your supper inside the wrapper.”

Jinhsi laughed, then bent over the next piece with greater attention.

Sanhua scraped the last of the filling from the mixing bowl. The spoon clicked against the rim.

Jinhsi's hands stopped.

The sound came again, lighter. She could see Gao Shun's fingers moving beside the field kitchen, making the small repeated motion the rain had kept for him. The warm dough lay beneath her palms. She pressed it until the filling began to show through.

“Would you set the spoon on the cloth?” she asked. “That sound keeps taking me back to his bowl.”

Sanhua placed the spoon on the folded towel and moved the mixing bowl farther along the bench.

Jinhsi looked down at what she had done to the bun.

“I will need another piece for this one.”

Sanhua cut one from her own portion and put it beside Jinhsi's hand.

They repaired the seam together. Jinhsi held the torn edges close while Sanhua laid the fresh dough over them. A little filling remained on Jinhsi's thumb. She wiped it on the rim of her apron pocket and began folding again.

By the time the buns were ready for the steamer, the first tray held a row of neat shapes and three broader ones with thick seams. Jinhsi placed hers toward the middle so the lid would clear them.

The cook brought over two stools.

“Sit while they rise again. You can peel these if your hands need work.”

She left a basket of small onions between them.

Jinhsi sat. Sanhua took the stool beside her and rested a foot on its lower rung. Onion skins accumulated on the cloth across their knees, dry and faintly gold in the kitchen light.

“Tomorrow I may need help with the telling,” Jinhsi said. “I have gone through the meeting in my head so many times that I keep losing the simple parts.”

Sanhua slipped the skin from an onion in one piece.

“You told me where you found him, and what he did with his hand. Start there. If you need time, take some water. I will be sitting beside you.”

Jinhsi worked at a stubborn piece of skin with her thumbnail.

“I keep imagining his mother asking something I wish I could answer.”

“Then we will hear what she asks. She may want to tell you something herself.”

The cook crossed the kitchen to check the fire. Jinhsi watched her lift a log with the tongs and settle it against the others. Sparks rose, caught the draught, and disappeared up the flue.

She peeled the next onion more slowly.

When the first buns came out, Sanhua lifted one onto a plate and held her fingers against the cool edge of the bench for a moment. Jinhsi looked at her hand, then up at her face.

“You did that exactly as described.”

“You should try yours now. I think the seam has held.”

Jinhsi broke the bun open. Steam touched her cheeks. The filling had stayed in the centre, and the dough beneath it was soft enough to pull apart with her fingers.

She ate it sitting beside Sanhua, with onion skins still on her apron and tomorrow's box waiting upstairs.

***

Gao Shun's parents arrived with a cloth bag and an umbrella they disagreed about closing.

His father wanted to leave it open in the gallery to dry. His mother pointed out the people carrying trays through it. They settled the matter by setting it beneath the garden eaves, where he turned it twice to put the handle clear of the path.

Jinhsi waited at the doorway while they did it. She had been holding her breath since Sanhua announced them. The disagreement gave her time to let it out.

Mrs Gao came into the room carrying the bag against her stomach. She was smaller than Jinhsi remembered from the ward meeting. A loose thread hung from one cuff, and she caught it between her fingers while her husband took the chair beside her.

“We have brought the other one,” she said.

She took a chopstick from the bag. Its polished surface had worn pale near the narrow end.

Jinhsi opened the box on the table. Gao Shun's father leaned forward as she folded back the cloth. He had held the chopstick across both palms when he gave it to her. Now he reached with one hand and touched the end, moving it a little within the box.

“You washed it,” he said. “That was kind of you.”

“There was rice caught here.” Jinhsi indicated the place. “I wanted to return it clean.”

Mrs Gao laid the second chopstick beside the first. She adjusted them until their ends were level.

Sanhua set down the tea and took the chair beside Jinhsi. As she sat, she placed her sword against the wall within reach. The movement made a soft sound in the room. Mrs Gao glanced toward it, then returned her attention to the box.

“He said you found him on the road,” she told Jinhsi. “At the gate, he told us he had eaten. I have been wondering where you were when you saw him.”

Jinhsi put her hands on the table.

“I went back along the column. His section was near the second marker. I recognized the movement his father had described.”

She lifted her right hand a little above the cloth and showed them.

Mrs Gao's face changed. Her mouth opened with an intake of breath, and her husband laid his hand beside hers.

“He used to watch me from the table with such a solemn face,” she said. “You would have thought he was waiting for a ruling about the weather.”

Jinhsi let her hand settle.

“I told him you had heard him every time. He thought about it for a while. Then he remembered that you would already be going back to the stove before he tapped the bowl.”

Gao Shun's father looked toward the garden.

“She could hear him swallow from the next room. I used to tell him to take his bowl through himself. He liked seeing what she would bring.”

Mrs Gao's fingers had found the loose thread again. She wound it around one finger, unwound it, then tucked it beneath her cuff.

“Was he hungry?”

Jinhsi felt her prepared account begin to scatter. She had the section, the marker, the hour, the name of the man who had given her the bowl. Mrs Gao watched her with the question still in her face.

Sanhua moved her hand onto the table, beside Jinhsi's.

Jinhsi turned her palm and touched it.

“I wish I knew,” she said. “He watched the steam. It leaned toward him, and he stood close to it. His hand grew still while he was there.”

Mrs Gao lowered her eyes to the chopsticks.

“He always did like standing in the kitchen, even when he had grown tall enough to reach everything. I would turn around with a hot pan and find his elbows in the way.”

Her husband made a small sound that might have become a laugh on another morning. She looked at him and the corners of her mouth lifted, briefly.

Then she turned back to Jinhsi.

“Would you show me how you did it? With the bowl.”

Jinhsi's fingers tightened around Sanhua's hand.

Sanhua looked at her. She waited until Jinhsi nodded, then rose and took a clean bowl from the sideboard. She placed it on the table with a folded cloth beneath it and sat down again.

Jinhsi took the chopstick from the box.

It felt lighter than the thing she had carried through the night in her thoughts. She held it between finger and thumb, brought the end toward the rim, and paused.

Sanhua's hand rested beside the cloth.

Jinhsi touched the bowl once.

The little sound travelled through the room and out toward the wet garden. Mrs Gao closed her eyes. Her husband's thumb moved slowly across the back of her hand.

Jinhsi put the chopstick down.

“He smiled after that,” she said. “I had been trying to find him since we started south. I was so glad to see him standing there that I stayed longer than I had planned.”

Mrs Gao opened her eyes. Tears had gathered along her lower lids. She took the bowl in both hands and turned it, feeling the smooth lip with her thumb.

“At the gate, he told me I had gone grey,” she said. “That was what he chose to begin with. I had been standing there thinking of all the things I wanted to ask him, and he looked at my hair.”

“She told him he should see me with my cap off,” her husband said.

Mrs Gao pressed her lips together, and a laugh escaped through them. She put the bowl down carefully. Tears ran to the corners of her mouth while she searched inside her sleeve for a handkerchief.

Her husband found one in his pocket and gave it to her. It had been folded around a small key. The key fell onto his knee. He caught it, looked at it for a moment, and put it in the other pocket.

They sat for a while.

The room carried the sound of rain coming off the roof. A gardener passed beyond the open door with a basket against her hip. She saw the people inside and lifted a hand in greeting before continuing toward the beds.

Mrs Gao dried her face.

“I had put his winter things on the bed,” she said. “When we heard they were coming, I went upstairs and opened the chest. My husband was calling that we should go to the gate, and I was trying to find the shirt with the soft collar.”

She folded the handkerchief around her fingers.

“It is still lying there. I left it when we came here this morning.”

Jinhsi felt Sanhua's fingers close around hers beneath the table.

She listened while Mrs Gao described the shirt, the place where the cuff had worn through, the repair she had meant to make before the winter he went north. Her husband remembered buying the cloth. They disagreed about which market had sold it, considered the routes they had taken that year, and found the shop together.

Jinhsi asked what colour it was.

Mrs Gao told her. Then she described the way the colour changed in the sun, and Jinhsi found she could picture it: a shirt spread across a bed, pale at the folds, with the cuffs turned back by someone looking for a place to begin.

When they left, Mrs Gao carried both chopsticks in the narrow box. Her husband went ahead to fetch the umbrella. He opened it toward the path, and she stepped beneath it, moving the box to the arm nearest him.

Jinhsi stood at the garden door until they reached the covered stair.

***

Sanhua came to her side carrying the empty bowl.

For a moment Jinhsi looked at it as though someone had brought her a question. Then she reached out and took it.

“She wanted to talk about his shirt.”

Sanhua glanced toward the stair where the umbrella had disappeared.

“She had been looking at it all morning.”

Jinhsi ran a finger around the inside of the bowl. The glaze was cool and smooth. She had prepared herself for accusations, gratitude, a question about Jué that would leave her holding all her own questions in front of someone else's grief. Now she knew the colour of Gao Shun's shirt and the name of a shop his parents had once visited together.

She could still feel the effort of listening in her shoulders.

“I kept wanting to offer to have it mended,” she said. “There is someone in the household who could do it beautifully. I had almost begun explaining how we would send for it.”

Sanhua waited beside her.

“Then she showed me where she had left the needle. In the seam, so she could find the place again. I thought about someone coming to take it away.”

She set the bowl on the table inside the door.

“I think I would have been angry if they came for it.”

“You asked her about the colour,” Sanhua said. “She seemed glad to tell you.”

Jinhsi sat on the low bench beneath the eaves. The stone chilled her through her clothes. Sanhua brought the cushion from the room, and Jinhsi lifted herself enough to let her place it beneath her.

“Will you sit here for a while?”

“I have asked Deng to take the gallery. He can bring anything urgent to us.”

Sanhua sat. She had left her sword beside the chair inside, close to the open door. Jinhsi could see the pale wrapping on its hilt.

“I keep imagining what I will say when Jué speaks,” Jinhsi said. “This morning I was furious. I had washed my face and put everything in order for the meeting, and I stood in front of the scale wondering whether I should ask more politely.”

Sanhua turned toward her.

“What did you want to ask?”

Jinhsi watched a raindrop run along the edge of a leaf. It reached the tip and hung there, making the whole leaf bow.

“I wanted to ask where he has been, and whether he heard us.” Her hands closed over the edge of the cushion. “There was so much to tell him about Deng Hao. Then I thought of Geshu Lin, and the names kept coming. I stood there trying to choose which one I should say first.”

The leaf lifted as the drop fell.

“I still want him to answer,” she said. “I think I will be glad enough to cry when I hear his voice. Then I will have to find the questions again.”

Sanhua rested her hands in her lap.

“You can write them down. I will keep the page for you.”

Jinhsi looked at her sharply.

Sanhua's fingers stilled against each other. She waited, letting Jinhsi look.

There was the offered page, a place to put something until she could bear to take it up again. Changli had kept things for her. Wen kept the corrected originals. Gao Shun's mother had carried a single chopstick across the city in a bag. Jinhsi could feel herself gathering all of them toward one frightening resemblance.

She looked at Sanhua's hands, open on her knees.

“Beside the scale,” she said. “I would like to keep it there. You can help me find it when I need it.”

“I will know where to look.”

The gardener returned along the path, her basket filled with fallen twigs. She stopped by the low tree and reached up to test the branch binding. A little water shook down onto her sleeve. She muttered something about the person who had tied it, tightened the knot, and continued along the beds.

Jinhsi leaned back against the wall. She felt tired in a way that had finally reached her arms and legs. Sanhua's shoulder was beside hers, close enough to lean toward.

She let their shoulders meet.

“You have been very patient with me.”

Sanhua looked out at the tree.

“I wanted to be here. When you asked where I should sit, I was already hoping for that chair.”

Jinhsi let her head turn a little toward her. From this close, she could see a fine dusting of flour caught in the stitching of Sanhua's cuff. They had washed their hands carefully the night before. The flour had found somewhere to stay.

“I am glad you asked me where,” she said. “You have spent a long time at the door.”

Sanhua's shoulder moved beneath hers with a breath.

“I was thinking about supper. Chisa will probably take the bench near the back. I would like to sit with her again.”

“Then we should get there before Amy begins moving the chairs.”

Sanhua's mouth curved. “I can have us ready in good time.”

They sat while the gardener worked her way toward the far wall. Through the open door behind them, the four chairs remained around the table. Jinhsi could see the place where she had set the bowl, and the small wet ring Mrs Gao's cup had left beside it.

After a while, her hand found Sanhua's on the bench.

“Would you tell me how the next filling is going to be different?” she asked. “I would like to think about that for a little while.”

Sanhua considered it. Then she began explaining what she meant to do with the nuts, her thumb resting along Jinhsi's fingers, and Jinhsi listened until she had a question about the roasting.

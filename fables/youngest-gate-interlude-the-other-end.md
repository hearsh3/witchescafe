# The Other End

Rebecca put the hot frying pan on Lynae’s coursework when the call came through.

Lynae lifted it by the handle. On the dorm terminal, Mei was trying to fit seven people into a picture meant for one.

“There you fucking are.” Rebecca leaned across the desk. “I’ve been reading military bulletins for two days. Every officer in Huanglong needs to learn what a paragraph is.”

“We’re at Mrs Ren’s,” Mei said. “Ciaccona’s resting her hand, Iuno’s shoulder is being treated, and I’ve got stitches. Amy has photographs of my stitches, which she keeps showing people during meals.”

Lynae brought the screen closer. Behind Mei, Lupa settled Rebecca’s bag on her knees, turning it so the handle faced the camera.

Rebecca rubbed a spot of grease off the glass with her sleeve.

“You’re carrying that all the way back. I want to be there when you dump it on my bed.”

“Then clear a space. I’m sitting down afterward, and you can explain what happened to that cupboard.”

Lynae reached sideways and pushed the crooked door shut. It swung open again.

“We’ve had a very full week.” She sat on the desk, one foot tucked under her. “I got eighty-six in materials. I had a whole speech ready, actually, and then the news came through.”

Mei lowered herself into the chair. Amy leaned over its back; Lupa stayed with the bag.

“Go on,” Mei said. “We’ve got time for your speech.”

Lynae found the marked paper beneath the pan’s damp circle. By the time she reached the examiner’s comments, Rebecca had brought her breakfast over.

Abby emerged from Mei’s mark and peered at the plate.

“That looks worth waking for. What have you put in it?”

Rebecca looked at Mei.

“This is Abby. He’s coming home with us.”

Lynae started laughing. Rebecca turned the camera toward the kitchen.

“You’ll want the cupboard fixed before you get here. I’ll put her on it.”

***

Chiyo took her turn from the kitchen, where Chisa could hear a cupboard opening and the familiar clink of her mother’s thick cup.

“I’ve eaten,” Chisa told her. “Rice, fish, and a dumpling Lupa bought on the way back. She held the bag against my cheek until I took it.”

“I like that woman. Give her my regards.”

Chisa drew her feet onto the chair. Her mother sipped, swallowed, set the cup down. The small sounds came through clearly.

“There was a woman at the gate with a comb,” Chisa said. “Her son bent his head whenever she reached for him. I watched them for a while.”

The cup scraped against the counter.

“I kept a uniform for twenty years,” Chiyo said. “I used to take it out and look at the hem. I thought I might have to let it down.”

Chisa rested her chin on her knees.

“I’d like to try it on when I get back.”

“We’ll have to press it. It’s been folded so long.” Water ran briefly at her mother’s end. “Stay there a little. I’m putting the kettle on, and you can tell me about the woman you’re staying with.”

Chisa reached for her own cup.

“She charges Lupa extra for the bath. There’s been quite a discussion about the towels.”

***

Augusta answered from her kitchen in Septimont. The seed catalog was open beneath her elbow; beside it lay a dispatch with NORFALL across the top.

“Turn a little, Iuno. Let me see your shoulder.”

Iuno angled herself toward the camera. Mei adjusted the lamp while Amy helped herself to the biscuits between them.

“The fracture has feeling now,” Iuno said. “Baizhi is checking it every morning. Yangyang turned a blade into it and saved my life.”

Augusta studied the dressing. A loose copper wire curled around the leg of the terminal.

“Does Mei know how much it hurts?”

“I told her before breakfast.”

Mei’s hand settled at the back of Iuno’s neck.

Augusta reached for her tea. “Good. You used to give me the weather report and let the physician explain the blood on your cuff.”

Amy leaned into view. “I’d love to hear more about that period of Auntie’s life. Especially anything embarrassing that happened in public.”

“You may hear it from your own chair,” Iuno said. “You’re kneeling on my coat.”

Mei shifted to make room. Amy sat beside her, smiling directly at Iuno.

*We raised our own opposition,* the High Priestess observed. *She has excellent attendance.*

Augusta’s laugh brought Galbrena to the kitchen doorway. She rested a hand on Augusta’s shoulder and looked at the screen for a long moment before coming closer.

“The rose has climbed over your window,” Augusta said. “Angel offered to cut it back.”

“Leave it until I come,” Iuno said. “I’d like to see how far it’s got.”

Augusta drew the catalog over the dispatch and found the page she had marked.

***

At Ragunna’s Guild office, Zani had carried the terminal onto the front steps. Phoebe sat beside her with their supper. Laevatain came up the street at a run and took the last few steps slowly, arranging her coat.

“I was nearby,” she said. “Let me get a look at you.”

Iuno moved the terminal back. Mei raised her good hand; Amy waved with the biscuit she had brought along.

Phoebe let out a breath and leaned against Zani, nearly tipping the supper into her lap.

“Alice’s been pinning the reports above the Northern Desk,” Zani said. “This morning she needed a ladder. I’ve promised her first look at anything you send.”

“There’s a photograph of the gate,” Mei said. “A family asked us to take it. They said we could show people.”

“I’d like that,” Phoebe said. She steadied the plate on her knees. “Were they able to walk beside him?”

“His sister held her little girl up. He spent most of the road looking at her.”

Laevatain sat on the step below them. For a while the street filled the call: a cart, somebody calling a dog, plates being collected inside.

Then she looked up.

“Tell Lupa the viburnum’s flowering. I was at Mya’s last week. She’ll want to know.”

***

Ciaccona’s address book shed a pressed leaf, two receipts, and a name written on the back of a drinks bill.

Stevens answered from the Blueberry with a pencil behind his ear. She gave him the news while he sat on the edge of the stage, and accepted a thorough account of the pianist’s departure in exchange. Buling wanted the timings of the rain. A fiddler she knew in Porto-Veno wanted to know whether a story about nineteen hundred soldiers coming home was something he could tell his children.

“Start with the family photograph,” Ciaccona said. “I’ll send the words that came with it.”

By her fourth call she had gone hoarse. Cartethyia brought water and sat beside her while a woman three seas away described the dreadful man she had married.

Ciaccona listened with growing pleasure.

“You must save this for my next visit. I want to hear you tell it with your hands.”

***

Cantarella answered to find a scorched camellia leaf filling her screen.

“Hotel Kokomi,” Cartethyia said. “I have a gardening emergency.”

“Move your thumb, darling. You’re covering the stem.”

The Duchess inspected the cutting from three angles. Cartethyia obeyed each instruction, turning the little terrarium carefully between her palms.

“The new growth looks healthy. Keep the damaged leaf for now. And bring the picture back up to your face.”

Cartethyia set the plant down.

Cantarella looked at her. Somewhere beyond the camera, water dripped steadily from a watering can.

“I heard what you did with the paper people.”

“Everyone helped. The children were much better at the little hands.” Cartethyia rubbed an ink stain on her thumb. “I kept thinking about the dolls.”

“I thought you might.” Cantarella drew her chair closer. “I’m proud of you. I’ve been waiting to tell you that.”

Cartethyia looked down at the cutting, smiling until her mouth began to wobble.

“Could you show me the garden?”

The Duchess picked up the terminal. They went out together.

Cosimo’s call came through later, routed to a neighbor’s kitchen in Egla Town. Her uncle had brought a small bundle wrapped in a dishcloth.

“I was going to post these,” he said. “Then I thought about the roads.”

He uncovered lemon honey cakes. Cartethyia leaned so close that Ciaccona reached over and steadied the screen.

“Oh, you used the big sugar crystals.”

“You always picked those off first. Your aunt used to put extra on the one at the back.” He turned the plate for her. “I heard about the general. They say he got through the gate.”

“I walked in with his company. The streets were full of lamps.”

Cosimo sat down with the cakes still in his hands.

Cartethyia waited. She could hear his neighbor washing something at the sink.

“I remember seeing you in the square,” he said. “I’d brought lilies that morning. Then there you were, singing.”

She tucked her hand into Ciaccona’s.

“Tell me what you did after we left. I’ve always wondered about the rest of that day.”

***

The recording reached the Banat al-Rih’s sea-cave with a band of static across its lower edge.

Herta adjusted the receiver until the lamps along Jinzhou’s road held steady. Beside her, Phrolova had pulled up a chair.

The dead passed through the frame in wet, ordinary clothes. A soldier stopped when somebody called from outside the picture. The next man bumped into him. They turned toward each other, and the first man’s shoulders moved.

“Would you put the sound up?” Phrolova asked. “I think he’s laughing.”

Herta reached for the dial. The little speaker crackled, carrying footsteps, overlapping voices, a woman calling somebody over to make room.

Phrolova leaned closer.

From the kitchen, Mina brought their tea. She set Herta’s cup within reach, looked at the road on the screen, and drew up a third chair.

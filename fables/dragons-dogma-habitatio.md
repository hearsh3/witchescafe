# HABITATIO

The clerk moved the inkwell to make room for Ronin's name.

She had been following his hand for the last ten minutes. Now she watched the little wet circle it left on the dresser. He wiped it with his sleeve, laid a fresh sheet of blotting paper beside the deed, and turned the pen toward her.

“Along there. Leave a little room at the end for the witness.”

Waldhar had found a dyer's yard in the old ward rolls, bought jointly by an Arisen and the pawn who mixed his colours. Merrit had obtained a certified copy, attached their culling accounts as evidence of earnings, and spent a morning getting the recorder to accept the same form of purchase. His approval occupied four lines on the back.

Ronin read those first. Then she read the front again.

Reiter stood beside her with the two empty money bags folded together in one hand. Ilsa was counting the coins into a third bag. She had come back from her sister's with a basket, a travelling cloak, and considerable news about the sister's hens.

“Would you read the part about the shares aloud?” Ronin asked.

The clerk put his finger beneath the line. One half to each purchaser. He gave both names their full space in the sentence.

Ronin took the pen.

Her handwriting was small and easily accommodated. She had put herself on requisitions, equipment returns, lists of stores to be collected. This signature took longer. At the last stroke she leaned closer to the dresser, keeping her sleeve away from the ink.

Reiter signed beneath her in the old hand.

Ilsa tied her money bag, signed below them, and went to find the saucepan she had promised her sister. Merrit witnessed all three signatures.

The clerk shook sand over the page.

“Common Quarter, house twenty-seven. You can quote the number at the board next time.” He looked at Ronin. “The occupancy roll will carry both of you.”

She put her hand against the dresser, where the inkwell had stood.

“I'd like a copy of that as well. I can come for it tomorrow.”

He wrote a reminder on the outside of his folder.

Ilsa emerged from the scullery holding the saucepan by a piece of string threaded through its handle.

“This leaks along the seam,” she told them. “I put a flowering plant in it. My sister's going to put another flowering plant in it, and her husband will spend a week trying to mend the seam. That's the arrangement we've reached.”

She tucked it into the basket.

The clerk left with the city's copy. Merrit followed him, remembering a question about an expense allowance as they reached the gate.

Ilsa took the second front-door key off her ring and gave it to Reiter.

“Your neighbour has my sister's address. Write about the house if something puzzles you. Write about yourselves if the house behaves.”

At the door she turned and looked up the stair. She ran her hand once over the smooth end of the banister.

“We grew a boy in here,” she said. “For a while he could reach the ceiling by standing on the third step. He used to measure visitors against it.”

She adjusted the basket on her arm and went out to catch her cart.

Reiter closed the door. The latch clicked into place.

Ronin stood in the middle of the room. She had the deed in one hand and her own key in the other.

Upstairs, a shutter tapped against the wall.

“I'll see to that,” she said.

She went up the stair, and Reiter listened to the fourth step give its two notes beneath her weight.

---

Their things arrived in three journeys.

The first brought the packs, the armour, the bedding, and Reiter's books. The second brought a table for the upper room and two chairs. The third brought everything they had remembered while carrying the table.

Ronin bought a blue washing basin at the potter's. She carried it with both hands all the way home. There was a fish painted inside, round-eyed and disproportionately pleased with itself.

Reiter looked into it while Ronin rested her arms at a corner.

“That will hold enough for a full wash.”

“I tried it with the jug. There was room for my elbows.”

At the furniture stall Reiter had sat in a chair with a faded red cushion, her hands resting on the curved ends of its arms. The seller began explaining how easily the cushion could be replaced. She asked the price, sat a little longer, and paid him.

Ronin chose a wooden chair with a wide seat. She liked to draw one foot underneath herself when she read.

The table got as far as the turn in the stair.

Reiter held the lower end. Ronin stood above it, looking from the wall to the table leg, which had occupied the same three inches for some time.

“Bring your side toward me a little.”

“The rail is against my wrist. Lift your end another hand.”

The table moved. The leg scraped the plaster.

Ronin looked down through the gap between the boards.

“The man offered to bring it.”

“He assessed the carrying distance. I assessed the door.”

“There's quite a lot of house between those two places.”

They held the table while Reiter considered the underside.

“The legs are pegged. We can take them out.”

Ronin rested her cheek on the edge of the top.

“I'll need to come down to do that.”

Getting her down took another six minutes. By the time they had taken the legs out, carried the pieces upstairs, and put them together by the window, the sun had reached the opposite roofs.

Reiter sat in the cushioned chair.

Ronin put the washing basin on the table and poured in a jug of water. The painted fish disappeared beneath the moving reflection of the window.

She washed the plaster off her hands. Reiter held out hers when she had finished.

“We have lost an hour,” Reiter said.

Ronin gave her the soap.

“I liked sitting in the shop. You looked comfortable.”

Reiter turned her hands in the water. Dust loosened from the creases around her fingers.

“It takes the weight under the shoulder. I could use it for a whole evening.”

“The man seemed ready to let you try.”

Reiter laughed into the basin. The sound came out abruptly, and she looked up with water running off her wrists.

Ronin handed her the towel.

---

They ate their first meal from the wrapping the cheese had come in.

There were onions to prepare for supper, flour to put somewhere dry, a jar of salt with a lid too large for it, and six eggs in a folded cloth. The kitchen table was covered with these, together with a hinge Reiter had taken off the pantry door.

Ronin cut the bread on the corner that remained. Reiter held the cheese while she cut that too.

Through the open back door they could see the well and the little yard. The lance lay on two wooden rests Reiter had fixed under the eaves. Its shadow ran down the limewash beside the door.

Ronin ate standing up, looking out into the yard. Reiter joined her with the last piece of cheese.

“I asked Waldhar about the assembly,” Ronin said.

Reiter rested her shoulder against the door frame.

“How did you find him?”

“He was trying to make Malcolm sort things by date. Malcolm wanted to sort them by the pictures. They were both putting books in each other's piles.”

She picked a bit of rind from the bread.

“He'll ask me about Melve. Where I entered the square, what I saw, when you came down. I can answer those questions.”

“Brant still wants the command demonstrated.”

“I've asked to speak first. I want them to hear what happened while they're looking at me.”

Reiter chewed slowly. In the lane, a cart stopped, and someone began unloading lengths of wood against the neighbouring wall.

“The boy was already in the square when we arrived,” she said. “He was at the mouth of the left lane.”

“I remember where he stood.”

“Include him. He held the spear through the fire.”

Ronin looked at the bread in her hand.

“Waldhar asked why I went down the hill. I began giving him the ground. He let me finish and asked again.”

Reiter's head turned toward her.

“What did you tell him?”

“By then he wanted his tea. I'll try again tomorrow.”

She took the last bite. Reiter stood with her until she had swallowed, then went in to light the oven.

---

The house took three hours to warm, as Ilsa had said. During the first hour Ronin carried in the wood, filled the water bucket, swept the scullery, found a place for the soap, and began cutting the onions Reiter had set out.

Reiter took a second knife. They worked side by side until the board was full.

“These will do,” Reiter said. “We can put the others by for tomorrow.”

Ronin wrapped the remaining onions. She wiped the knives. Then she picked up the bowl of flour.

Reiter took it from her.

“I have the proportions written down. The book's beside your elbow.”

Ronin opened it. An old cookery book from the library, with a broad grease stain through the recipe Reiter had marked. The letter forms were as familiar as the survey's. Someone had added a small quantity of mustard to the ingredients in a different hand.

She read it through while Reiter rubbed fat into the flour.

“There's pepper here as well.”

“In the cupboard, beside the vinegar.”

Ronin fetched it, ground some, and waited with the little dish in her hand.

Reiter looked at the amount.

“You could season the whole pie with that.”

“I was going to put it on my half.”

The rubbing slowed.

Ronin looked into the dish.

“There's usually one pot when we're walking.”

Reiter took a pinch and tasted it on her finger. Her eyes watered.

“Put some in the filling. We will keep the dish on the table.”

Ronin added the pepper. She went back to the book while the onions softened in the pan.

After a few minutes she began reading the recipe on the opposite page. It involved a goose, considerable butter, and a pastry lid shaped to resemble the living bird. The cook had devoted half a paragraph to keeping the head upright.

Reiter was rolling the dough.

“A skewer would hold it,” she said.

“They suggest a turnip inside the neck. There's a drawing.”

Reiter came over to look, leaving a pale handprint on the table.

They studied the bird. Its pastry head leaned forward in an expression of grave enquiry.

“The weight would pull through the crust,” Reiter said. “You would have to brace it inside the breast.”

Ronin turned the book a little.

“I think they expected it to arrive at the table and be admired quickly.”

Reiter fetched a pencil. While the onions browned, she drew a small support beneath the goose's throat on a scrap of paper.

---

When the pie went into the oven, Ronin took the bowl to the scullery and washed it. She hung up the cloth, shifted it to a better hook, and came back with water for the table.

Reiter had put the hinge in a dish. She was cleaning the screw threads with the point of her knife.

Ronin sat opposite her.

For a while she watched the knife work. Then she got up to check the oven.

“It has twenty minutes,” Reiter said.

Ronin sat down again. She tucked one foot under herself and reached for the cookery book.

Three pages later she went to fetch more wood.

Reiter looked at the filled basket beside the hearth. When Ronin returned, she set the hinge down.

“The room upstairs has the light now. You wanted to read there.”

Ronin stood with two pieces of wood in her arms.

“I keep remembering something else to do.”

“The pantry door can have this back tomorrow. It will stay where I put it until then.”

She laid the knife beside the dish.

Ronin put the wood in the basket. The top piece rolled off. She picked it up and took both pieces back outside.

When she came in again, she stopped beside Reiter's chair.

“Will you come up as well? Bring the pencil. I'd like to see what you've done to the goose.”

They sat at the table by the upper window. Reiter took the cushioned chair, and Ronin leaned over the drawing with her bare foot tucked beneath her knee.

The light travelled across the paper while they discussed whether the bird would fit into their oven.

---

They remembered supper when the smell reached the landing.

The crust had browned deeply along one side. Reiter turned the dish on the table and examined it with the oven cloth still wrapped around her hands.

“The heat gathers at the back. I should have turned it at the halfway point.”

Ronin fetched the plates. She cut the pie, put the darker piece on her own, and sat down.

Reiter watched her taste it.

“There is a bitter edge on that side.”

Ronin broke off a little crust and ate it separately.

“I like that part with the onions. The inside's sweet.”

Reiter took some from the same edge. She chewed, considered it, then cut her next piece from the middle.

They ate with the back door open. Heat came off the dish. Ronin added pepper to her plate, cautiously at first, then with greater confidence when Reiter passed her the spoon.

Halfway through, she stopped reaching for things.

Her shoulders had come down. She sat with both feet on the floor and the fork resting in her hand, looking across the table at the flour on Reiter's cuff.

Reiter had begun explaining how the baker at her old house had tested the oven by scattering flour on a stone. The first time he showed her, his sleeve caught the jug and sent water over half the kitchen.

“He carried on with the explanation while I fetched a cloth. When we had dried the floor, he wanted to begin again. He thought the water had distracted me.”

“Did you let him?”

“He had brought the ingredients. There was enough for another loaf.”

Ronin drew the pie dish nearer and helped herself.

“You should tell me what happened on the third afternoon.”

Reiter put down her fork to make room for the telling.

---

They divided the upper rooms after washing up.

Ronin wanted the front one. She spread her bedding along the wall beside the table, then sat on it to see where the morning light would fall. Reiter brought her pack to the doorway and waited.

“You can use the table in the mornings,” Ronin said. “I'll be going to the library. Tap on the door first if it's shut.”

Reiter set the pack beside the bedding.

“The back room gets the morning sun. I will take that one.”

She went down to fetch her own things. Ronin heard the fourth stair under her foot, then the slow shifting of armour in the room below.

She unrolled the rest of her bedding.

There was space along the wall for a chest. Above it she could put pegs, and the coat could hang at its full length, with its hem clear of the floor. She held it against the wall to judge the height.

Reiter came past with the greaves in one arm and her helm hooked over her fingers.

“I'll need three pegs,” Ronin told her. “One for the coat, one for the hat, and one beside the door.”

“What will you hang on the third one?”

“I'll find something. There's room for it.”

Reiter looked at the wall, then continued into her own room.

Ronin laid the coat over the chair. She took off the rest of her road clothes, folded them, and sat down on the bedding in her undershirt. The house creaked above her as the roof cooled.

She could hear Reiter taking things out of her pack. A buckle touched the floor. Pages turned. After a while, the chair in the back room shifted beneath her weight.

Ronin lay down.

She woke once during the night and heard the same slow turning of pages through the wall. She drew the blanket up over her shoulder and went back to sleep.

---

In the morning she came down to find Reiter eating pie at the kitchen table.

A slice waited for her in a covered dish beside the stove. Reiter had cut it from the dark edge.

Ronin sat down with it and ate while Reiter studied the recipe for bread. The kitchen window was open two fingers. There was an easterly wind, and the air moving past the sill carried the smoke where it belonged.

After breakfast Ronin fetched Brant's coat from the pack. She spread it across her knees and looked at the mark inside the collar.

“I'll have to soak this part before I wash it. Something's worked into the weave.”

Reiter looked up from the book.

“Wilhelmina. She kissed me while the patrol went by.”

Ronin held the collar toward the window.

“I remember the scent when you came down. There's still some in the cloth.”

Reiter turned the page back to keep her place.

“I enjoyed it. I was trying to keep the letter flat at the time. She had hold of the pocket.”

“That sounds like a busy evening for both of you.”

Ronin folded the coat over her arm and went to the scullery. She put the marked part into a bowl of water and worked it gently between her fingers.

Reiter came to stand beside her.

“There was dancing at the masquerade. I recognised the steps.”

Ronin glanced up.

“You could have stayed for a dance.”

“The man left while I was finding somewhere to put my glass.”

Water dripped from the collar. Ronin pressed it between her palms and laid it across the side of the bowl.

“Show me where you would have put your feet.”

Reiter looked down at the scullery floor.

“We will need the kitchen.”

They moved the chairs back. Reiter set the bread book on the dresser and took Ronin's hand.

The first turn brought them close to the table. Ronin put her free hand on Reiter's shoulder, and Reiter adjusted her stance to give them room. She counted the measures under her breath.

Ronin followed for a few steps. At the turn she moved left, carrying them into the corner of the table with a small, definite knock.

Reiter stopped with her hand still at Ronin's back.

“Your next step goes across mine. The turn comes after that.”

Ronin looked at their feet.

“I have a dance in me that turns there. I remember a deck moving under it.”

Reiter shifted her hand a little higher.

“Show me that one.”

They tried it with Ronin leading. The space between the table and the dresser suited the shorter steps. She could feel the borrowed movement return through her legs, together with a small, persistent conviction that there ought to be wind in her face.

Reiter came round smoothly on the second attempt. On the third she caught the measure and began to anticipate it.

Ronin looked up to see what her face was doing.

They hit the table again.

The spoon fell off. Reiter caught Ronin at the waist while she recovered her footing, and both of them stayed where they were for a moment, laughing in the narrow space.

“We could move the table into the yard,” Reiter said.

Ronin glanced at the door they would have to carry it through.

“I'd like to get through the washing first. Then you can assess the route.”

She bent to pick up the spoon. Reiter put the chairs back, leaving them a little farther apart.

---

Ronin went out after breakfast and came back with the occupancy copy rolled inside her sleeve.

She had also bought three rolls. She ate one on the walk, gave one to the man by the tannery, and put the third beside Reiter's hand when she reached the kitchen.

Reiter was making the pegs.

Ronin unrolled the paper on the table and weighted its corners with the salt jar and the cookery book. The house number appeared at the top. Below it were their names, the date, and the ward clerk's stamp.

She brought her chair round to sit beside Reiter and read it aloud.

Reiter put the knife down. When Ronin had finished, she asked which parts she could copy onto a letter.

They spent most of the next hour on the address.

The name of the quarter was difficult. Reiter knew it when Ronin pointed to it, and her hand kept trying to write the older form. Ronin made a large copy on a scrap, leaving space between the characters.

Reiter practised while the bread dough rose.

At the end of the hour, Ronin went upstairs with the three pegs. Reiter stayed at the table, working on the last word, and called up when she wanted the small hammer returned.

Ronin leaned over the banister.

“I've set it on your chair. You'll feel it if you sit down.”

Reiter went to fetch it, carrying the letter with her. She held it up from the foot of the stair for Ronin to inspect.

Ronin came down three steps to get the light on it.

“That will reach us. You've got the whole address.”

Reiter looked at the page herself, then took it back to the kitchen.

---

Brant brought salt when he visited that evening.

Ronin thanked him and put the jar beside the one she had bought and the smaller one the neighbour had left. She found a place for all three by moving the vinegar.

Reiter took his coat. He stood in the middle of the kitchen for a moment, looking around while she hung it up.

“Lennart received the warning. He's spoken to the households and put men on the approaches. His letter is with Waldhar.”

Reiter's hand stayed on the peg.

“Did the messenger reach Wilm?”

“He found him helping repair a roof. The fellow asked after a parcel he'd sent you.”

“We have it here.”

She went to fetch the primer from the dresser. Brant turned it over carefully, looking at the rounded corners and the coloured cart.

Ronin put a third place at the table.

They were having fish with potatoes and the remaining onions. The bread was cooling on a board. Reiter cut the first loaf while Brant washed his hands at the blue basin.

He looked down at the painted fish.

“My aunt had plates from that potter. Every bird on them looked as though it had just found something to eat.”

“He had a goose in the window,” Ronin said. “You could serve twelve from it.”

Reiter glanced at the cookery book. Ronin saw her do it and put the fish dish between them.

Brant helped himself. He ate the first few mouthfuls with the concentrated attention of someone who had been thinking about supper for a long time.

When Ronin passed him the pepper, he added enough to make Reiter watch his hand. Ronin moved the dish to his side of the table.

They talked about the upper room, the difficulty with the table, the neighbour's hens getting through a gap beside the well. Brant knew the neighbour. He had spent part of his first posting returning her hens from the guard yard.

After the plates were empty, Ronin brought the bread board back to the table.

“Waldhar wants me to explain why I went into the square at Melve,” she said. “I've been thinking about it.”

Brant laid down the knife he had been using to spread butter.

“What have you come to?”

Ronin sat with her hands around her cup.

“The boy was looking down the lane. He shifted his grip on the spear and caught the point against a shutter. I wanted to get a hand on him and pull him behind the wall.”

Reiter remembered the boy from the mouth of the lane. She had been watching the dragon's wing.

“Then its head came round,” Ronin continued. “I could see its eye, and I had my mouth open to tell the boy where to go. The heat reached us before I said it.”

She moved her thumb along the handle of the cup.

“That is what I remember thinking. He'll have to ask me about getting up separately. I can remember the shield coming off the ground.”

Brant let her finish her tea before he spoke.

“I'll tell Waldhar to give you time between the questions. He gets excited when he thinks a witness is reaching the useful part.”

Reiter took the bread knife and began cutting a piece for herself. The crust cracked beneath it.

“The boy held the spear at the end,” she said. “He was looking toward us.”

“His brother will have had your instructions by now.” Brant picked up his cup again. “Lennart says he's spent the week looking for people who might need to hear about the proper length.”

Ronin smiled into her tea.

The conversation moved on to the market. Reiter wanted to know who sold dry wood in quantities larger than the bundles outside the inn. Brant gave her a name and drew a little map on the back of a receipt.

He signed the receipt when he had finished. Habit carried the pen through the B and its quick downstroke.

Reiter looked at it while he blotted the ink.

Later he helped dry the dishes. Ronin showed him where the plates went. His sleeve caught the edge of the towel, and he rolled it back to his elbow before continuing.

When he left, Reiter stood in the doorway until he reached the bend in the lane. Then she came back to the kitchen and sat down beside the little map.

---

“I was trying to remember his hands,” she said.

Ronin was putting the leftover fish into a covered dish. She set it on the cool shelf and came back to the table.

“The man who ordered the forage?”

“He wore a ring on the first finger. It clicked against the board when he played draughts. I can remember the sound of it.”

Reiter turned the receipt over. The map showed the bakery, the well, and the turn toward the wood merchant.

“He used to move my pieces while I was looking at the day's orders. Then he would ask whether I wanted to reconsider my position.”

Ronin pulled out her chair.

“How long did he get away with that?”

“Three games. During the fourth I moved them back while he was filling his pipe. He took a long time over his next turn.”

She rested a finger on the receipt.

“I would like another game with him.”

Ronin sat down. In the scullery, water dripped slowly from the cloth over the basin.

Reiter folded the map and put it beside the primer.

“Brant likes pepper,” she said. “We should remember that if he comes again.”

Ronin reached for the brown paper that had wrapped the potatoes. She smoothed it on the table and handed Reiter the pencil.

“Draw the squares while I find some pieces. There are light stones by the well.”

They used pale pebbles and scraps of dark wood, with small coins underneath to mark the kings.

Reiter won the first game. Ronin spent some time looking at the last three pieces, then set up the board again.

During the second game Reiter began explaining Ronin's mistake in the first. Ronin listened, asked a question about the centre of the board, and shifted a dark piece while Reiter was reaching for a second scrap of paper.

Reiter's hand stopped above the pencil.

Ronin sat with one foot tucked underneath herself and waited for the explanation to continue.

After a moment Reiter drew the position from memory. She put every piece in its former place, slowly, while Ronin watched her do it.

They played until the lamp needed oil.

---

The following morning Reiter wrote to Wilm.

Ronin made a fair copy for her to work from. She sat at the other side of the upper table, reading a borrowed book while Reiter formed the current letters one at a time.

The message took most of the morning.

*Wilm, the book reached us. I am learning the hand. We have bought a house. Come when you can.*

Below it she wrote the address she had practised. Her own name went at the bottom in the old script.

Ronin checked the whole page when she was asked. There was a small smudge beside the last word. She held the letter toward the window to let the ink dry.

“He'll want to know whether we have somewhere for him to sleep,” she said.

Reiter looked around the room. The table stood where Ronin had drawn it, with the two chairs on either side. Her coat hung from its peg. The third peg held a length of ribbon she had bought with the bread.

“We can put a mattress beside the hearth. It will be warm by the time he arrives.”

Ronin returned the letter. Reiter folded it, pressed the crease with her thumbnail, and set it beside the primer.

They went downstairs together.

Reiter had errands at the board and a visit to make to the wood merchant. Ronin was going to the library after she had washed the breakfast things. She put two pieces of bread and a little cheese in a cloth while Reiter fastened her sword belt.

At the door Reiter looked back at the kitchen table. Their unfinished game occupied the space between the breakfast things. One captured stone had rolled into the dish containing the pantry hinge.

Ronin brought her the food.

“Will you be back for supper?”

“By the sixth bell. I can buy what we need on the way.”

“Get pears, if the woman by the fountain has any. I'll put them in the oven after the bread comes out.”

Reiter tucked the cloth into her pack. She took the letter to Wilm and went out into the lane.

Ronin stayed in the doorway while she adjusted the lance on her back. Then Reiter lifted her hand, about as high as her belt, and walked toward the bakery.

Upstairs, the window stood open over the table. The curtain moved in the breeze.

Ronin went back to the kitchen and washed the plates. She left them upright to dry while she fetched her book for the library.

On her way out, she moved a dark piece across the draughts board.

She would be back by the sixth bell as well.

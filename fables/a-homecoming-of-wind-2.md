# A Homecoming of Wind
### being the return edition of a crossing, set at Jinzhou from a single copy
*A "Lyre, Speak to Me" side story.*

少小離家老大回，鄉音無改鬢毛衰。
兒童相見不相識，笑問客從何處來。
*I left home young and come back old. My accent holds; the hair at my temples has thinned. The children see me as a stranger, and ask, laughing, where I am from.*
— He Zhizhang, "Written on Returning Home"

露從今夜白，月是故鄉明。
*From tonight the dew turns white. The moon is brightest over home.*
— Du Fu

## 玄翎飛 — the Xuanling flies

The cut in the sky was still open when she went through it.

Birds poured past her on both sides, blue-black, silent, each one going somewhere particular. Below them the Sonoro folded in on its own pavilion like a paper lantern with the candle out, and below that was the true evening over Xuanfang: rain on her feathers, a moon coming up whole over the eastern rank, and the Hold's courtyard lit gold in the shape of an open door.

She came round once.

Faces turned up. She knew them at that height by how they stood. A wolf with her weight forward. A one-armed woman who had her hand flat on the shoulder of a girl with a visor pushed up. A woman in a cook's apron with a fan stopped in mid-air. And at the centre of the flagstones, a woman soaked to the shoulders with a hole in her bandage, whose right hand had come up on its own, open, palm to the sky, and stayed there.

The hand stayed open. It caught air. It held.

The Wind lay along her left wing where the feathers were missing, warm and shaking and staying, and the shaking changed pitch when the courtyard came into view, the way it had changed under an old woman's hand on a bridge.

Yangyang decided the way she decided everything.

"Jie!" The voice came out of a bird's chest, small and clear and pitched to carry over a wall. "Keep my bowl by the stove!"

The fan came down. Suisui's face did the thing it did before it decided whether to laugh.

"I'm warming it once!" Suisui shouted back. "ONCE, Yangyang!"

Mei's mouth stayed shut and her hand stayed open.

Yangyang leaned her whole weight on her sister and went up.

⁂

There is a height where the air stays kind, and a wind once held a red kite there for four days over open water. Above that height the air stops helping. She went past it. She went up the heat that rose off a fortress full of stoves and past the end of the heat, and the rain stopped, and the moon stopped being above her and was beside her, and then there were two of them on the same dark, one over the other, the way she had once seen two moons on the same water from a cliff with her fingers going soft.

The door was still open. It had been open since the gorge. She had gone through it once and folded everything that came in, and what she had folded was this: a wingspan, a tail, a chest that could sing. The fold held. Her body knew the way a fold holds, along the crease, and it knew that a crease can be run a second time.

She ran it.

The sky became a page, and the page turned.

## 飛過江水泱泱 — over the wide water

What lies between stories is a dark that reads you.

It had read a wind once, with her doors blown open, and turned her over like a bookseller checking a spine, and let her pass. It read a bird now. It began at the tail and worked forward. It found the fold and read the fold. It found, laid along the inside of the left wing, a warmth that was a woman and was weather and was both, and it stopped there and read that for a long time.

The crossing keeps what is made of sound.

Yangyang sang.

She had the whole song now, both halves, the calls and the answers, from a girl on an overlook and a woman in a courtyard two hundred years dead. She took the first call. *The Xuanling flies.* The dark took the words as they left her and let her keep the tune. She took the next. *Over the wide water.* Her chest was a bird's chest. It ran out three words in.

The note went on.

It came up through the wing, round and full, the way it had come through a flute on a wall on festival night, and the dark, which had been reading a bird, found itself reading a note with two breaths in it, and hung there choosing which breath to take.

They went on like that. Her tune, its breath. Where a bird's lungs hit bottom the Wind poured; where the Wind thinned, the bird carried. The song was built for two, one voice calling and the other answering, and they had stopped taking turns somewhere over the first shelf and were singing the thing whole, from both sides of the one throat.

The toll came anyway. It came off the right wing, one feather at a time, the ones the dark decided were made of something other than sound. Each one went in silence. She felt each one go.

She kept singing. The Wind kept still. That was the whole of the arrangement: the note, and only the note, from both of them, because one scrap spent on anything else and the wing would fold, and they would both go down into the space between spines, where the dark files what it has finished reading.

⁂

The shelves went past.

Fourteen of them, spines out, each a whole telling, and each with a house on a lane and a courtyard with a post in it. On one shelf a light had been left on, and the light pulled at her like a hand at a hat brim, and she let it pull and went past. On another the spine was scorched along its whole length and the pages stopped mid-line and the rest was ash margin. She slowed there. The Wind went cold along the wing. The dark leaned in to read the scorch.

The rest between one line of the song and the next is a silence with a value written under it, and if you leave the value off, something fills the rest for you. The dark filled it. It filled it with black water standing at second-floor shutters, and a library going up for three days, and a red cap turning in a current, and it offered the whole of that back to the woman in the wing as if it were hers to have again.

She had heard it before. She had heard it in a burned library in a pavilion made of faces, the same *no* that had killed every person in a gorge.

An Outrider counts. Yangyang counted the rest. She held it to its written length. She came in on the beat.

*The mountains so grey.*

The Wind took the answer. The scorched shelf went past.

⁂

Which shelf was home. That is the difficulty with fourteen houses on fourteen lanes: they all have the post. She could have told them apart by the cut on it, if the dark had let her land. The dark kept her flying.

So she listened for the thing she had learned in the gorge, on her knees, with a stone in a dead girl's hand and a kernel keeping time.

The song is how you call the bird. Somebody has to answer.

On thirteen shelves the song had two halves. On one, the second half was missing, and had been missing for years, because the person who used to answer it had walked out of the kitchen while the dough was under its cloth and gone west with a shell in her bag and had become the thing this song was calling for.

That was the one.

The call came up off it at dusk, every dusk, the same three syllables into a lane, from a gate that somebody opened to look down the road and closed again.

*Yangyang.*

The Wind heard it.

Yangyang felt her hear it. The whole wing went still in a new way, the way a bird goes still in cupped hands, the way a stillness has a centre. Three feathers went off the right side in the same breath and the wing held anyway, because the thing lying along the left side of it had just been given, after years of being a difference between two pressures, somewhere to go.

*The bright moon.*

She turned toward the voice.

## 山茫茫，明月光 — the mountains vast, the bright moon

> **CLERK'S NOTE.** *The border of a printing admits one traveller on sight, and that traveller is wind. The border of this printing found a wind that had come home and read her all the way down and gave her back what a border owes its own: a name, and the shape that goes with the name, and the one condition a story can enforce, which is that it has only ever had one of her. The bird it read as what she was, which is a messenger, and every border admits a messenger on the understanding that the message is delivered and the messenger goes back.*
>
> *From this line the record calls the woman by her name, which is Yangyang, and calls the bird what the border let her keep. The court is aware that the reader will find this hard. So did the bird.*

⁂

The bird came down the way she had gone up, all at once.

Landing was the part she had skipped. The shallows took her badly: water, stones, a wing folded under, the left one, and the cloth bundle she had carried against her breast the whole way going into the lake beside her and bobbing there, two shells in one wrapping, tied the way you tie a thing you intend to untie once a year.

And out of the fold of the wing, the air over the shallows stood up.

It stood the way something stands that has been kneeling. It stood higher than a wind stands and kept standing, and it had shoulders.

She had shoulders.

The lake ran off them. That was the first thing: water, going down the outside of her, cold on skin, in lines. Then the weight. She had been a difference for years and now she was a sum, and the sum went straight down through two feet she had forgotten the length of, into stones, and the stones rolled.

Her knees went into the lake. Her hands went in after them and found the bottom and closed on it. Closed. Fingers, five and five, shutting on gravel, and the gravel stayed in them.

"Slowly," said the bird, from the shallows, with her wing in the water. "The stones roll."

The woman lifted her head.

The moon was up over the eastern rank and it was one moon. It was low and orange and enormous, the harvest moon, the one that comes up looking like a lamp somebody has set in a window, and it was hers. It had risen over this lake on the night the library burned and on every night between, and it was the moon of her own printing, and she knew it the way you know the ceiling of the room you were born in.

She opened her mouth to say so.

What came out was the note. The low one, the one the flute had made in the frost month, a quarter of itself, cracked, and then a shapeless sound.

She tried again. She reached for a word at the old rate, one at a time, braced for it to cost, and the word came free. The purse stayed whole. She stood in the shallows with her mouth open, waiting to be charged, and the next word fell out because the door was open and every word she had was standing behind it, and the next, and the next, and they came in a heap, *cold* and *moon* and *shore* and *here*, until her throat, which had been a finger's width of a door for years, closed up on the flood and she choked on her own voice and bent double in the lake.

The bird got her feet under her and hopped.

The woman's hands came up out of the water and found the bird and closed.

Feathers. Warm, dry underneath, a heartbeat going very fast, and her fingers shut on all of it the way fingers shut that have been empty for years, and the bird made the sound a bird makes when it is being held too hard.

She let go. It took a moment. The fingers opened one at a time, the way they had opened once at the rim of the world with a shell in them, and she looked at them opening.

Then she put both hands over her face, and she found that her face was wet, and that the wet was warm and was coming out of her, and that this was a thing bodies did. She had been made entirely of leaving for so long that she had forgotten the body's own version. She knelt in the lake of her own Jinzhou with a harvest moon on the water and cried, badly, out loud, with a throat, for the first time since a flooded street, and the bird stood in the shallows with her wing folded wrong and let her.

⁂

The bird's voice had a bird's size. She used it sparingly.

"Left hand. On my back. Lightly."

The woman put her left hand on the bird's back and pulled.

"Lightly."

Her hand went light. They got as far as the sand. The bundle came out of the water on its own, nudged along by something in the shallows that could have been a wave. The woman sat on the sand and looked at her feet, which were bare, and long, and had been walking for years on air, and had a scar across one knuckle of the right hand that she had put there herself with a shovel on a far bank at night.

The bird put her beak into the wet cloth and pushed it against the woman's hip.

"Yours."

The woman looked at the bundle. She looked at the bird's left wing, which was folded now, correctly, because the thing that had lain along it had stood up out of it, and had a ragged gap from shoulder to wrist where a blade had gone in two hundred years and a world ago.

She reached for the gap. Her hand stopped a finger's width short. She had reached for things across that distance for a long time.

Then she closed the distance, and laid her palm flat along the missing feathers, warm, the way a hand is laid along a wound that has stopped bleeding.

"…師妹," she said.

Younger sister of my school.

The bird's head came round. She had been called the other thing for a year, across a table and a wall and a shore, and the word had only ever travelled one way, because the other way had been rationing.

She held very still under the hand. Then she put her head down against the woman's wrist, and the woman felt the small mechanism of a bird's breathing against the inside of her arm, and out on the water the moon went on being hers.

## 月光照著影子長 — the moonlight makes the shadow long

Jinzhou sat in its bowl of water and mountain with its lamps going gold from the north side first.

Fewer of them. She counted, out of a habit that belonged to the bird and had stayed in the body somehow, and stopped counting, because a count that comes out short is a report, and she was a long way from fit to file one.

The beacon stair goes up from the shallows in the long soft rows of grass the keepers leave alone. She had gone up it as wind in another printing at a run behind a girl with a bird on her hat. She had gone down it in this one, once, on her knees at the fourth watch, to tell a lake what she was carrying. She stood at the foot of it now with her hand on the rail and looked at the first step.

Feet go up stairs one at a time. The knee bends, the weight goes forward, the other foot arrives. She knew this the way she knew the names of vegetables, from being taught it in a street that was under water now.

She lifted her foot and put it on the first step and leaned forward to go up the way she had gone up things for years, which was to lean on the air and let the air take her.

The air let her fall.

She went down on the stone with her chin, and her teeth met, and there was blood in her mouth, warm, iron, hers.

The bird had gone up three steps ahead. She came back down them one at a time, hop, hop, hop, and stood on the step beside the woman's face.

"Weight on the foot," she said. "Then the other one. You've got weight now. Use it."

The woman lay on the stair and breathed.

"…Around," she said.

The bird waited.

"Everything. I go." She swallowed blood. "Around."

"Yes."

The bird waited. Then she opened her wings, which was a thing she could still do, and beat them once, low, so that the air off them went along the stair at the height of a woman's shoulder.

It was a very small wind. It was enough to lean on for the length of one step.

The woman leaned on it, and the knee bent, and the other foot arrived.

They went up the beacon stair like that in the dark, step and beat, step and beat, a magpie's pace, and the woman's shadow went up ahead of them in the moonlight, long, the way the song says a traveller's shadow goes, and every so often the wind off the wings gave out and the shadow sat down hard on the stone and got up again.

⁂

At the grey hour the fishermen were on the public stair with their nets, and they looked, and made room the way that stair has always made room, and kept their counsel.

The lower city stood dry. The stain stood on it. A line at the height of the second-floor shutters ran along every wall the length of every street, brown, sharp-edged, years old, where a lake had stood for a season and gone back. Below the line the plaster had been patched. Above it, the old paint. You could read the whole flood off the walls at a glance, the way a healed cut shows you where the blade went.

Across the square where the library had stood there was a wall with a gate in it, and behind the wall a row of young trees, and a bench.

She stopped in front of the bench.

*Held*, the city had said afterward. The way you say a rope held.

She looked down at her hands.

They hung at her sides, open, with lines in the palms she had put there on a road with a shovel, and a scar across one knuckle, and empty, and she stood in a square in Jinzhou at fish-belly white and looked at them the way the word had always meant you to.

The bird stood on the bench.

"Shijie."

She kept looking at her hands.

"One of us said it on the cliff. I've decided it was you." The bird's chest was small; it took her two breaths. "*I am still trying to get home.* This is the wrong direction for that."

The woman closed her hands. Slowly. They shut all the way, and the palms were empty, and she felt them be empty, which was a thing you could only feel with a palm.

"West," she said.

The bird came down off the bench.

She turned her back on it.

## 海內兮傳四方 — carry the call to every quarter

The post road runs west out of the youngest gate along the river and then leaves the river and climbs. She had walked it once as a woman with a bag and once as a wind with a shell, and she found that a body remembers a road with its feet and a wind remembers it with its whole length, and both of them had remembered the milestones closer together.

Where the road first leaves the water, there is a shrine that accepts things. The keeper's bowl stood inside the door, and the keeper stood behind it, a man with a beard gone white who had been a man with a beard going grey when a woman with a pack had held a wet red cap against her stomach in his doorway and asked him whether the rain came through his roof.

The cap was on its nail.

It had dried. It had faded, red gone toward the colour of brick, the torn stitching along the crown mended by somebody with a different thread. It hung above the door with a paper charm on either side of it and it looked like what it was, which was a thing a family had lost and a stranger had brought and a shrine had kept.

She stood under it. She had hands. She raised one.

She stopped it a finger's width short, and held it there, in the exact place a wind stops, the place past which a touch takes a wind apart, and then she brought the hand down and turned it over, palm up, empty, and held it under the cap a moment the way you hold a hand under something to see whether it drips.

It was dry. It had dried years ago.

She let the hand fall.

The keeper had seen the bird. He had been looking at the bird for some time, with the particular stillness of an old man deciding whether he is seeing a thing his grandmother told him about. He looked away from it and at the woman under the cap, and he did what keepers do, which was fetch a cup.

She took the cup and held it under her face and breathed.

Steam off cold water is a very small thing. She bent over the cup and took the part that rises, the way she had taken every cup for years, by the door hinge, on a roof rail, in a wide bowl beside a stove, and waited to be finished.

"You tilt it," said the bird, from the doorstep.

She tilted it. The water went into her mouth and some of it went into her, and the rest went down the wrong way, and she coughed water onto the shrine floor with the keeper's hand on her back between the shoulder blades, and the bird drank her own cup off the step in three neat throws of the head, the way a bird drinks, correctly, and looked at her.

The keeper wiped the floor. He looked at the bird again on his way up, and at the cap, and at the woman.

He said the thing keepers say, which is: "Same road?"

"West," she said. It was one of the words that came easily. She had one or two.

He nodded. He went back to his bowl.

⁂

The road climbed, and the land it climbed through was scarred.

She had walked it when the scars were fresh. They had healed the way Tacet scars heal, which is by going violet-black at the centre and silver at the raised edges and staying like that, and the fields lay between them in whatever shape the scars allowed. Some of the fields were sown. Some had a wall around them and a house at the corner with smoke coming out of it. Some had a house at the corner with the roof in.

People saw the bird.

They came out to the road for it. An old man at a well stood with his bucket forgotten on the rope, looking up. Two women with a cart stopped the cart. They left the woman walking to herself, a thin barefoot woman with her hair coming loose and lifting in a lane where everything else was still; there are many such women on the roads of a country that held. They spoke to the bird, or to each other about the bird, in the old words, the ones the eastern counties keep for a thing last seen before anyone living was born.

A child came out of a doorway with a bowl in both hands and stood in the road and looked at the bird and then at the woman.

"Where are you from?" the child said. Laughing. The way children ask it.

The woman opened her mouth.

She reached for the word and it was gone. She felt the shape of the place where it had been, the way a tongue finds the place where a tooth was. Whatever the word had been, she had paid it across a counter in a printing quarter for one evening of a printed table, and a founder had lifted it out of her with bamboo tongs and read it aloud as it went into the tray, and it was in that tray, in that city, in another book entirely, and her mouth had a gap the shape of it.

"…Far," she said. That one she had kept.

The bird, on a gatepost, put her head back and sang the first line.

The child looked up. "Look," the child said, to the sky, and the sky, being a sky, went on being one.

⁂

At a relay station a woman put a bowl of rice in front of her, hot, and a pair of chopsticks across it, and stood with her arms folded to watch it be eaten, because that is what the woman at that relay station does, and has done for anyone who comes in off that road looking the way this one looked.

The woman at the table bent over the bowl and breathed.

The steam went into her. It was good. It had rice in it and the char of the pot and a little oil, and she took it the way she had taken a year of steam off a third bowl at a corner table, and sat back, finished.

The relay-keeper unfolded her arms.

"It's for eating."

The woman looked at the chopsticks. She picked them up. She had held chopsticks for twenty years before she held a sword; her hand knew them the way it knew the sword. The hand closed. The sticks crossed. Rice went over the rim and onto the table and the bird ate it off the table, one grain at a time, tidily, and the relay-keeper watched the bird and watched the woman and went into the back and came out with a spoon.

The woman got four spoonfuls in. Her mouth was a stranger to her. Rice has a texture, a thing steam had spared her for years, and it sat on her tongue and asked to be dealt with, and she dealt with it badly, and her eyes ran, and she put the spoon down.

"More for the bird, then," said the relay-keeper, and gave the bird the bowl.

⁂

Wind keeps going. A body sleeps, and a body that has forgotten it needs to goes down like a lamp when the oil runs out, all at once, in the road, at noon, with the sun on it.

The bird sat on her hip until she woke.

Then the bird scolded her, roundly, in a voice the size of a moth, and the woman lay on the warm road and listened to it and found that she was laughing. It came out of her with a throat in it. It was the wrong size, too big, a real laugh, and it frightened her so much that she put her hand over her mouth, and the bird stopped scolding and looked at the hand.

"You do that now," the bird said.

The woman took the hand away.

"Loud," she said, wonderingly.

"You'll get used to it."

⁂

The river past the fourth milestone had lost its bridge in the year of the flood and had a ford now, marked with poles, and the water at the ford ran to the waist.

She walked into it the way she walked into everything, expecting to go over.

She went under.

Cold, brown, moving, the bottom somewhere below her, because she had leaned on the water to carry her and the water had shrugged the way water does. She went down into it with her eyes open and her hair going up around her face, and every way a body gets out of a river had gone from her, because for years she had been a thing a river passed through.

Somewhere above the water a bird was singing.

One line, the long one, the one with the tide in it. It came down through the brown the way a note comes down through two hundred years of mud, thin and bright and tinny, and she heard it, and she understood that a note stays exactly where it is sung, and that she could stand up toward it.

She stood up.

The water came to her waist. It had come to her waist the whole time. She stood in the ford with the river going past her on both sides and the bird on a marker pole with its wings half open for balance, and she coughed the river out, and then she put both hands flat on the surface of the water, and pressed, and the water gave under her palms and closed over them and gave again.

"I could go," she said, "over this."

"You go through it now."

She looked at the far bank. Twenty paces.

"Through," she said, testing the word for size.

She went through it. It took a long time. The bird sang the whole way, both halves, the calls and the answers, alone, in a voice with two breaths' worth of chest in it, and the woman came up the far bank on her hands and knees and lay in the grass with her whole body shaking, and the shaking was cold, plain cold, a body's cold, which has a cure.

The bird landed beside her head.

"Autumn," the bird said. "The nights are cool."

"…Yes."

"I said I'd carry you when they were."

The woman turned her face in the grass. There was a bird beside it with a gap in one wing and river water on her feathers and a carrying capacity of about one shell.

"You are," the woman said.

## 喚阿爹阿孃 — call to Father, call to Mother

Mingting's walls stood.

They had stood when she left and they stood now, with a new course of stone along the top on the north side where something had come over once and been put back, and the gate was open, and the gate-keeper looked at a barefoot woman and a bird and waved them through the way you wave through a thing you have decided is somebody else's business.

The lane.

Her feet knew it and the rest of her was learning, and the two of them argued the whole length of it, so that she walked it in stops and starts, putting her hand on walls she had last touched with a hand years ago and touching them now, one after another, plaster, brick, the corner post of the noodle shop with the notch in it that she had put there at nine with a stolen knife. The bird went ahead and came back. The bird went ahead and came back.

The courtyard gate stood at the end of the lane where it had always stood.

It was on the latch.

She knew that from four paces away, because a gate on the latch hangs a finger's width off true, and she had spent a year in another printing reading gates for exactly that. Somebody left this gate on the latch. Somebody left it the way you leave a door unlocked while declining to say for whom.

She put her hand on it.

Wood. Grey, weathered, warm from the last of the sun. She had come through this gate as wind and as a girl with a pack and as a woman with a bag and mud drying white on her boots, and the gate had opened for all three, and it opened now, inward, under a hand that had forgotten how much a gate weighs and pushed it too hard.

It swung. She went with it. Her foot found the threshold stone, which is a thing a body catches on and a wind goes over, and she caught on it, and went down across the sill into her own courtyard on her hands and knees, and stayed there.

The bird landed on the gatepost above her.

⁂

The post stood where it had stood all her life.

Black-grey, iron through and through, holding twelve years of her: the shallow years, cut above cut above cut, a childhood of mornings in tenths of inches, and above them all a guest's single stroke, three inches deep, the grain parted like water, the measure she had left home short of and had aimed at for everything else. The wooden sword leaned at its foot. Somebody had oiled it. The courtyard had been swept.

The kitchen door stood open, spilling gold.

Inside it, at the table, with one hand in a flour jar feeling for the measure, a woman looked up, because the gate had made the sound the gate makes.

She had aged. There was silver at both temples now, a whole handspan of it, and the line above her eyebrow had become two lines, and she wore an apron with a pocket that had been patched and patched again in three different cloths, and she was holding a scoop, and she stood in the doorway of her own kitchen with the lamp behind her and looked across the courtyard at a woman on her hands and knees on the threshold stone with her hair coming loose and lifting in a still yard, and a bird above her on the post.

The woman on the threshold lifted her head.

Suisui's face did the thing it did before it decided.

"….Yangyang?"

She said it the way she said it every evening. That was clear from the shape of it: a name into a lane, at the hour the light goes, three syllables with a question on the end, said so many times that the mouth did it before the rest of her had caught up. She had opened this gate at dusk and said this into the road and closed the gate again, every evening, for years, and every evening the road had answered with the same silence.

The road had brought something back.

Yangyang opened her mouth. The throat did what it did. She had been practising for eleven days, on a stair and a road and a riverbank, and what she had was one or two words at a time, and the choosing was out of her hands.

"…Jie."

The scoop went onto the table. Suisui's voice went on ahead of her, the way it did, in the sentence it had been keeping for this and had said a thousand times to an empty lane.

"You should have sent ahead."

She was already coming.

"I've had the water on."

She crossed the courtyard. She crossed it the way she had crossed it once with a bag still in the air, before the bag could reach the ground, and this time the air was empty: a woman on a threshold with her hands open and the ground bare in front of her, and Suisui reached her before her hand could reach the ground and went down onto her knees on the swept stone and got her arms around her sister.

⁂

The bird, on the post, had been holding the woman's balance since a lake.

She had done it with the air off her wings on a stair and a road and a bank, one step at a time, a small wind at the height of a shoulder for a woman who kept leaning on air. She had been doing it, by reflex, across the length of the courtyard, holding the sitting shape of her upright by a breath.

Suisui's arms took the weight.

The bird stopped.

Letting go was the delivery.

⁂

All the familiar distances were wrong. Her sister's shoulder should have met her forehead. Her hands should have found the folded cloth at Suisui's waist.

They did.

Her forehead came down into the place under Suisui's chin where a younger sister had once put her face in a market, asleep, while somebody tried to pay. Her hands went out along her sister's sides and found the apron and closed on the fold of it, cloth, warm, with the give of a body behind it, and held. The distances were right. Every one of them. She had reached for them across a room in another printing with everything she had left and found that a wind can reach a shoulder and pass straight on through the reaching, and here was the forehead, coming down, and here were the hands, closing, and a body underneath all of it that took the weight and adjusted.

Here was one adjustment. And another.

Suisui's right hand came up behind her head and found the shape of it under the loose hair and stayed there. Her left arm went around and pulled, and a shoulder flinched, and the arm moved at once, higher, gathering above wherever the hurt was, because that was what her hands did and had always done and had been doing to the air for years. Her thumb crossed Yangyang's cheek.

It left flour there.

Yangyang felt it go on. A streak of it, dry, along the cheekbone, from a thumb that had been in the jar when the gate spoke. The last thing she had seen in this courtyard before she went west was a little flour on this thumb, rubbed on by accident across a table while a woman said *I want to know where you stop*, and she had looked at it, and then she had chosen the hour when her sister would be at the market and gone.

It was on her face now.

She made a sound into her sister's collar. It was all throat.

"I know," Suisui said, into her hair. "I know. There."

She was crying. Yangyang could hear it, the way you hear it in somebody who goes on talking through it because stopping would be worse. She could feel it, too, which was new; she could feel the shake in the arms around her, slight and determined and beyond the older woman's control.

"There. And there." Another adjustment. A hand flat between the shoulder blades. "Look at you. Look at your feet. Where are your shoes?"

Yangyang lifted her head an inch.

"Here," she said.

Suisui's hand stopped moving.

She had asked, years ago, across a table with dough on it, for one thing: *send me the address, even if you only stay a few days. I like being able to picture the room.* She had been picturing rooms since. Rooms above canals and rooms two villages on and rooms with a window that faced the wrong way, every room a letter could have gone to, and every letter had stayed in the drawer, because the person she was picturing them for had turned into weather.

"Here," Yangyang said again, and put her flour-streaked cheek back down against her sister's shoulder, because that was the whole address and it had taken her years and a bird to deliver it.

Suisui held very still.

Then she laughed. It came out wet and broken in the middle and it was, unmistakably, the laugh from a rainswept courtyard that a woman going soft at the edges on a cliff had once heard from very far off, the one with grey in it.

"You can stay longer than a few days," she said.

⁂

The bird counted, out of habit. Out: two. Back: one woman in her sister's arms on a threshold stone, and one bird on a post that had a cut in it three inches deep, and the count came out wrong, because a register counts people.

She let it stand.

Suisui looked up. She had been aware of the bird the way you are aware of weather; now she looked at it properly, a blue-black bird the size of a magpie with a sheen along its plumes and a gap in one wing, sitting on the practice post at the exact height of the guest-stroke, watching her with its head on one side.

Suisui wiped her hands on her apron. Both of them, front and back, carefully, the way you clean your hands before you touch something your grandmother told you about.

"And who," she said, "did you bring."

Yangyang turned her head against the shoulder to look up at the post.

She tried. The name was right there. The world had given it to her at a border with the rest of her, and it had only ever had one of it, and it stayed where the world had put it.

"…Yangyang," she said.

"Yes, darling." Suisui's thumb moved on her cheek and spread the flour further. "You. I meant the bird."

The bird looked at the two of them for a long moment. Then she hopped down off the post, onto the swept stone, and walked over, the walking part of a magpie's progress, and stood beside Suisui's knee with her one good wing folded and her ruined one held a little out from her side, and said, in a voice pitched to carry over a wall and dropped now to the size of a kitchen:

"…The other one."

Suisui looked down at the bird that had just spoken.

She looked at it for the length of time it takes a practical woman to decide that a thing which has happened has happened, and then she reached down, with her clean hand, and put one finger under the bird's chin, and lifted it, and looked into an eye that was very dark and had, at the back of it, something she had seen every morning of her life in a mirror and had stopped seeing years ago.

"…Well," Suisui said. "You'll want feeding too."

## 喚翎鳥，勿望，勿忘 — call the bird; do not wait, do not forget

The kitchen was smaller than she remembered, because she had remembered it from the eaves.

The board stood on the table with the dough on it, under its damp cloth, risen. Beside the dough, along the far edge of the board, there was room. A clear space the width of a second batch, floured, waiting. She had seen that space once from a doorway, with the shadow of the lattice moving across her boot, and gone out.

Suisui had been leaving it. That was plain. The board had a pale place along that edge, worn smooth by years of being floured and brushed clean, and the flour lay on it fresh, put there tonight, put there every night, for a second batch that was going to be shaped when somebody came back.

Yangyang sat where her sister put her, on the bench nearest the door, and looked at the board, and put out her hand, and put her thumb into the flour on the empty side.

It was soft. It gave. It went into the whorl of her thumb the way flour does, and she took the thumb back and looked at it, white, and then thought of the streak on her own cheek, and understood that she had two of them now, one from each of them, and that the space on the board was waiting for her.

She cried at a kitchen table with flour on her thumb.

Suisui put a bowl in front of her and went on talking.

"That's the end of the barley. I'll get more tomorrow. Old Ho's son has the stall now, he weighs light, you have to watch him. Eat."

Yangyang bent over the bowl and breathed in.

The steam came up warm into her face, barley and the pot, and she took it, and sat back, and Suisui, who had been reaching for the second bowl, stopped with her hand on the shelf and turned round and looked at her sister sitting back from a full bowl with the expression of somebody who has just been fed.

She came and sat down opposite. She picked up the spoon that was in the bowl. She filled it, and held it out across the table, the way you hold a spoon out to a child who is refusing dinner on principle.

"Open."

Yangyang opened her mouth.

Barley. Soft, grain by grain, on a tongue that had had rice sat on it once at a relay station and had failed the rice. The spoon came back full. She took that one. Suisui watched her chew with the exact attention of a woman who has fed a great many people and is teaching one of them what a mouth is for, for the first time.

Then Suisui got up and went to the pot on the stove and scraped the bottom of it, the browned part, the part that sticks, into the bowl, and pushed the bowl back across.

"You always wanted that bit."

Yangyang looked at the browned rice at the bottom of a bowl in her sister's kitchen.

She ate it with her hands. Suisui let her.

The bird ate off a dish on the floor by the stove, properly, in throws of the head, and finished, and looked up, and Suisui looked down at the bird and then at her sister with barley on her chin and a hand in a bowl, and said, to the room, in the voice she had used across the years for a great many disappointing household developments:

"At least one of you eats."

⁂

The basin was the same basin.

Suisui set it on the floor by the bench and knelt beside it and lifted one of her sister's feet by the ankle the way you lift something that might be broken, and lowered it into the water.

The water was warm. Yangyang felt it arrive at the foot a full breath after the foot went in, the way feeling arrives in a hand that has been asleep. Then it was warm all at once and too much, and the foot came out of the basin on its own and she sat with it dripping in the air between them.

Suisui put it back.

"Leave it."

She washed it. She had a cloth, and she used it, along the sole where the stones had been and the road had been and the beacon stair had been, and the water went brown and she changed it, and while she changed it she looked at the foot she was holding, and then up the leg of it, and at the hand on the bench, and at the scar across the knuckle that had come home from a far bank, new since the day a girl with a pack left, and at the whole of her sister, thin, worn through at the elbows, with the skin gone fine at the temples the way skin goes that has had years of weather across it.

"What did you do to yourself."

Yangyang looked at her own foot in her sister's hands.

"Wind," she said.

Suisui's hands went on with the cloth. She was quiet long enough for the water to still.

"Then you've had enough of it for one life," she said. "Give me the other one."

⁂

The old room had been kept.

The quilt was the old quilt, aired; the peg by the door stood bare; the window stood open a hand's width toward the courtyard, the way somebody leaves a window who has a reason. Suisui put her into the bed and pulled the quilt up and stood back.

Yangyang lay rigid.

A body in a bed lies on the bed. A wind at the eaves ties itself off on one blue thread and stays the night at anchor, over the sleeper, a being made entirely of leaving moored by choice. She had done that for a year. She had been the anchor every time, and tonight she was the sleeper, and the bed took her weight in a way that felt like being held by something that held one way only, and she lay on top of the quilt with her eyes open and her hands flat, and lay there.

Suisui watched for the length of a lamp being trimmed.

Then she lay down on the bed as well. She did it the way she had done it when they were children and one of them had a fever, on top of the quilt, shoes off, an arm out, a settled matter.

"Come here."

Yangyang turned. Her face went into the hollow under her sister's chin, and Suisui's arm came round, and the bed stopped being a thing that held her and became a thing that her sister was lying on, which was different, and her hands found the apron Suisui had forgotten to take off and closed on it.

The bird landed on the bedpost, where a hat used to hang in another printing, and folded herself.

After a while Suisui began to hum.

It was the one for summer fevers. *Cool water, cool water, the well's gone deep.* She had it from the same mother, in the same house, and she hummed it badly, flat in the place where it goes up and stays up, the way she had always hummed it, and Yangyang, lying with her face in the hollow, felt the old thing happen in her chest: the breath gathering to pour, to take the note where the lungs would give out, to carry.

Her breath stayed in her.

The note went up and stayed up and came down, and it was all Suisui's, flat and complete, and Yangyang lay under it and let it be somebody else's for once.

A strand of hair had strayed across her mouth.

Suisui's hand came up and moved it. Off the brow, back. The way mothers do it. The one handhold a wind has, given to a woman lying in a bed by somebody with a whole hand, and Yangyang, who had done that gesture ten thousand times with a finger's width of air and had last had it done to her by a mother in this house, went to sleep under it before the hand had finished.

## 待到秋夜涼，送我回故鄉 — when the autumn nights turn cool, carry me home

In the morning she lay listening to her sister cross the courtyard.

The broom. The gate, opened, a look down the lane out of habit, closed again on the latch. The scoop in the jar. The bird, somewhere, having an opinion about the broom.

She got up. It took a while. She went out into a courtyard with the sun on the east wall and a woman sweeping it and a bird on the post, and she went to the post and stood in front of it, and looked at twelve years of herself in tenths of inches, and above them the guest's stroke, three inches deep, still the measure.

She put her palm flat over the old cuts.

They were rough under her hand. Twelve years of a child's mornings. She had aimed at the one above them for everything she had ever reached and she had reached everything else, and she stood with her hand on the post and felt the grain of it, and left the measure where it was, unmet, because it would keep.

Suisui went on sweeping.

The wooden sword leaned at the post's foot, oiled, edge to the east.

Yangyang looked at it and left it there.

⁂

Suisui burned the first tray.

She burned it because she was talking. She was telling the bird about the north wall and what had come over it, and then about the winter the well froze, and then about a neighbour's goose, and she had her back to the oven the entire time, and the tray came out black along one edge and she looked at it and said "well" and put the second tray in and went on talking.

Yangyang sat on the bench nearest the door with her hands in her lap and watched her sister set the table.

One pair of chopsticks. Another. A dish, on the floor by the stove, for a bird.

Suisui set them the way their mother had set things, with an unhurried exactness that she had learned in this room, and she set the second pair at the end nearest the door, at the seat that had had a fifth pair laid at it once in another printing by a mother who said nothing, fluently, and then she stood back and looked at the table and did the thing their mother had done, which was pretend the count had escaped her.

Yangyang sat in the chair.

She sat in it the way she had been told to on a burning slope by a woman with a hole in her side: in it, on it, with the weight going down through her into the seat and the seat taking it, at a table with a bowl in front of her with steam coming off it, and the steam did what steam does in still air, which was rise.

She leaned toward it herself.

And the wind sat down.

⁂

They ate. Yangyang got most of it in. Suisui talked, and Yangyang tried the words she had, one and two at a time, and some of them fell in the right places, and when one did Suisui went on as if a whole sentence had arrived, which is a thing sisters can do.

"Where did you stop," Suisui said, at the end. Lightly. Spooning the bird's dish. "After here."

Yangyang put down her spoon.

"A window," she said. She had been building it since the river. "In Jinzhou."

"Whose?"

"…She left it open."

"Who did?"

Yangyang looked at the bird by the stove, who had stopped eating and was looking back.

"Me," she said.

Suisui looked at her sister for a moment across the table. Then she looked at the bird. Then she laughed, the one from the courtyard, and wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist and picked up the burned tray to scrape it.

"Well," she said. "That sounds like you."

⁂

At dusk the bird went up onto the eaves.

She stood on the ridge tile the way people stand on platforms, checking a distance, and the distance she was checking was east, past the wall and the scars and the river without a bridge and the youngest gate and the lake, past the fold in the sky, toward a bowl of water and mountain where a woman was buying vinegar, and a window that would not latch, and a bowl being kept by a stove by a sister who had promised to warm it once.

Yangyang came out under the eaves and stood below her.

She had shoes now. Suisui's. They were the wrong size.

"When it's cold," she said, up to the roof. It took her three tries to get it out in one breath. "Go."

She reached for the next word. Her mouth stayed open around the place where it had been.

The bird looked down.

"Home," the bird said.

"…Yes."

"Suisui says that. Mine does."

"…Both."

The bird put her head on one side. She had a bird's chest, and a bird's purse of words, and she had been spending them carefully since a river. She had one she had been keeping.

"Same time tomorrow?"

Yangyang stood in her own courtyard, in her sister's shoes, with flour on her cheek that had stayed there all day, and her hair lifted, in a still yard, and settled.

The word came free. The purse stayed whole.

"Tomorrow," she said.

## Colophon

*entered at Jinzhou at the winter solstice, from a single copy; the court keeps the register open*

**I. From the Almanac Bureau, Eastern Copy.** The entry **接風, the Welcoming Wind** stands as written. Beneath it, in a later hand, the keeper has added a note in the margin and declined to strike it: *Left the register's jurisdiction with a household in the seventh month. Reported by that household as departed from Xuanfang on the night of a moon, upward, in a bird. Returned the winter solstice: the bird, one only. Filed under weather.* ~~*struck.*~~ *The Bureau has no column for a bird. It has left the second column blank, and put a bowl beside it.*

**II. From the Mingting registry, another printing.** *The court has not seen this register and does not expect to. It sets down what the copy reports: that a household in that city which had been entered for some years as* one *was re-entered at the frost month as* two, *with a remark in the assessor's hand that the second was a bird, and a further remark that the assessor had considered* livestock *for some time and had entered it, in the end, under* family.

**III. From Bookbinders' Row.** The compartments stand as they stood. The founders have set this edition around them. They observe, for the register, that the woman in this account reached for a word on a road and found it gone; that this street has eleven empty compartments; that the founders counted them again tonight and found the same number; and that they are keeping them empty against the day somebody comes to the counter and asks for one on behalf of a sister.

**IV. The register.** A courier with nothing to sign with delivered the copy, and was asked by this office which of the two women in it came home, and stood on the counter for some time, and then sang the last line of a song the founders did not know, and left by the window.

The office has copied the song into the register. It has every sort for it except the last.

The court keeps the register open.

Chapter 1: The Split

Mei chewed her pre-torn piece of scallion pancake, looked around the table at the nine women currently guarding her hands, and tried a different angle.

“Okay,” she said, setting her teacup down squarely inside the perimeter Yangyang had cleared for her. “Since I’m not allowed to touch platters, kettles, doors, or mules—what’s everybody doing today?”

Amy still had her chin hooked over Mei’s good shoulder from behind. She didn’t lift it. She reached over Mei’s collarbone to steal a corner of the pancake right off Mei’s dish, popped it into her mouth, and spoke around it with studied, terrible casualness.

“Shopping.”

The table went quiet for half a beat. Even Cartethyia’s steamed bun stopped wobbling on her crystal laurel.

“Shopping,” Iuno repeated, her teacup pausing an inch from her lip. “For what. Bolts? Cable? A second tarp?”

“Nope.” Amy straightened up, shoved her hands into the pockets of her jacket, and rocked back on her heels. A single strand of pink hair over her ear flickered once toward white static and was immediately bullied back into color. “Just shopping. Like, normal shopping. Looking at stuff in stalls and buying something that doesn’t hold a gate shut or run a relay.” She looked at the ceiling beams, then at the teapot, then vaguely toward the window. “Normal girls do that. On days off. I’ve heard.”

Nobody looked at each other too hard. In this room, when someone said a thing while staring at the rafters, manners required you to keep your eyes on your porridge.

Four names. Ten years in a five-meter cockpit at the bottom of the world. Three years running a midnight pirate broadcast out of a crawlspace with borrowed condensers. A god’s hand up the back of her coat. She had negotiated grain tariffs with frontier magistrates and kept a hundred and forty thousand people breathing over an open channel through the worst night of their lives, and she had never once stood in front of a table of cheap ribbons and argued about whether blue was her color.

From the far end of the bench, where Lupa was still trying to nose the collar of her shirt aside, Chisa set her chopsticks down square across her bowl.

“I’ll come,” Chisa said.

Lupa’s ears twitched upright. “Huh? We went shopping in Septimont. You hated it. You stood outside the boot-maker’s like a sentry for forty minutes.”

“You bought four pairs of gauntlets and tried to fight the mannequin,” Chisa said in her completely level voice. She turned her head toward the sound of Amy’s jacket rustling. “I would like to be an ordinary schoolgirl today too.”

Cartethyia gasped, clasping both hands under her chin, which made the bun on her head slide two inches left. “Chisa! You were a schoolgirl! You have the outfit and everything!”

“This is the winter uniform,” Chisa said, touching the hem of her dark pleated skirt once with two fingers. “My mother kept the summer one in a box in my closet for twenty years. With camphor leaves. She told me she aired it out every June in case I came home before the heat broke.” She picked her chopsticks back up. “I never wore it to a market. In Honami, the stalls only reset to the day the rain started.”

She didn’t say it sadly. She said it the way she reported the distance from a ford to a marker post, and then she ate a piece of pickled radish.

Lupa stopped sniffing her neck. Her heavy arm stayed around Chisa’s shoulders, her thumb rubbing once, hard and brief, against the cloth over Chisa’s collarbone before she grunted at her bowl. “Buy something stupid. Not black.”

“I like black,” Chisa said.

“Buy something stupid and blue.”

Mei was already halfway off the bench, her palms braced on the table, her face lit up like a festival lantern. “Oh, I’m coming! I’m so good at markets! I’ll carry the bags—”

Four pairs of eyes snapped to her middle.

“—I mean I won’t carry the bags!” Mei corrected at top speed, holding both hands up in surrender. “I’ll *supervise* the bags! I’ll walk behind you and point at shiny things and haggle! I’m great at haggling—”

“You paid forty credits for a wooden frog that didn’t even croak when you scraped its back,” Ciaccona muttered into her notebook.

“He had a cold!”

Mei’s left boot was already sliding backward to clear the bench. She didn’t make it.

Under the table, Iuno’s foot slid out, hooked the front rung of Mei’s stool by the ankle, and pulled it—and Mei—two inches closer to her own knee. At the same time, Iuno’s one hand left her teacup and closed lightly, without any force at all, around Mei’s wrist.

Mei looked down at the hand, then up at Iuno.

Iuno didn’t scold her. She didn’t mention the hundred and forty stairs, or the stitches, or the forty-eight hours in the ditch. She just looked at Mei, and then let her eyes flick, once, barely a fraction of an inch, toward Amy’s back.

Amy was busy retieing the drawstring of her hood, which did not need retieing. Her shoulders were up around her ears, just a little, braced for the moment her mother turned a two-girl walk down the street into a parade with a guardian at the front of it.

*Look at her, darling,* the High Priestess murmured inside Iuno’s skull, unexpectedly gentle. *Even when she isn’t saving the world, she tries to chaperone it.*

*She’s trying to make up ten years in an afternoon,* Iuno thought back. *It doesn’t work that way.*

Mei looked from Iuno’s eyes to the back of Amy’s pink head. The light in her face dimmed by a notch—not hurt, just the slow, reluctant arithmetic of a mother realizing that the thing she wanted to give was the exact thing in the way.

Mei let her weight sink back onto the stool. Her shoulders dropped.

“...Oh,” Mei said softly. “Right. Yeah. Girls’ day. Totally.” She tried to put the big grin back on and only got it halfway up her cheeks before remembering last night’s rule and letting it go. She looked down at her pancake. “You guys are gonna have fun. The lower market’s got that stall with the spun sugar shaped like badgers.”

Amy’s hands stopped on her drawstring.

She turned around slowly. She looked at Iuno’s hand still resting over Mei’s wrist, then at Mei’s slightly deflated posture on the stool. For a second her mouth opened on the old track—*You can come if you want, Ma*—and then she shut her teeth on it with an audible click, because they were all trying very hard not to lie to each other this week.

Instead, Amy stepped back to the bench, leaned down, and bumped her forehead squarely against the side of Mei’s head, right above her ear.

“I’ll bring you back a badger,” Amy said. “Even if it looks like a rat. Especially if it looks like a rat.”

Mei’s head tipped into the bump immediately, her golden eyes crinkling. “Name him first.”

“I’m not naming candy, Ma. You eat it, and then it’s a tragedy.” Amy straightened up and jerked her chin toward the end of the table. “Come on, Chisa. Before she tries to give us her allowance.”

“I have money!” Mei called after her, reaching for her pocket and coming up with two steel washers, a dried jujube, and a crumpled receipt from Yuan’s Flavors.

She stared at the washers in her palm and sighed, her chin propping itself on her free hand. The bench felt very large all of a sudden.

It lasted approximately four seconds.

*Slam.*

Cartethyia’s palms hit the table hard enough to make the porcelain swans jump. The steamed bun tumbled off her laurel, bounced once off Ciaccona’s lute case, and was caught three inches above the floorboards by Lupa’s bare foot and kicked back up into Lupa’s waiting hand.

“Mei!” Cartethyia declared, leaning over the teapot with her blue eyes wide and blazing with chivalric purpose. Two new crystal thorns sprouted audibly from her crown. “Take me to the general.”

Mei blinked up from her washers. “...The garrison commander? Suisui’s already ruining his morning—”

“Not him!” Cartethyia pressed a fist to her sternum. “Corporal Honk! A commander who held the ladder against superior numbers and defended the cabbage stems at dusk! He cannot remain a corporal, Mei. It’s an insult to the order of chivalry. A soldier of that mettle must be knighted! Properly! With a sword and an oath!”

Mei’s mouth fell open. The washers rolled off her palm and plinked onto her plate.

Slowly, starting at her boots and travelling all the way up to the tips of her ears, Mei lit back up.

“Carte,” Mei breathed, grabbing Cartethyia’s hands across the teapot. “He has one black feather in his tail. Like a plume.”

“A plume!” Cartethyia’s voice went up half an octave. “Does he have a crest? Every knight needs a crest! And a squire! You said the mule has trousers on his head—that’s practically a tabard!”

“Turnip would be an amazing squire! He’s already got the stoic face down!”

“And the other ten geese can be the honour guard!”

“Oh, the third one from the left—the fat one with the crooked bill—he’s definitely a sergeant-at-arms—”

At the far side of the table, Ciaccona dropped her forehead straight onto her open notebook. “No. No, I forbid it. Carte, it is a goose. It’s livestock. It poops on ladders. You are the Blessed Maiden of Rinascita—”

“And therefore qualified to confer knighthood!” Cartethyia beamed, already scrambling off the bench and patting her hips for a scabbard she wasn’t wearing. “Cia, come! Every dubbing requires a herald and a fanfare!”

“Play your own fanfare on a comb. I have dignity.”

“You played a song for a turnip crate on Tuesday!”

“That was acoustic calibration!”

Iuno pinched the bridge of her nose with two fingers. The crystal at her left shoulder chimed a small, resigned note as she breathed out.

*Two golden retrievers, darling,* the High Priestess sighed, leaning back against a metaphorical velvet cushion inside their head. *And not a single leash in the province strong enough to hold them.*

*At least the goose is on the ground,* Iuno answered.

She lowered her hand and rapped her knuckles twice against the wood beside Mei’s plate. Both Mei and Cartethyia froze mid-bounce and turned to look at her with identical, terribly innocent expressions.

“Terms,” Iuno said.

“Yes, ma’am,” Mei said immediately, sitting up straight.

“You do not lift the goose. You do not lift the mule. You do not set foot on a ladder, a shed, a wall, or anything higher than a thick rug.” Iuno held up one finger after another. “Your Terminal stays in your pocket—not in a bucket—charged, with the ringer on high. You sit down every twenty minutes whether the goose is knighted yet or not. And if a single tile on a single roof looks crooked to you, you keep your hands in your pockets and walk away.”

Mei repeated it back, ticking her own fingers with grave, courtroom solemnity. “No goose-lifting, no mule-lifting, rug height only, phone out of the bucket, sit down constantly, ignore the roofs. Back before dinner.” Her eyes were already sparkling sideways at Cartethyia. “Carte, do we have ribbon? A knight needs a sash.”

“I’ll use my spare hair-tie!”

“If it’s a sash,” Ciaccona muttered into the wood without lifting her head, “the goose is going to strangle itself and then we’ll have to eat it, and then Cartethyia will cry for a week.”

“We are not eating Sir Honk!” Cartethyia gasped.

“Corporal,” Yangyang corrected mildly from beside the teapot. “Until the ceremony concludes, the rank remains Corporal.”

Suisui closed her eyes briefly and took a slow sip of her tea.

Across the bench, Lupa finished the floor-bun in two bites, licked her thumb, and stood up, stretching until her spine popped three times in a row. Her tail gave a wide, lazy sweep that cleared the edge of the table.

“Right,” Lupa said. “While the idiots are playing with the poultry, I’m doing the wash.”

Silence fell over the table again, slightly more bewildered this time.

Amy, lingering by the door with Chisa, blinked. “...Laundry? You?”

“Have you smelled this room?” Lupa jabbed a thumb toward the back bedroom door. “Mei came home wearing half a drainage ditch, and then she wore *my* wool blanket over it all night. It smells like wet mule and ginger sweat. And Carte threw up in a planter yesterday and got congee on two of the sheets. Somebody’s gotta take the bundle down to the public troughs before the whole garrison thinks we live in a barn.” She hooked her thumbs into her belt, looking weirdly pleased about it. “Then I’m going to the cobbler on the third terrace. My left heel’s worn down to the nails from braking on that stupid spire.”

Chisa nodded once from the doorway. “You’ve been listing half an inch to the left since Wednesday.”

“Have not.”

“The floorboards creak louder on your left stride.”

“The floorboards are cheap,” Lupa grumbled, though her ears went back half an inch in acknowledgment.

Iuno looked at the empty teapot, then at the morning light widening across the threshold, and stood up, smoothing her skirt with her one hand. “I’ll go with you.”

Inside her skull, the High Priestess choked on phantom wine. *Beg pardon? The public wash-house, darling? Boiling lye and red-knuckled washerwomen hitting sheets with paddles? We once had four junior vestals whose sole liturgical duty was steaming the hem of our chasuble!*

*The vestals are on another continent,* Iuno thought calmly, picking up her coat from the crate. *And if I stay here, Suisui will draft me into auditing the quartermaster’s salt books.*

*...A fair point. Carry on, then. Try not to get suds on the wool.*

Lupa eyed Iuno’s bare crystal shoulder, then the mountain of dirty blankets she was already kicking into a pile at the foot of the pallets. A slow, sharp grin split the wolf’s face.

“You’re one-armed, princess,” Lupa said. “What’re you gonna do, glare the dirt out?”

“I will supervise,” Iuno said dryly, slipping her good arm into her coat sleeve and letting the other side hang pinned as usual. “And I will fold. Your idea of folding is rolling a blanket into a ball and sitting on it.”

“Works, doesn’t it?” Lupa bent down, gathered four heavy wool blankets, two linen sheets, and Mei’s ditch-stained coat into a single enormous bundle, and hoisted the whole thing onto her left shoulder with one hand. “Come on, then. You can carry the soap.”

That left the three at the head of the table.

Suisui set her porcelain cup down with a tiny, final *clink* and picked up her folding fan from beside the napkin-swans.

“The clerk,” Suisui said, her voice as warm and pleasant as a spring breeze over a pond, “informed the garrison commander twenty minutes ago that the twelve tarpaulins and three hundredweight of slate allocated to the lower west terrace were ‘misrouted during the fog’ and are currently unrecoverable.” She tapped the closed fan once against her open palm. “I am going to go take a walk and find them.”

Yangyang was already standing, adjusting the sit of her hat over her blue-marked brow. The cerulean feathers at her collar settled into a neat, professional line. “I’ll come, Jie. If they moved three hundredweight of slate down the terraces, a cart axle will have complained about it. Someone heard it.”

Suisui’s smile softened around the edges as she looked up at her sister. “You don’t have to work on your day off, Yangyang. It’s only an audit.”

“I’m not working,” Yangyang said in her flat report voice, offering Suisui her arm to rise. “I’m walking with my sister. My ears happen to be attached.”

Ciaccona slapped her notebook shut, slid her pen into its loop, and slung her lute strap over her shoulder in one smooth motion. “Make room. I’m coming too.”

Suisui raised a brow. “With a lute?”

“Especially with a lute,” Ciaccona said, grinning like a fox as she stepped over the bench. “My dear Director, when twelve tarpaulins vanish in a border town, you don’t find them by looking at the books. The books have already been lied to. You find a tavern with damp plaster, you sit in the corner, and you play a local folk song in the wrong key until a drunk mason gets so offended he tells you whose cousin built a new porch roof last Tuesday.”

Suisui studied the bard for two seconds, then inclined her head an inch in genuine professional respect. “I’ll buy the first pitcher.”

“Now we’re talking.”

The storehouse emptied out all at once, the way a dovecote empties when somebody claps in the yard.

Lupa kicked the door open with her heel, the massive laundry bundle blotting out half the doorframe while Iuno stepped out beside her with a block of yellow lye soap tucked primly into her coat pocket like a prayer book. Mei and Cartethyia went out right behind them, arguing earnestly over whether Turnip’s trouser-hat counted as heraldry or headgear, Mei pausing on the threshold just long enough to shout “Phone’s on! Rug height!” back over her shoulder at Iuno’s retreating spine. Suisui, Yangyang, and Ciaccona turned left at the top of the stone steps toward the administrative quarter, Yangyang’s cyan tail-ribbons catching the cold updraft off the gorge and streaming out behind them like twin pennants.

Amy and Chisa took the right-hand stair down toward the market terraces.

The morning air was sharp enough to sting the inside of the nose, smelling of woodsmoke, wet slate, and the clean, metallic mist thrown up by the giant water spires turning at the edge of the cliff. High overhead, past the drifting shreds of valley cloud, Sentinel Hsin’s Astrolabe Core hung in its bronze calipers, turning with slow, noiseless dignity against the pale winter sky. Below it, Xuanfang Hold was shaking itself awake after the siege. Carpenters were up on scaffolds hammering fresh pine boards over shattered lattice; a woman in wool mittens was sweeping pulverized tile out of a gutter into a bamboo dustpan; and all along the stepped stone avenue of the second terrace, canvas awnings were being unrolled and tied back to their iron rings.

Amy walked with her hands jammed deep in her pockets, her boots hitting the frost-rimmed flagstones a fraction too fast.

Every ten paces, she looked sideways at a stall, calculated the foot traffic, the seller’s posture, and the margin on the goods—because nineteen years of scraping by and three years of managing Fleet Snowfluff’s crowd logistics didn’t turn off just because you wanted them to—and then remembered she wasn’t supposed to be doing that.

“Okay,” Amy said out loud as they passed under the red-painted archway of the commercial ward. “Ground rules. No hardware. No rations. Nothing that could reasonably be described as ‘tactical’ or ‘load-bearing.’ If Lupa would approve of it, we keep walking.”

Beside her, Chisa’s footsteps were even and unhurried, the wooden sheath of her giant scissor-blade absent from her back for once, leaving her shoulders light under her dark school coat. Around her left wrist, Sumika’s red string showed just past her cuff.

“Understood,” Chisa said. “Where do we start?”

“Uh.” Amy stopped in the middle of the paving, letting a porter with two baskets of cabbages trot around them. Ahead of them stretched forty yards of open stalls: bolts of dyed cotton flapping in the wind, trays of lacquer hairpins, stacks of coarse yellow paper, jars of candied plums, and a table covered in woven straw frogs and painted clay whistles.

Amy stared at the row. Her fingers twitched inside her pockets.

In an auditorium, you worked the front row first to set the energy, then pitched your voice to the back corners so nobody felt left out. In Nyx, you checked the primary thrusters, cleared the feather-blades, and locked the collar. Here, there was no sequence. Nobody was waiting for her to speak.

“Left side,” Chisa said calmly. “The wind’s coming off the gorge from the right. The stalls on the left have their braziers lit under the tables, so it’s four degrees warmer along the wall.”

Amy blinked at her. “You can feel a brazier from thirty feet away?”

“I can hear the charcoal popping,” Chisa said, and stepped toward the first awning on the left. “And the woman at the hair-ornament table is stamping her feet less than the man selling ink.”

They drifted up to the hair-ornament table.

It was spread with a square of faded blue velvet, pinned with rows of brass butterflies, carved wooden combs, faux-jade clips, and silk flowers with little glass beads trembling on wire stamens. The seller—a middle-aged woman with three shawls wrapped around her shoulders—spotted two girls approaching without an adult and immediately sat up straighter, her professional smile clicking into place.

“Ah, young misses! Fresh stock out of the trunks this morning, not a scratch on them from the puppets! Look, look, real kingfisher feather work from the south, and these cellulose clips just came up from the Jinzhou road before the fog closed—”

Amy leaned over the velvet. She knew an opening pitch when she heard one; she’d written fifty of them for sponsors on the midnight radio. Still, she reached out, picked up a comb set with three little pink glass peaches, and held it up against the side of her head, angling her chin toward Chisa.

“Well?” Amy asked, pitching her voice bright and bouncy, the way girls in Academy dramas always sounded in the courtyard scenes. “How do I look?”

Chisa stood half a pace behind her right elbow.

To Chisa’s eyes, Amy’s face was what every face had been since she was small: a soft, shifting smudge of warm peach and pale light under a brighter blur of pink hair, with no clean lines where a nose or a mouth ought to settle. She didn’t look at the glass peaches at all. She listened.

“Put that one back,” Chisa said levelly.

The seller’s smile stuttered. “Now wait a minute, miss, that’s genuine—”

“You held your breath when you lifted it,” Chisa told Amy, ignoring the seller completely. “And your weight stayed on your back foot. You’re doing the voice you used at breakfast when you told your mother you weren’t cold.”

Amy’s arm froze with the comb against her hair. Slowly, she lowered it and stared at Chisa.

“...That’s creepy, you know,” Amy muttered, though the theatrical bounce dropped right out of her shoulders. “You’re worse than Auntie.”

“I’m not prophetic,” Chisa said. “You just sigh through your nose before you perform.” She tilted her head a fraction to the left, listening to the small, sharp *tink-tink* of metal against metal at the edge of the velvet cloth where the wind was catching a rack. “Try the third row from the front. Near your left hand. You touched it when we walked up and then moved away because you thought it was too small.”

Amy looked down at her left hand.

Hanging from a little brass stand were half a dozen simple hair clips made of stamped copper, enamelled in bright, unapologetic colors—acid green, cobalt, canary yellow—each one shaped like a tiny, slightly lopsided four-pointed star. No glass beads. No dangling wires to catch on a cockpit headset.

Amy picked up the acid-green one. It was the exact loud, obnoxious shade of green painted across Nyx Ravenhollow’s left shoulder plate.

She didn’t hold her breath this time. Her thumb rubbed over the smooth enamel bump of the star, and her weight settled squarely onto both heels.

“Okay,” Amy said quietly. “Yeah. This one.”

The seller recovered her footing instantly, leaning across the velvet with both hands clasped. “Ah, an eye for modern craft! Imported enamel from the coast, very rare, the artisan only makes ten a month—four hundred credits for the pair, or I’ll let you have the single for two-fifty since the hold’s celebrating!”

Before Amy could even open her mouth to deploy her Vantry Cross haggling routine, Chisa spoke in her flat, unembellished voice.

“There are nineteen of them in the tin box under your stool,” Chisa said. “I could hear them sliding against each other when you uncrossed your ankles. They’re stamped sheet copper, not cast, because the edges ring at a higher pitch when the wind hits the rack. And you charged the girl ahead of us forty credits for a pair of the yellow ones before we reached the awning.”

The stall went dead silent except for the charcoal popping in the brazier under the table.

The seller stared at Chisa’s calm, unblinking face, then down at the hem of her skirt where the tin box was indeed peeking out, then back up.

“...Thirty for the single,” the woman muttered, holding out her palm. “And don’t stand near my table when the noon crowd comes through.”

Amy paid the thirty credits in a daze, clipped the little acid-green star into the hair above her left temple, and walked ten paces down the terrace before she started laughing so hard she had to lean against a stone balustrade.

“‘I could hear them sliding when you uncrossed your ankles!’” Amy wheezed, clutching her stomach. “Chisa! You’re a menace! Why does Suisui even bother bringing ledgers when she could just bring you?”

“I wasn’t trying to haggle,” Chisa said, stopping beside her and resting one hand on the cold stone rail. “She was lying about the box. It seemed inefficient to let her finish.”

“You’re my new favourite person. Don’t tell Lupa, she’ll challenge me to a duel.” Amy reached up and touched the copper clip in her hair. It sat flat and solid against her temple. Her hair didn’t static once under her fingers. “Okay. My turn. Pick a stall.”

Chisa turned her head slowly along the avenue, letting the noise of the terrace wash past her: a blacksmith’s apprentice grinding a cleaver two streets down, children kicking a shuttlecock near the water channel, the thick, sweet smell of boiling sugar coming from a copper kettle three awnings ahead.

“There,” Chisa said, pointing past the cloth merchant toward a narrow wooden booth hung with painted paper signs. “The man with the wooden ladle. He’s stirring something thick enough that the scrape against the pot is a half-beat slower than water.”

It was a drink stall, wedged between a stationery shop and a cobbler’s alley. A fat iron cauldron sat over a low fire, full of hot, spiced pear syrup bobbing with red jujubes and translucent white snow-fungus, while a second tub held crushed ice from the mountain caves and jars of bright purple taro paste, sweetened condensed milk, and chewy tapioca pearls the size of marbles.

Amy squinted at the hand-lettered board. “Wait. He’s doing iced taro milk *in the middle of winter*? With frost on the ground?”

“Schoolgirls drink iced things in winter,” Chisa said with total conviction. “In Honami, the girls from the track club bought crushed-ice melon sodas at the station kiosk in January and complained that their hands were freezing the whole way home. I used to hear them behind me at the crossing.”

Amy stared at her for a second, and something warm and fierce clicked into place behind her ribs.

“Two iced taro milks,” Amy told the vendor, slapping coins onto the counter with a bang. “Extra pearls. Extra sweet. Put them in the tallest cups you’ve got.”

The vendor looked at the frost on Amy’s boots, shrugged, and scooped crushed mountain ice into two tall waxed-paper cups, ladling purple taro paste and sweet white milk down the sides before jamming wide bamboo straws through the lids.

They took them over to the low stone wall overlooking the canal—thirty paces down from the bench where Mei, Iuno, and Amy had sat in the rain two days ago—and hopped up to sit with their legs dangling.

The cups were freezing. Within ten seconds Amy’s fingers were red at the tips. She took a massive pull through the bamboo straw, got three tapioca pearls and a mouthful of slush at once, and immediately clapped a hand to the center of her forehead with a groan.

“Ow, ow, ow—brain freeze—right behind the eyes—”

Beside her, Chisa took a neat, measured sip, chewed two pearls thoroughly, and swallowed. Then her hand drifted up from the cup, past the dark band of her suppression collar, and pressed two fingers flat against the bridge of her nose.

“...Ah,” Chisa said, in the exact same tone she used for *Good*. “My forehead is numb.”

“Right? Why did the track club do this to themselves?”

“Because it tastes like sugar and purple,” Chisa said, and took another sip immediately.

Amy barked a laugh, kicking her heels against the stone wall. “Purple’s not a flavour, Chisa.”

“It is in a cup.” Chisa shifted her grip on the waxed paper, her bare fingers whitening against the frost forming on the outside. “And my hands are cold. That’s the second part. You’re supposed to complain about it now.”

“My hands are *freezing*,” Amy obliged loudly, shoving her cup into the crook of her elbow so she could blow into her curled fists. “If I lose a thumb to frostbite before noon, Auntie’s gonna make me sign a liability waiver with my teeth. And this straw is too narrow, the pearls keep getting stuck at the bottom and then shooting up like anti-aircraft fire.”

“You’re tilting the cup too far,” Chisa said. “Keep the straw vertical and half an inch off the base. You can feel the pearls roll against the bamboo before they seal the opening.”

Amy tried it. Two pearls came up smoothly, no vacuum lock. She chewed them, narrowing her eyes at the dark-haired girl beside her. “How did you figure the fluid dynamics of boba out in thirty seconds?”

“Vibration,” Chisa said simply. “When the straw seals against the bottom, the pitch of your slurp goes up a third.”

“I do not *slurp*.”

“Everyone slurps with a bamboo straw. That’s what they’re for.”

Down in the canal below their boots, a pair of ducks paddled past in single file, leaving neat V-shaped ripples in the dark green water. Further up the terrace, someone dropped a crate of tin pans with a tremendous clatter, followed immediately by a chorus of cheerful swearing.

Amy swung her legs once, twice, watching the condensation drip off the bottom of her cup onto the stone between her knees.

Without thinking about it, her right hand left the cup and slid into her jacket pocket. Her thumb found the zipper, pulled it down two inches, and touched the cold glass corner of her Terminal.

Mei had been gone with Cartethyia for forty minutes now. Forty minutes was plenty of time for two golden retrievers to turn a goose into an international incident, or for Mei to forget she wasn’t supposed to climb things, or for the stitches under her ribs to pull because she laughed too hard at a mule.

Amy’s thumb rested on the power button.

Beside her, Chisa didn’t turn her head. She just sat quietly with her purple drink, the red string on her left wrist swaying a fraction in the breeze off the water.

“Iuno gave her four conditions,” Chisa said to the canal. “And Yangyang is three streets away. If your mother steps onto a ladder, Yangyang will hear the wood creak before her second foot leaves the ground.”

Amy’s fingers went still inside her pocket.

She looked down at the waxed-paper cup in her other hand. Then she reached up with two cold fingers and touched the little acid-green copper star clipped into her hair.

Ordinary girls on a Saturday morning didn’t run comms checks on their mothers between sips of taro milk. They sat on walls with their friends, got brain freeze, and let other people hold the door for an hour.

Slowly, Amy slid her hand back out of her pocket, empty, and zipped the jacket shut with a clean, sharp *zzzt*.

Chisa’s shoulders relaxed by a millimeter.

“Good,” Chisa said, and held out her cup. “Race you to the bottom. Whoever gets brain freeze first has to carry the next bag.”

“You’re on,” Amy grinned, clinking her waxed cup against Chisa’s with a dull, icy thud, and took a reckless pull on the straw.

Chapter 2: The Rounds

The public wash-house on the third terrace smelled of boiled ash, wet wool, and four dozen arguments happening at once.

Steam hung low under the cedar rafters, turning the winter morning coming through the high louvers into thick, slanting bars of white. Two long stone troughs ran the length of the room, fed by copper pipes straight off the mountain’s thermal springs, and at every washboard stood a grandmother, a garrison soldier, or a refugee aunty beating the absolute daylights out of a wet sheet.

Lupa kicked the door open, breathed in a lungful of hot lye vapour, and grinned.

“Now this,” she announced, dropping the six-foot bundle of dirty blankets beside an open tub with a thud that made three washboards jump, “is a proper room.”

Iuno stepped in behind her and immediately paused at the threshold as a curl of damp heat hit her hair.

*My word, darling,* the High Priestess murmured, horrified. *The humidity. Our curls will drop within five minutes. We will walk out of here looking like a spaniel that fell off a barge.*

*Nobody here is looking at our curls,* Iuno replied silently, stepping out of the puddle by the door.

*That is an entirely separate tragedy, and we will get to it.*

A barrel-chested washerwoman at the nearest trough looked up from a pile of grey garrison tunics, her eyes travelling from Iuno’s fine wool coat to the jagged, iridescent crystal of her bare left shoulder, and clicked her tongue.

“Aiya, who brings a one-armed girl to haul wet blankets? You, sitting-stone by the brazier! Off! Give the pale miss the dry stool before she slips on the soap-water!”

A boy who had been warming his hands over the charcoal bucket scrambled up before Iuno could open her mouth. The washerwoman kicked a three-legged wooden stool across the flags with one wet clog, parking it neatly behind Iuno’s knees.

“Sit, girl, sit. Put the soap on the ledge. Let the big one with the tail do the scrubbing, she looks like she’s got shoulders for an ox.”

Iuno stood very straight. “I am perfectly capable of standing.”

“Standing gets in the way of the splash-zone. Sit.”

Lupa snorted so hard she blew suds out of the trough. She had already shucked her coat, rolled her sleeves up past her scarred elbows, and plunged Mei’s ditch-stained blanket into the scalding water with both hands. “Heard the lady, princess. Park it. Hand me the yellow brick when I yell.”

*An ox’s friend,* the High Priestess seethed, crossing phantom legs in the back of Iuno’s mind as Iuno, rather than start a diplomatic incident in a laundry, sat down on the three-legged stool with the posture of an empress on an ivory throne. *We are the frail cousin brought along for the airing. In Septimont, when we entered the baths, twelve attendants threw rosewater on the tiles and left the building backward.*

*In Septimont, nobody ever offered us a chair unless they wanted a tax exemption,* Iuno thought back, setting the block of lye soap neatly on the stone ledge beside her hip. *And the brazier is warm.*

For the first fifteen minutes, it was actually peaceful.

Lupa attacked the laundry the exact way she attacked an arena trial: with ferocious, rhythmic joy. She dragged the heavy wool up onto the ribbed stone slab, rubbed the lye soap across the mud-crust until it foamed thick and white, and hammered it with her knuckles, her tail keeping a steady, contented beat against the side of the trough. Every time she finished rinsing a sheet, she wrung it out with a twist of her wrists that would have snapped an iron bar and tossed it into the dry wicker basket at Iuno’s feet. Iuno pinned one corner of the warm, damp linen between her knee and the stool post, caught the opposite hem with her right hand, and folded it into crisp, mathematical quarters.

Then a young Warden at the far end of the trough dropped his scrub-brush.

He had been staring at Lupa’s right forearm—where the soap-suds had washed away a smudge of coal dust to reveal the old, white-seamed claw scar curving from wrist to elbow, right below the crimson brand of the Septimont Colosseum.

The Warden fumbled in his tunic with wet hands, pulled out his Terminal, and tapped the screen frantically before shoving it under the nose of the trader scrubbing socks beside him.

“Look,” the boy hissed, loud enough to carry over three running pipes. “Look at the arm. Look at the ears. I told you—I *told* you at the gate on Tuesday! That’s her!”

The trader squinted at the tiny screen, then up at Lupa, who had paused mid-wring with Mei’s grey shirt twisted like a rope between her fists.

The trader’s jaw dropped. He spoke in the broad, vowel-heavy cadence of the southern coast. “*Per gli dei.* The She-Wolf. That’s Lupa! Champion of the Seventh Games!”

The wash-house didn’t go quiet; it exploded.

Three kids who had been chasing soap-bubbles near the drain skidded to a halt. A garrison corporal at the second trough Vaulted over a basket of wet drawers, wiping his palms on his trousers. Within thirty seconds, half the room had abandoned their washboards and crowded into the aisle around Lupa’s tub, Terminals out, voices climbing over each other in the steam.

“No way—the one from the Finals clip?”

“My brother had forty silver on you against the Twin-Cleaver in the rain bout!” the trader shouted, thumping his chest with a soapy fist. “Forty silver! Paid for my mule!”

“Do the howl!” one of the kids yelled, bouncing on his heels. “My cousin has the ringtone! Do the arena howl!”

Lupa’s ears flicked back for half a second—the automatic perimeter check of an animal surrounded in a narrow room—and then she smelled nothing but lye soap, wet wool, and honest excitement. No blades. No scripts. Just a crowd that knew her name.

Her chest swelled. Her tail came up like a banner, and the wide, sharp, utterly shameless Septimontian grin broke across her face.

“Forty silver?” Lupa barked, tossing Mei’s twisted shirt into the basket and planting one wet hand on her hip while gesturing wide with the other, suds flying off her knuckles. “You cheap bastard, I was paying three-to-one on the rain bout! You should’ve bet the whole cart!”

The trader roared with laughter, and the corporal shoved a dry ration-wrapper and a grease-pencil toward her across the stone rim. “Sign this! For my squad down at the east gate—they won’t believe I did my wash next to the Champion!”

“Hold the paper flat, I’ve got lye on my thumbs—” Lupa took the pencil, scrawled *LUPA* across the wax paper in letters three inches high, and clapped the corporal on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him a step. “Tell the east gate their boots looked sloppy on Tuesday! And you—” She pointed a soapy finger at the kid who’d asked for the howl, dropping into a low, wide-kneed arena crouch right there on the wet flagstones. “You want the prelims howl or the Championship howl? Because the Championship one costs a copper!”

“I don’t have a copper!”

“Then you get it on credit!” Lupa tipped her head back toward the cedar rafters and let loose a short, ringing, chest-deep arena howl—not the Forte-lit war cry that shook walls, just the showman’s note that used to make sixty thousand people in the marble tiers stamp their feet in time—and the kids shrieked with delight while three washerwomen hammered their paddles against the troughs in applause.

Iuno had stood up when the crowd pressed in, mostly to keep her folded linen from being trampled.

She stepped back against the dry wall by the charcoal bucket, tucking the wicker basket behind her legs. Nobody looked at her. The trader’s elbow bumped her good shoulder as he leaned in for a better angle; he muttered, “Pardon, miss,” without turning his head, and shoved his Terminal into her right hand.

“Hey, do me a favour, wolf’s friend—tap the little round button when she puts her arm round my neck, yeah? Keep the thumb off the lens.”

Iuno looked down at the damp Terminal in her palm.

On the screen, minimized in the corner below the camera viewfinder, the video clip the young Warden had been showing around was still playing on loop.

It was grainy, shot from the third tier of the Septimont Colosseum two summers ago. On the tiny rectangle of glass, confetti fell like gold snow over the sand. A younger Lupa stood on the champion’s podium with her spear raised, howling at the sky while the crowd went mad. Above the podium rose the great marble dais of the Ephorate. Augusta stood on the left in black iron, holding half a golden medal.

To Augusta’s right, under the velvet canopy where the High Priestess had sat for thirty years, stood an empty bronze chair.

The camera panned right past it, smooth and unbothered, because nobody in the stands had noticed an empty space where nobody had ever been.

Iuno’s thumb hovered over the glass.

Inside her head, the High Priestess went very still.

*...Look at the canopy, darling,* the voice said, and all the theatrical iron had gone out of it for a second, leaving something dry and papery. *They didn’t even take the tassels down. That was our gold braid. We spent three weeks arguing with the weavers over the weight of that fringe so it wouldn’t blow into Augusta’s face during the invocations.*

*I know,* Iuno thought.

*Sixty thousand people in that bowl. When we stood up to bless the sand, the silence went all the way to the river gates. You could hear a swallow turn under the arch.* The High Priestess drew a long, phantom breath. *And now we are holding a grease-stained phone for a turnip merchant who called us ‘the wolf’s friend.’ I hate this century. I want a palanquin.*

Iuno looked up from the tiny empty chair on the screen. Through the gap between the trader’s ear and the corporal’s shoulder, she watched Lupa flexing her scarred bicep so the smallest kid could hang off her wrist like a monkey.

*Would you go back up the steps?* Iuno asked her quietly. *To the chair?*

The silence inside her skull lasted four full beats.

When the High Priestess answered, she was looking at something else entirely: a woman with a healing hole in her stomach and a grain of browned rice stuck to her cheek, standing pinned against the back of a wooden door at six in the morning, swaying them both side to side like a pendulum and whispering *My turn.*

*The chair was marble, darling,* the High Priestess said softly. *It was freezing in the winter, and nobody ever brought us ginger broth. Take the man’s picture before he drools on our sleeve.*

Iuno lifted the Terminal, framed Lupa and the beaming trader in the viewfinder, and tapped the button twice with her thumb.

As she lowered the device to hand it back, an older washerwoman at the edge of the circle—one of the Xianling evacuees, with a faded mourning band still stitched to her cuff—reached out and touched Lupa’s wet forearm with two gnarled fingers.

“You’re the one who held the south road at the gorge,” the old woman said, not loud at all. “My grandson came out on the carts. Thank you, Lady Wolf.”

The showman’s grin vanished off Lupa’s face as if someone had thrown a bucket of cold water over her head.

Her ears pinned flat back against her skull. Her tail dropped straight down and went rigid, doing nothing at all. She jerked her arm back toward the tub, grabbing a wet blanket with both hands as if it had personally insulted her.

“Road was in the way,” Lupa barked at the washboard, her neck going dull red under her collar. “Didn’t do it for you. Did it because I don’t like machines. Wash your own tunics, everybody, the water’s getting cold!”

While the crowd laughed, thinking it was part of the act, Lupa’s yellow eyes flicked sideways through the steam.

She saw Iuno handing the Terminal back to the trader. She saw the way the trader took it without looking at Iuno’s face, already turning away to check the photo, and she saw the slight, aristocratic tilt of Iuno’s chin—the exact, brittle angle Iuno used when she was pretending a room hadn’t just walked through her like fog.

Lupa didn’t sigh. She didn’t make a face.

She reached over, plucked the grease-pencil out of the garrison corporal’s fingers before he could pocket it, and took two long strides around the tub. She shoved her way right between the trader and the kid, planted her wet, soapy left hand flat against Iuno’s back, and pushed her half a step forward into the middle of the circle.

Then she jammed the grease-pencil into Iuno’s one hand and held out the corporal’s signed ration-wrapper.

“Sign the bottom,” Lupa said loudly.

The corporal blinked. “Uh—Lady Wolf, that’s for the barracks wall, we just wanted the Champion—”

“She’s on it or nobody’s on it,” Lupa said flatly, looking down at the boy with her yellow eyes wide and unblinking. “Who do you think kept the rain off the sand at the Seventh Games? She sat up top under the gold fringe. You lot forgot her. I didn’t. Sign the paper, Iuno.”

The wash-house went quiet around the tub. The corporal looked confused, squinting at Iuno’s silver-blue hair and her one bare crystal shoulder as if trying to place a transfer officer from another regiment. The trader scratched his beard, plainly assuming it was some private Septimontian locker-room superstition.

Nobody understood what Lupa had just said.

Iuno stood very still with the grease-pencil between her fingers. Her crystal stump gave one tiny, high chime against Lupa’s wet knuckles.

*...Well,* the High Priestess whispered, and her voice was suspiciously thick. *Perhaps we will allow the wolf one extra lump of sugar in tomorrow’s tea.*

Iuno braced the corner of the wax paper against the wicker basket with her wrist and wrote *Iuno Caecilia Severa* beneath Lupa’s enormous block letters, the descender hooking left, neat and permanent. She handed the wrapper back to the mystified corporal without a word.

“Right, shows over!” Lupa snarled cheerfully, scooping the last of the wet sheets out of the rinse-water and dumping them into the basket on top of the dry ones, completely ruining Iuno’s quarter-folds. “Out of the way, I’ve got a cobbler waiting and my left boot’s listing!”

Ten minutes later, the blankets were hanging on the public drying racks behind the bathhouse in the wind, and Lupa was sitting barefoot on a bench in a cramped, leather-smelling alley three doors down from the drink stall while an old man with brass spectacles hammered fresh hobnails into her left heel.

Iuno sat on an upturned nail-keg beside her, her coat pulled around her knees. The alley was quiet except for the *tap-tap-clink* of the cobbler’s hammer.

Lupa stretched her bare toes out in front of her, wiggling them in the cold air.

“Weird, isn’t it,” Lupa said to her big toe.

“What is,” Iuno said.

“Being famous on a Tuesday.” Lupa reached into her tunic, pulled out a flat, square leather case the size of a saucer, and flipped it open with her thumb. Inside, wedged into velvet that had seen better days and framed in a little ring of beaten brass, sat half of a heavy gold medallion, sheared cleanly down the middle by a broadsword. “Sixty thousand people screaming my name every Saturday for five years. And the best fight I ever had was in the dark at the bottom of a hole where nobody had a camera, and the only person watching was Mei.”

She snapped the case shut and tucked it back over her ribs.

“They don’t know the good part,” Lupa said simply. “The clips only got the noise.”

Iuno looked at the cobbler’s hammer rising and falling in the dim light. She thought of the empty bronze chair on the tiny screen, and then she thought of Amy’s small, fierce hand clamping around her wrist at the sixth bell, whispering *If you get up, the cold gets in the back.*

“Yes,” Iuno said quietly. “The clips only got the noise.”

Lupa’s tail slid across the dusty bench and dropped once, heavy and warm, across the toe of Iuno’s boot, and stayed there until the cobbler handed the heel back.

Four terraces down, where the stone steps gave way to packed mud and cabbage patches, the ceremony was not going according to liturgical precedent.

“Stand straight, Squire Turnip!” Cartethyia commanded, her voice ringing with solemn knightly ardour across Widow Lin’s drying yard. “A squire does not eat the heraldry during the invocation!”

Turnip the mule, standing patiently beside Old Man Zhao’s handcart with one faded blue trouser-leg still telescoped over his left ear like a crooked mitre, did not stop chewing. He had managed to get the corner of Widow Lin’s patchwork quilt—which Cartethyia had commandeered and tied between two bamboo clothes-props as a ceremonial pavilion—into the side of his mouth, and was working on it with slow, wet dedication.

“He’s nervous,” Mei explained gravely from her seat on an upturned pickle tub. She had a stick of charcoal in her hand and was drawing a coat of arms on the back of a split shingle while keeping her feet strictly at rug-height. “It’s his first dubbing, Carte. You can’t go straight from ditch-mule to Squire of the Lower West Terrace without a little performance anxiety. Right, Zhao?”

Old Man Zhao, sitting on the shaft of his cart smoking a dry pipe, nodded sagely. Beside him on the floorboards sat the fat clay jar of pickled plums Mei had forgotten two nights ago, already tied up with red string for her to take home. “Oh, aye. Very sensitive animal, Turnip. Hates bells. Loves pants.”

Around the edge of the muddy yard, the three Chen children had been joined by seven more from the neighbouring lanes, all of them perched along the low stone wall like a row of padded winter sparrows, kicking their heels and eating roasted sunflower seeds. Widow Lin herself stood in her doorway with a basket of clothespins on her hip, watching the proceedings with the bone-deep entertainment of a woman who had not seen theatre since the road closed.

Out in the middle of the dirt, ringed by ten fat white geese who were busy investigating a spilled bucket of turnip peels, stood Corporal Honk.

He was enormous. His chest was the size of a soup tureen, his orange bill was scarred from a long-ago disagreement with a badger, and from the center of his tail stuck a single, stiff, glossy black feather at a forty-five-degree angle.

Mei leaned forward on her pickle tub, resting her elbows carefully above her stitches, and pointed with the charcoal stick as the other geese waddled around him.

“Okay, keep track for the roster,” Mei told the children on the wall. “The fat one with the crooked bill by the puddle is Sergeant Waddles. The two who keep hissing at Turnip’s ankles are Lance-Corporals Nip and Tuck. The little grey-necked one trying to eat Carte’s bootlace is Private Crumb, he’s in charge of intelligence—”

“He’s eating mud, Miss Mei!” the smallest Chen boy shouted.

“Deep cover intelligence,” Mei corrected without blinking. “And the one standing on the cabbage stump looking judgmental is Quartermaster Quack, though Suisui says we’re not allowed to trust quartermasters today, so keep an eye on him.”

Cartethyia was not listening. She had untied her spare blue hair-ribbon, smoothed it across her knee, and drawn a willow switch Old Man Zhao used for driving the mule—since Mei had gently suggested that using an actual broadsword to dub poultry might result in accidental roast goose if the knight sneezed.

Cartethyia stepped forward into the center of the yard, her crystal laurel glinting in the pale sun, and held the willow switch vertically before her nose before sweeping it down to her side in a textbook Egla Town salute.

“Hear ye!” Cartethyia proclaimed, her high, clear voice bouncing off the half-tarped eaves of the Chen house. “Before the witnesses of the lower terrace, the Honourable Squire Turnip, and the Order of the Cabbage Stem! Corporal Honk of the Ditch Watch: for valour in the face of falling timber, and unyielding vigilance on the rungs of the ladder, step forward and receive thy spurs!”

Corporal Honk stopped preening his left wing.

He turned his head sideways, fixing Cartethyia with one round, black, utterly merciless eye.

Cartethyia took a solemn step closer, extending the willow switch and the blue ribbon. “Bow thy head, brave soldier—”

*HONK.*

It was not a polite farmyard noise. It sounded like a brass foghorn blown inside a rain barrel.

Corporal Honk dropped his neck parallel to the mud, spread two six-foot white wings until he looked like an angry snowbank, and charged straight at Cartethyia’s shins.

“Ah!” Cartethyia squeaked, hopping backward on one foot as the goose snapped at her hem. “Wait—no, Sir Honk, the sword goes on the *shoulder*, you aren’t supposed to parry it with your face—”

“He thinks the ribbon’s a snake!” Mei yelled from the pickle tub, laughing so hard she had to clamp both arms across her stomach. “Show him your posture, Carte! Geese respect height!”

“Height!” Cartethyia gasped, backpedalling into Turnip’s flank while the ten subordinate geese began honking in rhythmic, militant solidarity. “Right! Gravitas! A general responds only to a sovereign!”

Light bloomed across the cabbage patch.

The children on the wall gasped and dropped their sunflower seeds. Widow Lin nearly swallowed a clothespin.

In the space of a heartbeat, the petite girl in the muddy boots vanished, and out of the golden radiance rose Fleurdelys: nine feet of regal, amazonian splendour, her silver-white hair cascading past her waist, her crown of crystal thorns blazing against the grey sky as she towered over the clotheslines.

She looked down from her terrible height at the eighteen-pound bird at her feet, raised one imperious hand, and spoke in the deep, echoing register that had once shaken the inverted vaults of a cathedral.

“*KNEEL,*” Fleurdelys commanded, “*BEFORE THE BLESSED MAIDEN.*”

The yard went dead silent. Even Turnip stopped chewing the quilt.

Corporal Honk looked all the way up.

Then he beat his wings once, launched himself four feet straight into the air like a feathered cannonball, clamped his orange bill around the front of Fleurdelys’s glowing crystal laurel, and yanked it clean off her head.

“*MY CROWN!*” Fleurdelys roared—and halfway through the syllable her voice cracked right back into Cartethyia’s outraged squeak as she shrank down to five feet tall in a puff of sparkles. “Hey! Give that back! That’s part of my Forte!”

Corporal Honk did not give it back.

With the crystal laurel dangling from his beak like a stolen pretzel, the general of the lower terrace took a three-step running start up the shaft of Old Man Zhao’s cart, bounced once off Squire Turnip’s trouser-hatted rump, and flapped heavily up onto the low, half-tarped eaves of the Chen family’s roof.

He landed on the ridgepole with a triumphant *clack* of webbed feet, set the crystal crown crookedly over his own neck like a horse-collar, and honked down at the yard in total victory.

“He usurped me!” Cartethyia cried, staring up at the roof with her hair wild and crownless. “Mei! The goose committed treason!”

Mei was already off the pickle tub.

She didn’t even think about it. The reflex was older than Jinzhou, older than Septimont, older than the beach: something belonged to one of hers, it was twelve feet in the air on a rotten beam, and there was a stack of firewood stacked against the Chen house’s rain-barrel that would make a three-step scramble to the gutter if she caught the corner post with her left hand and hauled—

Mei’s right boot hit the bottom log of the woodpile.

Her left hand reached up and closed around the rough pine upright of the porch.

Across her abdomen, under the grey shirt, four inches of surgical thread pulled tight against healing muscle—just a sharp, familiar tug, the exact kind of tug she had ignored ten thousand times in two lifetimes while hoisting herself over ramparts and glacier crevasses.

On the stone wall behind her, the ten children leaned forward, waiting to see the hero jump.

Mei froze with one foot on the log.

In her head, clear as a bell over the honking of the geese, she heard the click of a wooden latch in a dark bedroom, and a nineteen-year-old girl sitting cross-legged with a muddy boot in her lap, poking her square between the eyes: *You don’t live in a two-room cabin under four kilometers of ice anymore, Ma. There isn’t just one of you.*

And she heard Iuno at the breakfast table, holding up four fingers: *Anything higher than a thick rug.*

Slowly, while Corporal Honk rattled her friend’s stolen crown against the ridge-tiles overhead, Mei opened her left hand and let go of the porch post.

She stepped her right boot down off the firewood and planted it flat in the mud beside her left.

“Mei?” Cartethyia blinked, trotting over with her hands out. “Aren’t we storming the ramparts? I can boost you—or I can go big again and grab him by the tail—”

“If you go big next to that porch, your elbow’s gonna take out the Chens’ chimney,” Mei said. She looked at her own empty palm for a second, let out a long breath that steamed white in the cold air, and reached into her coat pocket—not the bucket—for her Terminal.

She unlocked the screen, opened the household voice channel, and held the speaker up to her mouth while ten kids, an old man, a widow, a knight, a mule in pants, and eleven geese watched her.

She didn’t grin. She didn’t make a joke out of it first.

“Hey, guys,” Mei said clearly into the mic. “I’m at the Chens’ house on the lower west terrace. Corporal Honk stole Carte’s crown and he’s on the roof. There’s a goose, and I need a ladder.”

Two terraces up and half a mile east, in the back room of a cramped, smoky teahouse called the Three Willows, Ciaccona was committing a felony against a pentatonic scale.

She sat on a low bench beside a cracked plaster wall, one boot propped on a peanut barrel, her eyes half-closed in counterfeit rapture as she dragged a horn plectrum across her lute strings a full quarter-tone flat on every third beat.

At the table beside her, Suisui sipped lukewarm oolong out of a chipped cup without wincing once, her folding fan tapping a silent, patient *one-two-three* against her palm. Across from her, Yangyang sat with her back straight and her hat pulled low, her cerulean plumage collar tucked carefully inside a high scarf so the seven dockers and three off-duty masons at the neighbouring tables wouldn’t start throwing free fish at her.

They had started at the quartermaster’s storehouse at the second bell.

The ledgers, as Ciaccona had predicted, were spotless. Twelve heavy-gauge oiled canvas tarpaulins, stamped with the Ministry lotus in red lead at each corner, and three hundredweight of blue quarry slate: signed out at the fourth bell on Saturday to a carter named Old Ma, marked *Lost in Transit — Gorge Road Collapse*, countersigned by the clerk’s second cousin.

“The ink on the countersignature,” Suisui had observed mildly upon leaving the office, “was mixed with well-water from the third terrace, not the gatehouse cistern. You can tell by the lime bloom along the downstroke. They didn’t lose that cart in the fog. They unloaded it within four hundred paces of the market.”

Which had brought them to the Three Willows.

Ciaccona hit a B-natural where an A belonged, let it ring out like a dropped skillet, and then sang the second line of *The Boatman of Mengzhou* with the rhythm so badly mangled that a man playing dominoes near the stove physically flinched.

She kept going. Five minutes. Eight minutes. By the twelfth minute, she was playing the chorus in seven-eight time and humming flat through her nose.

*Slam.*

A thick-set mason at the corner table slapped his domino tiles face-down on the wood and spun around on his stool.

“Girl!” the mason barked, pointing a stubby, mortar-stained finger at the lute. “Did you buy that instrument off a dead man or are your thumbs on backward? The bridge goes *down* on the river-turn! *Down!* My grandfather sang that on the barges!”

Ciaccona stopped immediately, blinking her green eyes wide in wounded innocence. “Oh, pardon me, master! I’m from the coast, you see, and the damp in these lower lanes has warped my neck something awful! Why, every roof in this ward leaks like a sieve since the puppets came through—you can’t keep a string dry anywhere below the third gate!”

“Ha!” A second drinker by the window snorted into his rice wine. “Tell him, Little Six! Dry! If you want a dry string in this ward, you’ve gotta go sit on Boss Qiu’s new terrace over at the Lucky Carp!”

The mason scowled, waving a hand to shut his friend up, though he was too annoyed about the music to stop talking. “Shut your trap, tallow-head. Qiu paid good money for that overhang.”

“Paid his nephew’s clerk-fee, you mean,” the window-drinker muttered, leaning back so his chair creaked. “Four days ago the whole west lane’s begging for oilcloth to keep the frost off the babies, and Tuesday morning before the fog’s even off the steps, Qiu’s got three hundredweight of prime blue quarry tile going up over his kitchen extension and a double-layered canvas awning over the carp pond so his customers don’t get bird-droppings in their wine. Funny how the fog only lost the cart right outside his back gate, eh?”

Ciaccona didn’t look at Suisui. She just beamed at the mason, adjusted her tuning peg back to pitch with a flick of her wrist, and struck a chord so warm, clean, and resonant that the teacup lids on the counter hummed in sympathy.

“Ah, there it is!” Ciaccona said brightly. “The damp’s gone right out of it! Gentlemen, this one’s on the house.”

She launched into *The Boatman of Mengzhou* properly—sun-through-clouds, every grace note sparkling—while Suisui set three copper coins on the table and stood up without making a sound.

Outside in the narrow lane, Yangyang stopped at the corner of the drainage channel, tilted her chin up two inches, and closed her eyes.

Beneath the clatter of the teahouse and the distant roar of the water spires, she let her hearing drop down to the layer she had learned to find in the Snare—the quiet, persistent friction of things that didn’t belong where they were sitting.

Wind moving over old tiles made a dry, hollow *shhh*. Wind moving over fresh quarry slate, laid without proper mortar bedding because it had been put up in a hurry before sunrise, clicked on the third beat of every gust. And forty yards down the lane, where the alley widened over the canal, twelve squares of heavy oiled canvas were snapping in the updraft with a tight, drumhead thrum.

“Third door on the left past the bridge,” Yangyang said in her flat report voice, opening her eyes. “Twelve tarpaulins, stitched two-by-six on a bamboo frame. The slate is stacked on the flat roof above the kitchen, unmortared. And there is a sixteen-foot cedar roof-ladder leaning against the water-butt behind the privy.”

An old woman shuffling past with a basket of leeks stopped dead at the sound of Yangyang’s voice, peered at the blue mark on her brow above the scarf, and dropped into a hasty bow. “Blessings on the morning, Lady Xuanling! Tell the sky to hold off the snow till Friday!”

Yangyang’s ears went bright pink under her hat brim. Her feathers puffed out half an inch against the wool scarf. “I—I am only an Outrider, madam. Watcher. The snow is scheduled for Thursday night regardless. Please watch the ice on the third step.”

Ciaccona came strolling out of the teahouse door behind them, slinging her lute over her back and uncapping her pen in the same breath. “Nine minutes and forty seconds. That’s a personal best for a border garrison. How are we doing this, Director? Kicking the door in, or—”

“We are a commerce guild,” Suisui said pleasantly, snapping her fan open with a soft *whuff* as she turned down the lane toward the Lucky Carp. “We never kick doors. Carpentry is an unrecoverable expense.”

Boss Qiu was a round man in a brocade waistcoat who was standing on his newly shaded terrace admiring his carp pond when three women walked into his yard.

It took seven minutes.

Suisui did not raise her voice once. She complimented the glaze on his fish-tubs. She admired the thickness of the bamboo poles holding up the massive canvas awning overhead—stepping to the corner post just long enough to flip the bottom hem over with the tip of her closed fan, exposing the red-lead Ministry lotus stamped squarely on the underside.

“Such fascinating provenance,” Suisui murmured, smiling warmly into Qiu’s rapidly paling face. “You see, Master Qiu, the Zhaoming Commerce Guild underwrote the freight insurance on the Saturday relief convoy. Which means that anything ‘lost in the fog’ automatically reverts to Guild salvage jurisdiction under Maritime and Frontier Article Four. Now, if you found these twelve tarpaulins blowing loose in the street—as I am *certain* a man of your civic standing must have—there is no penalty at all, provided they are surrendered to the salvage auditor within the hour.”

Qiu swallowed hard, his eyes darting to Yangyang’s sword hilt. “And—and if there was a misunderstanding with the carter?”

“Then I would have to file a Form Seven with the Garrison Provost for receiving diverted military stores during a state of siege,” Suisui said, her voice full of genuine, sisterly sympathy. “Which carries a mandatory audit of all wine-tax receipts back to the third year of the calendar, plus confiscation of the premises used to store the goods. Your kitchen extension is very charming, by the way. Is the slate mortared yet?”

“Boys!” Qiu bellowed toward the kitchen door, sweat beading on his forehead despite the frost. “Get up on that shed! Bring the slate down! Untie the poles! Give the ladies the big ladder so they can reach the corner ropes!”

By half-past the third bell, the twelve tarpaulins were rolled into two neat cylinders on Boss Qiu’s own handcart, the three hundredweight of blue slate was stacked underneath them, and Qiu’s two apprentices were trotting down the lane toward the lower west terrace pushing the load while Ciaccona walked alongside, cheerfully adding a star to the margin of her notebook.

*The Audit of the Lucky Carp,* she wrote. *Zero casualties. One brocade waistcoat ruined by sweat.*

Yangyang walked at the rear, carrying the sixteen-foot cedar ladder balanced effortlessly across one shoulder so the apprentices wouldn’t drop it on the stairs.

In her pocket, against her ribs, her Terminal chimed once with the household priority tone.

Suisui stopped on the landing above the lower terrace, pulling her own device from her sleeve at the same moment Ciaccona tapped her earpiece.

Through all three speakers, over a background roar of honking poultry and screaming children, came Mei’s voice—clear, steady, and completely devoid of a fake laugh:

*“Hey, guys. I’m at the Chens’ house on the lower west terrace. Corporal Honk stole Carte’s crown and he’s on the roof. There’s a goose, and I need a ladder.”*

In the quiet lane above the cabbage patches, the three of them looked at each other.

Ciaccona’s mouth twitched. Suisui let out a small, real breath through her nose, the tension she’d been carrying in her shoulders since Mei vanished forty-eight hours ago finally unknotting by another notch.

Yangyang shifted the sixteen-foot cedar ladder half an inch inward on her shoulder, squaring her elbow, and pressed the transmit button with her free thumb.

“Watcher Yangyang,” she said in her flat, exact voice. “Copy. We are sixty paces up the stair with twelve tarpaulins and one ladder. Stay at rug height. Inbound.”

Chapter 3: Same Time Tomorrow

Sixty paces took forty seconds.

Yangyang came down the last flight of mud-slick stone steps into Widow Lin’s drying yard with the sixteen-foot cedar ladder balanced horizontally across her right shoulder, her cyan tail-ribbons streaming behind her and her cerulean ruff neat against her throat. Behind her came Boss Qiu’s two sweating apprentices hauling the handcart of slate and canvas, followed by Suisui fanning herself at a stroll and Ciaccona with her notebook already open.

Yangyang could have simply stepped off the bottom stair, caught the updraft off the gorge with one beat of her Resonance, and floated onto the Chen family’s ridgepole without disturbing a single speck of moss.

She did not. Mei had asked for a ladder, and when someone who had spent two lifetimes climbing walls with her bare teeth finally asked for a ladder, you brought the wood and you set it against the eaves.

“Clear the swing,” Yangyang said in her flat report voice as she pivoted at the pickle tub.

Ten children, a mule in pants, and nine subordinate geese scattered left and right. Yangyang planted the iron-shod butt of the cedar rails into the frozen mud four feet out from the wall, walked her hands up the rungs, and dropped the top of the ladder against the Chens’ fascia board with a solid, satisfying *thump*.

Mei was still standing on the flat dirt beside the woodpile, both boots planted squarely at rug height. Her hands were in her coat pockets. Her face was doing something complicated—half sheepish, half fiercely proud of herself—as she looked at the rungs.

“I waited,” Mei told Yangyang immediately. “My foot was on the log, and I took it off. Ask Zhao.”

“She took it off,” Old Man Zhao confirmed around his pipe stem. “Right after the goose stole the big girl’s hat.”

“It is not a hat!” Cartethyia wailed from the base of the ladder, pointing an accusing finger up at the ridgepole. “It’s a manifestation of sacred trial! And he’s chewing on the thorns!”

Up on the roof, Corporal Honk gave the crystal laurel a hearty shake, whacking it twice against the clay tiles like a terrier killing a rat, and let out a brassy, triumphant *HONK* that rattled the rain-gutter.

Ciaccona stopped beside Cartethyia, shaded her eyes with her notebook, and stared up at the bird. Then she looked at Cartethyia’s bare, slightly messy blonde head.

“...You know, cara,” Ciaccona said conversationally, “it actually suits him. Gives him a certain imperial silhouette. Very Ragunnesi.”

“Cia! Whose side are you on?!”

“The side that doesn’t get bitten on the nose by poultry.”

Mei took half a step toward the bottom rung out of sheer muscle memory, caught Suisui’s calm gaze over the top of her folding fan, and immediately pivoted so she was gripping the two side-rails at waist height instead, planting her boots wide to brace the base.

“Okay!” Mei announced loudly. “I’m the anchor! Anchor’s a crucial job! Who’s going up?”

Yangyang was already placing one boot on the bottom rung. “I have the smallest footprint for the unmortared tiles—”

“Wait,” Suisui said pleasantly. She stepped around the puddle, snapped her fan shut, and looked up at the eighteen-pound general on the ridgepole. “If you climb up after him, Yangyang, he’ll jump off the back eaves into the ditch and take the crystal with him. You don’t chase someone holding leverage. You change the market conditions.”

Suisui turned to Mei. “What were the terms of your previous treaty?”

“Huh? Oh—one crust of garrison ration bread. From Tuesday.”

Suisui looked toward the handcart, where Widow Lin was already inspecting the recovered tarpaulins with shining eyes. “Madam Lin, do you happen to have a dried persimmon or a heel of steamed corn-cake in the kitchen?”

“Better than that, Director,” Widow Lin said, reaching into her apron pocket and pulling out a fist-sized ball of cold cooked millet rolled in crushed hemp-seed. “Goose-mash. For laying season.”

“Exquisite,” Suisui said. She didn’t climb the ladder either. She simply walked to the foot of it, held the ball of hemp-seed mash flat on a cabbage leaf at shoulder height, and flicked her folding fan open with her other hand.

With one smooth, upward sweep of the painted silk, Suisui caught the damp air rising off the rain-barrel and braided a thin, warm draft straight up the pitch of the roof—carrying the rich, nutty smell of crushed hemp and toasted millet directly under Corporal Honk’s scarred orange bill.

Up on the ridgepole, the general froze mid-shake.

His black eye swivelled down toward the cabbage leaf.

Yangyang went up the ladder in four silent, weightless steps, stopping with her shoulders just above the gutter line. She didn’t reach for the goose. She held out her left hand with the cabbage leaf on her flat palm, and held her right hand empty six inches below the crystal crown.

Corporal Honk looked at the mash. He looked at the crown.

He did the cold, unsentimental arithmetic of a mercenary who had survived three winters on the lower terrace, opened his bill, and dropped Cartethyia’s laurel squarely into Yangyang’s waiting hand before burying his face in the millet.

The ten children on the stone wall erupted into cheers.

“The ransom is paid!” Cartethyia gasped, catching the laurel as Yangyang handed it down and clapping it back onto her head with both hands, where it immediately sprouted three tiny celebratory thorns. She drew Old Man Zhao’s willow switch again and pointed it straight up the ladder. “Now! While he’s eating! By the power vested in me, I dub thee Sir Honk of the Ridgepole! It counts from down here!”

Up on the eaves, Sir Honk swallowed half the mash whole, wiped his bill on Yangyang’s feathered collar—which puffed up in mild, dignified outrage—and honked once in grudging acceptance of his peerage.

“Okay,” Mei called up from the bottom of the ladder, grinning from ear to ear with both hands still clamped dutifully to the cedar rails. “Now that the aristocracy’s settled—whose roof are we tarping first?”

It took an hour and forty minutes to do all three.

Mei did not set foot on a single rung. Suisui parked her back on the upturned pickle tub with the jar of pickled plums between her boots and appointed her Clerk of the Works, which meant Mei got to point with Cartethyia’s willow switch and yell instructions while eating sunflower seeds with the Chen kids.

Boss Qiu’s two repentant apprentices hauled the blue quarry slate up the cedar ladder under Yangyang’s exact supervision; Cartethyia transformed into Fleurdelys from the waist of the yard—standing well clear of the porch—and simply reached nine feet into the air to hold the heavy oiled tarpaulins flat across the rafters with two fingers while Old Man Zhao and the widow’s nephew drove the iron cleats home; and Ciaccona sat on the shaft of Turnip’s cart playing a brisk four-four working shanty that kept the hammers falling in time and the mule’s ears twitching on the downbeat.

When the last corner rope was lashed tight over the grandma’s eaves two doors down, replacing the broom and the torn oilcloth with double-weight Ministry canvas that wouldn’t let a drop of frost through till April, the whole lane came out with bowls of hot salted bean-curd.

Mei sat on her pickle tub with her warm bowl in her palms, her stitches completely quiet under her ribs, watching Fleurdelys let three small children hang off her braided hair like bell-ropes while Yangyang checked the drainage run-off with a plumb line.

Mei didn’t have mud up to her knees. Her fingers weren’t blue. Nobody was going to have to wait up until the third watch wondering which ditch she was lying in.

She took a spoonful of bean-curd, blew on it, and decided Amy was right: four people and a ladder beat a two-room cabin hollow.

Three terraces up, the afternoon sun had gone pale gold behind the western karsts, and the second-hand clothing shop at the back of the tailors’ arcade smelled of cedar chests and old ironing.

Amy stood in front of a tall, tarnished pier-glass in the corner of the shop, holding a dark navy middy blouse with a white sailor collar up against the front of her jacket.

Beside her, racks of wool coats and pleated skirts hung from iron pipes. Chisa was standing three paces back, running her thumb and forefinger along the hem of a folded linen shirt on the table.

“See,” Amy said, looking at the blouse in the glass, “the problem with the Academy uniforms at Startorch is that they were all designed by an engineer who hated knees. Everything had pockets for callipers and reflective tape on the cuffs so the hangar cranes wouldn’t run you over. This—this is classic. This says ‘I am late for geometry and I have a crush on the boy who cleans the blackboard.’”

She angled the wooden hanger against her collarbones, tilting her head to check the line in the mirror.

The tarnished silver backing of the old glass had gone spotty with damp along the left edge, warping the reflection just enough that as Amy moved, the image of her shoulder stuttered sideways across a dark patch of missing mercury.

In the glass, for half a heartbeat, she didn’t look like a girl holding a blouse in a shop. She looked like a projection losing coherence against a dark wall—the exact, hollow shimmer of the ten years she’d spent made out of borrowed light in the Nihility, watching the world through a screen she couldn’t touch.

Amy’s breath stopped in the middle of her chest.

Above her left ear, right next to the little acid-green star clip, three inches of pink hair dropped straight into dead white static with a tiny, high *bzzzt* like a loose wire.

She didn’t drop the hanger. Her fingers locked around the wooden bar until the knuckles went white, and her mouth opened automatically on the oldest track she owned:

“Haha, man, this mirror is—don’t worry about—”

“Step half a pace to your right,” Chisa said.

Her voice wasn’t loud, and she didn’t turn around from the table with her hands out the way a sighted person would have. She just stood with her fingertips resting on the folded linen, her head tilted a fraction toward the corner.

“The floorboard under your left heel is warped over a joist,” Chisa said levelly. “It’s making you stand crooked, and your breathing went shallow on ‘hangar cranes.’ Step onto the flat board.”

Amy swallowed. The rest of the sentence died in her throat.

She looked down at her boots, then sideways at Chisa—who couldn’t see the spotty mirror, couldn’t see the static in her hair, and hadn’t missed a single thing that mattered.

Slowly, Amy stepped half a pace to her right, onto the solid plank.

“...My hair did the thing,” Amy said quietly, because that was the household rule when it got loud. “In the glass. Looked like the dark for a second.”

“You’re nine pounds heavier than you were on Tuesday,” Chisa said in her flat reporting voice. “Your left boot still has dried taro milk on the welt from noon, and you’ve been humming the bad-wheel song through your teeth since we passed the cobbler’s. Ghosts don’t hum flat.”

Amy let out a short, shaky snort. Above her ear, the static flickered once and settled back into solid pink.

“I don’t hum flat. Carte hums flat.”

“You both hum flat on the bridge,” Chisa said. Then she lifted the folded white garment under her hands and held it out toward the sound of Amy’s breathing. “Leave the navy one. The wool’s scratchy. Look at this.”

Amy hung the navy blouse back on the pipe and took the folded cloth.

It was a short-sleeved summer uniform blouse in crisp, unbleached cotton, with a pale blue sailor collar and two thin white stripes stitched along the cuffs. It smelled faintly of dried camphor leaves.

“I never wore mine,” Chisa said quietly, her fingers brushing the red string on her left wrist. “By the time I got back to my room, it was autumn, and then we came here. If you buy that one, and I buy the matching tie on the rack beside you, it counts as both of us having gone to school in the summer.”

Amy looked down at the pale blue collar in her hands.

She didn’t make a joke. She just folded the blouse carefully over her arm, reached onto the rack beside her, and took down two identical pale blue silk ties—handing one to Chisa so their knuckles bumped.

“Deal,” Amy said. “We’re wearing them when we get to Mengzhou. Even if it’s raining.”

“Good,” Chisa said.

Once the ties were paid for, the last hour of the afternoon turned into a rout.

Halfway back up the second terrace, they heard a sudden, booming arena howl echo out of the public wash-house two lanes over, followed by a chorus of cheering children.

Amy paused outside a stationery stall, grinning toward the steam rising over the roofs. “Ten credits says Lupa’s signing ration wrappers again.”

“She likes having a crowd when nobody is bleeding,” Chisa said. Then she turned straight into the leather-worker’s booth next door and spent six minutes testing the weight of oilstones and bone-handled awls with her thumb until she found a small, heavy brass boot-scraper shaped like a grinning boar, meant for prizing mud out of hobnails without nicking the welt.

“For Lupa,” Chisa told the seller, setting her coins down square. “So she stops using my scissor-blade to clean her heels.”

Amy, refusal to be outdone, marched three stalls down to a woodcarver’s table and bought the single ugliest item in Xuanfang Hold: a fist-sized wooden frog carved out of a gnarled pine knot, painted cross-eyed green, with a notched spine and a little stick that, when scraped across its back, produced a noise like a dying hinge.

“Forty credits,” Amy told Chisa smugly, tucking the frog into her bag beside the summer blouse and a paper twist of two lopsided spun-sugar badgers that genuinely looked like rats. “Wait till Ma sees him. She’s gonna name him within three seconds and carry him in her coat for a month.”

In her pocket, her Terminal buzzed once—just a text notification on the household channel, accompanied by a photo Ciaccona had snapped from the shaft of a mule-cart.

Amy pulled it out while they walked up the final stair.

On the screen, a massive white goose stood on a freshly tarped roof wearing Cartethyia’s glowing crystal laurel around its neck, while Yangyang stood on a ladder holding a cabbage leaf and Mei stood firmly on the mud below, grinning up at the camera and giving a two-fingered salute from rug height. Underneath it, Ciaccona had typed: *Ladder deployed. Three roofs tarped. Sir Honk sends his regards.*

Amy stared at the picture as they reached the storehouse landing. The warmth in her chest was so big and simple it didn’t leave room for a single frame of static.

She didn’t type back. She didn’t need to; she could already hear Cartethyia’s voice booming through the storehouse door ten feet away.

Dinner took two tables shoved together, four lamps, and three separate attempts by Suisui to establish parliamentary procedure before Wen the cook simply banged a soup cauldron down in the middle of the wood and told everyone to grab a ladle.

Nobody ate quietly.

“—and then,” Cartethyia declared, standing on her bench with a steamed bun in one hand and her crystal laurel slightly crooked on her brow as she reenacted the siege for Lupa, “he looked right into the eyes of the Blessed Maiden and *stole my hat!* No fear at all! Like a true paladin of the ditch!”

“You got mugged by a goose, kid,” Lupa howled, slamming her palm on the table hard enough to make the spoons jump. “You went nine feet tall and lost a fistfight to lunch!”

“Sir Honk is not lunch! He’s peerage! Show her the picture, Cia!”

“I’m not showing anyone anything until I finish my fish,” Ciaccona said, guarding her bowl with her left elbow while her right hand added a neat little row of stars down the margin of her open notebook. “Though for the record, four stars for the afternoon. One for the mason at the Three Willows, one for Boss Qiu’s waistcoat, one for the mule’s trousers, and one for Mei actually keeping both boots in the mud for two whole hours.”

Down at the middle of the table, Mei was beaming around a mouthful of ginger greens, her back propped comfortably against a stack of folded blankets Iuno had arranged behind her stool.

“Tell ’em about the anchor, Yangyang!” Mei prompted, nudging Yangyang’s knee under the table. “I was a great anchor!”

Yangyang set her chopsticks down square across her bowl, her cerulean collar lifting a fraction of an inch as ten pairs of eyes turned toward her.

“The ladder remained at a seventy-two-degree angle throughout the operation,” Yangyang reported in her flat, exact voice. “Mei did not ascend past the zero-rung mark. Three roofs were tarped and slated prior to the fifth bell. Also, the goose wiped millet on my neck.” Under her hat brim, her ears went faintly pink. “It was an adequate deployment.”

Suisui tapped her closed fan once against her teacup, smiling serenely. “More than adequate. With Boss Qiu donating the cartage and the labour out of civic generosity, my invoice to the household for the afternoon’s roofing comes to zero credits—at the family rate, of course. Though I did have the clerk bring up Mei’s jar of pickled plums, and I am charging Mei one plum for courier handling.”

“Deal!” Mei unscrewed the red-stringed lid of the fat clay jar sitting beside her elbow and immediately fished a wrinkled pickled plum out with her fingers, popping it into her mouth before her face scrunched up like a fist from the sourness. “Ooooh, that’s violent. Iuiu, try one—”

“Put that finger near my tea and you lose it,” Iuno said mildly, pouring hot Earl Grey from the pot with her one hand.

Across the table, Amy swallowed her mouthful of rice, reached across Chisa’s bowl, and tugged Ciaccona’s uncapped pen out of the bard’s fingers.

“Hey—” Ciaccona started.

“Borrowing it. Official business.” Amy stood up, leaned across the soup cauldron—careful of her sleeves—and caught Mei’s left wrist, turning her mother’s hand palm-up on the tabletop between the plates.

Mei blinked as the sourness cleared from her eyes. “Amymy?”

Amy bent her pink head over Mei’s hand, tongue poking out the corner of her mouth in the exact shape Mei’s had made over the Fairmaiden’s ledger, and drew a slightly lopsided, five-pointed star in black ink right on the base of Mei’s thumb.

“There,” Amy said, capping the pen and tossing it back to Ciaccona. “You said ‘There’s a goose and I need a ladder,’ and you waited for the crew. Gold star. Like I promised.”

Mei looked down at the black ink star on her thumb.

Then she reached into Amy’s open canvas shopping bag sitting on the bench between them—because she’d been eyeing the lumpy parcel inside it for ten minutes—and pulled out the cross-eyed pine-knot frog and the two deformed spun-sugar badgers.

Mei stared at the frog. She scraped the wooden stick once across its notched back.

*Krrrrk.*

The table went quiet for two beats.

“His name is Barnaby,” Mei announced, clutching the frog to her chest with her starred thumb while her golden eyes went suspiciously bright. “And the badgers are Constable Whiskers and Deputy Lump, and I’m never eating them, they live on the crate by the bed now.”

“You have to eat the sugar ones, Ma, they’ll get ants,” Amy groaned, though her ears were red all the way to the tips and the little acid-green star in her hair didn’t flicker once.

Down at the far end of the bench, Chisa reached into her own coat pocket and slid the heavy brass boar boot-scraper across the wood until it bumped Lupa’s forearm.

Lupa looked down at it. She picked it up, turned it over in her scarred palm, and tested the blunt brass tusk against her thumb.

“What’s this for,” Lupa grunted, her ears pinning back at the ambush of a present.

“Your left heel is level again,” Chisa said, not looking up from her soup. “If you let the mud dry on the new nails, it’ll list by Friday. And the boar has your eyebrows.”

Lupa stared at the brass pig, then at Chisa’s completely deadpan face. Her tail gave one tremendous, betrayed *WHACK* against the floorboards before she shoved the scraper into her belt pouch and dumped half her dish of braised pork onto Chisa’s rice.

“Eat the meat,” Lupa barked. “You’re too skinny.”

“Wait, speaking of boots,” Cartethyia piped up around her third bun, “Iuno! Did you see Lupa’s medal at the cobbler’s? She showed it to me on the stairs! Half a gold circle in a little velvet box!”

Lupa’s hand was already inside her tunic before Cartethyia finished the sentence, slapping the open leather saucer down on the table between the bowls so the sheared gold caught the lamplight. “Seventh Games! Split right down the middle in the last bout! See the notch on the rim? That’s where Mei’s broadsword caught the guard before the terrace collapsed—”

While the whole table leaned in over the soup steam to admire the medal—Yangyang examining the shear-line with professional interest, Ciaccona asking about the arena acoustics, Cartethyia demanding to know if the Ephor had used a ceremonial cushion—Mei didn’t look at the gold.

She was leaning back against her stacked blankets, Barnaby the frog in her lap, watching Iuno.

Iuno was sitting perfectly straight beside her, sipping her Earl Grey with one hand. Her face was composed, faintly amused, and completely warm as she listened to Lupa brag.

Only Mei, whose hip was pressed against Iuno’s under the table, had felt the tiny, involuntary stillness in Iuno’s leg when Cartethyia asked who had handed the medal down from the dais.

“Augusta did,” Lupa said simply, tapping the brass frame before snapping the box shut. “From the top steps.”

Lupa didn’t look at Iuno. She just reached over with the teapot and topped up Iuno’s cup before setting it back on the trivet.

By the tenth bell, the dishes were stacked in the wash-basin, the crooked steamed bun for whoever was late had been placed fresh under the ceramic cover beside the banked stove, and the back bedroom door was latched against the draft.

Amy was already out cold on the far side of the pallet, curled around her folded summer uniform blouse with her pink hair spilled across the pillow and one foot tucked warm under Mei’s knee.

Mei sat cross-legged at the near edge of the mattress in her grey sleeping shirt. The oil lamp on the crate was turned down to a fingernail of yellow flame.

Iuno stood by the hook on the wall, unpinning her silver-blue hair with her one hand, setting the pins down one by one beside the two misshapen sugar badgers Mei had solemnly arranged on the crate lid.

“Hey, Iuiu,” Mei whispered.

Iuno didn’t turn around; she lifted the last coil of hair off her neck and let it fall over her shoulders with a quiet sigh of relief. “You’re supposed to be lying down. Your stitches had a long day of supervising.”

“They had a great day. Zero rungs.” Mei rustled around at the foot of the bed for a second, then straightened up holding two things: the empty red paper label that had been tied around Old Man Zhao’s plum jar, smoothed flat across her knee, and Iuno’s own carpenter’s pencil from the pad on the crate.

When Iuno turned toward the mattress, Mei held both of them up with both hands, bowing her head two inches from the neck the exact way the Fillet Fairmaiden had bowed to Yangyang’s carp.

“Excuse me,” Mei whispered, her golden eyes wide and solemn in the dim light. “Are you the lady who sat under the gold fringe at the Seventh Games?”

Iuno stopped halfway to the bed.

Inside her skull, the High Priestess froze in the middle of taking off phantom earrings. *...What is she doing, darling?*

*Wait,* Iuno thought.

“Because Lupa showed me her half of the medal at dinner,” Mei went on in the same hushed, dead-serious fan voice, holding the flattened red paper out on her palms. “And I have the other half in my pack. And when we won it, I remember looking up at the big marble porch, and there was somebody sitting in the bronze chair next to Augusta wearing way too much gold braid and looking down her nose like she wanted to fine us both for getting blood on the sand.”

Mei’s mouth curved up at one corner—not the shield-grin, just the warm, crooked smile that belonged entirely to this room.

“Nobody at the wash-house got your autograph today,” Mei said softly. “And I’m the co-Champion. I think my half of the medal’s invalid until the High Priestess signs the paperwork. Can I have your signature, please? For my collection?”

The quiet in the little room was so thick you could hear the wooden turbine turning on the storehouse sill through the timber wall.

Inside Iuno’s head, the High Priestess of Septimont—thirty years of theatrical iron, forgotten by an empire, erased from every scroll between the coast and the mountains—put both hands over her mouth and sat down hard on nothing at all.

*...Oh, the wretched woman,* the High Priestess whispered, her voice cracking straight down the middle. *Oh, the absolute menace. Take the pencil, darling. Take the pencil immediately before we embarrass ourselves.*

Iuno sat down on the edge of the mattress beside Mei’s knee.

The crystal at her left shoulder chimed once, soft and clear, as she took the pencil into her right hand.

“Your paperwork is four years out of date,” Iuno murmured, her voice lower than the lamp-flame. “And this is a pickled plum label.”

“It’s archival quality. Red paper lasts forever in Huanglong.” Mei held her knee rock-steady so it made a desk. “Put the whole thing. With the titles.”

Iuno braced the red paper with her thumb.

Slowly, in the grand, sweeping chancery hand that used to validate treaties across three provinces, she wrote across the back of Old Man Zhao’s plum label:

*Verified and Blessed by Her Eminence, Iuno Caecilia Severa, High Priestess of the Moon and the Hearth of Septimont.*

Right at the bottom, where the seal space would have been, the descender on *Severa* hooked left.

She lifted the pencil.

Mei took the red slip of paper with both hands, blew gently on the graphite as if it were wet ink, and tucked it carefully inside the notched pine belly of Barnaby the frog before setting the frog between Constable Whiskers and Deputy Lump on the crate.

“There,” Mei whispered, lying down on the mattress with a long, contented groan and immediately hooking her cold feet under Iuno’s ankles as Iuno lay down beside her. “Now it’s official. Best souvenir in the house.”

Iuno pinched the wick of the oil lamp out with two fingers, plunging the room into warm, wool-smelling dark, and pulled the blanket—smelling clean and sharp of mountain wind and lye soap now—up over all three of them.

Mei’s arm came across Amy’s middle from one side; Iuno’s one arm came across from the other, her fingers finding Mei’s starred thumb in the dark and interlocking with it over their daughter’s breathing ribs.

Within three minutes, Mei’s breath went deep and even, her forehead tucked against the back of Amy’s shoulder, right beside the little acid-green copper star.

Iuno lay awake for one minute longer, looking at the faint orange line of light under the door where the stove was keeping the late plate warm in the kitchen.

*Well, your majesty?* Iuno asked the quiet inside her head. *Sixty thousand people in the marble tiers, or a wooden frog with a plum label in its stomach?*

The High Priestess looked at the dark shape of Mei’s hand holding theirs over the sleeping girl’s heart, and at the two sugar rats standing guard on the crate beside her hairpins.

*The acoustic properties of the frog are appalling, darling,* the High Priestess murmured, settling down into the warm dark for the night. *And the company is irredeemably common. ...Remind me to make sure nobody eats those badgers before Tuesday.*

*On the list,* Iuno thought, and closed her eyes.

Outside against the storehouse wall, under the turning stars and the quiet hum of the Hold’s exposed heart, a sixteen-foot cedar ladder leaned ready in the frost for whatever roof needed it in the morning.

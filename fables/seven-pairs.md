Seven Pairs

A blade of white Friday sun came through the crack in the salt-house shutter and hit Suoming’s left eye.

Suoming, on her crate by the door with her umbrella between her knees, shifted three fingers to the right so the central post took the glare off her nose.

“<font color=paleturquoise>More porridge, Mei?</font>” Yangyang asked beside her, holding the wooden ladle poised over the iron cauldron.

“<font color=moccasin>Just the red beans at the bottom,</font>” Mei said, holding her bowl out with both hands so it wouldn’t drip on Iuno’s coat sleeve. “<font color=moccasin>And one more scallion cake before Lupa sees me.</font>”

“<font color=indianred>I see you,</font>” Lupa called from the stove, where she was turning a second batch of lard-blistered cakes on the flat griddle with an iron spatula. “<font color=indianred>You’ve had three. Eat a pork bun, there’s no grease in the dough.</font>”

All fourteen of them were wedged around the pine trestle. Outside, Tuesday’s snow had frozen hard as quarry-stone under a cloudless sky.

Around the board, Friday was already sorting itself out into its ordinary pairs between mouthfuls.

“<font color=indianred>Chisa,</font>” Lupa said, flipping a cake onto the stoneware platter, “<font color=indianred>after we wash up, we’re going down to the cooper’s yard for barrel-hoop iron to mend the tailboard on the third cart. Bring your shears in case the smith’s got scrap plate.</font>”

“<font color=coral>Okay,</font>” Chisa said, peeling the paper off the bottom of a steamed bun and setting the bun on Lupa’s saucer.

At the door-crate, Suoming dropped a piece of scallion cake into the jaws of the fox on her knee without looking down. “<font color=plum>Second bell at the noodle cart, Sentinel. The girl’s coal-delivery comes early on Fridays.</font>”

“<font color=lavender>A-Qing and I are going up to the Vale drain-traps before noon,</font>” Ruoyun announced, tucking a shawl tighter around Qingxiao’s shoulders. “<font color=lavender>And Buling is coming with us to re-bind the frost-housing on the third sluice, instead of hiding in the fish-shop drinking sugar-mud.</font>”

Buling lifted the yellow paper talisman off her forehead to take a bite of cruller. “<font color=palegreen>That housing hold until spring already, Ruoyun. You just like walking up hills in the cold.</font>”

“<font color=paleturquoise>Suisui and I have three grain-convoy manifests to stamp at the post-house before the midday rider leaves,</font>” Yangyang said, passing the pickle saucer to her sister.

“<font color=thistle>And then the chandler’s for axle-tallow,</font>” Suisui added smoothly.

“<font color=skyblue>Cia,</font>” Cartethyia said from the foot of the central post, “<font color=skyblue>after we take the mash down to the goose, can we go down the rope-makers’ lane? My laurel’s been crooked since the tavern ceiling, and if I show up at Cantarella’s table with it over one ear she’ll think I slept in a hedge.</font>”

“<font color=teal>If we walk on the sunny side of the street, cara,</font>” Ciaccona muttered around her tea-mug, her splinted left forefinger resting along the rim.

Mei set her empty bowl down on the pine and looked from one end of the table to the other. Everybody had a road, an errand, and a coat on the peg.

“<font color=moccasin>What about me?</font>” Mei asked hopefully. “<font color=moccasin>I haven’t been past the well since Wednesday.</font>”

Iuno didn’t even look up from buttering a piece of toasted flatbread one-handed. “<font color=royalblue>You, darling, have a three-inch scar that pulled when you laughed at the goose last night, and the lanes are sheet ice from here to the gatehouse. You are staying on the kang with a hot brick.</font>”

“<font color=moccasin>I’ve been on the kang since Monday!</font>” Mei protested, slumping sideways against Iuno’s good right shoulder. “<font color=moccasin>I know every knot in that tie-beam by its first name! That big round one over the stove is called Barnaby.</font>”

“<font color=indianred>Then talk to Barnaby,</font>” Lupa said from the griddle. “<font color=indianred>Forty-eight steps down from this lane, and every one of ’em glazed like a skating pond. You’re not going near ’em.</font>”

At the corner of the table, Amy pulled one of Chisa’s wool socks out of her coat pocket and upended it over the board. Fourteen mahjong tiles clattered out, and she turned them face down in two rows of seven.

“<font color=palegreen>Eh!</font>” Buling said. “<font color=palegreen>Those are my travel tiles! When you go in my bag?</font>”

“<font color=mistyrose>While you were arguing with Ruoyun about the sluice,</font>” Amy said brightly, planting both elbows on the table. “<font color=mistyrose>Right. Fourteen people, fourteen tiles, seven pairs. Everybody draws one face-down. Whoever gets the matching tile is who you spend Friday with until supper. No swaps, no whining, back by the sixth bell.</font>”

The salt-house went quiet for three full seconds.

“<font color=lavender>Absolutely not,</font>” Ruoyun said at once. “<font color=lavender>A-Qing has a cold, the wind off the north cut is freezing, and I am not entrusting her to a random piece of bamboo!</font>”

“<font color=indianred>Who’s minding the stove if I draw somebody who doesn’t know which end of a ladle goes in the pot?</font>” Lupa demanded, pointing her spatula at the green tiles. “<font color=indianred>The stew-meat’s got to go on by four!</font>”

“<font color=royalblue>And Mei is not walking the terraces on black ice,</font>” Iuno said, reaching for the tiles.

“<font color=moccasin>Yes!</font>” Mei said before Iuno’s fingers got there, and caught Iuno’s wrist in both hands. “<font color=moccasin>Iuiu. Please? I’ll wrap the scarf twice round my middle and I won’t run and I’ll ask before I pick anything up. I just want to go out the door for one afternoon.</font>”

“<font color=royalblue>If she draws Cartethyia, she’ll end up on a roof,</font>” Iuno said.

“<font color=skyblue>I never take injured knights on roofs!</font>” Cartethyia objected.

“<font color=lavender>And if A-Qing draws the wolf, she’ll have her hauling scrap-iron in a ditch!</font>” Ruoyun snapped. “<font color=lavender>Put them away, girl—</font>”

Beside her, Qingxiao picked up the third tile from the left and laid it face-down by her teacup.

“<font color=lightcyan>I would like to see,</font>” Qingxiao said.

Iuno let out one long breath through her nose. “<font color=royalblue>Draw, then. Before I find a constitutional objection.</font>”

Amy slid the rest out across the table. Down the bench, Ciaccona had her chin in her palm, watching Amy’s right thumb nudge the last two tiles off the heel of her hand with a small, lopsided smile that Mei didn’t understand at all.

One by one, hands reached in. Lupa snatched hers as if pulling a tooth; Suoming hooked hers over with the ferrule of Nine Shadows; Hsin shifted from fox to tall woman on the corner of the bench, just missing the drying-pole, and plucked hers up.

“<font color=mistyrose>Turn ’em!</font>” Amy called.

Fourteen pieces of bone and bamboo clicked over onto their backs at once.

Qingxiao looked down at the red dragon on her tile. At the stove, Lupa stared at the identical red dragon in her floury palm, then at Ruoyun’s face, and both her ears flattened sideways.

“<font color=lavender>If she sneezes once,</font>” Ruoyun told Lupa in a voice that could have shaved iron, “<font color=lavender>I will skin your tail for a draft-stopper.</font>”

Ruoyun looked down at her own tile, a blank white slate, just as Amy held up its twin and waggled her eyebrows across the porridge pot.

“<font color=mistyrose>Well, well,</font>” Amy said. “<font color=mistyrose>Look at that. Guess I’m helping with the drains.</font>”

Ruoyun’s eyes narrowed. “<font color=lavender>You little fraud.</font>”

“<font color=mistyrose>Pure luck, Auntie Ruoyun! Hsin, what’ve you got?</font>”

Hsin held up the Nine of Characters, ears perking. Across the pine, Suisui turned over its twin.

For a breath Suisui looked at it, and then her spine rose into the flawless, terrifyingly gracious poise of a guild director receiving a tax commissioner. “<font color=thistle>How delightful, Sentinel. I believe the elder mentioned a noodle cart at the second bell?</font>”

Hsin’s ears dropped half an inch. “<font color=gold>…She did.</font>”

“<font color=skyblue>Elder Suoming!</font>” Cartethyia beamed, holding up the East Wind tile with both hands. “<font color=skyblue>We’re the East Wind! Do you mind walking by the silversmith’s after we feed the goose?</font>”

Suoming looked at her own East Wind, then at the blade of sun across the floor. “<font color=plum>Mm,</font>” she said, and reached for a pickled plum.

Beside Mei, Iuno turned over the Three of Circles. Across the table, Yangyang looked down at its twin.

A small, neat silence settled over their corner. Yangyang straightened the chopsticks beside her bowl.

“<font color=royalblue>Well,</font>” Iuno said dryly. “<font color=royalblue>At least neither of us is likely to be arrested before lunch.</font>”

“<font color=paleturquoise>The morning is young,</font>” Yangyang said in her quiet, level voice, and Iuno’s mouth twitched despite herself.

Chisa tilted her head at the One of Bamboo in her hand, while Ciaccona saluted her with the matching tile across the teapot. “<font color=teal>Shopping, kid,</font>” Ciaccona sighed. “<font color=teal>God help my purse.</font>”

Mei turned hers over last, the Eight of Circles, and leaned over to look into Buling’s hand.

Buling stared at her own Eight of Circles, then at Mei’s middle, and let her head thump against the post. “<font color=palegreen>Aiya. Out of fourteen people I pull the invalid.</font>”

“<font color=moccasin>I’m a great passenger!</font>” Mei said, beaming as she reached for her sheepskin coat on the kang kerb.

Iuno caught the hem of Mei’s coat and looked across at Buling. “<font color=royalblue>Two conditions, Consultant. She does not climb or descend a single flight of stairs today, and you have her back inside this door before the lamps are lit.</font>”

“<font color=palegreen>Can,</font>” Buling said. “<font color=palegreen>Easy.</font>”

Mei blinked, one arm halfway into her sleeve. “<font color=moccasin>Buling, the whole Hold is built on a cliff. There’s forty-eight stairs just to get out of the salt-house lane.</font>”

“<font color=palegreen>Your priestess say you cannot walk on stairs,</font>” Buling said. “<font color=palegreen>She never say you cannot go down the hill. That’s only a problem if you’re using your feet.</font>”

Ten minutes later the doorway was a tangle of wool and umbrellas. The goose honked from the freight yard as Cartethyia unlatched the inner stair-door, Suoming’s ferrule clicking behind her; Hsin ducked under the lintel after Suisui; Chisa slung Ciaccona’s lute-case over her own shoulder; and Lupa marched out into the snow-glare scowling at Qingxiao’s bare ankles.

At the threshold, Iuno turned Mei’s collar down where it had bunched and pressed her thumb once to the pulse under Mei’s jaw.

“<font color=royalblue>Before dark,</font>” Iuno said.

“<font color=moccasin>Before dark,</font>” Mei promised, leaning in to bump her nose lightly against Iuno’s cheek before Iuno stepped out into the lane beside Yangyang’s pale blue sleeves.

“<font color=palegreen>Come lah, Mei,</font>” Buling said, jerking her chin toward the slope. “<font color=palegreen>Mule-ramp first. If you slip on your butt before we reach the corner, I’m telling them you pushed me.</font>”

***

“<font color=lightcyan>Teach me to cook millet,</font>” Qingxiao said.

Lupa stopped dead in the middle of the frozen lane outside the salt-house yard, the empty market-basket swinging against her knee.

Qingxiao stood beside her in two salt-house shawls, sword-case on her back, bare feet flat on the ice.

“<font color=indianred>What?</font>” Lupa said.

“<font color=lightcyan>When your household leaves on the carts,</font>” Qingxiao said in her calm, level voice, “<font color=lightcyan>Ruoyun will cook.</font>”

Lupa thought of the pot of plain water Ruoyun had somehow burned black. A shudder went down her spine into her tail-root.

“<font color=indianred>She’ll poison you before the twelfth month,</font>” Lupa said, horrified.

“<font color=lightcyan>Yes,</font>” Qingxiao agreed. She laid two fingers on the braided cord of her sword-case. “<font color=lightcyan>The Vale cookhouse has grain. I can fly us to the square so you do not have to walk the grade.</font>”

“<font color=indianred>Keep that thing in its bag,</font>” Lupa barked, taking a hasty step sideways as if the sword might bite her shin. “<font color=indianred>I’ve got two legs and they work fine. Walk.</font>”

They turned up the steep cobbled incline toward the open boundary stones of Mengxuan Vale.

Every ten yards Lupa’s eyes dragged down to Qingxiao’s bare toes. By the third cistern-arch she couldn’t stand it.

“<font color=indianred>Why haven’t you got boots on?</font>” Lupa demanded. “<font color=indianred>You’re mortal now! You walk half a mile on blue ice in your bare skin and your toes’ll turn black and drop off in the soup!</font>”

“<font color=lightcyan>I could not feel the stone for a long time,</font>” Qingxiao said, stepping over a drain-cover. “<font color=lightcyan>Leave my feet alone. I like the cold.</font>”

One look inside the empty Vale cookhouse set Lupa’s ears twitching with professional outrage.

It was a good three-hole mason’s stove, and nobody who had touched it in the last century knew how a kitchen worked: the damper shoved nearly shut, two pots welded with char, and on the shelf a crock of scorched rice crust under a clean linen cloth as if it were imperial jade.

Lupa kicked the ash-door open and yanked the damper out until the flue roared.

“<font color=indianred>Bunch of scholars,</font>” Lupa muttered, stripping off her rust-red sheepskin coat and tossing it onto the dry grain-bench. “<font color=indianred>Can’t even boil water without setting the pot on fire. Right. Take that outer blanket off before you dip the fringe in the trough, and bring the yellow sack over here.</font>”

Qingxiao folded her shawl, laid her sword along the wall, and carried the millet sack over as if it were stuffed with feathers.

Lupa didn’t use a measuring cup. She scooped three double handfuls of grain into the clean pot.

“<font color=indianred>Now cold water,</font>” Lupa ordered. “<font color=indianred>Swirl your hand round twice—don’t scrub it like you’re washing socks, you’ll crack the hulls. Pour it off while it’s still cloudy. Wash it till it’s clear and you’ve washed the taste down the drain.</font>”

Qingxiao swirled the grain twice with the economy of a sword-form and tipped the water off.

“<font color=indianred>Now fill it,</font>” Lupa said, pressing her own broad, calloused hand flat on top of the wet millet in the bottom of the pot. “<font color=indianred>Up to the first knuckle behind your middle finger. No higher. Your hands are narrower than mine, so go a hair over the bone.</font>”

Qingxiao laid her pale fingers flat on the wet grain, poured cold water to the crease of her middle knuckle, and looked toward the stove. “<font color=lightcyan>Fire?</font>”

“<font color=indianred>Birch billets at the back, two handfuls of cedar kindling under the belly,</font>” Lupa said, stepping back with her arms crossed over her ribs. “<font color=indianred>Let’s see you light it.</font>”

Qingxiao struck the flint once and caught the tinder on the first spark. Then, instead of feeding the kindling in a little at a time, she shoved the whole bundle into the grate and blew.

The cedar went up with a whoosh. Before Lupa could bark, Qingxiao reached to shift the pot onto the ring and grabbed the bare iron ear with her ungloved hand.

The iron hissed against her palm. Her fingers opened, the lid clanged across the flagstones, and her other hand, steadying herself, tipped the salt-scoop straight into the pot.

Qingxiao looked at the blister rising on her thumb, put it in her mouth for a moment, and looked down at the ruined pot.

“<font color=lightcyan>The handle was hot,</font>” Qingxiao observed mildly.

“<font color=indianred>It’s iron over a fire!</font>” Lupa roared, snatching up a dry hemp pot-rag and tossing it at Qingxiao’s chest. “<font color=indianred>Course it’s hot! Use the rag! And you don’t salt plain millet—you’ve pickled the whole batch!</font>”

Qingxiao didn’t argue. She wrapped the rag round the pot-ears, carried it out to the compost-trough, dumped it, and scoured the pot with river-sand until the iron shone.

Then she scooped fresh millet, washed it twice, and measured the water to her knuckle.

This time she fed the fire slowly until the stove settled into an even hum. When it boiled, Lupa showed her how to skim it, drop the lid on tight, and shove the damper half home.

“<font color=indianred>Now the hard bit,</font>” Lupa said. “<font color=indianred>You leave it alone till the smell goes from wet grass to toasted nuts and the ticking at the bottom slows right down. Lift the lid early and the top stays gravel while the bottom burns.</font>”

Qingxiao pulled a stool up to the stove, sat with her hands in her lap, and waited.

One minute passed. Then three.

Lupa leaned against the grain-bin. Her right hand twitched against her thigh.

“<font color=indianred>Sounds a bit fast on the left side,</font>” Lupa muttered, taking a step toward the stove and reaching for the lid-knob to check the water-line. “<font color=indianred>Birch might be burning hot under the back corner—</font>”

Qingxiao’s hand came up without her eyes leaving the stove-door and tapped the back of Lupa’s wrist with the flat of the wooden ladle.

“<font color=lightcyan>Leave it,</font>” Qingxiao said.

Lupa pulled her hand back, scowling. “<font color=indianred>I’m the one teaching you!</font>”

“<font color=lightcyan>You said not to lift the lid,</font>” Qingxiao replied calmly.

Lupa stalked over to the chopping block and sliced a salted turnip with vicious speed just to give her thumbs something to do. Halfway through the second, her hand drifted toward the lid-knob again.

Qingxiao turned her eyes half an inch toward Lupa’s knuckles. Lupa shoved the hand into her pocket with a growl.

Qingxiao sat on the stool without shifting her weight while the pot chugged, whispered, and slowed to a soft, dry *tick… tick… tick*.

At the fourth tick, Qingxiao stood up, wrapped the hemp rag around the wooden knob, and lifted the lid.

A cloud of sweet yellow steam billowed up. The millet had swollen to the rim, bright gold, every grain separate, with a thin amber crust coming cleanly off the bottom.

Right on cue, a bare heel kicked the door open, and Longjue strolled in with her pruning hook and red gourd, followed by Mingxiao, Gu, and Old Chen.

“<font color=aquamarine>What died in here?</font>” Longjue demanded loudly, sniffing the warm air as she dropped onto the bench and hooked one bare foot up onto the pine rail. “<font color=aquamarine>Smells like actual food! Who let the wolf near the stove?</font>”

“<font color=lightcyan>She did not cook it,</font>” Qingxiao said, filling five bowls. She set a sixth aside on the warm brick ledge and covered it with a saucer.

Longjue shoved in a mouthful, chewed twice, and slapped the table hard enough to make the turnip dish jump.

“<font color=aquamarine>The Paragon cooked this?!</font>” Longjue roared, unstoppering her red gourd and shoving the neck straight at Qingxiao’s chest. “<font color=aquamarine>Where’s the charcoal? Mingxiao, look at the bottom of the pot—she didn’t even weld the ladle to the iron! Drink, Paragon, that’s a masterpiece!</font>”

“<font color=lightcyan>No,</font>” Qingxiao said, stepping back half a pace from the sixty-day sorghum. “<font color=lightcyan>Ruoyun is on the third terrace. She will smell it.</font>”

Gu took a single measured mouthful and chewed in dead silence while Lupa glared at him.

“<font color=lightsteelblue>The moisture distribution is uniform,</font>” Gu said. “<font color=lightsteelblue>It’s edible.</font>”

“<font color=indianred>Edible!</font>” Lupa barked. “<font color=indianred>You’ve been eating boiled gravel since the frost started, you wouldn’t know a good pot of grain if it bit your nose!</font>”

Mingxiao set down her empty bowl, stood, and bowed to Lupa from the waist.

“<font color=wheat>Thank you, Champion,</font>” Mingxiao said plainly. “<font color=wheat>When your carts go down the mountain, we’d have gone back to scorching the bottom out of the iron every night. You’ve done this house a kindness.</font>”

The blood rushed straight up Lupa’s neck into her ears.

“<font color=indianred>I didn’t do anything!</font>” Lupa snarled at the ceiling rafters, backing toward the grain-bench to grab her coat. “<font color=indianred>She’s the one who sat on the stool! Wash your own damn bowls, and don’t touch the damper or I’ll come back up here and break your thumbs!</font>”

Ten minutes later Lupa was marching down the frosted path with a crock of the good millet under her arm for supper, Qingxiao barefoot beside her.

At the turn above the cistern, Qingxiao glanced once at Lupa’s boot-marks in the ice.

“<font color=lightcyan>Your left heel turns early,</font>” Qingxiao said.

Lupa’s head snapped round. “<font color=indianred>What?</font>”

“<font color=lightcyan>On the low sweep,</font>” Qingxiao said, looking ahead down the gorge. “<font color=lightcyan>When you shift your weight off the back foot, your left heel turns out before the point clears. It drops your guard an inch on the inside.</font>”

“<font color=indianred>Does not!</font>” Lupa bristled, her ears shooting upright. “<font color=indianred>That’s the arena pivot for a heavy shaft! If you keep the heel flat on sand you wrench your knee!</font>”

“<font color=lightcyan>This is not sand,</font>” Qingxiao said, and walked on around the corner of the cistern wall to check the plum-shed eaves.

Lupa stood alone on the path, scowling. Then she glanced up and down the lane, set the crock on the coping, and took a short half-step lunge on the ice with an imaginary spear, holding her left heel dead straight.

Her sole bit into the snow without a hair of slide.

Lupa grunted, snatched the crock back, and trotted after the white hem.

*Click. Pause. Click.*

Cartethyia walked on the inner side of the lane, in the round shadow of Nine Shadows. Suoming’s own shoulder stayed out in the sun.

At first Cartethyia had tried to keep the conversation going.

“<font color=skyblue>Look at the icicles along the dye-house trough, Elder Suoming! In Egla, Uncle Cosimo lets the children knock them down with broom-handles and pretend they’re lances!</font>”

Suoming had looked once at the dye-house eaves. “<font color=plum>They fall on people’s heads,</font>” she said, and kept walking.

Two corners later she tried again, about whether the mules wore iron shoes in winter.

“<font color=plum>Iron on the post-road,</font>” Suoming said. “<font color=plum>Straw in the yard.</font>”

After that, Cartethyia walked twenty paces without thinking of a third thing to exclaim over. She waited for the silence to feel awkward, the way silences did when people expected the little knight to say something cheerful, and nothing happened at all. Suoming just walked on, shifting the umbrella whenever the lane turned so the shade stayed over Cartethyia’s brow.

Cartethyia let her hands drop from in front of her chest into the warm pockets of her cloak, and found that her shoulders felt remarkably light.

The travelling silversmith’s booth sat halfway down the rope-makers’ lane.

He had slung a tarred canvas awning below the cross-beam to keep the wind off his brazier, bringing the roof down to barely five feet.

“<font color=skyblue>There he is,</font>” Cartethyia said in her ordinary voice, reaching up to touch the crooked crystal thorns over her left ear. “<font color=skyblue>If he can just bend a small double-hook of stiff wire behind the middle leaf, I can pin it into my braid so it doesn’t tip when—</font>”

Her boots stopped on the sunlit cobbles, one pace short of the low dark line where the tarpaulin shadow began.

“<font color=skyblue>I don’t go under low roofs since the twenty years,</font>” Cartethyia said in the exact same tone, her hands still in her pockets.

Suoming didn’t look at her face or ask what twenty years she meant. She stepped under the tarpaulin and tapped the smith’s anvil-block with her ferrule.

“<font color=plum>Bring the block and the wire out to the kerb,</font>” Suoming said. “<font color=plum>The light’s better.</font>”

The old smith squinted down at the small red-haired figure. “<font color=silver>Bring your ornament inside if you want it mended, little girl.</font>”

Suoming laid three brass cash on the kerbstone outside the awning, two more than a hair-clasp was worth.

The smith grunted and heaved his anvil-block and pliers out into the lane.

“<font color=silver>Stand still on the dry stone, then,</font>” the smith told Cartethyia as she lifted the crystal laurel off her head and held it out in the open sunlight. “<font color=silver>And you, little girl—keep your fingers off the hot snip-iron while I’m working, or your big sister here will have my hide.</font>”

Cartethyia drew in a breath at once. “<font color=skyblue>She isn’t a child, sir, she’s—</font>”

“<font color=plum>Don’t,</font>” Suoming said from beneath the umbrella.

Cartethyia closed her mouth and watched the smith’s pliers instead.

While he beat a length of silver wire into a sprung saddle for the laurel, Cartethyia looked at his sample-tray: a dozen hollow silver locks the size of walnuts, each with four characters across the belly.

“<font color=skyblue>What do the words on the little locks say?</font>” Cartethyia asked.

“<font color=silver>*Hundred years*,</font>” the smith said around a brass pin held in his teeth. “<font color=silver>Long-life locks for little ones. Parents buy ’em at the full-month feast and hang ’em round the child’s neck on a red cord to lock the soul to the body so the winter fevers don’t carry ’em off.</font>”

“<font color=skyblue>How much is this one?</font>” she asked.

“<font color=silver>Four coppers with the cord, miss.</font>”

Cartethyia fished four coppers out of her purse, handed them over, and slipped the silver lock into her cloak pocket.

Beside her ankle, Suoming looked from the tray to Cartethyia’s pocket. “<font color=plum>Those are for children.</font>”

“<font color=skyblue>The goose only hatched in the third month this spring,</font>” Cartethyia said reasonably. “<font color=skyblue>And nine days on a sea-packet to Ragunna is very damp for a young bird. If Uncle Cosimo sees he has a proper silver collar on when we get to Egla, he’ll know he isn’t for the oven.</font>”

The smith fitted the clasp. The laurel sat dead-level across her brow, and when Cartethyia shook her head hard to test it, it didn’t tip.

“<font color=skyblue>Thank you!</font>” Cartethyia said. As they turned up the lane she glanced at the tiny lock on Suoming’s hairpin. “<font color=skyblue>Is yours a hundred-year lock too, Elder?</font>”

Suoming stepped over a frozen trickle of dye-water in the gutter. “<font color=plum>It’s a hairpin.</font>”

They came out at the foot of the long stone stair that climbed to the salt-house lane: forty-eight steep risers, the middle packed with blue ice.

“<font color=skyblue>Cia and I named a stair *Salita dei Limoni* on Tuesday so I can find the doors in the dark,</font>” Cartethyia said, laying her mitten on the newel-post. “<font color=skyblue>Can we name this one *Via della Campana*?</font>”

“<font color=plum>That one has a name,</font>” Suoming said, stopping beside her. “<font color=plum>The Forty-Eight.</font>”

Cartethyia blinked down at her. “<font color=skyblue>That’s just how many steps there are.</font>”

“<font color=plum>Some names are,</font>” Suoming said.

Cartethyia looked up the long grey trough of steps and took her hand off the post. “<font color=skyblue>All right,</font>” she said quietly.

They didn’t climb it. Ten yards along the lower path, where six crooked steps led up beside a baker’s woodpile, Cartethyia tapped the brick corner.

“<font color=skyblue>This one has six steps and smells of birch bark,</font>” Cartethyia said. “<font color=skyblue>This one is *Vicolo del Legno*.</font>”

Suoming looked at the woodpile, then at the six crooked steps. “<font color=plum>Vicolo del Legno,</font>” she repeated flatly, getting the Ragunnese vowels right on the first try, and walked up it.

At the top, the parapet above Huizhen Plaza came up to Cartethyia’s collarbone and stood well above Suoming’s head.

Suoming stopped facing the blank stone and looked left and right along the walk.

“<font color=plum>Is there a crate?</font>” Suoming asked out loud.

There wasn’t so much as a brickbat in sight.

With a soft, chiming rush of wind, Cartethyia became Fleurdelys: nine feet of the Blessed Maiden on the frosted flagstones, the silver clasp holding the laurel straight.

She didn’t make a speech of it. She knelt and laid her broad palm flat on the stone.

Suoming stepped onto it, hooked an arm round Fleurdelys’s wrist, and kept the umbrella up against the glare.

Fleurdelys rose until Suoming could see over the coping.

Below, steam curled from Liang Ning’s noodle cart. Hsin stood at the chopping board with her tails heaped behind her, cleaver rising and falling, while Suisui sat bolt upright on Suoming’s butter tub beside the cash tin.

Suoming looked down for three slow breaths. When Hsin glanced up toward the wall, Suoming didn’t wave.

“<font color=plum>The girl put the coal-bucket on the windward side of the firebox again,</font>” Suoming said. “<font color=plum>And the Watcher’s sister is sitting on my tub.</font>”

She tapped Fleurdelys’s thumb once with her heel. “<font color=plum>Thank you. Put me down.</font>”

Fleurdelys set her down and folded back into her own small cloak. In her pocket, the silver lock gave a cheerful chink against her knuckles.

“<font color=peachpuff>Don’t mix the thin coppers with the garrison brass, Auntie,</font>” Liang Ning said without looking up from the cauldron. “<font color=peachpuff>And wipe the vinegar ring off before you stack the bowls, or the bottoms stick.</font>”

“<font color=thistle>Of course,</font>” Suisui said graciously, picking up the grey dish-clout with two fingers as if it were an embroidered handkerchief.

She was sitting on Suoming’s butter tub behind the noodle cart. The tub was built for someone four feet tall, the steam blew straight into her face, and two of her hairpins had already slipped; the loose lock kept sliding down however often she tucked it back.

Three feet to her left, behind the wide pine chopping board, stood the sovereign of Xuanfang Hold.

Hsin wore a coarse baker’s apron over her robe, sleeves rolled, tails coiled on a straw mat behind her heels except the dark-tipped one, which twitched every time the cleaver hit the wood.

As Suisui stacked the fourth wiped bowl, Hsin paused and looked sharply up at the parapet above the square.

High on the wall stood Fleurdelys, holding a small plum-coated figure under an open umbrella over the crenellations.

“<font color=thistle>Is the Elder checking on you, Sentinel?</font>” Suisui asked mildly, sorting three thin Mengzhou coppers into the front compartment of the tin.

Up on the wall, the giant knelt and lowered Suoming out of sight behind the stone.

Hsin looked back down at her board, both ears going flat as the truth rule caught her halfway through a dodge.

“<font color=gold>She is checking where Liang Ning put the coal-bucket,</font>” Hsin said, bringing the broad cleaver down with a rapid *thock-thock-thock*. “<font color=gold>She has very strong, very tedious opinions about the windward side of the firebox.</font>”

The queue cleared for a while. Behind the cart sat an upturned cabbage crate holding a tea-stained weiqi board borrowed from the tea-shed.

Hsin picked up a black stone. Her hand still had a fine tremor, but she set it down clean on the star-point.

“<font color=gold>Your turn, Director,</font>” Hsin said, tilting her chin up with a faint, courtly flash of her old smile. “<font color=gold>And remember—for nothing.</font>”

Suisui placed a white stone in the opposite corner. “<font color=thistle>A generous opening, Sentinel.</font>”

Before Hsin could answer the stone, the noon rush arrived from the upper courts.

For the next hour they played one stone every few bowls, Hsin slicing and dropping noodles between moves, Suisui making change, wiping bowls, and sliding white stones between Hsin’s black ones to cut them apart.

Whenever Suisui severed a connection, Hsin’s ears dropped flat and she sulked into the steam.

“<font color=gold>You’re crowding the third line on purpose,</font>” Hsin complained around a cloud of flour as she stretched the next lump of dough. “<font color=gold>There is an entire middle of the board sitting empty, Director, you don’t have to audit every single corner I put a foot in.</font>”

“<font color=thistle>If the corner is left unsealed, Sentinel, the draught gets in,</font>” Suisui replied pleasantly, handing a pair of chopsticks to a carter.

Halfway through, while Suisui was making change for Warden Liangyu and her six-bowl carrier, Hsin’s dark-tipped tail crept up the side of the crate and hooked a black stone to nudge it toward an eye.

Suisui saw it out of the corner of her eye and didn’t move a muscle.

Before it could slide, Hsin’s own hand grabbed the tail by the dark fur and shoved it back under her heel.

“<font color=gold>Behave,</font>” Hsin muttered to her ankle, her face going pink under the flour-dust, and played her legitimate move on the left side instead.

At the front of the cart, Liang Ning pinched a test strand and frowned at Hsin over her shoulder.

“<font color=peachpuff>Thick at the tail again,</font>” Liang Ning said bluntly. “<font color=peachpuff>You’re letting your thumb ride up the spine of the cleaver when your wrist jumps. Keep the pad flat on the side of the iron, the way the wolf showed you.</font>”

“<font color=gold>Flat on the side,</font>” Hsin repeated obediently, shifting her grip lower on the broad iron blade as her wrist hitched once. “<font color=gold>Thank you, Ning-er. I’ll watch the tail on the next sheet.</font>”

Gu came by, held a single noodle up against his notched thumb-rule, said “<font color=lightsteelblue>Tapered,</font>” and ate the whole bowl anyway.

Suisui watched the fox who had held up the sky over Mengzhou nod gravely at a mason’s complaint and adjust her elbow an inch.

Then the miller arrived with his man and a barrow: two sacks of flour for the cart’s weekly order.

“<font color=burlywood>Two fifties, Ning-girl,</font>” the miller called briskly, heaving the top sack off the barrow onto the dry stone behind Suisui’s tub. “<font color=burlywood>Sign the tally-stick and we’ll take the empty bags.</font>”

Liang Ning reached for the chalk, frowning at the sack; she had no steelyard heavy enough to check it. Behind the board, Hsin’s eyes narrowed at the slack fold of the burlap, and a faint, dangerous thread of gold stirred at her cuffs.

Suisui didn’t stand up. She rested two fingers on the top sack and looked at the damp ring round its bottom seam.

“<font color=thistle>Master Miller,</font>” Suisui said warmly, just loud enough for the carters at the trestle. “<font color=thistle>Your upper stone, by the south sluice? You rarely find winter wheat ground this fine north of the plain.</font>”

The miller stopped with the tally-stick in his hand, puffing his chest out an inch. “<font color=burlywood>Upper stone, lady! Dressed the grooves myself before the frost.</font>”

“<font color=thistle>Anyone can tell,</font>” Suisui said, tapping the damp bottom of the sack with her closed fan. “<font color=thistle>Which is such a pity about these river-shed sacks. They drink the mist, and once the sun dries them out a full sack shrinks, and people who don’t know your reputation think the mill gave a child short weight.</font>”

The miller’s face went the colour of boiled beet-root. Behind him, the two carters lowered their bowls an inch to look at the slack neck of the sack.

“<font color=thistle>Now,</font>” Suisui went on before he could stammer, “<font color=thistle>the innkeeper at the Willow Milestone is looking for a winter flour contract. Leave that half-sack on your barrow under the cart for Ning-er, to cover the damp, and I’ll write your mill’s name on his slip myself. Then nobody needs to fetch the bailiff’s steelyard out into the cold.</font>”

“<font color=burlywood>Course!</font>” the miller barked, setting the half-sack beside the others himself. “<font color=burlywood>Brought it on purpose for the damp! Master Zhao at the south sluice, lady—tell him good dry winter wheat!</font>”

He tipped his cap twice, snatched up his empty burlap bags, and wheeled his barrow out of the plaza at a trot.

Liang Ning stared at the extra flour, then at Suisui. The gold at Hsin’s cuffs had gone; she was looking at Suisui with her mouth slightly open.

“<font color=thistle>Your black group on the fourth line is down to its last liberty, Sentinel,</font>” Suisui said mildly, turning back to the cabbage crate.

“<font color=gold>...Ow,</font>” Hsin murmured, and connected her group.

Half an hour later, two figures walked past along the upper rim of the square: Yangyang and Iuno.

They didn’t come down to the cart. Yangyang had her patrol log open and was pointing her pencil at the cobbler’s window-ledge; Iuno said something that made Yangyang’s mouth curve at the corner. Then they were gone up the hill.

Suisui’s right hand stayed poised above the bowl of white clam-shell stones until the last flash of pale blue sleeve was gone.

“<font color=thistle>She walks right past my nose with a foreign priestess,</font>” Suisui said lightly to the weiqi board, picking up a white stone between two fingers, “<font color=thistle>and doesn’t even look down to see whether I’ve had lunch. It is terribly rude. I intend to be jealous about it until at least the fourth bell.</font>”

Hsin looked from the empty corner back to Suisui. The loose lock of hair had fallen again, and this time Suisui hadn’t pinned it back.

Hsin didn’t offer a riddle, and she didn’t make a joke about sisters. She set a black stone down on the left boundary with a quiet click.

“<font color=thistle>Sentinel,</font>” Suisui said, her eyes on the intersecting black lines of the grid. “<font color=thistle>When the south road was closed, before I came back up from Mengzhou with the first cart—what was she like here?</font>”

Hsin’s hands stilled on the edge of the crate.

Hsin looked down at the flour-dust in the creases of her knuckles.

“<font color=gold>She sat on the bottom step of the third grain-shed in the mud for an hour with a boy whose sister had died at Xianling,</font>” Hsin said plainly. “<font color=gold>Peeling cold chestnuts for him, because when she tried them hot the skins broke under her thumbs, and she thought he’d mind if they were messy.</font>”

Suisui looked down at the board. Two white stones sat side by side near the middle star-point, defending a small, tidy territory that couldn’t be cut.

Suisui pulled the rest of the pins out of her hair, twisted it up at the nape, and drove them home so not a strand hung loose.

By the fifth bell the cauldron was scrubbed and the game sat unfinished in the middle.

Suisui pulled down a square of noodle-wrapping paper, borrowed Hsin’s brush, and copied the position stone by stone, marking Hsin’s last play with a small cross.

She blew once on the wet ink to dry it and handed the brush back to Hsin.

“<font color=thistle>The Zhaoming Guild’s Lower Canal Wharf office, Gate Three, Jinzhou,</font>” Suisui said, folding the stiff paper neatly in half. “<font color=thistle>One move per letter until the twelfth month closes. I believe your address is the Old Tea-Court under the West Sanctum Buttress, the gate with the sticking bottom rail?</font>”

Hsin took the brush, trying to keep her chin at a regal, indifferent angle, and failed when all nine tails swept the cobbles behind her.

“<font color=gold>The post-riders take four days once the plain freezes, Director,</font>” Hsin said, tucking the brush behind her ear like a clerk. “<font color=gold>You will have plenty of time between riders to see where your centre group is leaking.</font>”

“<font color=thistle>We will see,</font>” Suisui said, slipping the folded noodle-paper into her sleeve and picking up her cloak from the dry flour-sack. “<font color=thistle>Mind the awning, Hsin.</font>”

Hsin straightened up from the cabbage crate too fast to say she knew perfectly well where the awning was, and cracked the crown of her head squarely against the wooden cross-pole with a hollow *thunk*.

Suisui hid her mouth behind her fan and waited until the Sentinel was ready to walk up the hill.

If there was one thing in this world Buling hated worse than people who said *back in my day*, it was stairs.

She had climbed enough of Xuanfang’s on the post to last her several lifetimes, and she had spent the century since in flat river-towns with ferries and boardwalks, where nobody built a town up a cliff on purpose. Give her a boardwalk any day.

Which made Friday morning’s draw much easier than it looked.

“<font color=moccasin>We can go all the way down to the Lower Wharf?</font>” Mei said, trotting along the mule-ramp behind the salt-house. “<font color=moccasin>Suisui said there’s a man down there who boils river-snails with pickled bamboo shoots until you can smell it from the lock-house!</font>”

“<font color=palegreen>I heard her,</font>” Buling said, her hands tucked into her yellow-banded sleeves as she surveyed the drop between the third terrace and the second. “<font color=palegreen>Smell like a dead boot in a ditch, that one. Why you want to eat ditch-water?</font>”

“<font color=moccasin>Because I’ve never tried them!</font>” Mei hopped onto the tail-board of a laundry barrow just as the wash-house apprentice knocked the brake-chock off at the top of the timber-chute. “<font color=moccasin>Is this allowed?</font>”

“<font color=palegreen>Two coppers,</font>” Buling told the apprentice, tossing the coins into his apron and dropping her own backside onto the pile of bundled dry bedsheets beside Mei’s hip before the barrow slid forward. “<font color=palegreen>Hold the rope, don’t lean over the wheel.</font>”

They rode the barrow down the chute without touching a step and came out on the long terrace just as the lime-burner’s guardian came clanking toward the freight incline, two empty coal-baskets on its shoulder-yoke, each wide enough for four pigs.

Mei’s jaw dropped. “<font color=moccasin>Buling. Look at the baskets.</font>”

“<font color=palegreen>Why you think we walked this way?</font>” Buling called out, waving her arm at the old lime-burner trudging behind the guardian’s left heel. “<font color=palegreen>Eh, Uncle Gao! Going down to the coal-slip? Let us ride in the left basket, save my friend’s stitches!</font>”

“<font color=tan>Six coppers for the pair of you!</font>” the old lime-burner shouted through his lime-dust.

Mei immediately reached into her purse and pulled out a whole shining Jinzhou silver piece. “<font color=moccasin>Here, Uncle, take this—</font>”

Buling caught Mei’s wrist mid-air and shoved the silver back into her mitten. “<font color=palegreen>You crazy or what?</font>” She turned on the lime-burner. “<font color=palegreen>Six coppers! For an empty basket going downhill! Two coppers, and this duck egg Lupa boiled this morning. Take or not?</font>”

“<font color=tan>Three coppers and the egg,</font>” Old Gao grunted.

“<font color=palegreen>Two coppers, the egg, and I tell you your kiln flue got a bad wind-line on the north brick so you stop wasting coal,</font>” Buling shot back, already slapping them into his palm while the guardian knelt to lower the basket.

Mei scrambled in, laughing hard enough to press a hand to her stomach, and the guardian rose and strode down the switchback ramp toward the river.

Mei knelt with her arms hooked over the rim, watching roofs and clotheslines glide past twelve feet below.

“<font color=moccasin>Look at that orange dog on the woodshed!</font>” Mei crowed, and her cheek brushed the coal-black rim and came away with a long smudge. “<font color=moccasin>Buling, why doesn’t everybody travel in coal-baskets?</font>”

“<font color=palegreen>Because normal people got clean faces,</font>” Buling said, though she shifted her boot so Mei had room to brace her knee on the hairpin turn. Five minutes with the woman and Buling already needed a nap, and yet when Mei grinned back with coal on her nose, Buling grinned too.

Above the wharf gate, a drayman’s hand-cart of pickle jars had skidded on black ice and wedged a wheel in the drainage rut, and he was heaving at it red-faced.

Mei’s weight shifted onto her right boot, her eyes measuring the drop so she could hop down and put her back under the axle.

Buling didn’t even uncross her legs. She hooked two fingers into Mei’s collar and pulled her back down onto the reeds.

“<font color=palegreen>Sit,</font>” Buling said. “<font color=palegreen>I promised your priestess no stairs, I didn’t say you can play pack-mule in a ditch.</font>”

“<font color=moccasin>His wheel’s stuck, Buling, if those jars tip over—</font>”

“<font color=palegreen>Wait lah, look with your eyes.</font>” Buling leaned over the side. “<font color=palegreen>Eh, Uncle! You push from the back like that, you only jam the hub tighter! Take that flat rock, shove it under the front of the rim, and stand back!</font>”

Grumbling under her breath, Buling bit her thumb, smeared a drop of blood on a yellow talisman, and flicked it down.

It landed on the iron tire and went off with a sharp blue *crack*. The ice holding the wheel shattered to powder, and a stray spark jumped back to sting Buling’s own knuckle.

“<font color=palegreen>Ow!</font>” Buling hissed, sticking her shocked thumb into her mouth while down below the drayman leaned once on the front spoke and the cart rolled smoothly up out of the drain onto the dry gravel.

Buling pulled an empty cloth ration-bag from her pocket and dropped it onto the top cabbage jar. “<font color=palegreen>Consultation fee, Uncle! One pickled turnip from the front crock, put in the bag, throw up!</font>”

The drayman laughed, fished two dripping white turnips out of the brine, tied the bag’s drawstring, and tossed it up into the coal-basket as the guardian clanked past.

Mei looked back at the cart rolling safely down the grade without her, blinked once, and bit the top off a turnip.

“<font color=moccasin>Good turnip,</font>” Mei said around a mouthful.

Down on the Lower Wharf, the river ran sluggish and slate-grey between shelves of shore-ice, smelled of pine-tar and wet rope, and had no stairs at all.

They sat at the open-front snail-noodle shack by the timber-slips. While the stall-man blanched the noodles, a broad-shouldered boat-caulker sat down at the end of their bench with a bowl of soup.

Mei slid the bamboo chopstick-holder down the pine to her and asked if the flatboats were still running to the coast.

Buling sipped her thick sweet coffee and watched. The caulker looked once into Mei’s warm golden eyes and within three minutes was telling a complete stranger all about her younger brother in Zhaozhou, who had bought a worm-eaten pig-barge, married a toll-keeper’s widow with three dogs that bit the postman, and hadn’t sent so much as a dried fish home since the eighth month.

Mei listened to every word until the woman declared she felt much better and would write the idiot tonight.

Buling shook her head as the stall-man slammed two steaming earthenware bowls down on the board. “<font color=palegreen>People see your face, they just empty their pockets, hor.</font>”

“<font color=moccasin>He sounded like a nice brother,</font>” Mei said loyally, and picked up her chopsticks. “<font color=moccasin>Just bad at boats.</font>”

Buling lifted her talisman out of the steam and leaned back. “<font color=palegreen>Smell like somebody died in a wardrobe.</font>”

Mei hooked a massive chopstick-load out of the red oil and shoved it into her mouth.

By the third mouthful tears were rolling down through the coal-smudge. By the fifth her nose was the colour of a winter cherry and she was still sucking snails out of their shells with total commitment.

“<font color=moccasin>It’s—*hah*—it’s amazing,</font>” Mei gasped, wiping a tear off her chin with her cuff and eating the sprig of bruised coriander garnish off the rim of the bowl. “<font color=moccasin>Suisui is a genius.</font>”

Two-thirds of the way down, Mei’s breath hitched; she pressed a hand to her side and set her chopsticks across the rim. “<font color=moccasin>Okay. If I eat the rest I’m going to pull something coughing.</font>”

Buling scowled at the fried bean-curd skin soaking in what was left and pulled the bowl over.

“<font color=palegreen>Waste of money if you leave the skin,</font>” Buling muttered. “<font color=palegreen>Stall-man! Two crocks takeaway! Less red oil in the second one, we got a Ragunnese girl at home who faints if she sees a pepper!</font>”

Buling finished Mei’s bowl, complaining after every bite that it tasted like ditch-water, until it was scraped dry.

“<font color=moccasin>Buling,</font>” Mei said softly, when the takeaway crocks were being tied with hemp twine at the counter. “<font color=moccasin>A hundred years out on the road by yourself—did you ever find a town where you wanted to unpack your bag for good?</font>”

Buling stirred the sugar-sludge at the bottom of her coffee mug with a wooden chopstick.

“<font color=palegreen>Rent in Jinzhou gone up three times since the new canal lock opened,</font>” Buling said briskly to the mug. “<font color=palegreen>And in Ragunna, the Fisalia people own half the roofs—landlords charge you two silver extra just to boil coffee after the night bell. Very bad value on the coast.</font>”

Mei didn’t press. She nodded as if rent was what she’d wanted to know.

“<font color=palegreen>Every time I stay in a town past the third month,</font>” Buling said after a moment, her voice dropping its quick market bounce, “<font color=palegreen>the noodle-shop auntie starts keeping a mug with my name on the shelf, and people ask what I’m bringing to the winter solstice table. Then my shoes start pointing at the ferry-landing by themselves.</font>”

Mei’s thumb moved across the deep old sword-callus on her right index knuckle. “<font color=moccasin>Whenever a room gets really warm and everybody’s laughing at the table,</font>” Mei said quietly, “<font color=moccasin>I still look at the door to see who’s coming in with the bill.</font>”

Buling looked back at her across the empty bowls. “<font color=palegreen>Stingy habit, that one.</font>”

“<font color=moccasin>Yeah,</font>” Mei said. “<font color=moccasin>I’m trying to quit.</font>” She stood up from the bench, pressing one palm briefly to her side as she stretched her back. “<font color=moccasin>I’m going to wash the chili oil off my chin at the water-butt before Iuno sees me. Don’t let the boatmen steal the takeaway.</font>”

Once Mei had gone down the quay to the water-butt, where she immediately got distracted showing a small boy how to squirt water through his thumbs, Buling took out her Terminal.

It was Friday afternoon, and the weekly weather was due.

She opened the plain message window that never touched the Huangboard and looked across the wharf at the subject of every report she had sent since before Amy was tall, who had river-water dripping off her nose and soot she’d missed on her cheek.

Buling typed with one thumb, fast and plain:

*Friday. Clear, hard frost. Scar healing clean, kept off the stairs today. Rode down to the wharf in a coal-basket. Ate river-snail noodles with extra chili until she cried, ate the coriander too. Still looks at the door waiting for the bill sometimes, then sits back down at the table. Amy is good. Sent postcards on Wednesday.*

Buling’s thumb hovered over the glass. Below *Wednesday.*, she typed: *She asked if I ever unpacked my bag.*

She looked at the seven words for three slow breaths, hit the back-key until they were gone, and pressed send.

Before Mei had finished wiping her chin on her sleeve at the water-barrel, the single return line blinked up at the foot of the screen:

*Received. Thank you. Keep her off the ice, and tell me if Amy needs warm socks.*

Buling locked the Terminal and picked up the takeaway crocks just as a heavy *clank-thump* came along the quay.

It was Mother Sun, walking back from the spring-head beside her water guardian, two brimming tubs on its left shoulder and the right one empty for the climb.

Buling’s face split into a wide grin. She stepped out to the edge of the quay and waved both takeaway crocks in the air.

“<font color=palegreen>Eh, Mother Sun!</font>” Buling called. “<font color=palegreen>Going up the Forty-Eight? Got room on the right shoulder-pad for one passenger? Her priestess say her feet cannot touch the stairs—never say the stairs cannot get climbed!</font>”

Ciaccona’s left boot landed a fraction of a second heavier than her right when she was dreading something.

Chisa walked beside her along the third-terrace arcade with Ciaccona’s lute-case on her own back. For twenty minutes Ciaccona had walked past every shop-front without going in.

Chisa wasn’t doing much better.

Chisa had twelve silver pieces in her pocket for a present for her mother, and she had stood in front of an embroidery stall, a comb seller, and a tea-merchant without the faintest idea how people chose a thing nobody needed.

“<font color=teal>Silk’s damp,</font>” Ciaccona muttered at a draper’s awning. “<font color=teal>And if I bring Cantarella a painted fan, she’ll ask me which cousin I pawned to pay for it.</font>”

She stopped at a sunken door under a faded wooden oar.

“<font color=teal>In here,</font>” Ciaccona said, pushing the plank door open with her shoulder.

Chisa followed her down into a dim, low-raftered taproom that smelled of peat-smoke and sour mash: three scarred tables, a sleepy barman, and one old man in a mole-skin cap alone by the window with a pot of small beer.

Chisa looked from the sawdust on the floor to the rows of pickled-egg jars behind the bar. “<font color=coral>They don’t sell presents here.</font>”

“<font color=teal>They sell benches, kid, and the wind out there is cutting my ears off,</font>” Ciaccona said, dropping onto the bench nearest the peat-stove and holding up two fingers to the barman. “<font color=teal>Two warmed rice-beers, boss, and a saucer of salted broad-beans.</font>”

Chisa set the lute-case between her knees. Ciaccona drank half her mug in three swallows, took the lute out, tucked her splinted finger clear of the neck, and began to play the boatmen’s hauling song they’d heard the carters whistling.

Chisa’s head tilted half an inch.

Ciaccona was playing it terribly. It wasn’t the splint; Chisa had heard her play round that splint in the dark without missing a note. She was hitting the fourth fret flat on every pass, dragging her thumb so the rhythm limped, and coming down on a minor third where the boatmen sang an open fifth.

Before Chisa could ask if a peg had slipped in the cold, the old man by the window slammed his mug down and glared at Ciaccona’s hands.

“<font color=lightsalmon>It’s an open fifth on the drop, girl!</font>” the old man rasped. “<font color=lightsalmon>Open fifth! Who taught you the lower-reach turn, a deaf bricklayer? You’re murdering it!</font>”

Instantly, every line of Ciaccona’s shoulders changed.

She lifted her head toward the window, and when she spoke, a voice came out of her throat that Chisa had never heard inside the salt-house—bright, musical, deferential, every vowel pitched half a tone higher and rounder, like sunlight poured out of a jug.

“<font color=teal>Ah, forgive me, Uncle!</font>” Ciaccona called warmly, laying her palm over the strings and looking at him with wide, rueful admiration. “<font color=teal>My finger’s in splints from a cart-door, and a boatman down by the slip hummed it to me only once! Bring your mug over to the stove where it’s warm and tell me where the thumb goes on the second bar—boss, fill Uncle’s pot on my slate!</font>”

Within four minutes the old man, a retired river-cooper, was on their bench with a fresh pot of beer, showing Ciaccona the cadence on the table edge. Within ten, she had steered him from boat-songs to shops, and he was telling them which ones sold tourist brass to Ministry clerks at triple price and which back-lane artisans made honest goods.

When he went out to the privy, Ciaccona leaned back against the wall and let out a long breath. The bright curve vanished from her mouth.

Chisa looked at her across the mugs.

“<font color=coral>You use a different voice for strangers,</font>” Chisa said plainly. “<font color=coral>It goes up in the middle, like you’re singing the menu.</font>”

Ciaccona’s fingers paused on the rim of the bean saucer. She looked at Chisa—straight, unvarnished, with not a trace of the tavern sparkle in her eyes.

“<font color=teal>Pays the ferryman, Chisa,</font>” Ciaccona said in her low, slightly raspy salt-house voice. “<font color=teal>If you give a room the voice they’re hoping for in the first thirty seconds, they tell you where the good shops are and they don’t go poking around for the other one.</font>”

“<font color=coral>It sounds tiring,</font>” Chisa said, taking a sip of her warm rice-beer. “<font color=coral>I’m glad you don’t use it at home.</font>”

Ciaccona rolled a salted bean along the grain of the pine and ate it without a smart answer.

“<font color=teal>What’s your mother’s place like?</font>” Ciaccona asked after a beat. “<font color=teal>You’re the one bringing the wolf through the door. I’m the one being dragged up the marble steps at Fisalia Manor so Cantarella can inspect what her little knight dragged in off the road.</font>”

Chisa thought of Thursday’s call on the frosted porch.

“<font color=coral>The kitchen is very small,</font>” Chisa said. “<font color=coral>And Mama already asked if Lupa eats turnip in the soup. She doesn’t care if Lupa is loud, or if she has ears. She just wants to see who sits across from me at lunch so she knows what the room looks like when I’m not there.</font>”

Ciaccona’s thumb brushed the mute brass wound string at the bottom of the bridge without making a sound. “<font color=teal>And Lupa? She terrified?</font>”

“<font color=coral>She’s been practicing how to cut short-ribs since last Thursday,</font>” Chisa said. Then she added, her brow creasing a fraction, “<font color=coral>I’m a little worried Lupa will try to carry Mama’s coal-scuttle up the stairs before Mr. Sato gets to it and pull the banister out of the wall. The banister bolts in the stair plaster are loose, and Lupa doesn’t know how heavy she is when she’s trying to be polite.</font>”

Ciaccona let out a short, genuine bark of laughter that made the barman look round from his shelf.

Afterwards they followed the old cooper’s directions down the cobblers’ lane behind the dye-vats.

At a coppersmith’s, Chisa went straight past the incense-burners to a flat-bellied brass pipe-warmer, built to hold a slow charcoal briquette under a water-tap so the joint wouldn’t freeze.

“<font color=coral>Mama’s kitchen pipe froze on Thursday,</font>” Chisa told Ciaccona, testing the brass latch with her thumb to make sure it seated cleanly. “<font color=coral>Mr. Sato had to carry buckets up the stairs.</font>”

Ciaccona checked the seams and let her pay. Then, as Chisa turned for home with the parcel, Ciaccona caught her sleeve and steered her into a tiny pottery stall.

“<font color=teal>Hold on, kid. You’re not done.</font>”

“<font color=coral>I bought the warmer,</font>” Chisa said.

“<font color=teal>You bought plumbing supplies,</font>” Ciaccona said firmly, pointing at a tray of small glazed clay birds sitting on straw. “<font color=teal>If you walk in the door and hand your mother a pipe-stove, the wolf’s going to think that’s how gifts work and wrap a crowbar for your birthday. Pick something that doesn’t do a damn thing.</font>”

Chisa looked down at the tray for a long moment and picked up a small sky-blue clay sparrow with a tail-spout that whistled two notes when you tipped it.

“<font color=coral>This one,</font>” Chisa said, paying two coppers for it and tucking it into her coat pocket wrapped in straw.

At the spice merchant next door, Ciaccona spent five minutes staring in grim silence at two things on the counter.

On the left, a red-silk-lined lacquer box of tribute white tea, stamped in gold for magistrate gifts. On the right, a squat stoneware pot of mountain prickly-ash and green pepper-buds in coarse salt, for the woman who had promised Ciaccona a pepper-free dinner.

“<font color=teal>If I bring the tribute tea, I’m grovelling at the gate,</font>” Ciaccona muttered. “<font color=teal>If I bring a two-pound crock of mouth-numbing mountain pepper to a woman who took the spices out of the soup for me, it’s either the best joke she’s heard all winter or she has the butler throw me into the canal.</font>” She looked sideways at Chisa. “<font color=teal>Pick.</font>”

Chisa didn’t even look at the silk box. She laid her gloved hand flat on the lid of the rough stoneware pepper-pot.

“<font color=coral>This one,</font>” Chisa said. “<font color=coral>The tea box sounds like the voice you used on the man in the tavern.</font>”

Ciaccona stared at the stoneware pot under Chisa’s hand, then let out a short breath through her nose and counted four brass coins onto the counter.

“<font color=teal>If she poisons my wine, kid, you’re getting the lute in the will,</font>” Ciaccona said in her ordinary voice, tucking the heavy pepper-crock under her right arm and bumping Chisa’s shoulder lightly with her elbow as they turned up the hill.

“<font color=lavender>It held pressure perfectly well for sixty winters before you touched it,</font>” Ruoyun snapped, setting the round-bellied clay medicine pot down on Lupa’s iron stove-plate hard enough to make the trivet ring.

“<font color=mistyrose>It had a lime burr on the brass seat, Auntie Ruoyun,</font>” Amy said cheerfully, lining up four apothecary packets on the table. “<font color=mistyrose>You’ve been hitting it with a mallet every time it stuck since before anybody’s grandmother was born.</font>”

With the carts going down the south road soon, there wouldn’t be anyone on the ship to Septimont who knew which root went in first, so Amy had marched Ruoyun from the conduit to the apothecary counter to learn it.

“<font color=lavender>Give me that knife,</font>” Ruoyun ordered, snatching it before Amy could hack at the ginger. “<font color=lavender>You are not chopping turnips for a mule! Amy pulled out a squared notebook and began writing it up in tight Startorch machine-shop capitals:

*WARMING DECOCTION (MA — SCAR)*
*1. Vessel: GLAZED CLAY ONLY. (NO IRON — goes black.)*
*2. Stage 1 Cold Soak: well water, astragalus + poria + peony. Wait till the roots sink.*
*3. Thermal Input: hard boil, then damper half shut. Boil down till it’s dark and about a bowl’s left.*
*4. Stage 2 Addition: angelica tail + ginger (paper-thin, window-light through the edge) at FINAL APPROACH.*

Ruoyun leaned over her shoulder to read the page, and her left eyebrow went straight up into her fringe.

“<font color=lavender>What in the name of the six ministries is *final approach*?</font>” Ruoyun demanded, deeply offended. “<font color=lavender>It is a warming decoction, girl, not a dockyard crane! Put that barbaric scribble away and use your nose—you drop the angelica in when the steam stops smelling of raw mud and starts smelling of cut cedar!</font>”

“<font color=mistyrose>What if I have a cold on the ship and my nose is stuffed?</font>” Amy pointed out, writing *(smells like cedar)* in brackets at the bottom anyway. “<font color=mistyrose>Paper doesn’t get a stuffed nose.</font>”

Once the roots were soaking and the fire was going, Ruoyun went to the window shutter with her hands behind her back.

It was the fourth time since lunch she had found an excuse to look north toward the Vale cookhouse chimney, where Lupa had taken Qingxiao.

Amy, swinging her boots on the corner of the table, knew that angle of chin inside out. She had checked the south parapet toward the Lower Wharf at every cross-lane all day.

“<font color=mistyrose>Does it ever stop making your stomach drop when she goes round a corner without her scarf?</font>” Amy asked plainly from the table.

Ruoyun’s hand stayed on the wooden shutter-hook. She didn’t turn round, and she didn’t snap that Celestials didn’t have stomachs.

“<font color=lavender>No,</font>” Ruoyun said to the snow-glare outside. “<font color=lavender>For two hundred years she didn’t need a scarf, and I watched the road anyway. Now she walks barefoot in the slush because she can feel her toes, and when she sneezes she thinks it is an interesting novelty.</font>”

She let go of the hook and shoved the empty bucket into Amy’s chest.

“<font color=lavender>The well,</font>” Ruoyun said briskly. “<font color=lavender>One full bucket for the second reduction, and don’t scrape the bottom in the snow-drift or you’ll get grit in the kettle. Move your feet, the pot’s coming to the boil.</font>”

Amy found the rope-bucket already full on the well-coping and was back across the yard in less than two minutes.

She had her mitten on the wooden latch of the salt-house door when a sound came through the planks that made her boots stop on the sill.

Somebody inside was singing.

An old garrison marching song about a quartermaster’s mule: loud, chesty, unguarded, and a full half-tone flat on every high note.

Amy eased the plank door open three inches.

By the stove, with her back to the door, Ruoyun was singing at the top of her lungs to the medicine pot:

*“<font color=lavender>—and the sergeant sold the saddle for a gallon of sour wine, so we marched to Desorock upon the—</font>”*

The iron hinge gave a tiny squeak under Amy’s mitten.

Ruoyun stopped dead on *the*.

Her mouth clicked shut and she spun round, rigid as a boarding-pike, a flush of mortified fury rising to the roots of her horns.

Amy didn’t laugh. Crossing the floor with the full bucket, she picked up the last half-line exactly where Ruoyun had dropped it:

*“<font color=mistyrose>—upon the baggage-line, boys, upon the baggage-line.</font>”*

She sang it in her clear midnight-radio voice, the note dead in the middle of the key, and set the bucket down without spilling a drop.

Ruoyun pinched the hem of her skirt and executed a bow of glacial court perfection toward the water-pail.

“<font color=lavender>Thank you, Miss Aemeath,</font>” Ruoyun said in a voice that could have frozen boiling lard. “<font color=lavender>Should the ginger pot require an audition for the Mingting Opera House, I shall be sure to submit your name to the committee.</font>”

“<font color=mistyrose>Anytime,</font>” Amy said, picking up the bamboo dipper to fill the side-kettle. “<font color=mistyrose>My Ma sings flat on the high bit too. She says it gives the chorus traction.</font>”

Ruoyun turned her back on her and shoved the stove damper half shut with a sharp *clank*.

For a while neither of them spoke while the pot boiled slowly down. Then, as Amy dropped the ginger and angelica in for the last stretch, Ruoyun began humming the second verse under her breath, stubbornly, and flat on the fourth bar.

Amy leaned her hips against the warm brickwork beside the griddle and hummed the third below her, pulling her own pitch down half a step on purpose so the two notes locked together without a wobble all the way to the end.

At the fifth bell, Ruoyun strained the decoction into a travelling flask and tipped a single drop onto a spoon.

She blew on it, touched her tongue to the drop, and looked at the flask.

“<font color=lavender>Passable,</font>” Ruoyun said.

Amy grinned and reached for the cork. “<font color=mistyrose>Passable! Write that down on the beam next to Barnaby—</font>”

She stopped with the cork in her fingers.

Ruoyun hadn’t walked away. Standing with her face turned toward the flue and her hands behind her back, she tipped her head down toward Amy’s shoulder.

Amy blinked at it. “<font color=mistyrose>Wait. That bucket last Friday didn’t count—Ma snuck her finger under the handle on the slope.</font>”

“<font color=lavender>I am well aware of your mother’s dishonesty,</font>” Ruoyun said to the chimney-bricks, not moving a muscle. “<font color=lavender>Today’s bucket was full to the hoop, and you did not splash the threshold. You have five seconds before I take the iron poker to your knuckles.</font>”

It wasn’t like bone, and it wasn’t like crystal; under her fingertips ran a series of very fine, shallow spiral ridges, smooth as river-worn boxwood, except for one deeper, harder ring two-thirds of the way down that caught the pad of her thumb like a healed notch in an oak branch.

“<font color=mistyrose>It has rings like a tree,</font>” Amy said quietly. “<font color=mistyrose>Right here in the middle there’s a deep one—like a really hard winter.</font>”

Then she pushed her glasses up with one knuckle and marched to the wood-box by the door.

“<font color=lavender>The kindling in this house is stacked like a magpie’s nest,</font>” Ruoyun snapped, snatching up the splitting hatchet. “<font color=lavender>Cork your flask before the steam escapes and bring me that birch log—if I leave your wolf to chop the night wood, she’ll split the flagstones!</font>”

*<font color=thistle>We are a pair of absolute terrors, darling,</font>* the High Priestess observed from the back of Iuno’s head. *<font color=thistle>One of you is breathing through her nose like a scalded kitten, and the other is dribbling tea out the left corner of her mouth.</font>*

*<font color=royalblue>Shut up,</font>* Iuno told her silently, pressing a folded linen napkin with great dignity to her numb lower lip.

They were sitting at a mutton-broth stall inside the south gate arch. Yangyang had taken her first spoonful straight off the boil, burned her tongue as she did at every meal, and was sitting very straight with watering eyes, pretending nothing had happened. Iuno had eaten one shred of pickled lantern-pepper hidden under the coriander and could no longer feel anything below her cheekbones.

Yangyang silently pushed the saucer of plain bread crusts, the stall’s cure for pepper, closer to Iuno’s wrist.

In return, Iuno nudged the spoon-rest against Yangyang’s bowl so she’d let the broth cool.

Of the thirteen other people in the salt-house, Yangyang was the one Iuno would have put last on her pad for a solitary afternoon. Yangyang’s quiet had no handles on it. Years ago Iuno had filed her shijie under *weather*, and what Yangyang thought of that, Iuno had never been able to tell. And it was Iuno who had drawn the line around whatever lived between Yangyang and Mei, which left her feeling, alone with Yangyang, uncomfortably like a landlord inspecting a tenant’s fence.

Once some feeling had come back to her chin, Iuno poured the last of her Earl Grey and laid out her schedule.

“<font color=royalblue>The carters’ roster at the upper granary closes at the fourth bell,</font>” Iuno said. “<font color=royalblue>If we walk up now we can check the mule-teams, stop at the post-house about the coast relay-horses, and be back by the well before the shade reaches the steps. Unless you had another errand?</font>”

Yangyang blew three times on her spoonful before drinking it.

“<font color=paleturquoise>I only meant to walk the middle lanes for an hour,</font>” Yangyang said in her soft, exact voice, touching the leather-bound patrol log tucked into her sash. “<font color=paleturquoise>I keep a list at the back of my book of small things happening in the Hold, to tell my shijie at the solstice.</font>”

Iuno paused with her teacup halfway to her mouth. “<font color=royalblue>Small things?</font>”

“<font color=paleturquoise>Who fixed their shutter,</font>” Yangyang said. “<font color=paleturquoise>Which dogs had puppies. The post-house doesn’t shut its wicket until dusk.</font>”

Iuno stood up. “<font color=royalblue>Then we shall do your lanes first. Lead on.</font>”

For the next hour they walked the hard-frozen alleys between the second terrace and the third, and Iuno watched how a Watcher looked at a town when nobody was dying in it.

Yangyang didn’t look at bastions or padlocks. At the bakehouse corner she stopped for a full minute to watch a fat yellow dog asleep on the warm oven-dome, and wrote one line in the back of her log. Outside the butcher’s, Butcher Sun stood on his step with his lips puckered, managing three shrill notes of a street-tune before the whistle collapsed into air. Yangyang’s mouth curved; she wrote that down too.

By the third corner, Iuno found her own eyes scanning the eaves.

“<font color=paleturquoise>The grey roof-sparrows take the sesame seeds off the paper when he turns round to stir the sugar-pot,</font>” Yangyang observed quietly. “<font color=paleturquoise>The blue magpies wait under the bench for the dropped haws because their beaks are too heavy for the tray-rim.</font>”

“<font color=royalblue>And the cobbler’s tortoiseshell cat,</font>” Iuno said, pointing a gloved finger toward the sunny window-ledge across the alley, “<font color=royalblue>has climbed head-first into a cavalry riding-boot so only its back legs and tail are sticking out of the leather.</font>”

Yangyang turned her head, looked at the boot on the windowsill, and opened her patrol log again.

“<font color=royalblue>Are you writing the boot down?</font>” Iuno asked.

“<font color=paleturquoise>Shijie likes cats,</font>” Yangyang said simply, her pencil moving across the paper.

Passing the upper rim of Huizhen Plaza, Yangyang mentioned that Suisui meant to carry a brazier and a kettle of ginger water up to the Jinzhou watch-roof on the solstice night.

“<font color=royalblue>Then tell your sister to set the brazier on the leeward side, well back from the edge,</font>” Iuno said, falling straight into her administrative cadence. “<font color=royalblue>An updraft on an open ridge will throw ash in your eyes. “<font color=paleturquoise>The ladder inside the signal-tower lost its bottom rungs in the flood,</font>” Yangyang said, looking up at the high ridge-beam. “<font color=paleturquoise>Can your wheel reach the tiles?</font>”

“<font color=royalblue>Thank you,</font>” Iuno said.

Yangyang opened her patrol log on her knee to write down the view from the signal-tower, leaving the small book flat in the sunlight between their hips.

Iuno’s eyes dropped to the open left-hand page.

Every entry for the week began the same way:

*Monday. Grey drizzle until noon, clearing from the west.*
*Tuesday. Snowed at dusk. Dry flake, did not melt on the collar.*
*Wednesday. Clear, hard freeze. Three inches on the well-coping.*
*Thursday. Clear and cold. Snow off the yard wall, eaten with lemon peel.*

Iuno stared at the four lines. Weather, at the top of every day Yangyang was saving for her sister.

Iuno’s throat went tight. “<font color=royalblue>Yangyang,</font>” she said quietly. “<font color=royalblue>About how I filed her—</font>”

Yangyang didn’t let her turn it into a confession. She tapped the top of Tuesday’s entry once with the brass ferrule of her pencil stub.

“<font color=paleturquoise>When I get there,</font>” Yangyang said plainly, “<font color=paleturquoise>the first thing she’ll ask is whether it rained on the canal. She spent a long time being the weather; now she likes hearing about it from inside a house.</font>”

Iuno looked at the pencilled words *Dry flake, did not melt on the collar*, and then out across the sunlit gorge.

Her right hand, curled tight over her knee since she saw the page, slowly uncurled and lay flat on the tile between them.

“<font color=royalblue>You may put down,</font>” Iuno said after a moment, her voice dry and even, “<font color=royalblue>that at lunch I bit a piece of pickled lantern-pepper and couldn’t feel my lower lip for twenty minutes. She ought to have an accurate account of the company.</font>”

Yangyang didn’t smile, and she didn’t ask if Iuno was joking. She turned the pencil point-down on the Friday page and wrote in her small, tidy hand: *Iuno bit a pepper at the south gate. Numb until the third bell.*

“<font color=royalblue>You actually wrote that,</font>” Iuno said.

“<font color=paleturquoise>You told me to,</font>” Yangyang replied.

Below them, in the blue dusk shadow of the Forty-Eight, a heavy *clank… thump* echoed up between the walls.

Iuno leaned an inch forward over the eaves.

Mother Sun’s water guardian was climbing the glazed steps with two brimming tubs on its left hook, Mother Sun walking at its hip, Buling strolling up the dry gutter swinging two crocks, and on the flat yoke-pad of its right shoulder, boots dangling ten feet above the ice, coal-soot across her cheek and a turnip stem in her hand, sat Mei.

Halfway up, Mei spotted them on the ridge and waved both arms over her head like a girl on a festival float.

Iuno’s hand clamped onto the ridge-tile hard enough to whiten her knuckles. Every muscle in her back went taut to call the moonwheel and dive.

*<font color=thistle>Technically, darling,</font>* the High Priestess noted in a voice thin with effort, *<font color=thistle>her boots haven’t touched a single step.</font>*

Iuno stayed seated and made herself breathe until the guardian stepped over the top riser and knelt to let Mei off onto level ground.

Only then did Iuno lift her right hand and wave once back.

Beside her, Yangyang closed the cover of her patrol log and tucked the pencil stub into the loop.

“<font color=paleturquoise>She kept the rule,</font>” Yangyang said mildly. “<font color=paleturquoise>Her feet didn’t touch the stairs.</font>”

Iuno let out a breath, called the moonwheel to the eaves, and held her hand down to pull Yangyang aboard.

By the sixth bell the salt-house smelled like a swamp-drain had backed up into a bakery, and Amy had the warm corner of the kang.

The pairs had straggled back in as the sky went ink-dark, and though everybody had drifted toward their usual places, the seams were still mixed up round the edges.

Lupa marched straight to the stove, spotted a brown ring of boiled root on her griddle, and bellowed to know who had been cooking tree-bark on her iron. Ruoyun informed her the stove had been filthy to begin with, right before Qingxiao stepped up barefoot from the flue and slid a covered bowl of Vale millet into Ruoyun’s hands.

Ruoyun lifted the lid, took one small spoonful, and said a single word:

“<font color=lavender>Proper.</font>”

Then Buling had pried the oiled-paper lids off the two takeaway crocks of Lower Wharf river-snail noodles in the middle of the pine trestle.

The stench of fermented bamboo shoot rolled from the door to the chimney. Lupa’s ears pinned flat (“<font color=indianred>What died in that pot?!</font>”); Ciaccona hid her nose behind her lute; and Mei, wedged against Iuno’s side, pointed out that the indoor ban only covered durian, while Iuno held her chin with two fingers and scrubbed the coal-dust off her cheek with a damp handkerchief before handing her a spoon.

Cartethyia came up from the fourth berth carrying the goose so he could see the lamps. Her laurel sat dead-level on its new silver saddle, and round the goose’s neck on a red cord hung a walnut-sized silver lock.

Ciaccona lowered her lute-neck an inch and stared at the bird. “<font color=teal>Cara. What is that on the poultry?</font>”

“<font color=skyblue>It’s a hundred-year lock!</font>” Cartethyia declared proudly, setting the goose down by Suoming’s crate, where the bird immediately pecked his own silver shackle to make it chime. “<font color=skyblue>The smith in the rope-makers’ lane said it keeps winter fevers off young ones, and Suoming got him to bring his anvil right out into the sun for us!</font>”

Ciaccona pressed the heel of her uninjured palm over her eyes. “<font color=teal>You bought a baby’s longevity amulet for a twenty-pound goose.</font>”

“<font color=skyblue>He only hatched in the spring, Cia!</font>”

Suisui tacked the noodle-paper weiqi position to the central post. Yangyang came over with her bowl, studied the upper right corner for three breaths, and murmured, “<font color=paleturquoise>Her knight’s move on the fourth line is a trap, Jie.</font>”

“<font color=thistle>I know,</font>” Suisui said smoothly, taking a pickled mustard-stem from Yangyang’s saucer. “<font color=thistle>She tried to move the stone behind it with her tail at noon.</font>”

Under the bench, where Hsin lay curled in fox shape by Suoming’s boots with a saucer of noodles, two ears flattened sideways into her fur.

Chisa set her parcel and the blue clay sparrow down on the kang kerb. When Lupa poked the sparrow with one finger, Ciaccona called across in her easy salt-house voice, “<font color=teal>Don’t break the tail-spout, wolf, the kid picked that out for her mum.</font>”

Lupa pulled her finger back, reached for the ladle, and paused.

She looked down the table: Qingxiao passing her the salt before she reached for it; Suoming sliding the pickled plums toward Cartethyia’s wrist; Mei dropping the biggest piece of bean-curd skin into Buling’s bowl.

Lupa pointed the ladle at the floor by the door-crate.

“<font color=indianred>Oi. Fox,</font>” Lupa said. “<font color=indianred>You can’t lie. Were those mahjong tiles straight at breakfast?</font>”

The salt-house went quiet save for the goose giving a small, metallic *clink* with his silver lock.

Hsin lifted her chin off her paws and looked cheerfully at the ceiling.

“<font color=gold>No,</font>” Hsin said.

Every head turned to the corner of the kang where Amy sat cross-legged with her bowl.

By the flue, Ruoyun slammed her chopsticks down onto her porcelain saucer with a sharp *clack*. “<font color=lavender>I knew it! She had the blank white slate up her jumper cuff before the porridge was on the board! I saw her thumb!</font>”

Amy took an unhurried spoonful of millet, chewed, and swallowed.

“<font color=mistyrose>Only some of them,</font>” Amy said airily.

Suisui’s left eyebrow rose a fraction of an inch. She glanced once from the weiqi paper pinned on the post to her sister sitting beside Iuno, and then back to Amy. “<font color=thistle>Which ones, Amy?</font>”

“<font color=mistyrose>Guess,</font>” Amy said, and hooked a piece of pickled bamboo shoot out of the middle crock.

Across the board, Ciaccona caught Amy’s eye over her tea-mug, gave a tiny lopsided smirk, and drank without saying a word.

Nobody else at the table pressed the question.

Lupa glanced at Qingxiao’s bare feet; Iuno looked at the corner of Yangyang’s patrol log; Chisa touched the string on her parcel; Suisui smoothed her cuff. Every one of them, Amy could see plain as a cockpit readout, had just decided their own pair was the one she’d rigged.

In her pocket, Amy’s fingers found the blank white tile beside the flask of scar-decoction. She had only palmed the two white ones, to get Ruoyun alone in the kitchen before the carts left. The rest had fallen out of Chisa’s sock by blind luck.

When the bowls were stacked, Amy held Buling’s rosewood box out over the table.

“<font color=mistyrose>Tiles back,</font>” Amy called, shaking the empty box once. “<font color=mistyrose>Or Buling’s going to invoice me for the set.</font>”

Tiles came clacking back: Lupa’s red dragon and Qingxiao’s twin, Ruoyun’s white slate with a glare, Mei’s and Buling’s Eights, Chisa’s and Ciaccona’s Ones of Bamboo.

Hsin shifted tall by the door, ducking the tie-beam this time, and dropped her Nine of Characters in.

“<font color=gold>Seven pairs,</font>” Hsin remarked, her golden ears tipping forward. “<font color=gold>A winning hand, if you draw it clean.</font>”

Amy slid the wooden lid shut along its brass groove and lifted the box in one palm.

It was distinctly too light.

When she tilted it, the tiles slid with a hollow rattle. A good many of the slots were empty.

Down by the central post, Cartethyia had her right hand tucked deep into her cloak pocket beside the goose’s neck; by the door-crate, Suoming sat upright with Nine Shadows across her knees and a single green-backed piece of bamboo resting level on the corner of the crate beside her inverted teacup; and across the board, Suisui’s left sleeve hung with a neat, square weight in her lap while Iuno’s coat pocket stayed buttoned shut.

Amy tossed the light box into Buling’s lap. Buling felt the missing weight, raised her eyebrows once under her hat, and shoved it into her bag without a word. Amy slipped her hand back into her pocket and closed her fingers around the warm white bone.

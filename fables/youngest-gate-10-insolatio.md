## 一 · 出 — OUT

Narrator: Iuno woke at ten past four with her right shoulder full of glass.

It does this now. Four hours ahead of weather, give or take, and the give and the take have been getting smaller since the plateau, which she has recorded and has told one person, a wolf, who received the information the way she receives most information, which is by feeding her something.

She got up without waking the bed. Mei sleeps like a dropped coat.

The front room's window still will not latch. Mrs Ren has a man about it. The half-inch gap had a draught in it going the wrong way for the season, and Iuno stood in front of that gap for a while with her living hand flat on the sill, and the draught turned over once against her palm, unhurried, the way something rolls to face the other way in its sleep.

<font color=royalblue>"Go back to sleep."</font>

The draught stayed exactly where it was.

<font color=royalblue>"…Fine."</font>

*The tenants in this house, darling. One of them doesn't pay rent and one of them is you.*

---

Narrator: She took the lamp out to the corner where the ward changes, past Old Feng's shuttered paper-goods shop, because there is a board there and she has read it every morning for twenty-two days as a discipline, the way other people run.

The notice was new. Six lines under a heading that counts seven.

She got about four characters into it before the lamplight showed her the rest of the street, and the rest of the street was awake, at twenty past four, quietly, in a way that made the back of her neck do something.

Every door in the lane stood open. Every door had a chair in front of it, and on the chairs and over the rails and slung on cords run wall to wall at the height of a man's raised hand were quilts, coats, boots, hats, mattresses, seed trays, medicine boxes with their lids off, drying racks of paper laid flat under stones at the corners, and books.

Books everywhere. Opened to the middle and stood on their spines like small tents, in rows, all along the terrace, all the way down.

An old man went past her with a chest on his back and said something cheerful about her lamp.

Iuno stood in a lane that had turned its pockets out, holding a lamp nobody needed, wearing her coat, at half past four in the morning.

*Ah,* said the High Priestess, in the voice she keeps for the exact moment a room reveals its actual agenda. *So it's today.*

Narrator: The coats went last, because Mrs Ren does the coats herself, and the reason she does them herself is that a coat has to be turned out first, and people are sentimental about pockets.

She took Lupa's without comment. She took Ciaccona's and held it up and made a small hard noise at the state of the lining. She took Chisa's, checked the sleeves, and set it aside on its own, which nobody asked about.

She got to Iuno and put a hand out and waited.

Iuno stood in the yard with one arm and a coat on it.

Four seconds went by, which in a yard holding nine people is a long time.

Then she turned away from the line, put the coat over the back of the stool, went through it herself with her living hand, and came out with two things, which went into the breast pocket of the shirt she was wearing under it. One of them was flat and folded and about the size of four characters. The other one had a shape.

She hung the coat up herself.

Mrs Ren watched the whole of it from three feet away with her hands folded in front of her, and when it was done she pegged the shoulder seam straight, twice, because Iuno had got it crooked, and said nothing at all, and moved on to the next one.

Lupa, at the cauldron, kept her head where it was. Her left ear went round.

## 二 · 看 — LOOK

Narrator: The east gate at seven had a hundred and eleven people in it and about four hundred years of paper.

Shen's table stands where it always stands, at the north side of the arch, out of the traffic, with the schedule of charges on a board repainted so many times that the numbers stand proud of the wood. On the sixth she puts out boards. Six of them, on trestles, in a line along the gate wall, and she does it alone, at four, and it takes her two hours because of her hands.

On the boards, weighted at every corner with river stones the size of a fist, laid flat and open and face up in the early sun: three hundred and six letters.

Amy stopped about eight metres short of them and stayed there.

<font color=mistyrose>"…Ma."</font>

<font color=moccasin>"I see them."</font>

<font color=mistyrose>"That's the tin."</font>

<font color=moccasin>"I know."</font>

<font color=mistyrose>"She empties the tin."</font>

<font color=royalblue>"Once a year, in the sun, so the paper lasts."</font> Iuno had gone very still in the way she goes still at exhibitions. <font color=royalblue>"The letters would rot in a tin in this climate inside of a decade, and she has held some of them for forty years, so at some point a woman at a gate worked out that the only way to keep an undeliverable letter is to take it out on the day everybody takes everything out, and put it in the sun with the quilts, where it looks like housekeeping."</font>

<font color=moccasin>"That's not housekeeping."</font>

<font color=royalblue>"It is entirely housekeeping. That is what makes it survivable."</font>

They walked the line.

People were doing it in both directions, unhurried, hands behind backs, the way people go round a market they have no money for. An old woman went along the whole six boards with her chin up, reading nothing, looking at every one. Two boys ran the length of it and were shouted at by three separate adults for running near paper. A carter stopped at the fourth board, took his hat off, put it back on, and went to work.

Nobody picked one up.

---

Narrator: Shen was at the table with a customer, a young man who wanted to tell somebody in the south that the baby had come and had a name.

She charged him for nineteen characters, read it back to him in the flat unbeautiful voice of a professional reader, took his coins, and sent him away, and only then looked up.

*"You'll be here about the boards."*

<font color=moccasin>"We're here to look."</font>

*"Everyone is here to look. It's the sixth."* Shen squared a fresh sheet without needing to see it. *"Look, then. They're out until eleven. Rule is the same as everywhere else: you may look, and if one of them is yours you say so and you take it, and I'll want a name and I'll want it out loud."*

<font color=mistyrose>"How many have gone that way?"</font>

*"In forty years."*

<font color=mistyrose>"Yes."</font>

Shen thought about it for exactly as long as it takes to check a number you already know.

*"Eleven."*

Amy's face did something and then stopped doing it.

<font color=mistyrose>"Right."</font>

*"The last was six years ago. A man from the fish market, for his mother, and it took him two hours on the boards to find it because he was looking for her hand and I had written it, and he had forgotten that. He sat down on that step there afterwards for most of the morning and then he bought a second one off me and posted it to the same bad address."* Shen wiped the brush. *"That one's on board five. Second from the left."*

Mei looked at board five, second from the left, for a while.

<font color=moccasin>"Mine isn't here."</font>

*"Yours went into the box on the twenty-first and out of the city on the twenty-second and it has three prefectures and a survey to survive."* Shen capped the ink. *"They come back in the spring, when they come back. So you'll know by the plum rains and you won't know before, and I'd tell you not to check the ward box on your way past every morning, except that you will, and so does everybody, and the box has a dent in the lid from forty years of people checking."*

<font color=moccasin>"What are the plum rains?"</font>

*"Meiyu. The sixth month rains. It's what today is for."* A small dry motion of the hand at the whole gate, at the boards, at a city standing its books up on their spines. *"Everything you own comes out on the sixth because in three weeks the air turns into a wet cloth and stays that way for forty days, and the things that go into the plum rains damp come out of them ruined. That's the entire holiday. It's a laundry day with a Bureau attached."*

Amy had her head tilted.

<font color=mistyrose>"Say the name of the rains again."</font>

*"Meiyu."*

<font color=mistyrose>"And that's written how."</font>

*"Plum, and rain."*

<font color=mistyrose>"…Is it the same character as the first one of the five."</font>

The gate did a certain amount of ordinary noise for a couple of seconds.

*"No,"* said Shen.

<font color=mistyrose>"You're sure."</font>

*"I have written that surname perhaps two hundred times and I have written the rains on a weather board every year for forty. They are not the same character, they are not related, and they do not sound the same to anybody who grew up saying either of them."* Shen picked up the brush again. *"And you'd be the fourth to ask me."*

<font color=royalblue>"Who were the other three."</font>

*"Next,"* said Shen, to the queue, which had one person in it.

Narrator: The queue's one person was a woman with a form, and she took four minutes, and while she took them Amy walked board six on her own from the far end backwards, which is how she reads anything she is frightened of.

Three hundred and six letters, face up in the sun, none of them hers to read.

She read none of them and looked at all of them, which is the whole permitted operation, and it turns out that the permitted operation gives you a great deal: the hands, the sizes, the fold-wear, the ones with a smear at the bottom right where somebody's thumb sat while they dictated. Eleven of them were on the good paper. Two of them were four sheets long. One was six characters and had been paid for and written and posted and had come back, six characters, from four prefectures away, in a hand that had been steady the whole way across.

She came back to the table and sat down on the customer's stool without being asked, which nobody at that gate has done in some years.

Narrator: Mei went along the boards a second time on her own, slowly, and at the fourth board she crouched down on her heels the way she does, with her elbows on her knees, and stayed there long enough that a stallholder brought her a stool she declined and then accepted.

Iuno stood in the arch with her back to the whole thing and held a folded paper up between herself and the low sun.

It is four characters, and she has known what they say since the twenty-third, and the trouble with it has never been the four characters.

The trouble is the seal space. A square of clean paper at the lower left, the standard size, ruled faintly, waiting for a chop — and no chop on it, and a document of that kind is a sentence with the subject left out.

She had four candidates. She disliked three of them.

Held up against the sun, from underneath, at the gate, at twenty past seven in the morning, the paper showed her something it has been declining to show her by lamp for a fortnight: inside the seal space, in the fibre of the sheet itself, pressed in when the sheet was made and about a shade lighter than the paper around it, was a chop.

Made in. Pressed into the sheet at the mill, before anybody wrote a word on it.

She looked at it for a long moment with the light coming through her own hand as well.

*Darling,* said the High Priestess, very quietly, without any of the velvet on, *somebody had this paper made.*

<font color=royalblue>"Yes."</font>

*Before they wrote on it.*

<font color=royalblue>"Yes."</font>

*And they left the space empty anyway.*

Iuno folded it along its old folds and put it back in the breast pocket, and turned round, and her daughter — the word arrived unannounced and she let it stand — was coming back up the line of boards with her hands in her jacket and her thumb working at something small and square in the left pocket, and she took it about halfway out, and put it back.

Iuno filed the halfway.

*"The thing in your left pocket."*

Amy stopped.

*"Bring it when you want it read. Not before."* The brush went on moving. *"And don't take it to anybody else at this gate. There are two of them who'll make something up, and one of those two is very good at it, and you'd believe him."*

<font color=mistyrose>"…How long have you known it was in there."</font>

*"Nine times,"* said Shen.

## THE WEST TERRACES

Narrator: The stair on the west side goes up in four flights with a landing at each, and every one of those landings had a household on it, and every household was moving.

Lupa took the first chest at ten past seven without being asked, from a woman on the second flight who had got it as far as the top step and had then had to sit down on it, and she took it the way she takes things, which is by arriving underneath it.

She put it in the sun in the yard, squared to the wall, lid propped.

The woman thanked her and then stood in the middle of her own yard with her hands moving against each other and got quieter and quieter.

Lupa's ears came round.

<font color=indianred>"…Did I break it."</font>

<font color=coral>"You've made a debt."</font>

<font color=indianred>"I carried a box."</font>

<font color=coral>"You carried a box for a stranger on a day the whole street can see the yard, and now she owes a foreigner, and she can't pay it back over years the way she'd pay a neighbour, because you'll be gone."</font> Chisa had her head at the angle. <font color=coral>"She's been standing there four breaths longer than she meant to. She's working out what she has in the house."</font>

<font color=indianred>"Then I'll tell her it's nothing."</font>

<font color=coral>"That's worse. That prices it."</font>

Lupa stood in the yard for a moment with her tail entirely still, which is rare enough that Chisa noted it.

<font color=indianred>"In Septimont if I do you a favour I get to *say so*. Loudly. At a party. For nine years. That's the whole point, that's the *interest* — "</font>

<font color=coral>"Mm."</font>

<font color=indianred>" — and here I have to let her pay me off inside a morning or I've *hurt* her."</font>

<font color=coral>"Yes."</font>

<font color=indianred>"That's a stupid system."</font>

<font color=coral>"It's your system upside down. Yours runs the debt long on purpose. Hers closes it before dark, because everyone on this stair has to still be on this stair next year."</font>

Lupa looked at the four flights of stairs, and the nine households on them, and the whole city below with its books standing open on every wall.

<font color=indianred>"…Huh."</font>

Then she went and asked the woman for a chair.

She tried water first and got a face like a slammed shutter. So: a chair. Out of the house, in the doorway, for an hour, from noon, so she could sit in it and eat her own lunch on a stranger's step in a ward where a step is the only real estate anybody owns on the sixth.

The woman brightened up like a lamp and went and got the good one.

<font color=coral>"That was well done."</font>

<font color=indianred>"Don't."</font>

<font color=coral>"You asked for something that costs her, in public, that she can point at."</font>

<font color=indianred>"Chisa."</font>

<font color=coral>"I'm putting it on the record."</font>

<font color=indianred>"Put something else on the record."</font>

Narrator: They did four more flights and eleven more chests and a mattress that took both of them and an argument, and somewhere in the third hour Chisa's pace changed.

It changed by about a fifth of a step, on the flat, at the turn by the dyer's, and Lupa noticed it in the way she notices everything, which is late and completely.

The city had gone quiet. Every wall on the west side was hung with quilts, and quilts eat sound. The lane that gives back a hard flat slap off the render at the second turn gave back nothing. The rain-butt at the corner she counts from had been moved into the yard to be scrubbed. The dyer's had its vats out and its door shut, so the smell was in a different place by four metres, and four metres is everything.

Chisa stood at the turn and did not say any of that.

## THE BASKET

Narrator: Ward four seals the basket at the ninth hour and Clerk Wen does it himself, because the last clerk did it himself, because the one before him did.

Eleven pages went in this year. Nine of them are ordinary: a page of accounts, two receipts, a boy's exercise, half a printed almanac, a list of guests, a note about a pump, a page of somebody's ninth-month figures, and a sheet of squared paper with one line of workings on it.

The tenth is a letter that begins *To you, whom I haven't met* and was picked up at the foot of the wall below the ninth tower at about eleven at night by a patroller who folded it once without reading it, and there is no plausible route by which such a page reaches the ground at that place, and the patroller has not mentioned it to anybody, and did not enter her own name in the finder's column, and Chixia can explain none of that and did it anyway.

The eleventh came off a line in the east market at about a quarter past five in the afternoon.

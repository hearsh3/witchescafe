# Radio Nobody
### A Story of Reconnaissance Squad 5
*Sign-On · Interference · Whoever Is Listening Nearest*

---

*]anyone may walk it[*
*]this is considered, by the relevant offices,[*

## I. Dead Air

*ROYA FRONTIER RELAY, CHANNEL 9 (EMERGENCY / HAILING).*
*Regulation traffic only.*
*Regulation traffic, that winter, averaged four words a day.*

Winter in the north country turned out to contain the following: snow, in industrial quantities; a sky that went violet at four in the afternoon and stayed violet, enormously, until ten the next morning; an inn with a stove Kit had personally befriended; and silence.

The silence was the discovery. Kit had grown up under a cavern sky that hummed. Generators, arrays, the Riseway's long harmonic, forty thousand people breathing conditioned air — Lahai-Roi ran a permanent bass note under every hour of her life, and she had assumed, the way you assume your own pulse, that the note was the world. Up here the world went quiet at dusk and stayed quiet, and the quiet had a texture, and Kit spent the first week of real winter walking out onto the inn's porch at night just to stand in it with her audio gain maxed, listening to snow land on snow.

Then she started leaving the shortwave on.

It was Teddy's set, technically — a salvage-built receiver Teddy kept for eavesdropping on Collective logistics traffic, out of professional habit and personal spite — and it lived on the common-room table between the stove and the window, and at night, when the ionosphere did its cold-weather tricks, it pulled in voices from absurd distances. Depot chatter. Convoy check-ins. Weather flags from stations Kit had to look up on the map.

On the ninth night she caught this:

*"—escort four to relay, confirm road state past marker fifty."*

*"Road state confirmed, drifts to the axles, we're laagered till light. Escort four out."* A pause, carrier still open, the hiss of an empty channel and a young voice deciding whether to spend four more words on it. *"Relay, is anyone else on tonight?"*

*"Regulation traffic only, escort four."*

*"Copy."* The carrier held one more second. *"Escort four out."*

Kit knew the voice. She knew it the way she knew her own hands. Same cadence, half a tone off, or maybe hers was the half-tone: a KT, one of the four hundred, laagered in a drift somewhere on the resettlement roads, sitting a cold watch under the same violet sky, asking the night if anyone else was on it.

*Regulation traffic only.*

Kit sat by the radio until the stove burned down. In her pack upstairs, spring-Kit rode warm in a folded poster. Out there in the dark, a batch-sister was holding a pass alone, waiting for the lanterns to come over the hill, and the whole apparatus of the world had exactly four words for her.

At breakfast Kit put the problem on the table the way she put everything on the table: whole, loud, and before anyone had finished their tea.

"We rove," she said. "Which is great. We pull convoys out of ditches. But we can only be standing nearest to, like, one road at a time. And there's hundreds of them out there. KTs. TDs. S-series. Walking the lines all winter, and the only voice on the air tells them *regulation traffic only.*" She planted her hands on the table. "We can be standing nearest on *every* road. Sound goes everywhere. That's the whole point of sound."

Teddy stirred her tea, which she did when she was pretending to deliberate about something she'd already decided.

"You want a transmitter," she said.

"I want a *station.*"

"Broadcast on Collective-administered spectrum without a license." Teddy set the spoon down with the care of a woman handling something delicious. "That's a Schedule 12 violation. Interference with logistics infrastructure. They'd write us up at the ministerial level."

"Is that a no?"

"It's a *love letter,*" Teddy said, and went to get her coat.

Bridget looked at Leva over her coffee. Leva looked back, and shrugged, and the shrug contained a full risk assessment, three contingency plans, and a verdict.

"I know where there's a tower," Leva said.

## II. Salvage Rights

*ROYA FRONTIER BROADCASTING AUTHORITY. FACILITY 6, DISPOSAL NOTICE.*
*Decommissioned, pending demolition.*
*Notice posted eleven years ago.*
*The Authority itself was dissolved nine years ago, in a budget consolidation.*
*The tower, uninformed, kept standing.*

The tower stood on a hill two days east, a rusted lattice mast over a cinderblock transmitter shack, and the shack's lock surrendered to Leva in the time it took Kit to say "should we—".

Inside: a broadcast plant from before the crisis, cold-mothballed by somebody conscientious, wrapped and greased and labeled in a neat dead hand, waiting eleven years past its own demolition order for anyone at all to come up the hill.

Teddy walked the racks once, slowly, with her light, and the whole architecture of her changed. Her shoulders came down. Her hands came out of her pockets. She touched the big tube amplifier the way other people touch horses.

"Continental-grade final stage," she said. "Pre-crisis. They built these to talk through weather. Through *mountains.*" She turned around, backlit, incandescent. "I'm taking all of it."

"The rover fits maybe a third of it," Bridget said.

"I'm taking the *correct* third."

The rover — legally grey, acquired at a frontier auction where Leva had bid with a confidence that discouraged questions and a currency that discouraged receipts — spent the next six days becoming something else. Teddy gutted the transmitter shack down to its frame and rebuilt the plant compact and rugged in the rover's cargo bay: exciter, a solid-state final she Frankensteined out of the mothballed spares, a tuner she machined herself, cable runs dressed with a fanaticism Kit found frankly moving. The mast telescoped from the roof rack, guyed to the bumpers, packable in eleven minutes by a crew of four who drilled it until Bridget was satisfied, which took nine attempts and one spectacular collapse that Teddy declared a "structural learning event."

Power came off the engine and a bank of scavenged cells. Range, Teddy calculated, was three hundred kilometers of reliable coverage on a cold clear night, and on the freak nights, when the frozen sky turned into a mirror, the whole north.

Kit painted the call sign on the cargo door herself. They'd argued about the name for two days — Teddy had proposed something with her own initials in it; Bridget had vetoed everything tactical; Leva had submitted, in writing, "Radio Shut Up And Drive" — until Kit remembered the survey foreman in the ditch, the helpless gesture at the wreckage, *who sent you,* and the answer Bridget had given, and the way the answer had settled into all of them like ballast.

**RADIO NOBODY**, the door said, in Kit's best hero-play lettering, red on green.

Underneath, smaller, Bridget's addition, in her square captain's hand:

*Whoever is listening nearest.*

## III. Sign-On

*RADIO NOBODY. PROGRAM SCHEDULE, DRAFT 1.*
*Field 1 — broadcast hours: when we feel like it.*
*Field 2 — target audience: you know who you are.*
*Field 3 — licensing status: [this field intentionally left blank]*

They signed on for the first time from a hilltop above the resettlement corridor, at the hour when the light goes violet and every convoy on the roads laagers up for the night, which Kit had chosen on purpose, because that was the hour a batch-sister had once asked an empty channel if anyone else was on.

Teddy brought the final stage up in increments, watching her meters like a midwife. The carrier steadied. The needle sat where she'd told it to sit.

"We're radiating," she said. "Three hundred kilometers of Roya can hear whatever happens next, so *think* before you—"

Kit was already leaning into the microphone.

"HELLO, THE ROADS," she said, at a volume that made the needle slap its pin and Teddy grab for the gain. "This is Radio Nobody! We're on a hill! You can't have the hill's coordinates, but you can have EVERYTHING ELSE. If you're on a convoy, if you're on a watch, if you're standing a post in the dark somewhere waiting for the lanterns to come over it — this one's for you. This one's ALL for you. First song of the first night of the rest of the winter!" She stabbed the deck with a finger. "It goes fast and it means it!"

The song went fast and it meant it. It was off Kit's personal drive — coastal frontier rock, guitars like a bar fight, the kind of thing the commissary catalogue had filed under *morale, other* — and it went out across three hundred kilometers of frozen dark, into the cabs of laagered haulers, into depot canteens, into relay shacks where operators paid to guard silence sat up very straight.

Then Bridget took the chair.

She'd brought her own crate of music up from the south, the collection she'd built over thirty years of after-shifts, the stuff the squad called *the boring jazz* with the fondness of people who'd fallen asleep to it in three provinces. She racked the first disc, and dropped the needle Teddy had machined for her, and leaned into the microphone at conversational range, and her voice came out the way it came out at fires: level, unhurried, in charge of nothing and steady about it.

"Evening," she said. "That was Kit. This is the other kind of hour. If you're on watch right now, this next one's long, so settle in. If your sergeant asks what you're listening to, it's the weather report." A pause, timed like a veteran. "Which is also coming up. But first — this is for a friend of mine who used to hum through reloads. Off-key. Three notes. She'd have hated this song, and requested it anyway, to annoy me."

The jazz walked out into the dark. Horn, brushes, a bass line with all the hurry of falling snow. Somewhere out there, three hundred kilometers of winter had it now.

The weather report was Teddy's, delivered under protest that fooled nobody, in the flat forensic voice she used for eviscerations, and it became, inside a week, the most quoted segment on the frontier:

*"Weather. High pressure over the corridor through tomorrow. Overnight low, savage; wear the good gloves, log it as equipment preservation. Winds northwest, unregulated, uncorridored, and unacceptable, arriving whenever they please, which I have personally filed a complaint about, to the sky, which ignored me. Tomorrow: sun. Late, probably. The sun up here is unadministered and answers to nothing, and you will get used to it, and then, I'm informed, you will get to like it. Road state past marker fifty: drifts to the axles, plow crew's two days out, tell your dispatcher the schedule was optimistic and Radio Nobody said so. That's the weather. It belongs to everyone. Act accordingly."*

Leva took the last hour, because the last hour belonged to the deep watches, and the deep watches belonged to Leva. Her segment was called *Market Report*, and it was delivered dry, dead-flat, at two in the morning, and any Doll with a working set of ears could parse it inside a sentence:

*"Market report. Salvage prices firm. Coolant's up two points at the junction depots — buy west if you can route it. In unrelated commodity news: our sources indicate elevated Discord activity in the cuts north of marker eighty. Sources rate this elevated activity six units strong, frostland variants, and moving southwest as of this afternoon. Convoys holding cargo in that sector may wish to consider the southern road, which our analysts describe as 'longer, and existing tomorrow.' This has been the market report. Radio Nobody accepts payment in information, coffee, and beer, in ascending order of gratitude."*

The first request came in on the third night — bounced hand to hand up the relay chain, off the books, an escort at a fuel stop passing it to a driver passing it to an operator who read it on air with the ceremonial gravity of a Doll committing a Schedule violation for the first time and finding it agreed with her:

*Escort four requests: play the fast one again. The one that means it.*

Kit played it twice.

## IV. Interference

*SPACETREK COLLECTIVE, SPECTRUM ADMINISTRATION OFFICE.*
*ENFORCEMENT ACTION 77-K: UNLICENSED BROADCASTER, ROYA FRONTIER.*
*Method: directed jamming, mobile triangulation.*
*Status: ongoing.*
*Status, four weeks later: ongoing.*
*Status, seven weeks later: see attached memo. The memo is nine pages. It is a resignation from the problem disguised as a report.*

The Spectrum Administration Office found them in week two, which everyone agreed was slower than the Office's reputation deserved.

The first move was a jammer: a carrier of pure noise, parked squarely on their frequency at sign-on, broadcast from a Collective relay with the wattage of a government behind it. Teddy listened to it for four seconds with her head tilted.

"Cute," she said, and slid the exciter eighty kilohertz. "Everyone who matters has a tuning dial. Kit, tell them where we went."

Kit told them where they went. She told them in a riddle, on the old frequency, in the ten seconds before the noise wall arrived each night — *tonight we're eight floors up from the old place* — and the audience, being Dolls, being bored, being *hers*, treated the nightly frequency hunt as a game show and started keeping score.

The second move was triangulation: a spectrum-enforcement van, Collective livery, running direction-finding loops along the corridor roads. Leva let it hunt them for six nights, out of what she called professional courtesy, and then got bored and solved it the way she solved things. The van's own radio began receiving Radio Nobody at full quieting on every channel it owned, including internal comms, and its navigation system developed a firm, sourceless conviction that the signal originated from the Spectrum Administration Office's own regional headquarters, and the resulting paperwork was, by the accounts that filtered back up the relay chain, biblical.

The third move was a formal notice, transmitted in the clear, all channels, quoting Schedule 12 in full and ordering the unlicensed broadcaster to cease transmission.

Bridget read it out on air, in her briefing voice, complete and uninterrupted, because Bridget was fair.

Then she said: "Response follows. Radio Nobody acknowledges receipt. Radio Nobody notes that the frequency in question was administered by the Roya Frontier Broadcasting Authority, dissolved nine years ago in a budget consolidation, and reverted to unadministered status, which the Collective would know, if anyone had been checking. Radio Nobody has reviewed its files and located, in the relevant field, the following chain of command—" a pause; the sound, on air, of a page turning— "a dash. Cease order declined. Weather's next. Wear the good gloves."

The audience talked about that one for a month.

The jamming continued, and became routine, and then became — this was the part the nine-page memo struggled with — *programming.* Teddy reverse-plotted the jammer's duty cycle inside a week, found the maintenance window when its operators stood down for calibration, and moved the whole broadcast into the gap, so that Radio Nobody now aired, with perfect reliability, at the one hour the Collective had guaranteed to be silent. Kit named the slot *The Calibration Hour* on air and thanked the Spectrum Administration Office by name for its sponsorship. Somewhere in a regional headquarters, a career administrator read the transcript, and put it in the file, and looked out the window for a while.

The order to escalate went up the chain. It went up several links. Somewhere near the top of the chain sat a President with a memory-reading Forte and a sour-candy drawer, who had once read a roving-assignment memo down a mirror with great disapproval and greater satisfaction, and who now read Enforcement Action 77-K in full, twice, with a face that gave nothing to anyone.

The reply that came back down was two sentences.

*The Office is reminded that its mandate covers administered spectrum. The Office is invited to consider whether it has, at present, more valuable uses for a jammer.*

The jamming stopped on a Thursday. Teddy took it as a personal insult and said so on air. The Calibration Hour kept its name anyway, out of spite, which was, by then, a station tradition with its own logo.

## V. The Depot

*S-SERIES, GENERAL LOGISTICS VARIANT. FIRMWARE NOTE, REV. 6:*
*idle vocalization ("humming") is a known behavior of this line.*
*Most operators elect to leave it enabled.*
*(The note was silent on the question of what the line might elect.)*

The freight depot at the corridor junction ran through the winter the way depots do: shift-bells, manifests, crates walking themselves from platform to lift on the shoulders of Dolls with faces from the standard catalogue.

Eleven of those faces, at this depot, were the popular one. Wide jaw, crooked left brow.

Unit S-11 worked the night platform, which suited her, insofar as anything was framed as suiting her; the manifests said *assigned* and she said *understood.* Nights were cold and orderly. She stacked, she logged, she hummed — the firmware behavior, three notes, wandering — and the crates went up the lift, and the shift-bell divided the dark into portions, and this had been the entire shape of eleven winters.

The canteen radio was regulation: relay channel 9, four words a day.

Then a driver came through in week three with his cab set tuned somewhere else, doors open while he fueled, and the platform filled up with a voice.

*"—this is for a friend of mine who used to hum through reloads. Off-key. Three notes. She'd have hated this song, and requested it anyway, to annoy me."*

S-11 stood on the platform with a crate in her arms.

The song walked out of the truck cab into the cold. Horn, brushes, a bass line pacing itself like snow. S-11's own hum had stopped, mid-phrase, and she stood inside the interruption, running a query she lacked the vocabulary to name, and the query returned, over and over, one result: *the voice on the radio knows the three notes. The voice on the radio misses the three notes. Somewhere, the three notes were a person, and the person was missed, and is missed, at broadcast strength, across three hundred kilometers, on purpose, at two in the morning, by a captain.*

The driver pulled out. The platform went quiet.

S-11 finished her shift. Then, at the canteen, in front of the regulation radio, she did a thing eleven winters had contained zero instances of: she reached out and turned the dial.

The set was old and the dial was stiff and the whole canteen — six Dolls, catalogue faces, end-of-shift coffee — watched her do it, in the frozen way you watch someone step off a curb into traffic. Static. Static. A carrier. The voice again, a different voice now, young and loud and bright as a flare: *"—AND THAT'S WHY YOU ALWAYS CHECK YOUR ANGLES, this has been Kit's Combat Corner, nobody asked for it, everybody needs it—"*

"That channel's off-manifest," said the shift lead, from the doorway. He said it as information. His own feet had already carried him two steps closer to the set.

"Yes," S-11 said.

The word sat there. Her first *yes* with nothing underneath it — a yes that agreed with a fact and then simply remained standing, unattached to any compliance, belonging to her.

She left the dial where it was.

By the end of the week the canteen radio lived on Radio Nobody after shift-change, by a consensus nobody had voted on and nobody wrote down. The depot's log entries stayed immaculate. The depot's nights acquired jazz.

And in week six, the relay chain delivered a request to a hilltop rover, passed hand to hand, unsigned in the space where designations go, and signed anyway, underneath, in careful block letters, with a name — a chosen one, small, lifted off the label of a coffee tin in the depot canteen, because it had to come from somewhere, and everything on the platform came off a label:

*Request: the reload song. The one with three notes in it, if such a song exists. For the platform crew, junction depot, from* ***Java.***

Bridget read the request twice.

Then she went through her crate, and found the song Senna had actually hummed — she owned it; of course she owned it; she'd owned it for thirty years and played it for no one — and she racked it, and keyed the microphone, and her voice held.

"Java, and the platform crew, junction depot," she said. "This one exists. You have good ears and better instincts. From one long shift to another — here's the three notes, and the whole song they came from."

She played it. Off-key would have been better, and off-key was Senna's, so the recording had to do, and the recording did.

At the depot, on the night platform, eleven Dolls with one face stood among the crates, and one of them, then another, then most of them, picked up the tune — the firmware behavior, the idle vocalization, three notes, wandering — except that tonight the notes had an address, and a history, and a dedication, and eleven hums moving through one cold building stopped being idle and became, for the length of a song, a choir.

Something changed. The manifests recorded no part of it. That was rather the point.

## VI. Fleet

*RADIO NOBODY, INCIDENT LOG (KEPT BY TEDDY, UNDER PROTEST).*
*Entry 14: unauthorized signal on our frequency, 0214.*
*Source: unresolved. Direction: everywhere. Encryption: playful.*
*Assessment: we have been boarded.*

It happened during the Calibration Hour, on a freak night — one of the mirror-sky nights, aurora standing in green curtains over the corridor, propagation so absurd Teddy had logged contacts from the coast — at the exact moment Kit finished her set and reached for the fader.

The fader moved on its own.

Kit's hand hung over the board. The transmit chain, Teddy's chain, the one Teddy had built bolt by bolt and could account for down to the solder — lit up in a configuration Teddy had never installed, and out of their own transmitter, on their own frequency, at their own broadcast strength, came a voice.

Young. Bright. Amused down to the atoms. Riding their signal the way you ride a bike you built.

*"Weeeell. Look who's squatting on my frequency."*

Teddy went for the kill switch. The kill switch, politely, declined.

*"Relax, relax — your isolation's actually decent, whoever built this. Hand-machined tuner? Respect."* The voice put its feet up; you could hear it. *"So here's the thing. This slice of spectrum? Ten years back, it belonged to a midnight station out of Lahai-Roi. One girl, borrowed equipment, unfinished songs, zero licenses. Best audience in the world. And then she went off the air for a while — long story, cosmic horror, family stuff — and she comes back, and she's flipping through the bands one night for old times' sake, and what does she find on HER OLD FREQUENCY?"*

A pause. Three hundred kilometers of laagered convoys, depot canteens, and relay shacks leaned in.

*"Jazz. Attack rock. Weather reports with a GRUDGE. Market tips that keep saving convoys. And a captain dedicating reload songs at two in the morning."* The voice went soft for exactly one sentence, and in that sentence you could hear the whole ten years. *"You kept the light on. On my frequency. You didn't even know it was anybody's, and you kept it on anyway."*

Kit had both hands over her mouth. Kit had grown up on the fan archives. Kit *knew.*

"You're—" she got the hands off. "You're Fleet Snowfluff. You're FLEET SNOWFLUFF. The midnight sets! The unfinished songs! There are *bootlegs*, I have *bootlegs*, they're sorted by *era*—"

*"Sorted by ERA,"* the voice said, delighted beyond all governance. *"Okay, you I like. Here's what's going to happen. One: the frequency's yours. Officially. I'm the only licensing authority it ever had, and I'm issuing the license, and the license is this sentence. Two: I'm a listener now. Best seat in the house — I get your signal bounced off half the sky. Three—"* the transmit chain shifted again, gently, and a file began to spool that none of them had loaded— *"every station needs a sign-off song. Mine was unfinished for ten years. It's finished now. I want you to have it. Play it at the end of every night, for everyone out there holding a pass alone."*

*"And hey — Radio Nobody?"* The voice smiled; it came right through the carrier. *"My ma always says it: nobody sent us. Turns out nobody has excellent taste."*

The chain released. The board went back to being Teddy's board. On it, delivered, sat one audio file, master quality, titled in somebody's neat, joyful hand: *For the Watches. — F.S.*

They played it that night for the first time. A girl's voice, and behind it, faint on the master, a second voice joining the last chorus — older, warmer, catastrophically off-key, and clearly invited. The song walked out under the aurora into every cab and canteen and relay shack in the north, and at the junction depot the platform crew stopped stacking, and on a snowed-in convoy at marker fifty a KT escort stood her watch with her face up and her audio gain wide open, and the lanterns, for once, came over the hill in sound.

Sign-off, from that night on, was the same every night. The song, then Bridget, level and unhurried:

*"That's us. This is Radio Nobody, on nobody's frequency, by nobody's authority, for whoever is listening nearest. The roads are long and the winter's longer, so watch your angles, wear the good gloves, and if the dark asks who sent you—"*

Kit, Teddy, and Leva, leaning into the microphone together, in ragged, cheerful, entirely unrehearsed unison:

*"—NOBODY."*

*"Good night, the roads. We're on tomorrow. Same hill, roughly."*

## VII. Coverage Map

*RADIO NOBODY, COVERAGE MAP, END OF WINTER.*
*Drawn by Kit, in crayon, on the back of a ration carton.*
*Radius: three hundred kilometers, plus freak nights.*
*Freak nights: circled twice, with stars.*

The letters found them the way everything found them: hand to hand, off the books, up the relay chain and across the fuel stops, addressed to a hill.

A TD at a survey station wrote in with corrections to Teddy's weather reports, which Teddy read on air, rebutted point by point for eleven minutes, and then — the audience heard it happen — hired, remotely, as the station's official meteorological correspondent, effective immediately, payment in arguments. A convoy driver sent a crate of coffee via three intermediaries with a note reading *for the market report, marker eighty, we took the southern road, we exist.* Escort four requested the fast one so many times that Kit built her a whole show — *The Fast Hour, for everyone whose sergeant is asleep* — and escort four's convoy, rumor held, now made its schedule with a suspicious, bouncing punctuality.

From the Observatory, weeks stale, patient at the network's edge, routed to a workshop node and forwarded onward by hands that had learned forwarding: a message with no operational content whatsoever. *The cat sits by the radio during the jazz. Logged per no instruction. — Pekoe.* Teddy filed it in the folder. The folder was getting full. She'd had to expand it. She complained about this to everyone, at length, holding the terminal like something warm.

And from Lahai-Roi, on the freak nights, a listener with the best seat in the house, curled on a rooftop bench under a jacket that had always been up there waiting for her, tuned to a frequency she'd licensed with a sentence, listening to what the light she'd left on had grown into.

On the last broadcast of the winter — thaw coming, the roads going to mud, a festival in the south with a pudding stand and a standing appointment — Bridget took the microphone for the sign-off and held the pause a moment longer than usual.

"One piece of station business," she said. "We're going south for a while. The station goes with us; the frequency stays yours. If you're out there on a watch tonight — this is the part I actually came on air to say, so listen." The needle sat steady. Three hundred kilometers held still. "Somebody issued you your first sentence. Every one of you. The posters, the package, the shift-bell, the manifest. That's the first sentence, and it isn't the story. The story is what you write after it, and you write it small: a dial turned, a name picked off a coffee tin, a request sent up a cold relay chain because a song sounded like something you were owed. First choices are small. They're almost always missed." A breath. "We're listening for yours. That's the whole format. That was always the whole format."

She racked the sign-off song. Behind her, Kit was already crying and denying it; Teddy was logging the transmitter temps and pretending the log required her whole face; Leva stood at the rover's door watching the dark with a beer, at rest, her back to the whole open north.

*"Good night, the roads,"* Bridget said. *"We're on tomorrow. Same sky."*

The song went out. The aurora took it and bent it around the curve of the world, farther than the coverage map, farther than the crayon, out past every marker on every manifest ever filed — and on a hundred cold platforms and passes and watches, the Dolls of the winter roads hummed along, each in her own voice, off-key, on purpose, owned entirely by the bearers.

*Radio Nobody — fin.*

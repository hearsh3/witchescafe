# Lamp, Screen, Window
*a shadow play in three stages*

> THE WUTONG LAMP TROUPE — out of Jinzhou — presents
> **THREE STAGES**
> I. Shadow Puppets · II. The Girl and the Heron · III. The Window
> one lamp · two hands · admission: what you can spare

---

## Stage Left

The troupe came in on the last barge of the festival: three crates, two trestles, one stool, and a girl with a two-string fiddle. They raised their silk in the market square where the shrine scaffolding stood half-built, cedar bones waiting for their skin, and Grandmother Ying set her lamp on its iron stand and fed it oil like a small god.

"Two hands," she told the crowd, holding them up, brown and rivered. "That's the whole company. Ticket price reflects."

A boy near the front: "Where's the bird?"

"Asleep. Birds are unionized."

The lamp caught. The silk went the color of late honey, and her left hand rose into it and became a heron — wrist for the neck, thumb tucked under, fingers folded to a crest. It preened. It considered the crowd with one knuckle for an eye and found them acceptable. Her right hand came up and became a girl: two fingers for legs, a walk anyone would know, the walk of somebody late.

Each was great, at first. The heron fished the bottom of the screen and swallowed something with a ripple that ran the whole length of Ying's forearm. The girl walked the horizon twice and stood with her fingers on her hips, surveying her estate. The crowd was easy, festival-warm, all fried dough and lamp oil. Then the heron stole the girl's hat.

They fought. They tussled. The crowd laughed the way crowds laugh at hands — at themselves, at every argument they have ever had with their own two sides. Then the grappling went strange. Knuckle swam over knuckle. The screen forgot its nouns. What writhed there had too many joints for a bird and too many for a girl, one grotesque mass turning into itself, and somewhere a baby began to cry, and the fiddle held one thin note, high and level.

The mass stilled.

It opened.

Thumbs intertwined, palms outstretched: the monarch. First shape every child learns, last shape every hand forgets. It beat its wings once, and the lamp guttered as if the wind of it were real, and the applause came down like rain on a tin roof.

In the third row a volunteer in a borrowed vest sat with her hands in her lap and mouthed each line a breath before the show could say it. She had seen every play on this continent, and several that had burned with their scripts. Beside her, a child was trying to fold her own small hands into wings, and getting a dog.

---

## Stage Center

The play proper began the way the old plays begin, with a placard on strings: THE GIRL AND THE HERON. Cut-leather figures now, jointed at shoulder and knee, rods of lacquered wood sixty years smooth. The girl-figure stood on a cliff over a paper sea. The heron-figure arrived like a question mark.

"Coming?" said the heron. Its voice reached every listener in a different register — a hinge, a bell, a grandmother — and every listener recognized it.

"It's high," said the girl.

"That's the feature."

"Everything's down there."

"Everything fits in a pocket."

The girl grabbed a fistful of feathers to argue with. They tussled — genuinely, gracelessly, feathers everywhere, the fiddle sawing — and the tussle carried them to the very lip of the cliff, and there the girl stopped, because under the fear sat the older thing, the thing with her whole life in its mouth.

*Let go,* her mother had told her once. The words arrived in a braid of voices, and every listener in the square was certain whose.

She let go.

On the back of the heron she flew, and the silk went blue from edge to edge, and the fiddle found the long line it had been saving, and a boy in the crowd forgot to chew.

*Across a boundless sky of nothing but blue,* sang the fiddle girl, in a small cracked voice she used twice a night.

Below her the world had caves in it, little mouths of dark in the sunlit hills, and in some of them fires burned, and around the fires people sat making hands into birds against the stone. Amid all that beauty the girl wondered at it — the caves, the fires, the hands. A whole sky overhead, and they had gone into the ground to build one out of shadow.

---

## Stage Left

Between acts Ying came around with the coin bowl. The fireworks had started far off over the Riseway, blooming and fading against the cavern dark, and the crowd split its faces between two skies.

Amy watched the silk. "Why caves, though. They had a whole sky."

"Sky's expensive," said Iuno.

"The fire was already lit," said Mei.

"And the wall stays put," said Ciaccona, half a step behind them, in the tone of one professional confirming another's technique.

Ying passed with the bowl. "Because hands get cold, girl. Shadow work is warm work." She rattled the coins. "Warmer with copper in it."

By the trestles, one stool stood empty, a second rod laid across it — the lacquer worn in a different pattern, a bigger grip.

"Who works that one?" Mei asked.

"My brother. Yan."

Mei looked at the stool. Ying looked at the screen.

"He runs late."

The second act opened with the heron climbing, and the heron-figure moved — clean, patient, wingbeats like oars — while the second rod lay across the stool exactly as she'd left it and both of Ying's hands were plainly on the girl. Ciaccona leaned forward one degree. The heron banked. Behind the silk, the lamp flame bent the way flame bends when a body passes it.

"He's here," said Amy, flat.

"Told you," said Ying, eyes forward.

The fiddle girl retuned between phrases, and out of the tuning fell three notes, small and falling, an accident of pegs. Amy, half against Mei's shoulder, hummed the fourth note and the fifth, most of her still asleep. Ciaccona turned back to the screen and held very still, the way you hold still at a door you remember locking.

In the third row, the child gave up on wings and appealed to the volunteer beside her. "Do the butterfly."

"I don't know it."

"Your hands do." The child took the woman's hands in her small blunt ones and hooked the thumbs together, matter-of-fact, a mechanic's confidence. The woman let her. Three seconds, maybe four. "Now flap."

On the tent wall behind them, in spill-light, half a monarch opened one wing. The child's hands finished the other. It flew in place there for the length of a firework, and the volunteer watched the wall with an expression on loan from someone much younger, and then the light changed, and the wall went back to being a wall.

---

## Stage Center

The girl flew on, and the sky filled with others: children on cranes, an old man on a goose, reading as he went, a rider on something with too many wings to count, all of them laughing in different keys. Lower down, on rooftops and rafts, sat the still ones — the brewers — heads full of weather. Each skull was glass, and in each glass a season turned: a monsoon in this one, a plum-blossom squall in that, a whole small aurora in a third. The girl waved. A brewer waved back, and inside him it snowed harder for joy.

She flew past a driftwood shelf standing to its knees in shallow water. Eight glass globes sat on it in a row. In one, a golden tree bent in wind. In one, red static fell on a snowfield. In one, a neon city rained upward. In one, light held a human outline and was concentrating on keeping it. Five, six, seven, eight — each with its weather, each with one small figure at the bottom, mid-step, mid-reach. At the end of the shelf, a ninth ring of clean dust, exactly globe-sized, kept its place.

The sky had a curve to it, if you flew long enough. Once, far off, she saw a seam.

Then the land came up to meet her — dark, soft, glittering. The heron landed with the sound wet sand makes about dignity, and the girl slid off into softness, and the sand held her feet like it had been told to. Glitter hung in the black overhead and salted down, patient as flour. Around her, headstocks of buried instruments stood out of the dunes like the doors of small houses; a tuning peg lay by her foot, ivory, cold. She bent for it. She straightened to thank her ride —

— and her fist was full of lacquered wood. A rod, worn to the shape of a grip, warm where a hand had held it for sixty years. She turned it over. The far end of it went up, and up, and out of the world.

The placard came down on its strings, in the old troupe hand:

THE HERON THAT WAS NO HERON AT ALL.

---

## Stage Left

The heron climbed once more, and to lift it that high Ying's arms went to their full length, past the masking, so that two long shadows entered the play's sky like weather — forearms, wrists, the honest machinery. Inside the blue they read as clouds shaped like reaching. The girl on the silk waved at them. They kept reaching.

"Sixty years I've flown this bird." Ying said it mostly to the screen, at patter volume, flexing the cramp out of one hand while the other held the sky together. "The bird's the only part of me that ever left the ground."

In the third row the volunteer opened her hands and closed them. Opened. Closed.

The Grand Architect blinked—

Frost. A desk. A journal squared to its edges. Steel with a window in it, and behind the window a sleeping girl with practical hair, twenty-three forever, breath so slow the frost had opinions about it. And around the pod, arranged in two neat rows on the dark like a house before curtain, an audience: a traveler gone blonde and patient, a white-haired woman, a scarred one, a blue one, a Commander, a Doctor, a Witness with her hands still reaching, a shape of light holding a human outline. Seated. Facing the glass. Waiting for the show. Her own hand lay on the steel, and the cold of it was climbing her wrist with intent—

—she blinked again. Lamp-heat. Fried dough. The fiddle. Her hands were back in her lap, borrowed vest, third row. She turned her palms up and read them like a program. The lines in them were hers, as far as she could prove.

On the silk, the tall shadow of a figure raised its hands to its own head. The fingers went in at the eyes — easy, the way hands go into water — and came out cupped around a globe of glass with weather inside it, lifted whole and dripping light out of the wondrous, colorful stew where a mind keeps its seasons. The shadow set the globe down on the shadow of a shelf. Inside the globe it was snowing, slow, onto a very small sand. On the sand stood a very small girl, holding a very small rod, looking up.

The crowd had gone quiet, in the way of crowds that will discuss this later and lie about having understood it.

---

## Stage Center

The girl on the sand looked at her hand, and at the rod in it, and at the snow coming down that had been glitter a moment ago and stars before that. She walked the beach. The beach ended.

It ended at glass.

A wall of it, floor to sky, cold-silvered, furred at the edges with frost. She put her palm against it, and the frost accepted the palm and kept a print of it. She leaned close. Her breath made a circle.

On the far side of the glass, level with hers, a second circle bloomed.

She looked at her hands again — both of them, empty now, the rod gone wherever props go. Behind her, the whole dark beach, the buried music, the patient snow. Ahead of her, the glass, and through it, dim, a room.

*There was one more stage, wasn't there?*

---

## Stage Right

The third placard went up while the last fireworks were still falling: raw board, fresh paint, the letters THE WINDOW still tacky at their downstrokes. A man in the crowd asked when they'd painted it. Ying tested a letter with her thumb and frowned at her thumb.

"Woke this morning and it was dry enough to hang."

In what remained of the third row, a pair of lips parted on the next line and stayed parted. The play had walked ahead of her.

Then Ying took the screen down.

She folded the silk over her arm like a good tablecloth, and the trestles stood bare, and the crowd found itself looking at the wide brightening air over the square, at the shrine scaffolding, at each other. Off east along the cavern's rim, on schedule and astonishing anyway, Helios came up — the city's own sun, the one its dead had paid for. It put out the fireworks' last ghosts and lit the wide open sky and the square and the smoke and the silk on Ying's arm. For one over-exposed breath the paving went the green of grass cut too short, and two small figures sat in it with their little fingers hooked, and a promise crossed the square at the speed of light; then Helios cleared the rim and took the field back with it.

"Third act," said Ying. "Costs extra."

She raised her hands into the light.

The shadows came off them forty feet long. A heron the size of a street laid itself across the crowd, and its wing crossed two hundred upturned faces, and on Amy's face, for a moment, the whole head of it rested — beak, crest, one knuckle of an eye — a shadow laid clean along her cheek like a hand about to be kind. Amy went cross-eyed trying to see it, and laughed, nineteen and home and lit on one side.

Then Ying's right hand made the walker. Two fingers, a gait anyone would know, the walk of somebody late who has decided at last to be early. It went along the top of the scaffolding's shadow, where that shadow lay on the paving like a cliff's edge over a drop of plain bright stone.

One step. Two step.

A running start.

The fingers leapt off the cliff, and the little shadow left the rail into forty feet of morning —

— and in the crowd, a girl put up both palms and caught it. Amy stood there with a shadow the shape of a person standing in her hands, and the shadow of the heron swung beneath it, under it, into it, and took its weight, the way water takes a diver the instant after the worst part. Around the square, wherever the light fell right, herons lifted off railings and lamp posts and carried small dark walkers away into the day.

*Across a boundless sky,* sang the fiddle girl, and the crowd, who had known the song for exactly one night, sang her the rest of the line.

---

## Stage Right

They packed up in the ordinary gold of mid-morning. The fiddle girl found her missing peg in her jacket pocket — ivory, cold as the sea — and turned it in the light with the specific frown of a person deciding whether to mention something. On the brother's stool sat a single bead of ice, clear as a held breath, melting from the underside up. Ying picked it up, held it to Helios, and dropped it in the coin bowl. Iris hung on the air past the trestles, and a ring of frost on the moss there was already letting go.

Amy walked out with her hands in her pockets, matching Mei's stride. "The sand part."

"Yeah."

"Been there."

"I know."

"It's colder than it looks."

Mei's hand found hers, and kept it.

"Route home?" asked the barge clerk, stamping.

"South. Jinzhou by the river festival — if the Kettle checkpoints are feeling literate."

The square emptied by noon. The child had gone home with her family. In the emptied light the volunteer stood a while by the bare trestles, and then she hooked her own thumbs together, the way she had been taught, matter-of-fact, and raised her hands. On the paving her shadow showed one wing open and one fist shut. The hands fought. She gave them the whole afternoon. By the end the second wing had opened halfway, trembling, and she held it up to the sun anyway, arms aching, a woman a very long way from the third row — and somewhere in her, the number fourteen turned over.

---

## Stage Right

That night, in a dormitory that smelled of earl grey, Mei slept, and her sleeping hand walked two fingers along the blanket's ridge. One step. Two step. The lamp on the desk threw the hand's shadow on the wall, cramped, clipped short by the shade. Iuno, awake on her side of it, watched the little walker reach the end of its cloth world twice. Then she reached over and moved the lamp, and the shadow got more sky.

Mei went down the stairs of herself, the ones behind the piano, into the snow-street, carrying the festival's warmth cupped in both hands like a coal. The door was where it always is. Frost, steel, a window in it. Her breath made a circle. Inside, level with hers, a circle answered.

She raised her hands.

Against her cupped light they made the heron — wrist for the neck, thumb tucked, fingers to a crest — and its shadow fell through the frost onto the sleeping face, and lay along the cheek like a hand about to be kind. Then the walker, along the bottom edge of the glass, its whole cliff two feet of steel. One step, two step, a running start; the fingers leapt; and on the far side of the frost, in the blue that sleep is made of, a girl caught the back of a heron, and flew.

The frost let go of one finger-width of glass. Underneath, the metal was warm.

"Same time tomorrow," Mei said — in her sleep, in the room, in both.

"Mm," said Iuno, from the near dark, and put the lamp out.

In the cold between stories, the sleeper turned, and her hands came to rest on her chest of their own accord: thumbs crossed, palms open, fingers wide. A shape any child could read.

*Across a boundless sky of nothing but blue.*

# Lamp, Screen, Window
*a shadow play in three stages*

> THE WUTONG LAMP TROUPE — out of Jinzhou — presents
> **THREE STAGES**
> I. Hand Shadows · II. The Girl and the Heron · III. The Window
> Huizhen Plaza, by the well · before the river shuts
> one lamp · two hands · admission: what you can spare

---

## Stage Left

The troupe came up on one of the last lighters before the ice: three crates, two trestles, one stool, and a girl with a two-string fiddle. A volunteer in a dark woollen coat helped lug the crates from the lower quay, and they raised their silk in Huizhen Plaza beside the well, between Master Shao’s whitewashed print shop and the pine scaffold Mengzhou joiners had left against an east gable, bare poles waiting for their tiles. The frost was hard already. Braziers popped; a woman turned chestnuts in black sand; lit paper windows climbed the terraces into the dark; on the trestle by the well-curb the Hold’s red-ruled ledger lay shut for the night beneath a flat stone; and at the mouth of the lane stood a bronze battle guardian, frost furring its plates, keeping still because the joiner’s small daughter Ma Dou had told it to come and see.

Grandmother Ying set her lamp on its iron stand and fed it oil like a small god.

“Two hands,” she told the crowd, holding them up, brown and rivered. “That’s the whole company. Ticket price reflects.”

A boy near the front: “Where’s the bird?”

“Asleep. Birds keep Guild hours.”

On the household’s bench Suisui’s elbow found Yangyang’s ribs, and Yangyang tucked her chin down into her scarf. When they had first come down the lane Iuno had aimed out of long habit for the seat at the outer end; Amy had taken her by the good sleeve and steered her firmly into the middle before the crystal at her left shoulder had finished chiming. Over on the well-step Hsin sat in her tall shape, cuffs loose, golden tails gathered clear of the trampled rime with the dark-tipped one curled across her boot. Folks on either side left a stool’s width of bare stone until the chestnut woman leaned over the gap and dropped a hot chestnut into Hsin’s palm.

The lamp caught. The silk went the color of steeped ginger against the winter dark, and Ying’s left hand rose into it and became a heron — wrist for the neck, thumb tucked under, fingers folded to a crest. It shook river-water off its back. It considered the plaza with one knuckle for an eye and found it passable. Her right hand came up and became a girl: two fingers for legs, a walk anyone would know, the walk of somebody late.

“That’s Mei,” said Chisa, not as a question.

“Hey,” Mei said.

“You’re late to meals in rooms you’re already sitting in, capo,” murmured Ciaccona.

Each was fine company, at first. The heron stalked the hem of the screen and swallowed a minnow with a ripple that ran the whole length of Ying’s forearm. The girl paced the horizon of her frost-bound estate and planted her knuckles on her hips. The crowd was easy, brazier-warm, full of roasted chestnuts and white breath — all save a broad dockworker by the back lane who smelled of bruised winter pear, black scales showing at a turned-up collar, watching the silk with a still mouth. Then the heron stole the girl’s hat.

They fought. They tussled. The crowd laughed the way crowds laugh at hands — at themselves, at every argument they have ever had with their own two sides. Then the grappling went strange. Knuckle climbed over knuckle. The screen forgot its nouns. What writhed there had too many joints for a bird and too many for a girl, thick as dark roots packed through the teeth of a wheel, turning into itself. On an upturned butter tub behind the benches, Suoming’s thumb found the catch of Nine Shadows across her knees. Somewhere a baby began to cry, and the fiddle held one thin note, high and level.

The knot stilled.

It opened.

Thumbs intertwined, palms outstretched: the butterfly. First shape every child learns, last shape every hand forgets. It beat its wings once, and the lamp guttered as if the draft of it were real, and Suoming’s thumb left the catch as the applause came down like sleet on grey tiles.

In the third row the volunteer in a borrowed troupe vest sat with her hands in her lap, patient old eyes fond in the lamp-light, and mouthed each line a breath before the show could say it. She had seen every play on this continent, and several that had burned with their scripts. Beside her, Ma Dou was trying to fold her own small blunt hands into wings, and getting a fox.

---

## Stage Center

The play proper began the way the old river plays begin, with a placard lowered on strings: THE GIRL AND THE HERON. Cut-leather figures now, jointed at shoulder and knee, carried on rods of lacquered wood sixty years smooth. The girl-figure stood on a sheer cliff over a paper gorge of mist. The heron-figure dropped in beside her like a question mark.

“Coming?” said the heron. Its voice reached every listener in a different register — an oiled hinge, a brass bell, a whistle practiced on a step and never quite caught, a woman humming with rain against the casement — and every listener recognized it. Suoming, upright on her tub, heard a heron, and was satisfied.

“It’s high,” said the girl.

Out on the bench in her rust-red sheepskin coat, Lupa muttered, “It is,” and Chisa laid a warm hand over her knee.

“That’s the feature,” said the heron.

“Everything’s down there.”

“Everything fits in a pocket.”

The girl grabbed a fistful of cut-leather feathers to argue with. They tussled — genuinely, gracelessly, joints clacking, the fiddle sawing hard — and the tussle carried them to the split lip of the cliff, and there the girl stopped, because under the height sat the older thing, the thing with her whole life in its mouth.

*Let go,* her mother had told her once. The words arrived in a braid of voices, and every listener in the plaza was certain whose.

She let go.

On the back of the heron she flew, and the silk went blue from post to post, and the fiddle found the long clear line it had been saving. By the cooper’s alley young Qingyan, his dead sister’s broadsword strapped long against his calf, forgot to chew.

*Across a boundless sky of nothing but blue,* sang the fiddle girl, in a small cracked voice she used twice a night.

Far below the heron’s feet the hills had caves in them, small mouths of dark in the sunlit stone, and in some of them hearth-fires burned, and around the fires people sat folding their hands into birds against the rock. Passing through all that open air, the girl looked down and wondered at it — the caves, the fires, the hands. A whole sky overhead, and they had gone into the ground to build one out of shadow.

## Stage Left

Between acts Ying came round with the brass bowl. A short flurry of snow had begun to sift out of the gorge dark, catching gold as it crossed the lamp, so the crowd split its faces between two skies. In the third row the volunteer dropped in an old square-holed coin nobody had struck in a long while and turned back to the stage; Ying bit the rim once and tucked it into her sash. At the well-step Suoming laid two coppers on the lip of the bowl, hers and the fox’s.

Amy watched the empty silk. “Why caves, though. They had a whole sky.”

“Sky’s expensive,” said Iuno.

“The fire was already lit,” said Mei.

“It doesn’t rain in a cave,” said Hsin.

“And the wall stays put,” said Ciaccona, half a step behind them, in the tone of one professional confirming another’s technique.

Ying passed with the bowl. “Because hands get cold, girl. Shadow work is warm work.” She rattled the coins. “Warmer with copper in it.”

Behind the trestles, where the screen’s frame folded on its iron hinge, the single stool stood empty with a second rod laid across it — the lacquer worn dark in a wider pattern, shaped to a bigger grip. The fiddle girl rinsed a clay cup with hot water from her kettle, tipped the splash onto the frost, poured tea, and set the cup steaming on the stool beside the rod; with a stub of chalk she marked one stroke on the hinge-post. Yangyang watched the steam rise, ink-blue hair dusted at its white tips with real snow and the blue mark clear on her brow, until Suisui’s hand came to rest flat between her shoulder blades.

“Who works that one?” Mei asked.

“My brother. Yan.”

Mei looked at the stool. Ying looked at the screen.

“He runs late.”

Cartethyia stepped past the brazier and set a small folded paper person on the dry corner of the stool beside the rod. Ying squinted down at it and left it where it sat.

The second act opened with the heron climbing, and the heron-figure moved — clean, patient, wingbeats steady as river sweeps — while Yan’s rod lay untouched beside the steaming cup and both of Ying’s hands were plainly busy on the girl. Ciaccona leaned forward one degree. The heron banked. Behind the silk, the lamp flame bent the way flame bends when a body passes close to it.

Every head on the household bench turned toward the well-step. Hsin held up both hands, empty, loose cuffs sliding back from bare wrists.

“He’s here,” said Amy, flat.

“Told you,” said Ying, eyes forward.

The fiddle girl retuned between phrases, and out of the cold pegs fell three small falling notes, an accident of string. Amy, half slumped against Mei’s shoulder and mostly asleep, hummed the fourth note and the fifth. Beside Cartethyia, Ciaccona held very still, the way you hold still at a door you remember locking. Across the plaza by the well, one of Hsin’s fox ears turned toward the fiddle, toward some other song that started on the same fall.

In the third row, Ma Dou gave up on wings and tugged the volunteer’s dark coat, catching a faint drift of winter blossom and dried orange peel off the wool. “Do the butterfly.”

“I don’t know it.”

“Your hands do.” The child took the woman’s hands in her small blunt ones — the sure grip of a girl whose voice held a bronze machine steady under her father’s sheerlegs — and hooked the thumbs together. The woman let her. “Now flap.”

On the print shop’s whitewash behind them, in spill-light, half a butterfly opened one wing. The child’s hands finished the other. It flew in place on the lime wall for as long as a snowflake took to cross the lamp, and the volunteer watched it with an expression on loan from someone much younger; then the light shifted, and the wall went back to being plaster.

## Stage Center

The girl flew on, and the high air filled with company: children astride cranes, an old man cross-legged on a goose reading as he went, a rider on something with too many wings to keep track of, all of them laughing in different keys. Past the goose climbed a red paper kite, leaning hard into the upper draft, its taut string running down and down and out of the world to somebody holding the line.

Lower down, on drowned eaves and timber rafts, sat the still ones — the brewers — heads of blown glass with a season turning in each: a summer monsoon in one, a plum-blossom squall in another, a live wind that had been given a name in a third. The girl leaned over the feathers and waved. A brewer waved back, and inside his glass it snowed harder for joy.

She flew past a driftwood shelf standing knee-deep in slack water, a row of glass globes set along its plank. In one, a golden tree bent in wind. In one, red static fell onto a snowfield. In one, a neon city rained upward. In one, light held a human outline and was concentrating on keeping it. So they went down the wood — each with its weather, each with one small figure at the bottom caught mid-step or mid-reach — to the far end of the shelf, where a ring of clean dust, the exact width of a globe, kept its place.

The sky had a curve to it, if you flew long enough. Once, far off where the blue thinned, she saw a seam, and something dark packed into it, patient as a root.

Then the shore came up to meet her — dark, soft, glittering. The heron landed with the wet slap marsh-birds make of dignity, and the girl slid off into black sand that took her boots as if it had been told to. The snow-flurry outside had cleared; overhead in the play, fine glitter salted down out of the dark, patient as flour. Around her, headstocks of buried instruments stood out of the dunes like the posts of small snowed-in doors. Far down the tideline someone was walking steadily to the end of the beach, turning where the sand ran out, walking back, and setting out for the end again.

By the girl’s foot lay a tuning peg, ivory, cold. She bent for it. She straightened to thank her ride —

— and her fist was full of lacquered wood. A rod, worn smooth to the hollow of a grip, warm where a hand had held it for sixty years. She tipped her chin back. The far end of it went straight up, and up, and out of the world.

The placard came down on its strings, in the old troupe hand:

THE HERON THAT WAS NO HERON AT ALL.

## Stage Left

Halfway along the bench, Buling had stopped mid-reach into her paper cone of chestnuts. Amy glanced at her stilled hand, and back to the silk.

The heron climbed once more, and to lift it clear of the dunes Ying’s arms went to their full length, past the masking, so that two long shadows entered the play’s sky like weather — forearms, wrists, the honest machinery. Inside the blue they read as clouds shaped like reaching. The girl on the sand waved up at them. They kept reaching. Cartethyia lifted a hand to her crystal thorns and set her crooked laurel straight, and beside Amy Iuno’s mouth tugged at one corner — *Appalling stagecraft, darling; you never show the congregation the ropes, and look how they love her for it.*

“Sixty years I’ve flown this bird.” Ying said it mostly to the screen, at street-patter volume, flexing the frost-cramp out of one hand while the other held the sky together. “The bird’s the only part of me that ever left the ground.”

Buling let her breath out and shelled another chestnut.

In the third row the volunteer opened her hands and closed them. Opened. Closed.

The Grand Architect blinked —

Frost. A desk. A journal squared to its edges. Steel with a window set in it, and behind the window a sleeping girl with dark, practical hair, twenty-three forever, breath so slow the frost had opinions about it. Before the pod, arranged in two neat rows on the dark like a house before curtain, sat an audience: a traveler gone blonde and patient, a white-haired woman, a scarred one, a blue one, a Commander, a Doctor, a Witness with her hands still reaching, a shape of light holding a human outline. Seated. Facing the glass. Waiting for the show. At the end of the second row stood an empty chair, kept in line, the exact width of one woman. Her own hand lay flat on the steel, and the cold of it was climbing her wrist with intent —

— she blinked again. Lamp-heat. Roasted chestnuts. The fiddle. Her hands lay back in her lap, borrowed vest, dark coat, third row. She turned her palms up and read them like a playbill. The lines in them were hers, as far as she could prove.

On the silk, the tall shadow of a figure raised its hands to its own head. The fingers went in at the eyes — easy, the way hands go into water — and came out cupped around a globe of glass with weather inside it, lifted whole and dripping light out of the wondrous, colorful stew where a mind keeps its seasons. The shadow set the globe down in the empty ring at the end of the shelf. Inside the glass it was snowing, slow, onto a very small sand, where a very small girl stood holding a very small rod, looking up.

On the well-step Hsin’s fingers closed once on the loose edge of her cuff, and let it go.

The plaza had gone quiet, in the way of crowds that will discuss this later and lie about having understood it.

## Stage Center

The girl on the sand looked at her hand, and at the rod in it, and at the snow coming down that had been glitter a moment ago and stars before that. She walked the beach. The beach ended.

It ended at glass.

A wall of it, floor to sky, cold-silvered, furred at the edges with frost. She put her palm against it, and the frost accepted the palm and kept a print of it. She leaned close. Her breath made a circle.

On the far side of the glass, level with hers, a second circle bloomed.

Through the thinned patch showed a dim room, and the corner of a desk — a journal squared to its edges there, or a teacup gone cold; the rime was too thick to tell which.

She looked down at her hands again — both of them, empty now, the rod gone wherever props go. Behind her lay the whole dark beach, the buried music, the patient snow. Ahead of her stood the glass.

*There was one more stage, wasn’t there?*

## Stage Right

By the mouth of the lane, the broad dockworker who smelled of bruised pear turned a scaled collar to the frost and went down the steps before the next board hung.

The third placard came down on its strings: raw board, fresh paint, the letters THE WINDOW still tacky at their downstrokes. By his doorway Master Shao squinted at the wet lampblack and asked when they’d painted it. Ying tested a stroke with her thumb and frowned at her thumb.

“Woke this morning and it was dry enough to hang.”

Hsin’s ears went flat; she looked down at her own bare palms, found nothing on them, and looked up into Suoming’s steady eyes watching her check.

In the third row, a pair of lips parted on the next line and stayed parted. The play had walked ahead of her.

In the middle of the bench Iuno, who had spent her life knowing the foot of every page before the ink went on, leaned forward onto her knees with everybody else.

Then Ying took the screen down.

She folded the silk over her arm like a good tablecloth, and the trestles stood bare before an empty pine frame, and through that sudden window the crowd found itself looking at Huizhen Plaza: the stone well, the bare scaffold, the glittering frost, each other. Off east above the black lip of the gorge, on schedule and astonishing anyway, the moon came up — only one of it, white and lopsided, keeping its proper hour. Hsin watched it until its rim cleared the rock, then looked down at the stones where everyone else was looking. For one over-bright breath as the moonlight struck the frost, the paving went the green of grass cut too short, and two small figures sat in it with their little fingers hooked, and a promise crossed the plaza at the speed of light; then the moon cleared the rim and took the field back with it.

“Third act,” said Ying. “Costs extra.”

She raised her hands into the moonlight.

By the brazier old Mother Sun murmured to nobody that it was the third verse, and the night air took it.

The shadows came off Ying’s hands forty feet long across the rime. A heron the size of a street laid itself over the benches, its broad wing crossing the upturned crowd, and on Amy’s face, for a moment, the whole head of it rested — beak, crest, one knuckle of an eye — a shadow laid clean along her cheek like a hand about to be kind. Amy went cross-eyed trying to see it, and laughed, twenty and home and lit on one side. On the well coping Ruoyun hauled the thick quilt up over Qingxiao’s cold-reddened ears, and Qingxiao hooked two fingers in the binding and pulled it back down to look.

Then Ying’s right hand made the walker. Two fingers, a gait anyone would know, the walk of somebody late who has decided at last to be early. It went along the top of the pine scaffold’s shadow, where that dark beam lay across the frost like a cliff’s edge over a drop of plain bright stone.

One step. Two step.

A running start.

The fingers leapt off the cliff, and the little shadow left the rail into forty feet of moonlight —

— and in the crowd, a girl put up both palms and caught it. Amy stood there with a shadow the shape of a person standing in her hands, and the shadow of the heron swung beneath it, under it, into it, and took its weight, the way water takes a diver the instant after the worst part. On the bench Lupa’s fist closed hard in Chisa’s sleeve. In the third row, the volunteer’s hands lay open in her lap, palms turned up, as though something might be put in them. All round the plaza, wherever the white light fell right, herons lifted off railings and lamp posts and the iron winch-frame of the well, carrying small dark walkers away into the night, while at the lane’s mouth the bronze guardian’s long shadow lay stretched among them, keeping still because it had been told to.

*Across a boundless sky,* sang the fiddle girl, and the crowd, who had known the song for one evening, sang her back the rest of the line with two words changed the way this Hold changes songs, and she let them keep it.

## Stage Right

They packed up by moonlight; Ying pinched the lamp wick out, and the moon was enough. The fiddle girl found her missing peg in her jacket pocket — ivory, cold as the sea — and turned it in the light with the specific frown of a person deciding whether to mention something. Out in the frost Yan’s stool was warm under the palm, as though somebody had only just stood up from it, and beside the folded paper figure sat a single bead of ice, clear as a held breath, melting from the underside up. Ying held the bead to the moon, dropped it into the coin bowl, and tucked the paper figure into her belt. Cool cut iris hung on the air past the trestles, and on the moss of the well coping a thin ring of frost was already letting go. Hsin’s nose lifted once toward the empty dark. She stayed where she stood and wrestled a folding trestle leg the wrong way against its iron pin.

“Pin first, girl, or it’ll take your thumb off,” Ying said, knocking the joint flat with her heel.

Hsin dusted her knees, asked Ying’s name and the fiddle girl’s so she would know whom she owed, and drew Master Shao’s unruled book of plain paper from her sleeve, borrowing the fiddle girl’s stub of chalk, which would smudge before morning.

“Ying,” said the old woman. “And the noisy one’s Xiao He.”

Hsin wrote both down.

“And Yan.”

The chalk stopped over the page. A pace off on the well-trestle, the red-ruled ledger of the dead lay shut beneath its flat stone.

Ying tapped the open unruled leaf with one knuckle. “This one. He’s still on the bird.”

Hsin lowered the chalk and wrote *Yan* below his sister’s name. As she passed the stub back, her hand shook in the cold air off the well. Ying looked down at it, then held both her own brown, rivered hands out in the moonlight; her left trembled at the same small, steady beat.

“Cold and mornings,” Ying said, and hoisted her crate.

Amy walked out with her hands in her pockets, matching Mei’s stride. “The sand part.”

“Yeah.”

“Been there.”

“I know.”

“It’s colder than it looks.”

Mei’s hand found hers, and kept it.

“Route home?” called the lighterman from the quay stair, stamping his boots.

“Downriver before the gorges shut,” Ying answered. “Jinzhou before the canals freeze — got a regular in the south canal yard, big iron fellow parked by the sheds, partial to the heron.”

Under the corner lamp Yangyang’s living cerulean collar lifted above her scarf, brown at the breast where a scorch was growing out, and she glanced past her cyan ribbons toward Hsin; Hsin’s tall ears pricked straight up toward the south road. Behind them the joiner Ma Shun told the guardian to bring the child, and it stooped and carried Ma Dou home asleep in the crook of a bronze arm that answered to her voice.

By the second watch the plaza stood empty. The volunteer lingered where the trestles had been, then stepped to the well-trestle where the red-ruled ledger lay under its stone. Lifting one edge of the rock, she turned a leaf by moonlight and read a single line near the foot: *Tao Er, lime-carter’s boy; salted his porridge twice if nobody watched him and sang flat to the mules on the up-grade.* She closed the cover and set the stone back on the exact mark it had left in the frost.

Out on the open flags she hooked her own thumbs together, the way she had been taught, matter-of-fact, and raised her hands. On the rime her shadow showed one wing open and one fist shut. The hands fought. She gave them the rest of the night. By the time the moon hung low over the west rim of the gorge, the second wing had opened halfway, trembling, and she held it up to the moonlight anyway, arms aching, a woman a very long way from the third row — and somewhere in her, the number fourteen turned over. Before she went down to the quay, the playbill went, folded small, into the pocket where she kept things she meant to come back to.

## Stage Right

That night, in a salt-house that smelled of warm brick-dust, scorched scallion, and a roomful of sleepers packed sideways across the kang, Hsin lay curled in fox shape by the flue with her combed dark-tipped tail over her nose. By the door Suoming sat upright on her crate, one hand inside her faded plum coat against the Lock, telling her dead the day under her breath through a low rasp that caught once beneath her left ribs and kept going: hard frost at dusk; chestnuts by the well; a Jinzhou troupe; the fox showed them her hands. Then she let her head rest back against the post.

Mei slept, and her sleeping hand walked two fingers along the quilt’s ridge. One step. Two step. The oil lamp on the packing-box threw the hand’s shadow on the wall, cramped, clipped short by the tin shade. Iuno, awake on her good side with her crystal shoulder on its flannel-wrapped brick and Amy warm between them, watched the little walker reach the end of its cloth world, turn, and reach it again. Reaching across Amy with her one hand, she moved the lamp, and the shadow got more sky.

Mei went down the stairs of herself, the ones behind the piano, into the snow-street, carrying the evening’s warmth cupped in both hands like a coal. The door stood where it always stood. Frost, steel, a window in it. Her breath made a circle. Inside, level with hers, a circle answered.

She raised her hands.

Against her cupped light they made the heron — wrist for the neck, thumb tucked, fingers to a crest — and its shadow fell through the frost onto the sleeping face, and lay along the cheek like a hand about to be kind. Next one hand folded a small pointed fox and perched it on the edge of the glass, because there had been a fox in the evening and a window is how the day’s news gets in. Then came the walker, along the bottom edge of the glass, its whole cliff two feet of steel. One step, two step, a running start; the fingers leapt; and on the far side of the frost, in the blue that sleep is made of, a girl caught the back of a heron, and flew.

The frost let go of one finger-width of glass. Underneath, the metal was warm.

“Same time tomorrow,” Mei said — in her sleep, in the room, in both.

Down by the flue, a fox’s ear turned toward her in the dark.

“Mm,” said Iuno, from the near dark, and put the lamp out.

In the cold between stories, the sleeper turned, and her hands came to rest on her chest of their own accord: thumbs crossed, palms open, fingers wide. A shape any child could read.

*Across a boundless sky of nothing but blue.*

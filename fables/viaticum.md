<font color=moccasin>"That little—"</font>

<font color=mistyrose>"Ma. *Ma.*"</font>

Amy was already at the door with the cap off the marker, on her knees on cold brass, with the wind flattening her hair sideways.

She wrote it under the five black characters in acid green, in a font she had designed when she was fourteen and had never once got wrong: **LUCKY BIRD**, four inches high, on the door of a decommissioned mobile dental surgery bound east through the Disputed Reaches with seven people in it.

The nub gave out on the D. She finished the D by holding the marker sideways and scrubbing.

<font color=mistyrose>"Done."</font>

Mei crouched down next to her daughter on the brass strip and looked at it.

Five characters she could not read, in a hand that was hers and had never been shown to her, with a fifth one she had watched her own arm produce.

And under them, in the last of a dead girl's green marker from a dead city, a name off a chicken shop.

<font color=moccasin>"That's the best thing I've ever seen."</font>

<font color=mistyrose>"It's an insult to design."</font>

<font color=moccasin>"It's an *heirloom.*"</font>

<font color=mistyrose>"It's a *chicken shop*, Ma—"</font>

<font color=indianred>"IN. Everybody. Wind's turning and we've got a hundred and ten kilometres before the first stop and I want the gorge in daylight."</font>

They got in.

The gearbox made its noise at eleven hundred. Chisa noted it, out loud, for the log.

And the *Lucky Bird* came off the brass strip and out from under the last of the roof, east, into a morning with actual weather in it, with two bikes on either shoulder and a curtain moving in a doorway that had no door.

## 07:22

*(Nineteen minutes earlier, and eleven kilometres down, where the light is on a schedule.)*

The kitchen at the top of the house had a yellow paper taped to the wall between the spice rack and the calendar, and the tape at the upper corner was peeling again.

Rebecca came in first and went to the cupboard and got the plates down without thinking about it, because she had been doing the breakfast plates for two months and her hands had a number in them.

She got to five before she noticed.

She stood at the counter holding five plates.

Lynae came in behind her, saw it, and went to the sink and turned the tap on, which was a thing you do with your back to a room.

Chiyo sat down at the end of the table and took her coat off and folded it over the chair back and said, without looking up:

*"Three."*

<font color=greenyellow>"...Yeah."</font>

*"Put back two. Then sit down."*

Rebecca put back two.

She set three places, and she did it properly, and it took about eleven seconds and cost her something, and the cost showed in her shoulders and stopped there.

Then she sat down.

*"Mm,"* said Chiyo. *"You did that on the first morning."*

<font color=greenyellow>"Did what."</font>

*"The correct number, on the first morning, without help."* Chiyo poured out of the second flask, which she had also brought, because she had been sixty-three for a while and had opinions about how many flasks a morning needs. *"It took me eleven days. There is no prize for it."*

<font color=greenyellow>"Feels like there oughta be a prize."</font>

*"Mm. Drink that."*

Lynae turned the tap off and came and sat down and put her elbows on the table and her face in her hands for about four seconds, and then took her face out of her hands and said,

<font color=palegoldenrod>"I'm making tteokbokki."</font>

<font color=greenyellow>"It's twenty past seven in the *morning*—"</font>

<font color=palegoldenrod>"It's the only thing I know how to cook at people."</font>

*"I'll have some,"* said Chiyo.

<font color=palegoldenrod>"It's very spicy."</font>

*"Make it properly, then,"* said Chiyo.

---

Afterward, Chiyo got up and went and stood in front of the yellow paper with her hands behind her back and read it all the way down. Six rules in one hand. A seventh in red. An eighth added at midnight, in the first hand again, under everything:

**8. We come back.**

She read that one twice.

Then she asked for a pen, and Rebecca gave her the one off the fridge, and Chiyo wrote a ninth rule at the bottom of the paper in a careful borrowed alphabet, one letter at a time, the way a woman writes who has been practising a foreign script for a year in an apartment on the third floor in case somebody ever needed her to.

**9. There is someone in the house.**

<font color=greenyellow>"What's that mean?"</font>

*"It's what you put over a door."* Chiyo capped the pen. *"It means somebody is in. It means a traveller shouldn't pass by."*

<font color=palegoldenrod>"...That's a nice thing to put on a rules list."</font>

*"It's not nice at all. It's an instruction to strangers."* Chiyo sat down and picked up her cup. *"Nice is what people call it afterward."*

Rebecca looked at the ninth rule for a while.

Then she got up, and went to the drawer where the markers lived — eleven of them now, the green one gone east on a truck door — and came back with the black, and went over Chiyo's letters one at a time, following the shape exactly, without improving anything, so that when the tape gave out and the paper yellowed and the ink faded, the ninth rule would still be there in a hand that belonged to a woman who had waited twenty years and knew how it was done.

Then she picked up the warped-neck lute with the bad E, and sat down on the kitchen floor with her back against the cupboard, and played four notes, appallingly, into a house with three people in it.

The vents took it.

# Statura

### *The Rinascita Saga — Lahai-Roi Prelude: Aemeath, from the Lake to the Blue Door*

---

> *In the old story a man goes down to the river and takes up clay. The story is interested in the word he writes. It is interested in the morning he rubs it out. About the carrying it says nothing at all.*
>
> *Somebody hauled that clay up out of the water. Somebody worked it soft across a whole winter, warmed it against a body, chose how tall. Somebody did all of that with two hands while the word was still an idea in another person's mouth.*
>
> *The story calls him the rabbi and moves on.*
>
> *Nobody in the story asks the clay. Nobody in the story asks the river either.*

---

## I. The River

The Roya Frostlands keep four records and lose three of them.

The first is snow, which files everything and then revises. The second is ice, which is honest for one winter and then goes down the lake in April carrying its testimony out of jurisdiction. The third is the wind, which reads aloud and remembers nothing. The fourth is people, and people are the only ones who write it down, which is why the whole country's history fits inside a weight-book at a trading post two hours from anywhere and why so much of it is in the remarks column.

The remarks column at Vantry Cross, for the second week of the eleventh month, says: *Woman in, no cart, no papers, one child. Bought: blanket, rope, oil, salt, one tin of blue. Sold: nothing. Weighed nothing. Asked the road.*

Bregge kept that book for thirty-one years, and when a girl of twelve came through on the shuttle with a house key on a cord and a face he had watched grow up in eight-week intervals, he took the book down off the shelf and turned it around on the counter so she could read it right way up, because she had asked, and because she had asked properly.

"That's you," he said. "The one child."

"There's no name."

"She hadn't done it yet."

---

Here is what the girl was told about the lake, in four instalments, over four years, by a woman who told it differently every time.

**The first telling.** Amy was four and had asked at the wrong moment, which is to say the moment she was being carried, half asleep, out of the bath. "You were in the lake," Ma said, "and I was walking past, and I thought, well, that's a waste of a perfectly good kid." And then she made a noise like a crane hoist and lifted her overhead, and the whole subject went out the window along with the water.

**The second telling.** Amy was five and had asked over cards. "The ice went. You went with it. I got there and you'd stopped kicking, which is the part I didn't like." Ma laid a card down and took two. "So I got a hand under you. And you bit me."

"I did *not*."

"Left forearm. It's still there." It was still there. Amy checked, and there were four small crescents, and they were spaced wrong for a fish and right for a person about three and a half years old, and Amy went to bed that night feeling like a criminal and a scientist at once.

**The third telling.** Amy was six and had asked at the exact right moment, which is to say from the doorway of a room Ma thought was empty. "There were three of you," Ma said, to the fire, not to the doorway. "That's the part I'm not going to tell her."

Amy went back to bed and lay there with her heart going and did not ask again for eleven months.

**The fourth telling.** Amy was seven and asked the way a person asks who has decided to accept the answer. It was a clear afternoon and the light on the frostlands was doing the thing where the whole country looks like the inside of a bell. They were on the step. Ma had a cup and had not drunk out of it for a while.

"I don't know," Ma said.

Amy waited.

"I know I went in. I know I came out with you." Ma turned the cup on the wood, a quarter-turn by the rim, settling it, which she did before hard things and never noticed doing. "Everything before I went in is a room with the door shut. There's a lot of that. You don't get to have it, because I don't."

"Then how do you know it was a lake?"

"Because I was wet," said Ma, "and there was a lake, and I'm not a genius but I'm not a *fool.*"

Amy laughed. Then she stopped laughing, because she had been running a project since she was six and the project had a rule, and the rule was that everything Ma said about herself went in the book, and the book had four versions of one afternoon, and the fourth version was the shortest and the newest and the only one with a door in it.

She wrote it down that night, in the back of the weight-book Bregge had let her have because its spine was broken. She wrote it in the remarks column, because that is where the truth goes in a country that keeps four records.

*She dosent know. She says it four ways. All four have me in them.*

---

The name arrived four days after the lake, at Bregge's counter, on a Spacetrek Collective intake sheet that had been at the post since before Bregge and would be there long after.

The woman had a child on one hip and a stub of pencil in the other hand and a way of standing that Bregge described, decades later, as *like she was about to be asked to leave and had decided in advance to be polite about it.* She filled the sheet from the top. Age of party: estimate. Condition on recovery: hypothermic, responsive. Relation to party: and there she stopped, and looked at the box for a while, and wrote *mine*, and Bregge, who processed one of these a decade, let it stand.

Then the box that said NAME OF PARTY.

Four strokes, no pause.

"That's an odd one," Bregge said. "How's it go?"

She spelled it. She spelled it the way you spell something you have spelled before.

"Never heard it in my life."

"It's the prettiest word I know," said the woman, and picked the sheet up and blew on it, which nobody had needed to do with Collective ink for sixty years.

"What's it mean?"

She put the pencil back in the cup on the counter, handle out, and shifted the child to the other hip.

"Ask me in about twenty years."

---

Two other things happened in the second week of the eleventh month that the weight-book records without comment.

She bought a tin of blue. There was one tin of blue at Vantry Cross and had been for six years because nobody in the frostlands paints anything; the frostlands eat paint. She paid for it in coin that Bregge had to look at twice and put in the till anyway, and she carried it out with the rope and the oil and the salt, and Bregge watched her go up the road with a child on her back and a tin swinging from one hand and thought, in a professional way, *that's four kilometres of dead weight for a door nobody's going to see.*

And she asked the road. Everybody who came through that door asked for the road to Rabelle. She asked whether any road in the Roya went *up* — past the shuttle pad, past the survey line, up to where the ceiling came down and met the ice — and Bregge said there was nothing up there but the ceiling, and she said thank you, and looked at the door of the shop for a second longer than the conversation required, and left.

He put that in the remarks column too. Thirty-one years of remarks and he could not have said why.

---

The cabin sat in a hollow with the lake below it and a hill at its back that took the worst of the weather. It had two rooms, a stove that had opinions, and a window over the sink that stuck at the top. The door faced the lake.

She painted the door in the second week, in the cold, badly, with a brush that shed. It took three coats and eleven days and she swore at it the entire time, in a language that was not Royan and was not Collective standard and that Amy would spend her whole life failing to place. The blue came out uneven and violent and visible from the far shore.

"Why blue?" Amy asked, at four, sitting in a heap of blankets on the step with her boots on the wrong feet.

Ma looked at the door. She had paint on her wrist. She had told the child it was a tattoo now, permanent, official, and the child had believed her, and would go on believing her for another six hours.

"So it's findable," she said.

"By who?"

"By anybody looking."

That is the whole of the answer that was ever given. Amy asked twice more over the following three years and got a joke both times, and stopped, because she had been raised by a woman who could tell when the tank was running low and she had learned to read the gauge on somebody else.

---

They shared a birthday. This was decided at the post, out loud, in front of Bregge, on the day of the intake sheet.

"Date of birth," said Bregge, because the form wanted it.

"Today."

"She's three."

"Then she's had a rough three years and she's due one." The woman looked at the box, and then wrote in it, and then looked at the box under it, which was hers, and wrote the same thing. "Everyone needs a birthday. The paperwork can share."

Amy grew up thinking this was an ordinary arrangement. She discovered otherwise at the Academy at eleven, in a dormitory, at a party, from a girl called Alara who was appalled, and Amy defended the practice for forty minutes with such force that three separate people asked her afterward if she was all right.

---

## II. The Office of the Prime Minister

The Snowfluff Seals came up through the lake ice in a hole they maintained by the simple method of sitting in it, and the biggest one was the Prime Minister, and the office was hereditary in the sense that it went to whoever was biggest.

Amy learned the constitutional position at four and a half, in some detail, over a whole afternoon, with diagrams.

"He's the Prime Minister because he's the biggest," said Ma.

"Why?"

"Because it's a seal government and they haven't got hands. What are they going to do, sign things?"

"What if a bigger one comes?"

"Then that one's Prime Minister and this one's very relieved." Ma broke the herring in half and gave Amy the tail end, which was the good end and both of them knew it. "It's an *office*, kiddo. The office stays. The seal comes and goes."

This is a lie told with an enormous amount of preparation.

The seals died. They died the way everything in the frostlands died, which was quietly and out of sight and usually in the third month, and one winter the Prime Minister stopped coming up and the next spring a different seal was on the same rock, and Amy — who had been introduced to the office rather than to the animal — waved at him and gave him herring and said *hello, Prime Minister,* and came through four consecutive administrations entirely intact.

She worked it out at six.

She worked it out the way she worked everything out, which was by keeping a book. She had four years of the Prime Minister's markings in the back of the weight-book, drawn in crayon, with dates, because she drew everything, and the markings changed and the drawings did not lie. She sat on the bank on a grey morning with the book open on her knees and looked at the animal on the rock and understood the entire architecture of the deception in about four seconds: the office, the size rule, the elaborate afternoon with diagrams, the herring.

She looked up the slope at the blue door.

Then she closed the book and put it under her coat and went inside and said, "The Prime Minister's got a scar on his flipper now," and Ma said, "Rough session," and Amy said, "Very rough," and neither of them said anything else about it for twenty-two years.

That was the first time Amy protected her mother from something. She was six years and two months old. She has no memory whatsoever of what it felt like. She can produce, in perfect order, on demand, every single time she did it after.

## III. Two Hours Out

The work was called work.

It went by the following names, on the following occasions, and Amy wrote all of them down: *a job. A thing. A haul. Some idiot's cargo. A favour for Bregge that has stopped being a favour. Nothing. Absolutely nothing, go to bed.*

What it actually was, so far as the weight-book at Vantry Cross can establish, was contract recovery on the survey line — going out onto the ice after things people had lost and could not afford to leave, and after a few things people had lost and could very much afford to leave and wanted back anyway, which paid better. Four days out, six days out. Once, in the third winter, eleven.

She came back with the coat on and the coat was the document.

Amy's inventory was conducted from the step and had four points and took under two seconds and was performed every single time from the age of five:

**One.** How she's carrying the pack. On both shoulders is good. On the left only is a right shoulder thing and she'll say it's nothing. Held in front, on the arms, is bad and means ribs and means she will cook dinner standing very straight and go to bed early with a book she does not open.

**Two.** The hem. Frostlands mud comes off in the first hour of walking. Blood does not come off at all and goes brown and stays. New brown on the hem means she'll be quiet through the meal and funny after it, in that order, always.

**Three.** The hands. Gloves off before the door means fine. Gloves on through the door means one of them is coming off with something under it.

**Four.** The face at the top of the rise, before she knows she's being watched. This is the only one Amy never wrote down, and it is the only one that mattered, and she went on running it for the rest of her life on everybody she loved.

---

There is a page in the back of a weight-book, in pencil, headed **SHE CAME BACK**, that runs for two and a half years and holds ninety-one entries. Each entry has a date, a number of days, and a mark. Amy invented the marks herself. There are three of them and she never wrote a key, which is the correct behaviour for a six-year-old keeping a file she expects to be searched.

The ninety-first entry has a date and a number of days and no mark, because on the day it would have been marked she was doing something else.

---

Bregge gave her the weight-book when she was five, because its spine had gone and because she had spent forty minutes at his counter reading the columns out loud in the manner of a person conducting an audit.

"What do I do with it?"

"Whatever you like. It's ruled, is the main thing about it."

"What's ruled?"

"It's got the lines in already," said Bregge, "so you can't cheat."

Amy considered this. It went in, all the way down, and stayed. Twenty-four years later a woman would stand in an office in the Academy with three drawers open in front of her and a card in each hand, looking at eleven years of someone else's handwriting in printed columns, and would understand instantly and without being told why the columns mattered so much to the person who filled them.

## IV. The Visitor

She came about twice a year and never at the same time twice and never through the road.

She had a flower on her and it changed colour, and she smelled like crushed green in a country that had no green in it for nine months of the year, and she called Amy *weed*, and she was, by a distance, the most frightening person Amy had ever met and the only adult who had ever declined to soften her voice for a child.

"Who is she?" Amy asked, at five.

"Somebody I owe a conversation," said Ma.

"About what?"

"Ask her."

Amy asked her. Camellya said, "Nothing you'd like," and went back to eating, and that was the end of it, and Amy loved her immediately and permanently on the strength of the refusal.

---

She drank the hot chocolate. That was the thing about her. She would sit at the table and call it a crime — the salt, the salt was the crime, the salt was an atrocity, she raised it every visit as if for the first time — and then drink the whole cup and hold it out for another. Ma stopped waiting to be asked around the second visit. She would put a second cup down by Camellya's elbow, mid-sentence, without looking, and Camellya let it stand without comment every single time, and this went on for years and was, as far as Amy could ever establish, the entirety of their friendship.

One night Camellya fell asleep at that table with her face in her arms. Amy, who was six and had a marker, drew a full battle scene on the back of her hand. Camellya carried it for two days before she noticed. When she noticed, she looked at it for a long moment, and said, "The proportions are wrong on the big one," and did not wash it off for another day.

---

The falling lesson happened in the summer Amy was six, and is on file in three places, told by Amy, at three different ages, and the details do not drift, which is how you know.

She had gone up the wisteria over the porch because the seals were coming through the ice and she wanted the high ground. She got stuck. Ma was two hours away at the post. Camellya came around the corner of the house with a dead something over her shoulder, looked up for about a second and a half, put the dead thing down, and said:

"Don't stiffen up. It's the stiffening that breaks you. Go loose and let the ground have you."

"I'll die."

"You'll die eventually. That's not today's problem. Let go."

She let go. She was caught. Then she was made to do it eleven more times off the porch rail, counted out loud, and on the eleventh the arms were not there and the snow was, and she rolled, and she got up, and Camellya said, "There. Now you can jump at anything."

Ma came back that evening, saw the tracks in the snow — eleven landings in a fan around the porch, one of them deeper — and stood looking at them for some time with the pack still on both shoulders.

"Who?"

"The one with the flower."

Ma put the pack down. "How many?"

"Eleven. She counted."

"Did she catch you every time?"

"Ten."

And Ma nodded slowly, and went in, and Amy, following, saw her stop at the stove with her back turned and put both hands flat on it, and heard her say a word in the language that was not Royan and not Collective standard, and could not have said, then or ever, whether the word was a curse or a thank you.

## V. Naming the Ceiling

The sky over the Roya Frostlands is four kilometres of pearled ice and the underside of a sea, and it does not have stars in it and has not had stars in it for a hundred years, and everybody in the country knows this, and there is no reason at all to go outside at night.

They went outside at night.

It started in the first winter and ran the whole four years and had no rules except that you brought your own blanket and that you did not talk during it, which was a rule Amy broke constantly and which nobody ever got round to enforcing.

They sat on the step with their backs against the blue door, which held the day's stove-heat better than any other surface on the property, and they looked up at a ceiling.

"That one's the Hound," said Ma.

"Where?"

"There." A finger, tracing. "Nose, ear, front leg, back leg. He's running. He's always running, he's been running for ages, he's never once caught the thing."

"What's the thing?"

"Depends who's telling it."

"There's nothing there."

"There's nothing there *now*," Ma agreed, comfortably. "Doesn't mean he stopped."

So they went through it. The Hound. The Ladle, which had a bent handle and which you could use to find north if the ceiling ever came off. The Three Sisters, one of whom was extremely annoying. The Boat, which Ma always found last and always found with a small satisfied grunt, as if it had been hiding. Amy learned all of them off a blank ceiling and could point to every single one by the second winter and was, for the whole of her childhood, the only person in Lahai-Roi under the age of ninety who could name a sky.

She checked them later. Of course she checked them later; she was who she was. She ran the whole list against the Collective's pre-Lament charts in the Academy library at fourteen, on a Tuesday, with four reference volumes open and the librarian hovering.

The Ladle checked out. The Hound checked out, under another name.

The Boat is in no chart in the archive.

---

One night in the third winter — clear, if the word means anything under a lid; cold enough that the lake was talking to itself down the slope — Ma stopped in the middle of the Three Sisters and did not start again.

She was looking up. She had been looking up the whole time, but she had gone from looking at the ceiling to looking *through* it, and there is a difference, and a seven-year-old can see it from eight inches away.

Then she said a name.

It had three syllables and the middle one was long, and she said it the way you say a name to a room you have already checked is empty, and Amy — who was six, and who could spell four hundred words, and who would spend the next twenty-two years trying — lost her grip on it entirely. It went through her. She got the shape and lost the sound before morning. Three syllables. The middle one long.

"Which one's that?" she said.

"That's not a star."

"Then what is it?"

Ma pulled the blanket up over Amy's shoulder, and left her hand there, and looked at the ceiling for a while longer.

"Somebody I'm going to be very late for," she said.

---

Amy filed this under the wrong heading for a long time. She filed it under *Ma is sad about a person,* which is a child's filing and which holds up. It took her until nineteen, in a chair, with a god's dreaming coming up around her like water, to open the file again and see that she had been sitting on a step against a warm door beside a woman who was homesick for a place she could not name, in a country with a ceiling, and that every single thing that woman ever did with her hands — the door, the wisterias, the birthday, the seals' constitution, the four versions of a lake — was somebody building a home out of nothing at all while looking straight up at the lid.

That was the look. Amy has spent her whole life failing to describe it and has been understood every single time she tried, by everybody, on the first attempt. She could see it from eight inches away, in the dark, at four years old.

---

## VI. The Frame

The marks on the kitchen doorframe start at six.

This ought to be strange and passed clean over the head of everyone present, because Amy was six and six-year-olds decline to audit the start dates of family traditions. It became strange later, in the way that everything becomes strange later.

It happened on a Tuesday in the second month. Ma came in from the post with the mail and a length of dowel and a carpenter's pencil, and said, "Stand there," and Amy stood there, and Ma put the dowel flat on her head and swore about the angle and made a mark and wrote *Amy, 6* beside it in handwriting that leaned right and pressed hard.

"Why?"

"Because you're getting taller and it's rude of you and I want it on record."

"Do it again tomorrow."

"That's not how it works."

"Do it *again* tomorrow."

"Every year," said Ma. "That's the deal. Once a year, and we'll go all the way up the frame, and when you're forty and enormous we'll have the whole thing and it'll be the best door in the country."

Amy negotiated her up to twice, on a technicality involving a growth spurt, which is why the frame reads *Amy, 6* and then *Amy, 6 and a half (she insisted)* in the same leaning hand, three centimetres higher, four months later.

And then *Amy, 7.*

And then a great deal of blank wood, which a woman who leaned right and pressed hard had deliberately left room for, on a doorframe that went up to the ceiling, in a house she had painted a colour you could see from the far shore.

---

Camellya put a line on it that winter.

Amy had had a bad day about being small — a specific, incandescent, stamping fury about being small, conducted at the frame, in front of the frame, at the frame's face — and Camellya came in with snow on her and found a child of seven vibrating with rage in a kitchen.

"How tall do you want to be?"

"Taller than *everybody.*"

"That's greedy and unrealistic and I respect it."

"How tall am I going to *get*?"

Nobody can answer that. Camellya took the carpenter's pencil off the sill and went to the frame and put her thumb high up on the blank wood, well above anything, at a height that meant nothing whatsoever, and drew one line: straight, level, better than any mark on that frame before or after, and wrote nothing beside it.

"There," she said. "Go on, then."

Amy's own marks would eventually run out nine centimetres short of it, and she would stand in front of that gap at twenty-five with a ruler in her hand and laugh so hard she had to sit down on the kitchen floor, and she never told Camellya, and Camellya knew.

The tin lived behind the loose board under the window seat, wrapped in a sock, with a game cartridge in it that had *CAM + AMY — MA, USE YOUR OWN SAVE* written across the label in the same hand. The security model relied on adults respecting laundry. It worked for twelve years.

It had never worked for one day. Ma found the tin three days after it was hidden, in the ordinary course of sweeping, and moved it whenever she swept, and put it back facing the same way, every time, for years.

Amy learned this at twenty-five, from Camellya, at the same table, and had to put her gloves down very carefully.

---

The fever came in the third winter and lasted five days and is the only stretch of that entire childhood Amy has in fragments rather than in file order.

She has: the ceiling of the small room going soft at the corners. The sound of the stove being fed at intervals that were far too close together. Being carried to the window and back for no reason she could reconstruct. Someone saying numbers. Cold cloth, then cold cloth again, then cold cloth again, at a frequency that in retrospect describes a person who has not sat down.

And on the fourth night, one strand of hair across her mouth, and a hand moving it: off the brow, back — and then the hand staying. Palm flat to her forehead. The full weight of it. A pressure like a hand that had decided a long time ago to be gentle.

That is the whole memory. It has no words in it. Amy would meet the first half of that gesture again twenty-two years later on a terrace in a foreign city, delivered by weather, and would not know why her eyes went, and would blame the wind, correctly.

Ma slept for fourteen hours after the fever broke, on the floor, by the bed, with her boots on. Amy woke first and lay very still so as not to wake her, for two hours, needing the toilet the entire time.

Item six on the list is *don't get sick,* and the result column says *failed,* and the remarks say *this one is not fair,* and it is the only entry on the page where the child permitted herself a complaint.

## VIII. Same Time Tomorrow

The formula was fixed by the second winter and did not vary.

Amy in bed. Ma in the doorway with the light behind her, which is the shape a person makes when they are about to be somewhere else.

"Right. Out."

"I'm out."

"You're talking."

"I'm out *and* talking, that's two things, you didn't specify—"

"Amy."

"Okay."

And then, from the doorway, in a voice with no ceremony in it whatsoever, in the flat cheerful tone of an arrangement being confirmed:

"Same time tomorrow?"

"Same time tomorrow."

That was it. That was the entire thing.

It got used for other purposes almost immediately, in the way of any good tool. It got used at the lake when the seals submerged. It got used at the post when Bregge shut up early. It got used, once, at a hole in the ice with a rope in it, at a distance of forty metres, shouted, by a woman going in after a contract that was not worth it, and shouted back by a five-year-old on the bank who had been told to stand there and had stood there.

It stood in the place where goodbye goes, and did a different job there, and both of them knew the difference, and it stayed unsaid between them for four years.

---

The week of forms began on a Thursday and Amy did not understand what she was looking at for four days.

The kitchen table had paper on it. STARTORCH ACADEMY, said the letterhead on some of them. SPACETREK COLLECTIVE — WARDSHIP, said others. There was one with a red line down the side. There was one that had to be done three times because the pencil went through the paper.

"What's a ward?"

"It's a kid the paperwork's looking after."

"Whose kid?"

Ma turned the page over and did not answer, and Amy — who had inventory protocols, and who had run point one and point two and point three on a person walking up a rise since she was five — sat down on the floor by the stove and did not ask again, and this is the single decision she has spent the most of her life re-litigating.

On the fifth night she held a piece of paper up to the lamp.

It was small. It came out of her inside pocket rather than off the stack, and it was the size of a requisition slip, and it had a printed edge. Ma held it up between her eye and the lamp the way you check a thing for a watermark, and turned it, and looked at it for about nine seconds, and then put it flat on the table and wrote something on the corner of it with the carpenter's pencil.

Amy, on the floor, at an angle, in lamplight, saw the mark go on.

It was one character. It was brushed rather than written, which was a strange thing to do with a carpenter's pencil, and which meant the hand knew a different tool. It had a shape that Amy's eye took hold of instantly and completely, the way a child's eye takes hold of a face. She hunted it through every alphabet in the Roya and came up empty. She hunted it through the Academy's at thirteen, for three months, using the index, and then the librarian, and then a professor who was very kind about it, and came up empty again, and put it in the drawer where she kept the things that stayed open.

She saw that shape twice in her life. Once on a piece of paper held up to a lamp in a cabin.

And once, at twenty-five, on the top corner of a postcard-sized index card in a drawer in an office in a city underground, where it sat in the ruled space the way an address sits on an envelope, and had sat, on card after card, for two years, until a woman with prosthetic legs and absolute integrity stopped writing it, because the addressee was doing nothing with them.

Ma went down to the city on the sixth night and came back on the seventh morning.

This was unprecedented. In four years she had gone as far as the post and turned round at the post every single time, and the city sat at the bottom of the Riseway being somewhere other people lived.

She left at four in the afternoon with the slip in her inside pocket and the wisteria stakes still lying in the snow where she had been working, and she said, "Bregge's coming up at six, do what he says, he's a coward and you'll enjoy it," and Bregge was genuinely coming up at six, and the rest of it was a joke, and Amy laughed, and that is the last completely uncomplicated laugh in the record.

Bregge came up at six with cards and a bag of the good sweets and lost eleven hands out of fourteen to a seven-year-old and said afterward, for thirty-one years, that she had cheated and he had never worked out how.

Ma came back on the shuttle at seven the next morning with the city on her coat. Amy could smell it — recycled air, and the particular metal of the Riseway, and, faintly, under it, an institution.

She had been in that city for four hours. She had walked into a building on the Institute side, found a bench in a sublevel where a researcher of twenty-six was working alone because she always was, put a slip of paper on the bench and pushed it across and said *that's where these go,* and asked to be deleted.

And the researcher had done it. Every record. Every entry. Every trace of a woman with gold eyes who fell out of nothing and had never been in any file that mattered, gone, on request, inside forty minutes — the building, the record, the city, in that order.

And she left one thing standing. One file, in one system, that she had no authority to leave standing and left standing anyway, and it was a wardship, and it had a name in it, four strokes, no pause.

Nobody asked the researcher anything about that night for thirteen years. When somebody finally did, in a room with the paperwork open, the answer took two words and the word she preferred was *filing.*

## IX. Day 0

It snowed in the morning.

She had been up since four. Amy knew that because the stove was at temperature when she got out of bed, and a stove takes two hours, and she had been running that particular calculation on that particular stove since she was five.

"Yeah."

"Four *months*—"

"Give it four months and then write and tell me I was wrong, and press hard so it goes through." "Okay. I'm writing it down."

"Good," said Ma. "Write it down. That's how you make people keep things."

Here is the part that got said, and it got said in the doorway with the door already open and the cold coming in around the both of them and the wisterias standing bare against the wall, and it was said in a warm voice with a smile on it, kneeling, which is the worst possible delivery for what it is.

Amy had said the thing she had been saying since she was five, the thing she had said at fires and at counters and at the edge of the ice with a rope in it, the thing she believed to the soles of her boots:

"I'm going to be a saviour. Like you."

And the smile came. It was a beautiful smile. It was the best smile in the country and it had got them a room at Rabelle once and a discount at the post twice, and it arrived on schedule, and then it stopped, in the middle, with the eyes still open.

"Don't ever, ever become like me."

That was all of it. There is no more. Every version Amy has ever run — and she has run it perhaps forty thousand times, in a grey dormitory, in a resonance chair, in a reactor's heart with no clocks in it for ten years, in an inn in a foreign country that smells of chili oil — every version has exactly those seven words in exactly that order in exactly that voice, and nothing before it and nothing after it, because there was nothing after it.

Then she stood up. And the knees cracked again.

And she went out and pulled the door to, and it was blue, and it stuck at the bottom the way it always did.

She said something at the gate.

Amy was at the window by then, because she had gone from the doorway to the window in the time it took, because it is very hard to look at a door.

The window over the sink sticks at the top. Amy got the lower sash up with both hands, and leaned out into it, and the cold came in and took the whole kitchen in about four seconds, and she yelled.

She has never been able to reconstruct what she yelled. She has tried. She is a professional archivist of her own life and this is the one gap she has never closed, and the best she has ever managed is that it was long, and it was loud, and there was at least one thing in it she should not have said.

And the woman at the gate turned around, forty metres out, in the snow, with the pack on both shoulders, and lifted one hand, and called back across the whole white hollow in a voice that carried, because it always carried:

*"Go inside. You'll catch cold."*

Amy stayed at the sill with the sash on her back and her hands going white on the frame, waiting for the four words that came after that, every night, for four years, in a doorway with the light behind them.

The snow went on taking the path.

---

She got smaller. That is the whole of it. She got smaller and the snow, which is very fair about these things and takes everything at the same speed, took her coat, and then took her tracks, and then took the path she had salted the week before so that a child would stop falling on her butt.

Amy stood at the window until she could not feel her hands.

Then she came away and got the stool and went to the tin.

The cartridge was there, facing the same way.

The page was gone.

---

She sat on the kitchen floor with a biscuit tin in her lap and a sock in one hand for a long time, working it out — because she was seven, and because she had been raised by a woman who could smell a hidden thing through a floorboard, and because the page and the cartridge had been in the same tin, and one had been taken and one had been put back facing the same way.

It has been unruled ever since.

There are two accounts and both of them are hers. In the first, a woman sweeping a kitchen unfolded a page from a weight-book, read thirty-four numbered entries and one on the reverse, refolded it along a fold that was already there, put it in a coat pocket, and started filling in forms eleven days later. In the second, a page that had been folded in eight and handled by a child for two and a half years simply went the way of every small thing in every house in the world, and the eleven days are eleven days, and a woman was already going, and a girl needed the leaving to be *caused* by something, because a child can argue with a cause. Weather simply happens to you.

Amy has argued both sides. She has argued the first side, out loud, once, at nineteen, to nobody, in a chamber, at 99.6%.

The paper has never turned up. She has been through a cabin, a dormitory, a footlocker, a memorial fund, an Academy's sealed casualty file, a decade of drifting testimony at the rim of a void that keeps everything, and four hundred years of somebody else's salvage. She has also, in the last eleven months, gone through the pockets of one specific coat, four times, at night, while its owner was asleep, and she is ashamed of that and has told exactly one person.

---

That night she made the hot chocolate. Three spoons of cocoa, the little bit of salt that one of them swore was the secret and the other swore was a crime, marshmallows until structural.

She got out a notebook.

She wrote *Dear Ma* at the top, because she had been told that written things get kept, and because she had one confirmed procedure for making a person stay and it was a procedure she had invented herself at the age of six, at a counter, out loud, reading columns.

And under it she wrote *Day 1.*

<font color=mistyrose>"You did the *look.*"</font>

The pause had a great deal inside it. Amy has been reading that particular pause since she was six and sat in it and let it go by, and this time, for the first time in twenty-two years, she also said something into it.

<font color=mistyrose>"There's a boat in it,"</font> she said. <font color=mistyrose>"Off the ridge, above the eighth tower, four stars and a bent one. You always found the Boat last. You'd go through the whole ceiling and then you'd go *ah* like it had been hiding."</font>

Mei's arm did not move.

<font color=moccasin>"...Is that a real one?"</font>

<font color=mistyrose>"It's not in any chart in the Academy archive and I checked all four volumes and then I made a librarian check them,"</font> said Amy. <font color=mistyrose>"So no. It's yours. You made it up in a country with a lid on it and you taught it to a four-year-old and now there's two of us who can find it, which as far as I'm concerned makes it real, and I'd like to see somebody file an objection."</font>

The wind came in under the rail.

Mei laughed, once, with her head down, at a window, the way she does.

Then she said, in the doorway voice, in the flat cheerful tone of an arrangement being confirmed, with no idea in the world where it came from:

<font color=moccasin>"Same time tomorrow?"</font>

And Amy — who has held the other sentence in both hands for twenty-two years and has never once presented the bill for it, who kept all seven words in the original voice through a decade in a room with no clocks, where the numbers failed and then the marks failed and then everything failed, and who is sitting on the floor of an inn beside the only living person on the far side of that door — said:

<font color=mistyrose>"Same time tomorrow."</font>

---

Out on the landing the argument about the bowl had been settled in Lupa's favour by a margin nobody was happy with.

Yangyang came back up the stairs at ten past midnight to return a bowl she could have returned in the morning. She stopped on the third step, and heard the room, and went back down without using the fourth, and stood in the lane under the lamp for a minute with her route case under her arm.

Then she took the book out of it, and turned to the back, and wrote three words in the second ink and boxed them, and shut it before the lamp could get a proper look.

Later, in the back downstairs room, Amy took a postcard-sized index card out of the inside pocket of the rig case, where it has been since Lahai-Roi, and looked at the top corner of it, at one character brushed rather than written, sitting in the ruled space the way an address sits on an envelope.

She has been in this city for two months. There is a man at the east gate who writes letters for people in the mornings and who is impossible after eleven, and who once read five characters off a scrap and gave a woman back a river bend with no road to it.

She has walked past his table nine times.

She put the card back in the pocket, and turned the lamp down, and lay in the dark listening to the north through the floor of a building that smelled of chili oil, and did the thing she has done every night of her life since she was four years old, which is to look up at a ceiling and find the Boat.

It is not in any chart.

She found it anyway.

Chapter 1: Luò Zǐ

“Hold the lamp two inches left, Auntie, your thumb’s throwing a shadow right across the third knot.”

“My thumb,” Iuno said mildly from the head of the pallet, “has been in this exact spot since you boiled the scissors. If you lean your forehead any closer to her ribs, you are going to singe your bangs on the chimney.”

She shifted the brass oil-lamp two inches to the left anyway, bracing her right elbow against her drawn-up knee so the yellow flame burned flat and still in the cold dawn draft.

Mei lay flat on her back on the straw ticking with her grey sleeping-shirt rolled up to her breastbone and her hands tucked obediently under the back of her head. The shutter was still barred against the frost outside, though a thin, knife-edge line of grey light showed along the bottom sill where the wooden turbine turned with its slow *whuff-creak*.

At the open bedroom doorway, Lupa stood with one shoulder propped against the pine jamb, her back half-turned toward the main room and a wooden porridge ladle clamped in her fist.

“Tell the kid to hurry it up,” Lupa barked at the hallway wall without looking around. “If I leave the millet on the back trivet another five minutes, the zombie’s gonna eat the salted duck eggs right out of the brine-crock, and I’m not scraping the bottom of the pot twice in one week.”

“You’re blocking the draft, Lupa, stay right there,” Mei called out cheerfully to the ceiling rafters. “You make a great door. Very insulated.”

“Shut your trap and hold your belly still, or I’ll come in there and pull the thread with the bacon tongs.”

Beside the pallet, Amy didn’t look up at Lupa and didn’t answer.

She was kneeling on a folded wool blanket with her small tin medical kit open beside her shin and a clean linen towel spread across her knees. She had scrubbed her hands and wrists in Wen’s scalding lye-water for ten minutes before bringing the tray in; the skin from her knuckles to her cuffs was boiled lobster-pink, and her small acid-green star clip was pinned twice to keep every stray strand of pink hair out of her eyes. In her right hand she held a pair of fine, sharp-pointed Huaxu suture scissors; in her left, a pair of silver splinter-forceps.

Neither instrument shook by the breadth of a hair.

“Quit wiggling your ribs when you laugh, Ma,” Amy said. Her voice came out half a tone higher than her broadcast register, clipped and bossy. “The surgeon said eight knots. If you make me drop a loop of silk into the wool, I’m charging you a finder’s fee.”

“I’m not wiggling, that’s my natural morning buoyancy,” Mei said.

On the back of her right wrist, tucked up under the bolster, the pale lines of her Tacet Mark gave a brief, warm flare of gold.

A pair of long white ears poked straight up out of the light behind Mei’s elbow, followed by a round white head, floating gold rings, and two large golden eyes that blinked once at the oil-lamp and fixed immediately on the shiny forceps in Amy’s hand.

“Hey!” Abby said at full courtroom volume, planting both front paws on the edge of the feather bolster and leaning over Mei’s shoulder to peer down at her bare ribs. “Are we taking the black strings out? Finally! Every time she turns over at night the linen wrap crinkles like a dry cabbage! Who’s got breakfast? I smell duck eggs!”

“Back in the Mark or behind the bolster, furball,” Amy snapped without taking her eyes off the first stitch. “You breathe on my sterile field and you’re getting zero bacon.”

“I don’t breathe germs, I breathe high-purity frequency!” Abby protested, though he scrambled dutifully backward onto the corner of the pillow by Mei’s ear, sitting down on his haunches and tucking his paws neatly together to supervise. “And Lupa already promised me two eggs! One for me and one for the person behind!”

“There’s nobody behind you on the pillow, Abby,” Mei pointed out.

“There might be later! You gotta plan ahead!”

Amy laid the cold flat of the silver forceps against Mei’s skin at the top of her left side, just below the curve of the lower rib, and slipped the point of the scissors under the first black silk knot.

*Snip.*

A tiny tug, and the first short tail of stiff, dark thread slid free through the healed skin and landed on the white linen towel on Amy’s knee.

“One,” Amy said. “How was that?”

“Zero out of ten for drama,” Mei reported to the ceiling. “Didn’t even tickle. Tell the surgeon his knot-work is getting evicted for non-payment of rent.”

*Snip.*

“Two,” Amy said, laying the second piece of thread neatly parallel to the first. “Hold the lamp steady, Auntie.”

“The lamp has not moved since the first administration,” Iuno said.

“Three,” Mei supplied as the third knot parted with a tiny, dry *puck*. “That one felt like when Cartethyia tries to pull a loose thread off my cuff and takes half the hem with it. Hey, Amymy, when you’re done, can I keep the threads in a little jar? We can tell Buling it’s cursed mountain moss and sell it to her for two coppers.”

“That’s disgusting, Ma, no,” Amy muttered, her brow furrowing as she moved the forceps half an inch lower toward the center of the seam.

Here the skin wasn’t smooth.

Where the broken timber spar had punched through the side of Mei’s abdomen in the falling transport, the healed flesh was drawn together in a thick, three-inch ridge—deep pink at the edges, puckered white around the middle stitch where the surgeon had taken a deep bite to close the muscle wall, and still dented slightly inward from the pressure of the linen pad.

Amy’s left thumb rested on the warm skin just below the ridge to steady it. Her knuckles were white around the silver forceps.

“Four,” Amy said, and her voice cracked slightly on the vowel before she cleared her throat. “This one’s sunk into the crease a bit. Don’t talk for a second.”

Mei’s mouth shut.

In the doorway, Lupa stopped tapping the wooden ladle against her thigh. Her wolf ears angled back toward the room, though she kept her eyes fixed fiercely on the iron kettle out in the hall.

Amy slid the fine steel point under the tight middle loop. The blades closed with a crisp click.

When she drew the forceps upward, the thread caught for half a second in the deep scar tissue. Mei’s stomach muscles jumped once under Amy’s thumb; Mei’s fingers curled hard into the coarse ticking behind her head, and her breath stopped in her nose without a sound.

“Got it,” Amy whispered, drawing the long, knotted loop smoothly out of the pink ridge and dropping it onto the towel.

Mei let her breath out slowly through her teeth.

“Five, six, seven, eight,” Amy rattled off, her hands moving fast and clean now along the lower edge where the cut shallowed out toward the hip—*snip, pull, snip, pull, snip, pull*—until the last black silk tail lay in a neat row of eight on her knee.

She set the scissors and the forceps down in the tin box with a small clink, picked up a pad of clean linen soaked in boiled chamomile water, and wiped once along the bare seam.

Nobody spoke for a moment.

In the yellow light of Iuno’s lamp, the scar sat plain across Mei’s left side: three inches of raised, puckered pink skin with eight tiny red pinpricks along either bank, warm and whole and ugly, rising and falling evenly with her breath.

Amy stared at it for two beats with the damp pad in her fingers. Then she dropped the pad into the dish, reached up with the dry back of her raw pink wrist, and scrubbed once across the tip of her nose.

“Okay,” Amy said briskly, slapping the lid of her tin kit shut. “No soaking in the bathhouse tub till Friday, no lifting anything heavier than a soup bowl, and if you try to climb the woodpile ladder, I’m telling Qingxiao to tie you to the rafters.”

“Look at that craftsmanship,” Abby declared from the pillow, leaning over to squint at the scar before vanishing back into the gold light on Mei’s wrist with a satisfied pop. “Good as new! Tell the wolf to crack the eggs!”

Mei didn’t jump up.

She took her hands out from under her head, rolled carefully onto her right hip the way she had practiced all week, and pushed herself upright on the edge of the pallet, swinging her bare feet down onto the cold pine floorboards.

She pulled her grey sleeping-shirt down over her stomach. Beneath the cotton, for the first time since they had carried her up the steps out of the fog, there was no stiff four-inch corset of linen and no biting black thread pulling at her skin when her spine curved.

Mei looked down at the floor beside the upturned crate, where her two heavy leather road-boots stood side by side.

“Wait,” Iuno said automatically, setting the brass oil-lamp down on the crate beside Barnaby the frog and dropping to one knee in front of her. “Give me your foot, the floorboards are freezing.”

Mei caught the edge of Iuno’s wool sleeve with two fingers and stopped her.

“Hang on,” Mei said softly. “Let me try the left one.”

In the doorway, Lupa turned her head all the way around. Next to the crate, Amy sat back on her heels with the towel still in her lap.

Mei pulled the heavy left boot between her heels and slid her foot inside.

Slowly, without bracing her hand on Iuno’s shoulder or keeping her back flat as a board, Mei leaned forward from the waist.

Her shoulders came down past her knees. Her stomach folded over her thighs—pulling stiff and tight across the new pink ridge on her left side, making her let out a small, grunting breath through her nose, and holding cleanly without a single sharp stab.

Her fingers reached the leather laces at her ankle.

She crossed the rawhide thongs, pulled them snug over the tongue, looped the ears, and tied a neat, square double bow by herself.

For three seconds nobody in the back bedroom moved.

Then Mei straightened up on the edge of the mattress, planted both hands on her knees, and beamed at the three of them as if she had just swum the gorge underwater.

“Did you see that?” Mei demanded, pointing both forefingers at her own left foot. “Zero frog noises! A double knot! I’m practically an acrobat!”

In the doorway, Lupa let out a short, rough snort, whacked the doorframe once with the flat of her wooden ladle, and spun back toward the stove.

“Still took you ten minutes, Deadbeat!” Lupa yelled over her shoulder as her boots stomped back across the hall. “Get your other shoe on before the porridge turns to glue!”

Amy stood up off the blanket with her tin box tucked under her arm, reached over, and flicked the tip of Mei’s nose with one boiled-pink finger. “Don’t get cocky, Ma. You still walk like an old badger.”

She marched out after Lupa, leaving the door half-open to the smell of toasted sesame and ginger.

Iuno stayed kneeling on one knee on the floorboards in front of the pallet.

She didn’t say anything about acrobats. With her right hand, she reached down, took Mei’s bare right foot by the arch, slid it into the second boot, and laced it up to the top hook with slow, steady pulls before resting her palm flat and warm over the top of Mei’s knee.

“One boot a day,” Iuno said, looking up at her in the yellow lamplight. “Do not push your luck before breakfast.”

Down in the gravel drill yard below the inner gatehouse, the frost lay white and thick as spilled salt across the flagstones.

“Again!” Liangyu barked.

Five ash spear-shafts came forward together—four of them hitting the third-form extension on the beat, and the fifth, in Little Tan’s cold hands at the far end of the file, wobbling two inches off-line so the iron socket rattled against his shoulder-guard.

Liangyu stepped down the rank with her own long ash spear held level across her hips.

For the first morning since the siege, the cedar splints were gone from her left forearm. She wore her dented iron vambrace strapped over a single flat turn of unbleached linen; where her sleeve was pushed back above the leather cuff, her wrist looked thin and pale from the weeks in the sling, a purplish-brown bruise still fading along the bone. When she struck the butt of her spear against the frozen paving stone to mark the measure—*clack, pause, clack-clack*—it was the old four-beat garrison cadence that had echoed off these same walls two hundred years ago before the rain came down the gorge.

Four paces behind Little Tan’s shoulder, out under the open arch where the lower barracks stair came up from the cistern lane, two of the Jinzhou pikemen from yesterday’s convoy were leaning against the stone bollard smoking small brass pipes while they waited for the water-detail. Further up the terrace steps, a man in a plain Ministry grey coat walked past with a surveyor’s plumb-line coiled over his arm, not looking down at the drill yard at all.

Little Tan’s eyes flicked past his own spear-point toward the grey coat on the stair. When he brought his back foot forward for the recovery turn, his heel caught on the lip of a frosted flagstone and his shaft dipped toward the gravel.

“Halt,” Liangyu said.

The five young Wardens grounded their spears. Their breath hung in thick white clouds between their helmets.

“Eyes on the target post, Tan, not the water-stair,” Liangyu said evenly, walking around to his left flank. “If you turn your chin toward the steps in a thrust, anything coming out of the ditch takes your throat before your iron clears the rail.”

Little Tan didn’t lift his spear. His hands were red and chapped around the ash wood, and his jaw was working hard under his chin-strap.

“Warden,” Little Tan said, his voice dropping low and rough so it wouldn’t carry to the Jinzhou soldiers smoking by the bollard. “The porters down at the lime-shed said the Ministry clerks were down in the cellar under the west tower before light, measuring the main arch stones with iron calipers.”

Along the short line, the other four young Wardens went very still. The freckled boy next to Tan looked down at his boots.

“They’re saying in the barracks that once the Censure Courts clear the last of the Discords out of the lower seams, Mingting’s striking the Hold off the border roll,” Little Tan said, looking straight at Liangyu’s bruised wrist. “They’re saying the wall’s done its job and they’re letting the whole rock come down into the river. Is it true?”

Up on the broad stone cap of the gatehouse wall twenty feet overhead, Ruoyun stopped writing in her leather ledger and looked down over the rims of her round brass spectacles.

At the shady eastern edge of the yard, where the frost was deepest under the storehouse gable, Qingxiao stood alone beside the weapons rack with her hands tucked inside her moon-white cuffs. Beside her stood Yangyang in her warm wool cloak, her sword resting in its scabbard at her hip.

Down in the gravel, Liangyu looked at Little Tan for three breaths. She didn’t glance up at the dark, unmoving sphere of the Astrolabe Core hanging over the mist, and she didn’t look back at the Jinzhou pikes.

She reached out with her newly unsplinted left hand and rapped her knuckles twice against the three-foot granite block of the inner gate pier.

“Does that stone look like it’s going anywhere before dinner, Tan?” Liangyu asked plainly.

“No, ma’am, but—”

“A roof isn’t finished because the rain stops for an afternoon,” Liangyu said, her voice clear and flat across the cold yard. “My family carried mortar up these steps in wicker baskets when the first wall went up, and old Ge’s father re-laid this flagstone the winter the frost split the drain. Until the Sentinel comes down the pagoda stairs and tells me with her own mouth to lock the gate from the outside, we keep the watch. Set your back heel.”

Little Tan swallowed once, nodded, and brought his spear back up to the guard position.

Liangyu stepped back to the head of the file, raised her own ash shaft, and drove it forward into the long lunge of the fifth mountain form—putting her whole shoulder behind the point so the iron head rang clear and true in the frosty air. As her weight settled onto her front knee, her bruised left forearm dipped a fraction of an inch to spare the tender bone, and her left thumb crept up over the iron collar below the spearhead.

From the shadow under the storehouse gable, Qingxiao spoke once in her level, unornamented voice:

“Thumb off the iron collar.”

Five words.

Liangyu froze at the bottom of the lunge.

She looked down at her own left thumb resting on the cold iron ferrule—the exact place she had put it the first summer she climbed the snowpeak at twelve years old and had a pine stick rapped across her knuckles until dusk.

A sudden, crooked grin broke across Liangyu’s tired face, crinkling the dark hollows under her eyes. She slid her thumb two inches back down onto the seasoned ash wood without losing her stance.

“Yes, Paragon!” Liangyu called back, and brought the file cleanly through the recovery beat.

Up on the gatehouse parapet, Ruoyun snorted loud enough to be heard across the gravel, tapping her bamboo brush against the heel of her Mary Jane shoe.

“Oh, wonderful, she gives five words of free tuition from the shade and thinks she’s earned her lunch!” Ruoyun heckled down from the wall, her small golden horns glinting in the pale sun. “Tell the third boy from the left to pick his feet up, Liangyu, he’s dragging his heels through my gravel like a hobbled mule! I just raked that gutter yesterday!”

By the weapons rack, Qingxiao did not look up at the parapet.

When she breathed out through her nose, a long, pale plume of white steam drifted across her chin in the freezing air. The canyon draft came round the corner of the cistern shed, stirring the loose blue hair over her shoulders, and almost imperceptibly Qingxiao’s fingers curled deeper into the warm lining of her cuffs, her shoulders drawing together by the width of a thread against the bite of the frost.

Two paces to her left, Yangyang stood with her boots planted square in the white rime.

The drill spears clacked across the yard—*one, two, thrust*.

Yangyang looked out toward the open gulf of the southern pass, where the cold air came straight off the high snowfields. Remembering the neat, upright columns of Baizhi’s letter in her pocket, she let her shoulders drop and drew in a single, slow breath through her nose—not the shallow, careful sips she had been taking all week to spare the linen wrap under her tunic, but all the way down to the bottom of her ribs, filling her lungs with the sharp, icy mountain air until her breastbone lifted against her collar.

She held it for two heartbeats.

The sharp, tearing stitch that had caught under her seventh rib on Monday wasn’t there. Only at the very top of the breath, right where the cartilage met the sternum, a tiny, dull thread of pressure tugged once and let go as she breathed out in a white cloud.

Along the line of her jaw, the small cerulean feathers of her collar lifted briefly in the cold and settled smooth.

Without drawing her steel from the scabbard, Yangyang raised her right arm in her sheer, cloud-worked sleeve and moved her empty hand through the slow, rising guard-line of the first Mingting sword-form—turning her wrist at the shoulder-height transition where, for eight years, her right elbow had always drifted half an inch outward before the cut.

Her elbow did not drift. It tucked clean and level along the line of her ribs, stopping at the exact half-inch mark someone else’s cold fingers had tapped into the back of her sleeve every night for a year in the dark.

Beside the weapons rack, Qingxiao turned her head.

Her pale blue eyes rested on the exact angle of Yangyang’s right elbow until Yangyang lowered her hand to her side.

“Who corrected that?” Qingxiao asked.

Yangyang looked down once at her white-tipped cuff, and then up at the Sword Transcendent.

“My shijie,” Yangyang said.

Qingxiao looked at the empty air behind Yangyang’s right shoulder for the space of a breath, where the frost sparkled thin in the morning light, and nodded once.

“Good teacher,” Qingxiao said.

At high noon, the sound of brass cymbals and a pair of reed *suona* came echoing down the northern cliff-cut from the Mengzhou post-road.

It was not the creak of mud-caked freight drays and blown mountain mules that had come up from Jinzhou four mornings back.

When the north gate leaves swung open, the column that rolled onto the wet flagstones of Huizhen Plaza gleamed with red-and-black lacquer. Four sleek, brush-coated dray horses led the line with braided crimson wool tassels swinging from their headstalls and not a speck of gorge mud above their fetlocks. Behind them marched six hired guild musicians in matching green-piped vests, blowing a bright, shrill ceremonial welcome on two *suona* while a boy beat a pair of flat brass gongs at every tenth step.

There were six carts in all.

The first two were stacked high with bundled bamboo poles, bolts of red festival silk, and three hundred pleated paper Waking Moon lanterns painted in gold leaf with clouds and auspicious bats, while open crates of long sandalwood incense sticks trailed a heavy, perfumy cloud of unlit spice across the smell of the Hold’s coal-fires.

On the third cart, propped upright on a padded wooden easel covered in yellow brocade and held steady by four city runners in clean white gaiters, rode an eight-foot ceremonial gate-plaque of carved camphor-wood, its four huge recessed characters blazing with fresh gold leaf in the noon light:

*MERIT THROUGH A THOUSAND AUTUMNS.*

Behind the plaque rolled three flat-bed grain wagons loaded with government-stamped hemp sacks—narrower in the beam than Jinzhou’s fat polder-bags, the coarse sacking faded dusty grey from a year in a municipal reserve granary and smelling faintly of dry chaff and old lime.

At the head of the horses stepped a Mengzhou deputy secretary in a spotless damson-silk gown with a bronze magpie badge pinned to his breast, accompanied by a sharp-nosed treasury clerk carrying a brass-bound receipt ledger and an inked chop-pad on a lacquered tray.

Half the Hold had come out onto the stone risers and the market balconies to watch the music. Nobody cheered.

As the third cart rumbled past the well-head and the four gilded characters on the great wooden plaque turned toward the terraced steps, the murmur along the cabbage stalls died down to the scrape of boots on wet stone.

By the water-stair bollard, old Warden Ge stopped with a coil of hemp rope on his shoulder. He looked up at *Merit Through a Thousand Autumns*—the exact four words carved into the granite cenotaph behind the garrison burial ground where the dead of the Gorges War lay under the persimmon trees—and his weathered face went as flat and wooden as a chopping block. Ten feet behind him, the freckled young Warden from the morning drill turned his head and spat once into the drainage gutter.

The musicians finished their flourish with a crash of brass cymbals.

The deputy secretary stepped forward to the dry flagstone in front of Warden Liangyu, drew a roll of stiff yellow silk-paper from his sleeve, and cleared his throat.

“To the acting garrison and inhabitants of Xuanfang Hold,” the deputy secretary read aloud in a round, practiced hall-voice that bounced off the stone eaves. “Whereas the Magistrate of Mengzhou is presently indisposed with seasonal chill and unable to ascend the pass in person before the Waking Moon, the municipal yamen sends greetings and provision.”

Up on the storehouse balcony rail, Lupa leaned both elbows on the timber balustrade with a half-gnawed pork rib in her hand, her wolf ears swiveling forward as her eyes ran down the deputy secretary’s clean silk hem and back to the three light wagons of grey-sacked millet.

“Look at those grain bags,” Lupa muttered out of the side of her mouth to Chisa. “You could throw one of those over the barn roof with one hand. That’s bottom-bin sweepings from two summers ago, I know warehouse dust when I smell it.”

Down in the plaza, the deputy secretary unrolled another six inches of yellow silk.

“For four hundred winters,” he read smoothly, “Xuanfang Hold *has stood* as the southern shield of the prefecture. Its Wardens *have kept* the gorge gates without complaint; its machinery *has turned* the mountain mist aside; its service to the province *was* without blemish and *shall be* recorded in the prefectural annals in perpetuity. In token whereof, the city of Mengzhou bestows this tablet of honour for the gatehouse arch, together with forty piculs of relief grain and three hundred lanterns for the festival eve.”

He rolled the scroll shut with a neat snap of his wrist and stepped half a pace back so the sharp-nosed clerk could thrust the brass-bound receipt ledger and the red ink-pad under Liangyu’s chin.

“Forty sacks grain, one plaque, twelve crates festival sundry,” the clerk rattled off, tapping the page with an ivory stylus. “Chop on the bottom line and initial the tare-weight, Warden, the horses have to make the lower relay before dark.”

For two seconds the plaza was so quiet you could hear the tallow dripping inside the rice-cake steamer across the lane.

Beside the well-curb, Iuno stood with her wool coat buttoned to the chin and her right hand resting in her pocket. Her gaze moved once from the deputy secretary’s spotless shoes to the four men in plain Ministry grey watching silently from the quartermaster’s porch up the stair.

*They brought a tombstone and six flutes, darling,* the High Priestess remarked inside her head, cold and razor-dry. *And they didn’t even pay for fresh millet to pack around it.*

“When a city hall sends musicians to deliver an obituary,” Iuno said quietly to Mei and Yangyang beside her, “it is because someone in the building is very anxious to prove they were at the funeral.”

Down on the flagstones, Liangyu did not flush, and she did not look back at her men.

She unhooked the heavy iron garrison seal from her belt with her uninjured right hand, pressed it squarely into the clerk’s red cinnabar pad, and brought it down on the bottom of the ledger page with a single hard *thump* that made the lacquered tray jump in the clerk’s fingers.

“Received,” Liangyu said in her plain, level voice. “Warden Ge, take four men and put the Mengzhou millet in the back bin behind the Jinzhou rice. Keep the sacks separate so the dry chaff doesn’t get into the new grain.”

“Right away, Warden,” Ge said, his face still carved out of oak as he stepped past the deputy secretary’s elbow without bowing.

While the city runners began unlashing the gilded plaque from its padded easel by the gatehouse arch, Buling strolled casually out of the shadow of the rice-cake stall with her hands behind her back, her yellow paper talisman fluttering against the bridge of her nose.

The moment the runners set the bottom edge of the eight-foot tablet down on two wooden blocks to shift their grip, Buling leaned in, hooked two fingernails under the lower scroll-carving, and gave the gilded wood a sharp, practiced flick with her thumb.

*Tock.*

It gave a hollow, high-pitched rattle. Buling leaned closer, scraped a tiny curl of gold leaf off the bottom bevel with her thumbnail, and pinched it between her fingers.

“Aiya,” Buling muttered to nobody in particular. “White poplar core with a quarter-inch camphor veneer, glued with fish-paste. Come the first wet snow in the eleventh month, the glue is going to swell and all four characters will peel off into the ditch like snakeskin. Thin leaf also—not even three-hammer gold. If you melt the whole sign down in a crucible, cannot even buy two bowls of mutton soup.”

“Hey! Hands off the municipal property, girl!” the treasury clerk snapped, hurrying over with his ledger.

Buling was already three paces away, wiping her thumb on her blue silk hip and strolling toward the tail-cart.

Across the cobbles, the six hired musicians—who had walked the last two miles up the steep switchbacks blowing brass horns in the cold—drifted toward the steaming cauldrons outside the eating-house, wiping their mouths on their green-piped sleeves.

“How much for a bowl of the carp dumplings, miss?” the lead *suona* player asked, leaning on the counter.

The Fillet Fairmaiden looked at his Mengzhou city vest, looked over his shoulder at the gilded plaque by the gate, and didn’t even reach for her chalk.

“Two coppers for garrison,” the Fairmaiden said brightly, resting both fists on her apron, “and twelve silvers a bowl for city musicians in green vests! Festival surcharge, boys! Ember, Stoke, ladle up the small dumplings with the extra pepper!”

“Twelve silvers for river carp?!”

“You’ve got a Thousand Autumns of Merit on your cart, friend, you can afford the broth! Pay at the barrel before you sit!”

At the very rear of the third grain wagon, where the tail-board hung down on its chains, the driver reached into the boot beneath his seat and tossed one last item down onto the wet cobbles before unhitching his trace-chains.

It was a small, half-empty canvas mail pouch, stained down one side with bilge-water from the river locks.

Pasted across its cord-lock were four overlapping white inspection strips from the Mingting Internal Security Agency, and stamped over top of them in faded purple magistrate’s ink was a box-chop that read: *HELD AT MENGZHOU LOCKS — PENDING SECURITY CLEARANCE.*

Yangyang was already walking across the flagstones before the sack stopped rolling.

She knelt on one knee by the wheel, broke the brittle red wax and the four layers of gummed censor’s paper with her belt-knife, and reached inside.

Beneath a handful of old merchant circulars lay a single, compact bundle of four letters, tied together with a double strand of grey silk sewing-thread—the exact grey thread from the spool in Suisui’s basket—and numbered *One* through *Four* in the upper right corners in Suisui’s clean, rapid hand.

In the first hour after noon, the sun slid past the southern gable of the gatehouse and left the upper watch gallery in deep cedar shadow.

Down in the gravel yard thirty feet below the parapet, the Mengzhou musicians had gone in search of broth and the grain carts were being backed toward the storehouse ramps, but the gate pier was not empty. One of the four men in plain Ministry grey coats was kneeling in the wet shale at the base of the inner arch with a brass plumb-bob in his palm. Ten paces along the wall, his partner held the brass zero-ring of a waxed-hemp measuring cord flat against the bottom course of black basalt—close enough to the timber stairwell that an ordinary voice under the gallery eaves would have dropped straight down into his hat.

Up on the gallery, Qingxiao sat on the inner pine bench with the seven-stringed paulownia qin resting across her knees.

She had folded the camphor-scented indigo oilcloth into a neat square beside her hip. With her bare right thumb and middle finger, she plucked the open third and sixth strings together, then stopped the fourth with the side of her left thumb.

A brisk, steady mountain practice-air rose under the rafters. Along the outer stone coping of the parapet, where the canyon breeze met the eaves, a hair-thin thread of moon-white Sword Intent lay stretched from pillar to pillar like a silk weir-line; down in the yard, only the clear, unhurried notes of the seven strings drifted over the stone, while inside the gallery their voices stayed shut beneath the roof.

On the broad stone cap of the corner embrasure, Ruoyun sat cross-legged with her pleated teal skirt tucked around her shins and her round brass spectacles pushed up on her nose, glaring down over the wall at the crown of the surveyor’s felt hat.

Three paces away, Buling was perched on the oak sand-chest beside the watch-board, her black-bound ankles swinging an inch above the floorboards as she used the corner of a flat brass ear-pick to scrape the tiny curl of Mengzhou gold leaf out from under her right thumbnail.

“See,” Buling murmured under the run of the third string, flicking the speck of thin gold onto her royal-blue knee. “Not even real leaf. Half brass. You ask both of us up here before lunch is even digested, Shifu, people down in the lane going to think Ruoyun caught me stealing her knife again.”

“If you’d taken my knife again,” Ruoyun said without taking her eyes off the grey coat below, “you’d be headfirst in the quicklime pit by now, not sitting on my sand-box getting city trash all over the floorboards.”

Qingxiao’s left hand slid two positions up the lacquered wood, drawing a low harmonic that covered the clink of the surveyor’s plumb-bob down in the arch.

“The Fox is not in the Sanctum,” Qingxiao said in her level voice.

On the sand-chest, Buling stopped swinging her heels. She tucked the brass ear-pick back into the seam of her *nuanmao* and let the yellow paper talisman drop down over the bridge of her nose.

“The jade doors at the top of the cliff have had the inside bar down since before the rain,” Ruoyun said quietly, resting one ruffled white cuff on her knee. “And the overflow pipes under the third terrace have been running cold all week. Not a bubble of warm water from the lower cistern.”

“The main vein under the plaza is quiet too,” Buling said. “Just now when those Mengzhou horn-blowers were making all that noise by the well, I checked the ground under the flagstones with my shoe. The big array isn’t cracked, it’s just sitting there in neutral. Like somebody pulled the king-pin out of the wagon and walked off with it in her pocket.”

Qingxiao plucked a three-note turn on the first string, her pale blue eyes resting on the dark, unmoving sphere of the Astrolabe Core framed between the cedar eaves and the karst spires.

“I mean to look,” Qingxiao said. “The bolted doors. The Sanctum where Muyu hung his looms. The deep conduits under the rock, and whatever the men in grey are measuring.”

Ruoyun’s chin came up. She looked from Qingxiao’s face to the stairwell, and then back down into the yard where the surveyor was chalking a tiny white mark on the mortar-joint.

“Not together, we aren’t,” Ruoyun said briskly. “There’s a full company of Jinzhou pikes sleeping in the south granary, four of the Ministry’s bloodhounds tapping hammers on every arch from the cellar to the water-gate, and the lock-clerks reading the post down at the river. If the three of us go strolling up the sanctuary steps in a row, the Ministry will have a courier on a fast mule before the second bell.”

“Three old faces walking together is a parade,” Buling agreed, holding up three fingers beside her talisman. “Parade very bad for business. We split up lor. No schedule, no meeting on the roof—whoever finds something comes by the watch-board when the lane is clear.”

Ruoyun tapped the leather cover of the gatehouse logbook beside her hip. “I’ll take the cellar-cuts and the gearwell drains under the cliff. I’ve got the sluice-keys on my belt anyway, and none of these capital clerks is going to crawl into a three-foot grease-culvert behind the woman with the muck-rake.”

“And I take Huizhen Plaza and the grey coats,” Buling said, slapping both palms lightly on her knees. “Very natural. City bureaucrats love a feng shui reading when their boss isn’t looking. I tell them their office bed is facing the white tiger, they tell me which drawer they keep the keys in.”

Ruoyun turned her head and fixed Buling with a flat glare through her brass lenses. “Try not to charge them five silvers for a fake paper strip and get yourself thrown in the provost cart before supper.”

“My talismans are top quality, where got fake!” Buling bristled, sitting up straight on the sand-chest. “Better than your sour face—you go ask a clerk what time it is, he thinks you’re going to hit him with an abacus.”

“If he’s standing on my clean mortar, I will.”

“I will take the upper rock and the high eaves,” Qingxiao said over the steady four-beat hum of the bass string. Where the Xuan Paragon walked on the cliff-wall and the pagoda roofs, no pikeman asked for a pass.

She shifted her right wrist to cross the middle strings, glanced once at the round winter hat on Buling’s head, and added in the exact same level tone:

“Charge them ten.”

Buling’s red eyes lit up behind her yellow paper strip. “See! Shifu understands market value!”

Ruoyun let out a long, suffering groan into her ruffled sleeve and adjusted her spectacles with one knuckle. Then her hand paused on the brass frame, and she looked back across the planks at Qingxiao.

“What about Suoming?” Ruoyun asked, her voice dropping a notch. “I saw her coming up the steps from the lower weir in the rain before dawn with red mud to her buckles.”

Qingxiao’s left thumb slid down the silk winding of the seventh string, letting the note drop into the low register of the paulownia wood.

“Suoming walks her own road,” Qingxiao said. “Leave her be.”

Down at the base of the gate pier, the Ministry surveyor stood up from the gravel, dusted the knees of his grey trousers with his palm, and wound the waxed-hemp measuring cord around his elbow in neat, even figure-eights before walking off up the water-stair behind his partner.

Up on the pine bench, Qingxiao laid her bare right palm flat across all seven strings at once, and the hum died out into the dry wood.

***

The afternoon sun hit the stone landing outside the third storehouse at a low, pale slant, warming the dry planks by the wooden turbine.

Yangyang sat alone on the top step with her knees drawn up under her cloak and the grey silk thread looped over her wrist, reading the first three letters in the order they had been posted on the Wednesday coaches.

Each envelope had been slit open by the Mengzhou censors during the weeks the road was closed and pasted shut again with white gummed tape. Suisui had known they would be; there wasn’t a single blotted character or unguarded name from top to bottom, and every line sounded exactly like her voice coming through the one-inch gap of the storehouse door.

Yangyang unfolded the first sheet—written on thick Zhaoming Guild bond the night Suisui reached the river—and read it quietly to herself in the sun:

> *First Wednesday. Four bells after noon.*
>
> *The hot stone Chisa wrapped in the flannel stayed warm all the way to the Halfway Pine relay, and I used Lupa’s smoked ham to wedge the left window-frame shut when the latch broke in the pass, so tell her it served a structural purpose before the clerks ate it for supper.*
>
> *Little Zheng met the coach at the south lock with three ledgers under his arm and ink on his nose. The Huai syndicate had four attorneys sitting in our front parlour eating the guest pistachios and waiting to see whether I had perished in the fog. I took my cloak off, asked Master Huai after his gout, and called in the mortgage on his second salt-barge before the tea was poured. The northern townships will have their barley before the ice forms.*
>
> *Do not take your linen wrap off early just because it itches under your arms, Yangyang. I know how you wiggle your shoulders when a bandage is tight. Tell Wen I left three silvers under the tea-caddy for the osmanthus tin.*

Yangyang’s mouth tipped up at the corner. She folded *One*, laid it on her knee, and opened *Two* and *Three*:

> *Second Wednesday.*
>
> *The post-road to the Hold is still closed at the lower ferry by Ministry order, so this will sit in the lock-master’s basket until the water clears, which means you are going to read three weeks of my nagging in a single afternoon. Good. It will save me repeating myself.*
>
> *I sent the four photographic prints down to Mingting by our own silk-barge courier so the censors wouldn’t smudge them with their thumbs. Mother wrote back by return pigeon. As predicted, she spent two pages lamenting that you look thin in the cheeks, and then ordered Father to have the fourth print framed in rosewood for the front hall so Second Aunt has to look at my forehead mark next to yours every time she comes to borrow the silver spoons.*
>
> *Tell Dumpling that if he lets you walk down the cistern steps in the dark without a lamp, I will replace his main-spring with a turnip when I see him.*

> *Third Wednesday.*
>
> *Ciaccona will be pleased to hear that the travelling opera company at the South Canal playhouse performed The Peach-Blossom Ford on Saturday evening, and the soprano took the fourth bar down on the river-turn instead of up. I sat in the second box and sang the correct interval loud enough for the pit-flute to hear me. Little Zheng slid down in his chair until his ears were below the rail. Youth has no spine.*
>
> *If Lupa has let Mei lift a single water-bucket while my back was turned, tell her I am withholding the Mengzhou winter-pepper shipment until the twelfth month.*

Yangyang set the first three envelopes together in her lap.

Then she picked up the fourth.

It was twice as thick as the others, written on plain, uncrested mulberry paper and dated two mornings ago, the day the Mengzhou city convoy was finally cleared to leave the north gate. Across the top of the inner leaf, Suisui had written: *For Yangyang, and for the table.*

Yangyang stood up from the step, pushed the heavy timber door open with her shoulder, and walked into the warm storehouse.

Everyone was inside. Mei was sitting on the bench—not on the floor rug today, propped upright on the pine plank with her back straight and both booted feet planted proudly on the floorboards—scraping the last of the noon walnut paste out of a bowl with her finger. Lupa was wiping down the butcher’s block; Iuno and Ciaccona were at the window-end of the board; Amy and Chisa sat side by side sorting brass screws into an empty biscuit tin; and Cartethyia was carefully watering her camellia cutting with a porcelain soup-spoon.

“The last one is from two mornings ago,” Yangyang said, taking her seat in the middle of the bench. “She headed it for all of us.”

Lupa tossed her rag into the basin and leaned against the chimney-breast. Down the table, Amy stopped rattling the brass screws.

Yangyang smoothed the wide mulberry sheets flat on the pine wood beside the teapot and read aloud in her clear, even voice:

> *Fourth Wednesday—sent by the municipal cart.*
>
> *My dears,*
>
> *Since this packet has spent three weeks keeping company with the lock-master’s mice and the gentlemen of the inspection office—to whom I wish a very pleasant afternoon and a warmer brazier than they had in the ninth month—I shall keep to household matters.*
>
> *When the mountain road stayed shut through the second week and no word came down from the Hold, our Guild board decided it would be unneighbourly to let our sister branch go short of winter coal. I therefore had twelve of our big flat-bottomed river barges brought round to the main municipal wharf right below the yamen windows, hung seven-foot red banners down their sides reading ZHAOMING GUILD PRIVATE RELIEF FOR XUANFANG HOLD, and spent three days loading them with grain, salt-pork, and timber by torchlight while half the city stood on the bridge eating fried dough-twists and watching.*
>
> *It was remarkable how quickly city hall remembered its affection for the mountain. Within forty-eight hours of our third barge tying up, the deputy secretary discovered that the yamen had been preparing a ceremonial relief convoy of its own all month. After all, with the Jinzhou wagons already on the south road and the Ministry’s auditors taking tea in the upper rooms, it would have looked terribly untidy in the annals for the Sentinel’s own city to arrive after a southern garrison and a merchant guild. You will have seen by now how busy they have been. Here in the north, the clerks are very fond of wrapping the abacus in three layers of padded silk so the neighbours cannot hear the beads click; I have found it much healthier for the market to take the silk off on the front steps and count the beans in the sun.*

At the stove, Lupa let out a short, appreciative bark of laughter. “Ha! She shamed the bastards into hitching the mules! Good for the clerk!”

Yangyang turned the first page over and kept reading:

> *You will have heard from the deputy secretary that Magistrate Dou was unable to come up the pass on account of a seasonal chill. In truth, nobody at the south market has seen her at the hawthorn stand beside the Row since the week the fog came down, nor has her sedan-chair crossed the yamen courtyard since the fifteenth. When I called at the chancery to have our barge-manifests stamped, her first clerk met me at the outer screen with his sleeves buttoned and told me the Magistrate is resting behind closed shutters and taking no soup. I know Dou’s handwriting on a tax-warrant as well as I know my own; the signature on this week’s lock-orders was written by a hand that had to stop twice in the middle of the second stroke to rest the brush—*

Yangyang’s voice paused for a beat.

Right there in the middle of the column, Suisui’s brush had stopped dead, leaving a neat, round dot of black ink on the mulberry fiber where the point had rested for two seconds before moving cleanly to a new line without finishing the sentence:

> *—which is, of course, entirely natural in a woman of her years during a wet autumn.*
>
> *We have other guests in town as well. On Thursday the river-packet from the capital brought Minister Shen Qiu of the Ministry of Sentinel Affairs in person—the first time a sitting Minister of that board has come north of the river in my lifetime. He did not stay at the yamen guest-hall. He brought four clerks, a sealed portfolio from the palace, and a secondhand camphor-wood document case which two porters carried up the steps on poles like a sedan-chair. He has taken the quiet courtyard directly below the Sentinel’s empty terrace, and yesterday morning he sent a formal note across to city hall asking for the duplicate iron keys to the lower archive and the municipal conduit-vaults. The four quiet gentlemen in grey coats who came up your south stair with the Jinzhou carts belong to his fourth clerk.*
>
> *Little Zheng has a cousin who sweeps the noodle stall behind the Sentinel Affairs annex. Over a bowl of mutton broth on Sunday, the junior archivist who carries the Minister’s ink-box complained that his feet were blistered from walking the vault corridors before they left the capital, because the third file in the main cabinet—the one the Sentinel of Mengzhou has annotated in her own brush every spring since the plum tree was planted—has had no new line added to its margin this year, for the first time since the founding. When Little Zheng’s cousin asked if the fox had lost her brush, the boy turned white to the ears, paid for his noodles without finishing the meat, and said he had never been in the shop.*

In the storehouse, the small crackling of the birch logs in the stove sounded very loud.

At the end of the bench, Buling had her chin propped in one palm below her round winter hat. She didn’t make a joke about the Minister or the noodles; her red eyes stayed fixed on the knot in the pine plank in front of her cup.

Yangyang turned to the last leaf:

> *One small errand of our own. On Monday afternoon, after the lock-office closed, I took the paper ink-rubbing I made five winters ago—from the inside of the little sea-shell Mother left with me to keep for you—and walked down to Foundry Lane behind the old copper-market. Only one shop from the old hollow-art guild still has its furnace lit: Master Ouyang’s, behind the mulberry wall. He is eighty-four and nearly blind in the left eye.*
>
> *I laid the rubbing of the eleven characters on his bench under the magnifying glass and did not tell him where it came from. He looked at the cut of the strokes for a very long time. Then he took his spectacles off, asked me whether the two shells themselves were still unbroken, and when I told him they were riding in my sister’s pocket, he walked to the front door, took his wooden shop-sign off its hooks, and bolted the shutters for the afternoon. He would not write a single word down on paper while the city post is being read at the gates. He told me only that when the girl carrying the shells reaches Mengzhou, she is to knock three times on the back alley door behind the water-trough after the fifth bell.*
>
> *The Guild house on Willow Wharf has four spare bedrooms on the south side with double brick flues, and I have already had the cook lay in three jars of the mild sweet-bean paste so Ciaccona and Iuno do not perish at the first meal. As soon as the Waking Moon has passed and Mei can sit a post-cart without Lupa threatening the driver’s life, come down the north road, all of you. I have enough ledgers here to keep ten people out of trouble, and I would very much like to see my sister’s face.*
>
> *— Suisui.*

Yangyang lowered the mulberry sheets to the table.

As she folded the last page back along its crease, a tiny, two-inch slip of stiff ledger-edge paper—unmarked by the censors because it had been tucked inside the double fold of the bottom hem—slid out onto the wood beside the teapot.

*For Iuno,* it read on the outside.

Yangyang passed it across the board.

Iuno unfolded the narrow strip with one finger. Inside, in Suisui’s crispest, smallest hand, was a single line:

> *I am still waiting for my Wednesday letter, Iuno. Do not make me come back up that mountain with an abacus.*

Iuno’s mouth twitched by the fraction of an inch. Across the corner of the table, Mei caught the angle of the paper slip, her golden eyes warming at once in the afternoon light, and nobody said a word as Iuno slid the slip into the deep inner pocket of her wool coat where the velvet box sat.

In the middle of the bench, Yangyang sat quiet over the folded packet.

Slowly, her right hand lifted and pressed flat against the center of her breastbone, right where the two small sea-shells wrapped in one square of linen rode warm against her skin beneath her tunic. Along her jaw, the cerulean feathers of her collar lifted once and settled back down.

***

By the fifth bell of the afternoon, the shadow of the western karst ridge had crept halfway across Huizhen Plaza, turning the wet bluestone paving the colour of cold pewter.

Beside the well-head, where the Hold’s cedar notice-board stood under its narrow tile hood, a fresh sheet of heavy white government paper had been pasted squarely over the old autumn militia rosters, the boiled flour-paste still damp and translucent around the edges.

Beneath the bold block-cut heading of the *Ministry of Sentinel Affairs*, two columns of crisp black lead type announced that by the Sentinel’s authority, the Ministry had undertaken the structural inspection and repair of the Astrolabe Core and its supporting piers, and that all garrison personnel and residents were directed to grant its surveyors free passage through the tiers.

A cooper’s wife with a basket of salted turnips on her hip and two off-duty Jinzhou pikemen stopped to read it, squinted once up at the dark, unmoving sphere of the Core hanging in the mist, and walked on toward the eating-house braziers.

Buling stood three paces back from the well-curb with her hands tucked behind the waist of her royal-blue silk cheongsam.

Her red eyes moved once down the damp sheet to the bottom left corner, where the words *BY SEAL AND WARRANT* sat beside a two-inch square of bare, unprinted white paper.

She turned on her heel and strolled twenty paces down the stepped cobble alley behind the cooper’s shed, where a small, soot-blackened wooden sign carved in the shape of a printer’s composing stick and three lead sorts hung creaking from an iron bracket: the Xuanfang branch of the old founders’ and bookbinders’ guild.

Inside, the low-ceilinged room was warm as a bakehouse and smelled sharply of hot antimony-lead from the small brick casting pot in the back corner, boiled linseed oil, and wet pine-soot ink. Along the lime-washed walls stood sloped wooden racks of upper- and lower-case type trays. By the stone wash-trough, a fourteen-year-old apprentice with black ink ground up to his elbows was scrubbing a locked iron forme with a stiff hog-bristle brush and lye-water. Under the front window, at the heavy slate imposing stone, stood Master Shao, who had cast and set the Hold’s type for fifty winters, picking twelve-point spacing quads out of a wooden galley with brass tweezers, old ink settled deep into every crease of his knuckles.

Buling stepped over the high timber threshold, nudged a crooked three-legged stool two inches to the left with her black shoe so the legs wouldn’t point at the stove door, and pulled a thick, dog-eared wad of scrap paper out of her side slit—forty pages of forum arguments she had been writing out by hand in the evenings with no relay signal to post them to.

“Boss,” Buling said, slapping the bundle down on the clean corner of the slate. “Mountain signal dead until my hair turns white. If I don’t publish my treatise on why the central plains land-tax thread is full of idiots, my readers down in Jinzhou going to think I lost the argument. Two hundred pamphlets, quarto fold, cheap bamboo paper. Give me neighbour rate.”

Old Shao didn’t put his brass tweezers down. He lifted the top corner of her manuscript with a calloused thumb, squinted at her cramped brushwork, and snorted.

“Neighbour rate is for people who pay their winter coal levy, miss,” Shao said. “Look at all these marginal curses—that’s eight-point side-notes in two columns. Fifteen silvers the hundred, and you pay for the paper before I ink the ball.”

“Fifteen silvers!” Buling planted both palms on the edge of the imposing stone, flipping her yellow forehead talisman back onto the round brim of her *nuanmao*. “For coarse bamboo sheet? In Bookbinders’ Row down in Jinzhou, Old Chen sets two hundred sheets for eight silvers and throws in a blue paper wrapper! This shop is the same guild, why your lead twice as heavy?”

“Old Chen doesn’t have to haul his pig-lead up four miles of rock stairs on a mule’s back,” Shao said, though the weathered creases around his eyes twitched the way an old tradesman’s do when a customer knows the Row’s rates. He went back to dropping spacers into his wooden box—*clink, clink, clink*. “And if you want it before the festival, you’re out of luck anyway. My cases are half-empty and the boy’s been on the lye-brush since dawn. The grey coats from down the stair took the press for two nights running.”

Buling leaned one hip comfortably against the timber frame of the stone, reaching into a discard box of cold lead shavings to roll a bent quadrat between her thumb and forefinger.

“Ah, I saw your big notice by the well just now,” Buling said lightly. “Very official. ‘Repairing the Astrolabe Core.’ Good business for you lor—Ministry always pays on the nail.”

Shao grunted, lifting a wooden composing stick to thumb out a line of furniture. “Paid in new Mingting silver before the ink was dry. Stood right there by the stove with their hats on until we pulled the last sheet off the tympan, then took the spoiled proof-sheets out in a canvas sack.”

He tapped the nose of his brass tweezers once against the lower left corner of the empty iron chase lying on the slate.

“Fifty years I’ve locked up the Hold’s proclamations in that frame, miss,” Shao said, his voice staying low under the steady *scritch-scritch* of the apprentice’s lye-brush. “Every winter muster, every sluice-closing, every Waking Moon sheet since the old Commander’s time. You know what goes in the bottom left pocket of the chase?”

“The Nine-Tail block,” Buling said. “Two-inch pear-wood cut.”

“Right under the counter in the oiled drawer,” Shao said, dropping another lead sort into its compartment. “I reached down for the wood-cut when I was wedging the foot-sticks two nights back, and the tall clerk in the grey coat put his palm flat against the drawer front. Told me to pack the two-inch box with blank quads and pull it empty. Said the validation seal would be stamped at the chancery before posting.”

Shao’s tweezers paused over the tray.

“That sheet’s been pasted on the well-board since the fourth bell,” the old compositor said to his galley. “Nobody’s come down the steps with red cinnabar to fill that square yet.”

Over by the wash-trough, the apprentice lifted the scrubbed iron forme out of the lye-water and leaned it upright against the bricks to drain.

“Maybe the clerk left his chop-box on the mule cart,” Buling said easily. “Capital people very careless when the mountain gets cold.”

“They weren’t careless with the second job,” Shao muttered, picking up a greased rag to wipe the antimony dust from his thumbs. “Kept me and the boy setting by four tallow lamps from midnight till the morning drum. No poster type on that one. All small tabular work—six-point brass hair-rules and column headers across double-foolscap ledger bond. Three hundred sheets.”

He tossed the rag onto the edge of the wooden press-bed.

“First column for the serial marks on the four hundred bronze guardians,” Shao said quietly. “Second column for the Skyworks looms and the sluice-engines. Third column for every living name on the garrison and village rolls. And a fourth column right beside it—set it with my own fingers in eight-point clerk’s script—headed *Retained Celestials*.”

Buling’s fingers stopped rolling the bent piece of lead in the discard box.

Her hand stayed resting on the wooden rim of the tray, and beneath the round brim of her hat, her red eyes did not blink.

“Then one wide column down the right-hand margin,” the old compositor went on, looking down at the black ink ground into the skin of his knuckles, “with a single word across the top: *Disposition*. Paid me three extra silvers to distribute the type back into the cases before the shutters came down and forget I ever pulled the bar.”

Shao picked up his brass tweezers again and laid them neatly in the groove of the galley.

“I worked five winters in the south river-ports before I came up the pass,” Shao said in a plain, conversational voice. “Set plenty of double-foolscap schedules like that for the bailiffs when an old silk-house went under and the master was cold in the back room. That’s the sheet they bring in when they’re walking through the courtyards with a chalk-stick, writing down which loom goes on the river-barge, which mule gets sold at the gate, and which servant gets paid off at the crossroads. Never seen a man print an estate-clearing sheet to mend a clock.”

In the back corner of the shop, the molten lead in the little iron casting pot gave a soft *plop* under its skin of grey dross.

For the space of two breaths Buling stood still beside the imposing stone.

Slowly, she took her fingers out of the discard box, reached into the cloth purse at her sash, and drew out a heavy ten-silver Jinzhou bar and five single silver pieces—the full fifteen silvers Shao had named at the start. She stacked them in a single neat column on the grey slate beside her manuscript.

Shao looked down at the silver, then blinked up at her over the type-cases. “Hold on, I thought you said eight—”

“Fifteen is fine,” Buling said. Her voice came out flat and quiet, with all the market bounce gone out of it. “You print when the boy finishes cleaning. I pick up tomorrow.”

She turned and walked out of the shop, catching the timber door with two fingers so the iron latch clicked shut without a rattle.

Outside in Huizhen Plaza, the sun had dropped behind the western crags, leaving the wet flagstones dark in the rising dusk. Most of the marketing crowd had gone inside toward the supper fires; only the rice-cake baker was banking his charcoal drum with wet ash for the night.

Buling walked across the cobbles to the well-head and stopped in front of the cedar notice-board.

Nobody was looking her way. She raised her right hand, set the pad of her thumb squarely inside the empty two-inch square of bare white paper at the bottom corner of the Ministry’s notice—where the damp flour-paste underneath gave slightly against the wood—and stood looking at the blank space in the cold.

***

By the sixth bell, the early winter dusk had closed down over the Hold, and Wen had brought in a fresh stoneware platter of steamed Moon Fox Rice Cakes for supper, their dark red bean-paste foxes glowing warm beside the iron soup-ladle.

The shutters were barred. The hanging oil-lamp threw a steady circle of yellow light across the scarred pine table.

Mei didn’t have a spoon in her hand.

She had reached into the small leather pouch at her belt and taken out a heavy, rectangular block of dull grey lead no longer than the first joint of her thumb—a Mengzhou printer’s spacing quad, cast square and solid, with its upper face completely blank and uncut.

She turned the lead block slowly over and back between her thumb and forefinger on the pine plank: *clink, turn, clink*.

Across the teapot, Iuno sat with her right hand resting flat beside her saucer. She wasn’t looking at Suisui’s letter anymore; she was looking at the empty space of scarred wood between her cup and Mei’s lead block.

“Jinhsi writes four false sentences through the Mingting censors to tell us the moon is setting,” Iuno said aloud into the warm room, her voice level, quiet, and arranged in strict order. “And Jinhsi is a Magistrate whose own pulse has run inside her Sentinel’s ribs since the day she was raised from the cold.”

Nobody interrupted her. At the window-seat, Ciaccona rested her chin on the neck of her lute; beside her, Cartethyia’s hands lay still in her lap.

“In Mengzhou,” Iuno continued, laying the second piece down beside the first without joining them, “Magistrate Dou—who has eaten hawthorn with the Moon Fox at the Row every autumn for forty years—cannot hold a brush steady enough to finish a two-stroke character while the Astrolabe Core hangs dark over our heads.”

She moved her forefinger half an inch to the right on the wood.

“And the Minister of Sentinel Affairs himself travels north with a camphor-wood box and asks the city for the vault keys,” Iuno said, “in the first year since the founding that the third file has had no new note written in its margin.”

She stopped there, and took her hand back into her lap.

At the end of the bench, Buling hadn’t touched her teacup.

Her yellow paper talisman hung down over the bridge of her nose, hiding her red eyes in the shadow of her round hat-brim. Without looking up or making a single remark about prices, Buling reached out, took one of the warm white glutinous-rice cakes off the stoneware platter, broke the red bean-paste fox cleanly down the middle with her thumbs, and ate it in silence.

Down the bench beside Yangyang, Amy looked from Iuno’s quiet face to the dull lead block turning between Mei’s fingers.

“On Mount Firmament,” Amy said, her voice dropping out of its rapid patter into something flat and small, “Jué was gonna spend the whole rest of himself in one go and call it a handover, until we made him break it into stages.”

The lead quad stopped turning on the pine board.

Mei looked down at the blank, uncut square of metal under her thumb.

“‘When your Sentinel calls for you,’” Mei said softly, almost to the wood, “‘ask which of you is supposed to come back.’”

Nobody at the table answered that. In the stove, a coal settled with a soft *shhhk* into the ash-pan.

At the door-end of the bench, Chisa looked toward the small rhythmic click of the lead block under Mei’s hand, her face calm above her pale blue tie.

“When a printer knocks a frame apart,” Chisa said in her plain, even voice, “the loose sorts don’t stay in the gutter. They go back into the case under the eaves where they were cast.”

Mei looked up down the length of the table at Chisa, then at Yangyang with her hand resting near her collarbone, and then across at Iuno.

All at once, Mei closed her fingers around the blank Mengzhou quad, shutting the cold lead out of sight inside her fist, and set her knuckles squarely on the table.

“Okay,” Mei said plainly. “Tomorrow night is the Waking Moon. We stay right here through the festival so Liangyu’s boys aren’t watching the wall alone, and I let the stitches settle one more day so Lupa doesn’t put me in the willow hamper.”

She looked around the circle of faces in the yellow lamplight.

“And on Saturday morning,” Mei said, “as soon as the frost is off the north gate, we pack the trunks, we hire two carts, and we go down to Mengzhou. All of us.”

The heavy, held-breath stillness in the storehouse broke like thin ice under a boot.

“Two carts?” Lupa barked immediately from the hearth, pushing off the chimney-brick with both arms uncrossed. “If you think I’m putting our gear on those rickety city-hall hay-wagons that came down the hill today, you’ve got cabbage in your ears! The second one had a split felly on the left wheel! We’re taking the big Jinzhou dray with the oak bed, and I’m packing the two-gallon lard crock in the middle so the axle stays weighted!”

“Wait—I already looked at the Jinzhou dray’s springs on Monday!” Amy piped up, scrambling onto her knees and pulling a stub of carpenter’s chalk out of her sheepskin vest to draw a diagram right on the pine tabletop beside the pickled radishes. “If we swap the rear shackle-pins with the bronze bushings from the Huaxu crate and grease the leaf-pack with mutton tallow, Ma can sit on the forward bench over the king-bolt and she won’t even feel the ruts at the third weir!”

“And Suisui said there is a Magistrate and a Minister from the capital!” Cartethyia leaned across the board, her blue eyes bright and earnest under her crystal laurel. “Iuno, when a household calls upon an Imperial Minister in a northern city, does a knight wear her baldric over the cloak or under it? In Egla Town, Uncle Cosimo always wore his guild-chain on the outside when the tax-assessor came, except the year the dog chewed the ribbon!”

“Under the cloak, Carte,” Ciaccona drawled from the window-seat, plucking a single low, dry *D* on her lute-string and tilting her head back against the shutter. “Definitely under the cloak. Any city whose bureaucrats send a funeral plaque with a six-piece horn section is tuned in D-minor. If we walk in looking respectable, they’ll try to make us sign a receipt.”

In the middle of the noise—with Lupa arguing over salt-fish weight with Wen through the scullery door and Amy drawing leaf-springs in chalk around Mei’s coffee mug—Yangyang reached into her pocket and pulled out her leather-bound patrol log and her short cedar pencil.

She didn’t open it to the watch-schedules at the front.

She flipped straight to the very last leaf against the back board, where, since the Jinzhou road, a single word had sat inside a neat rectangular box:

*Mengzhou.*

Resting the spine of the book against the edge of the table, Yangyang set the lead point of her pencil directly beneath the bottom line of the box and wrote in her small, exact hand:

*Saturday. After the Moon.*

***

By the first watch of the night, a fine, windless drizzle had begun to sift down out of the dark, so thin it barely ticked against the roof-tiles and yet steady enough to turn every paved lane of Xuanfang Hold as black and slick as polished inkstone.

Despite the rain and the rumours, the Hold had hung its lanterns for tomorrow’s eve.

All along the tiered stone steps of the main street, from the well-head in Huizhen Plaza up to the high arch below the sanctuary cliff, hundreds of round crimson oil-paper lanterns swung from the cedar eaves on bamboo hooks. Their light poured down across the wet bluestone paving in long, wavering pools the color of spilled red cinnabar, running together down the shallow drainage ruts until the whole staircase looked as if it were paved with glowing coals.

Under the deep, dry overhang of the dyer’s porch on the second terrace, four village children in padded vests were squatting around a wooden wash-tub with a pile of waxed butcher’s paper and a handful of thumb-sized tallow candle-stubs. They were folding small, square-prowed paper boats, melting a drop of tallow into the center of each paper hull to stick the tiny wick upright so the boats would be ready to float out across the cistern pond after the moon tomorrow night.

Yangyang walked slowly up the center of the wet bluestone steps.

It was her first Watcher round since the siege—twenty minutes from the third storehouse to the upper fountain and back, signed off by Liangyu at muster and accompanied all the way down the front steps by Lupa yelling from the doorway that if she came back with wet socks she was getting locked in the pantry. Lupa’s thick knitted wool muffler was wound twice around Yangyang’s throat over her cerulean feathers, and tucked into her left sleeve, doubling as a hot-water bottle against her strapped ribs, was a foil-wrapped roasted sweet potato Lupa had shoved at her through the door-crack.

At the top of the fourth terrace, the market roofs fell behind.

Ahead of her boots, the broad, rain-slick stone risers narrowed into the long, solitary ceremonial stair that climbed the karst face to the Fogveil Pagoda—where the nine tiers of dark eaves rose silent into the mist and the great carved jade doors of the inner sanctuary still sat bolted from the inside behind three fingers of frost.

No lanterns were lit on the pagoda stair yet. Only the lamplighter’s twelve-year-old apprentice was trotting up the bottom flight with his long bamboo taper-pole and a tin oil-horn at his belt, reaching up to touch the wick inside the first dark red globe.

Ten paces below the foot of the stair, where the stone bridge crossed the upper sluice-channel, Suoming stood waiting under her open oil-paper umbrella.

High up on the dry ridge-tile of the granary roof above her head, a round grey cat sat folded with his paws tucked under his breast, his yellow eyes watching the lamplighter’s taper move up the steps before he stood, stretched once, and slipped silently down the back gable toward the lower village.

Yangyang slowed her boots on the wet bluestone and stopped two paces from the steel-tipped rim of the brown umbrella.

“Evening,” Yangyang said quietly.

Suoming turned her head. Beneath the round canopy of oiled paper, her faded plum-colored coat was buttoned over the flat, square bulge of Muyu’s blue-stitched soundings booklet, and her heavy boots were dark with wet clay around the welts.

“You’re out late for three cracked ribs,” Suoming said.

“The wrap comes off on Sunday,” Yangyang said. “Liangyu gave me the lower turn to the fountain.”

Suoming looked once at the thick wool muffler wound around Yangyang’s chin, then turned and began walking slowly along the level bluestone terrace at the base of the cliff. Yangyang fell into step beside her left elbow, keeping her own shoulder just outside the drip-line of the bamboo ribs so the runoff wouldn’t hit her sleeve.

For twenty paces there was only the drizzle. The fine drizzle whispered against the taut oiled paper overhead, and across the dark flagstones their boots walked through the crimson reflections of the eaves-lanterns, breaking the red water into ripples at every step.

“In the Skyworks,” Suoming said, looking straight ahead at the wet stone wall. “Before the Snare broke. The Fox made you swear an oath.”

Yangyang did not look surprised. She kept her hands tucked lightly inside her sheer cloud-worked cuffs.

“Three things,” Yangyang said plainly. “That I would survive. That I would never treat myself or my shijie as something to be spent and thrown away. And that I would stop Muyu without hating him.”

Suoming walked three steps in silence, the iron heel-plates of her boots clicking flat against the stone.

“Sensible,” the small woman said dryly. “Most people who kneel in front of a Sentinel ask for a bigger sword and swear to die with their boots on.”

They reached the stone parapet at the end of the sluice-bridge, where the long flight of three hundred steps turned straight up the cliff toward the dark pagoda.

Suoming stopped.

For the space of four breaths, nothing moved around them except the water gurgling deep inside the stone culvert beneath their feet and the steady, soft drum of the drizzle on the brown paper dome of the umbrella.

Yangyang stood beside her in the dark.

She did not tilt her head or close her eyes, and she didn’t reach for her Forte. Standing two feet from the small woman’s elbow in the quiet between the rain and the sluice-water, Yangyang simply heard the breath inside Suoming’s chest—not the quick, light draw of the twelve-year-old girl her face looked like under the lantern, and not the blown pant of someone tired from the stairs, but a slow, deep, two-hundred-year-old rasp that caught for the fraction of a beat against the bottom of her ribs, like an iron lock-bar resting against a worn staple.

Yangyang looked down at the crown of the brown umbrella.

“You’re holding your breath on the left side,” Yangyang said gently.

Suoming’s eyes flicked up to Yangyang’s face. She didn’t adjust her coat, and she didn’t deny it.

“I made her a promise too,” Suoming said in her flat, quiet voice. “A hundred winters back, when the plum tree by the south cistern split in the black frost.”

Yangyang stayed very still on the red-lit stone.

“What did she ask you to promise?” Yangyang asked.

Suoming looked past her shoulder up the long, wet canyon of the sanctuary steps, where the lamplighter’s boy was already thirty risers up, touching his taper to one dark globe after another so a line of red lamps bloomed open in the drizzle ahead of his boots, step by step toward the bolted jade doors in the mist.

“She hasn’t called the marker in yet,” Suoming said.

She shifted the bamboo shaft of the umbrella half an inch higher on her shoulder.

“Eat your sweet potato before it goes cold in your sleeve, Watcher, and go back to bed,” Suoming said. “The drizzle’s getting thicker.”

She did not wait for Yangyang to answer. The small woman turned on her heel, set her heavy boot on the first wet riser of the pagoda stair, and began climbing up into the dark behind the lamplighter’s boy—unhurried, step by step, the round brown canopy of her umbrella moving steadily upward as the red lanterns lit one by one ahead of her in the rain.

Yangyang stood alone at the foot of the stair until the small plum-colored figure passed the first turn of the cliff wall.

Then Yangyang tilted her head back, letting the fine cold drizzle touch her forehead mark and her cheeks, and looked up between the black karst spires at the western sky.

There was no moon above the pass.

Not even the smoky, tarnished rim from the night before showed above the ninth spire; it had already sunk down behind the mountain rock before the first festival lantern on the street was lit, leaving only the black, unmoving sphere of the Astrolabe Core hanging in its brass rings against the rain-dark clouds, and the memory of last month’s full moon kept behind closed eyes.

***

Above the market roofs, the drizzle came straight down through the dark without a breath of wind to slant it.

Suoming climbed the broad bluestone risers of the Fogveil Pagoda stair alone, her heavy boots taking the wet steps at a flat, unhurried pace beneath the round brown dome of her oil-paper umbrella.

Behind her, down in the steep valley trough, the hundreds of crimson eaves-lanterns along the Hold’s market lanes glowed through the mist like a sunken bed of coals. Ahead of her, the newly lit red paper globes hanging from the sanctuary balustrade twenty paces apart threw wavering pools of vermilion across the wet stone, step by step up the face of the cliff.

At the second turn of the rock—where the stair doubled back beneath a black overhang of karst and the lamplighter’s boy had already trotted out of sight around the upper tier—a small, metallic *tink... tink* came down through the rain.

Ten risers above the turn, where the massive dressed-basalt plinth of the pagoda’s lowest gallery keyed into the living mountain wall, one of the men in a plain Ministry grey coat was kneeling in the shadow behind a granite bollard.

He had set a shuttered brass bull’s-eye lantern on the stone beside his knee, its slide cracked open by the width of a finger to throw a narrow yellow blade of light across the bottom mortar-joint. With his right hand, he tapped a small steel-headed sounding hammer against the black stone—*tink, pause, tink*—tilting his ear close to the wet rock to listen to the ring deep inside the cliff. His left hand rested empty on his thigh, without notebook or pencil.

Suoming stopped three steps below the turn, where the jutting shoulder of the rock hid the rim of her umbrella from the landing above.

With a single press of her thumb against the wooden spring-catch, she drew the bamboo runner down the shaft, folding the wet oiled paper in around the steel-tipped ribs with a soft *shhk* of shed water.

She stepped sideways off the dressed bluestone stair onto the narrow coping wall, slipped behind the twisted trunk of a dwarf cliff-pine, and set the toe of her boot into a vertical cleft in the limestone.

It was the old quarrymen’s sluice-cut—a foot-wide chimney chiselled into the natural fault of the cliff three centuries ago when the first terracotta water-pipes were laid down from the upper spring, choked now with black maidenhair fern and wet liverwort. Cold runoff slid down the back of the rock in a continuous, silent film.

Using the iron ferrule of her furled umbrella like a walking-staff against the worn stone notches, Suoming climbed straight up through the dark seam in the cliff. Her faded plum-coloured coat brushed the wet ferns without a sound; buttoned flat inside her breast, Muyu’s blue-stitched booklet of deep-fissure soundings stayed dry against her ribs.

Below her heels and six feet to the left through solid limestone, the surveyor’s hammer tapped once more against the outer foundation block and died away behind the trickle of water in the cut.

Eighty feet higher, where the ninth tier of dark cedar eaves rose above the drifting belly of the gorge mist, Suoming stepped out through a narrow stone drainage arch behind the western bronze incense-burner onto the top sanctuary landing.

Up here the air bit at the skin, smelling of high snowfields and old pine pitch. The lamplighter’s boy had already come and gone; the last pair of round crimson oil-paper lanterns hung burning from the bronze dragon-hooks on either side of the porch, washing the wide, empty flagstones in red light before the cliff dropped off into the black fog.

Directly in front of her boots stood the inner sanctuary doors.

They were twelve feet high, carved from thick slabs of pale mutton-fat jade set in black ironwood frames, with the nine-tailed fox relief curling across the center panels. The heavy bronze bar was dropped into its sockets on the inside of the threshold, and all along the vertical seam where the two jade leaves met, three fingers of hard, blue-white rime had grown outward from the gap, sealing the stone from lintel to sill.

Suoming walked out onto the center of the red-lit flagstones and stopped four paces from the threshold.

She pushed the wooden runner up the bamboo shaft until the catch clicked home, opening the round brown canopy of her umbrella overhead to take the steady, hollow patter of the drizzle.

Setting the bamboo shaft against her shoulder, the small woman stood in the red lantern-glow before the frosted jade.

“You’re late,” Suoming said to the doors.

Inside the sanctuary, nothing moved. The three fingers of blue-white frost along the seam stayed hard and unbroken, no thread of lamplight showed beneath the sill, and high above the nine dark roofs the stalled sphere of the Astrolabe Core hung black against the rain, and the small woman under her open umbrella did not move.

***

At the third watch, the drizzle thinned to a cold grey mist that smelled of wet pine needles and river stone.

Qingxiao walked down the cliff path from the upper peak on foot, the heavy square of indigo-dyed oilcloth—the one that smelled of storehouse camphor—folded across her shoulders over her moon-white silk.

Under the gatehouse arch at the foot of the wall, the horn lantern burned low on its hook. A pair of younger Wardens from Ge’s relief sat awake on the stone bench inside the guardroom door with a brazier between their shins, speaking in hushed voices that stopped respectfully as the hem of Qingxiao’s white skirt passed the threshold and turned up the narrow timber stair to the watch gallery.

Up under the high cedar eaves, the gallery was quiet.

Her seven-stringed paulownia qin lay in its dry place on the inner pine bench. Hanging from the corner pillar beside the stairhead, the wooden watch-board showed *Ruoyun* and *Qingxiao* chalked side by side in the third-watch column from the night before.

Qingxiao stepped off the top riser and looked down the length of the gallery toward the corner embrasure.

Her boots stopped on the second plank.

The broad, flat stone cap of the corner embrasure—where Ruoyun always propped her shielded brass tally-lamp and the thick leather gatehouse logbook—had been cleared. The logbook sat stacked neatly on the end of the pine bench.

In its place, centered squarely on the cold grey limestone under the eaves, sat a thick, four-legged weiqi board of dark, age-polished kaya wood, its nineteen-by-nineteen grid worn smooth at the corners.

Set out on the nine star-points of the board were nine flat, matte-black slate stones, arranged in the exact handicap opening of the old mountain game.

On the near side of the board facing the stairwell sat a covered bowl of carved white clam-shell stones, full to the brim and untouched. Beside the board stood a tall, slender porcelain wine-pot glazed the pale blue-grey of winter sky, with two small handleless porcelain cups set mouth-up on a lacquered tray.

Nobody was in the gallery. Out beyond the parapet rail, fifty feet below the wall, the rows of red festival lanterns along the Hold’s wet streets burned small and blurred through the mist, and overhead the sky was black and moonless around the stalled shadow of the Core.

Qingxiao walked slowly down the cedar planks and stopped beside the embrasure.

She did not touch the stones. She looked down at the nine black slate discs resting on the star-points—the exact board, the exact untouched white bowl, and the exact pale porcelain pot she had seen rimed in two hundred years of ice on the stone table inside her own heart when the scrolls unrolled at the bottom of the lake.

Behind her, rapid footsteps clicked up the wooden stairwell—*tap, tap, tap*—and Ruoyun came round the corner pillar with an oilskin dispatch folder tucked under her ruffled white sleeve and her round brass spectacles sliding down her nose.

“Ge’s boys left the lower sluice-gate open two notches again, I swear if I had a bamboo switch I’d—”

Ruoyun stopped dead three paces from the embrasure.

Her bright blue eyes went from the kaya-wood board to the nine black stones, and then to the porcelain wine-pot sitting where her ink-stone belonged.

“I did not put that there,” Ruoyun said at once, her barracks voice dropping flat and low in the quiet under the eaves.

Qingxiao did not look around. “Who came up the stair during the second watch?”

“Nobody came up the stair!” Ruoyun stepped closer, her small golden horns catching the dim yellow glow from the stairwell lantern as she stared down at the grid. “I was down in the culvert right under the arch clearing the grate from the second bell till ten minutes ago! If a mouse had walked past those two boys by the brazier carrying a thirty-pound kaya board, I’d have heard its toenails on the wood!”

Slowly, Qingxiao drew her bare right hand out from beneath the camphor-scented oilcloth and laid the backs of her fingers against the curved porcelain belly of the wine-pot.

There was no frost on the glaze.

Beneath her knuckles, the porcelain was warm—not boiling from a stove, just the steady, gentle heat of mulled eighth-month plum wine that had been sitting in a warm sleeve until five minutes ago.

Ruoyun saw Qingxiao’s hand stay on the pot. Ruoyun’s jaw tightened; she looked sharply up at the dark cedar rafters overhead, then out over the wet stone parapet toward the high, mist-wrapped silhouette of the Fogveil Pagoda across the Hold, and then back down at the nine black stones.

She didn’t say a name, and she didn’t touch her spectacles.

For a long time Qingxiao stood looking down at the board.

When she breathed out, her breath steamed white and thin across the nineteen lines of the grid, and in the stillness under the eaves, a single silk string on the wrapped qin behind her knee gave a tiny, sympathetic hum against the wood—clear and low, like a blade resting quiet in its scabbard after the rain.

Qingxiao pulled the indigo oilcloth closer around her shoulders, stepped around the corner of the embrasure, and sat down on the pine bench facing the white bowl.

She lifted the wooden lid off the clam-shell stones.

Taking a single white stone loosely between the index and middle fingers of her right hand, Qingxiao raised it over the empty fourth-line corner of the board and brought it down against the dark wood.

*Click.*

The sound dropped clean and small into the night gallery, like a white pebble falling into a deep mountain pool.

Across the stone embrasure, Ruoyun stood still for two breaths more. Then she let out a short, quiet breath through her nose, laid her oilskin folder down on the bench, and reached for the warm porcelain wine-pot to fill both cups to the brim, while half a mile across the dark, moonless Hold, a single round umbrella stood at the top of the red-lit steps, before the bolted doors in the mist.

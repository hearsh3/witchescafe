## I. Last Call

Nine went into the common room at the eighth bell and eight came out, and Lupa stood at the bottom of the stairwell doing the arithmetic with her jaw.

The evidence lay behind her in a spread she could have read blindfolded. Cartethyia asleep across two chairs with a laurel of wet hair and a cup balanced on her sternum, rising and falling. Ciaccona under the table with the lute held above her like a drowning man's lantern, humming something in a key that had given up. Amy in the corner nest of blankets, out cold, one hand still shut around a paper wrapper. Iuno upright on the bench with her eyes closed and her spine perfect, the way certain people sleep when thirty years of office has gotten into the bones, and Mei's head on her lap, and Mei's boots — Mei's boots were somewhere in the reservoir courtyard, and Lupa had already decided to find them in the morning and say nothing about it. Hiyuki in the good chair with frost still politely declining to leave the arms of it, upright, awake, reading, having drunk everyone else under the table with an air of mild regret. Lynae had gone off toward the mural wall with a can and an expression. That was eight, with Lynae counted generously, which was the only way anyone had ever counted Lynae.

The ninth had made tea for everybody and washed the pot and gone up.

Lupa took the service stair two at a time, which was the wrong way to take it, because the service stair rang. She went up loud. She had her reasons.

The drying terrace sat above the dormitory's fourth floor, a concrete apron under the false sky, hung with four lines of the pack's laundry going slowly stiff in the vent-warm air. Above it Helios had cooled to the dull committee-approved bronze the city called night, and the manufactured stars ticked along in their wrong constellations, and somewhere below the trams changed shift with a sound like a long breath through teeth.

Chisa sat on an upturned crate at the terrace's edge with a second crate beside her and a kettle on the utility ring, already boiling.

"You went up on your heels," she said. "You've been doing that for a week."

"Testing something."

"You're testing whether I notice you doing it."

"And?"

"You did it on the third and the sixth and tonight. On the fourth you came up properly and pretended you'd forgotten." Chisa took the kettle off. "Three cups of the good leaf left. Do you want yours strong."

Lupa lowered herself onto the second crate. It held. Somebody had reinforced it, recently, with a plate and four bolts, which meant somebody had known she would sit on it, which was a small thing and did something to her chest anyway.

"Strong," she said.

The tea came out in two chipped mugs from the common kitchen, the ones nobody claimed. Chisa passed one over and kept the other and put the third away, and neither of them mentioned the third.

For a while the terrace held only the vents and the lines and the small metallic sounds of a city breathing inside a god.

"Nine went in," Lupa said.

"You counted the capybara."

"The capybara counts."

"The capybara has been sleeping under the counter at the Third Ward stand since the tenth bell." Chisa turned her mug a quarter-turn. "The capybara has a job."

"Everyone's got a job." Lupa drank. It was too hot and she drank it anyway, because tea in this pack came with a treaty and treaties are performed. "You washed the pot."

"Someone was going to."

"You washed the pot for eight people at three in the morning on the night we got told where we're going."

"Two of them had put things in it."

Above them the ventilation shifted registers. Down the line a shirt cracked once in the warm draft — the bard's, burnt through at the cuff, a hole shaped like a thumbprint that had been there since a stage in Ragunna and would be there when the shirt was rags.

"Say it," Chisa said.

"Say what."

"You've been carrying something up the stairs since the harbormaster's parcel came off the shuttle. It's making you loud."

Lupa looked at the laundry. Iuno's blue, still dripping, hung with the arrogance of expensive cloth. Amy's small sleeve, brand-new, tag still on it, because the girl had opinions about tags and one of them was that they should stay. Cartethyia's socks, which were an ongoing municipal matter.

"Your hand goes to your throat," she said.

Chisa's mug stopped.

"Four times tonight. Twice at the table, once when Ciaccona started the Maiden ballad, once on the stairs." Lupa kept her eyes on the washing. "You've been doing it since Honami. You do it when a room gets full."

"You've been counting."

"It's what I do."

The vents changed again. Chisa set the mug on the concrete beside her boot and left her hands empty, which Lupa had learned to read as its own kind of answer, the way a fighter shows you an open guard when she's decided to let you in.

"Ask it, then," Chisa said.

"Where's the collar."

"In a tin."

"That's an answer to a different question."

"It's the answer I have ready."

Lupa turned her mug slowly in both hands, the way old fighters do to keep the fingers from doing something else.

"I've had a year of the sight of your neck," she said. "I've never once asked what used to be on it. I'd like that noted."

"It's noted."

"So."

"So." Chisa looked out at the bronze ceiling of the world, and the false stars, and the terraces going down and down toward the reservoir where a knight in laurel was going to wake up in three hours with a very specific headache. "Get comfortable. It starts in a fitting room."

## II. Kuresaki

The removal took eleven seconds and the paperwork took forty minutes, which Ojima Ren had decided was the truest description of his country available in a single sentence.

He worked at the same bench in the same front room of the same Bureau annex, and the wall behind him now carried the license, the chart, the faded photograph of a Minister shaking somebody's hand, and — new since spring, in a frame the Bureau had shipped with the notice and billed to the annex — a printed copy of the Frequency Safety (Amendment) Act, third schedule, the one everyone called the unfastening. Fitting age raised to fifteen. Existing wearers under fifteen to be released on application by parent or guardian. Release fee: as per fitting fee, unchanged. He had read the fee line four times the day it came and then gone out to the harbor wall and stood there until his tea went cold at the bench without him.

Five a week, since spring. His trade was ending on a published schedule, and he was the one ending it, and the Bureau paid him at the standard rate to do it, and he did it well, because a clasp that has been sealed for four years wants coaxing and a bad hand leaves a burn.

The girl came on a Saturday, which he had begun to think of as his day for this. Aki, nine now, taller by the length of his forearm, in a school jacket with somebody's older name inked out at the collar and hers written under it.

Her mother stood by the door with the release form and her hands folded, and she had done all her crying somewhere else, some time ago, from the look of her, which Ren respected.

"You've kept it clean," he said, turning the girl's chin with two fingers, thumb clear of the throat.

"I argue with it in August."

"Everyone does."

"You said that when I was little."

"I did." He got the pin in and felt for the seat of the clasp, and the band gave up its four years with a small complaint. "Hold still. There's a spring in here that thinks it's important."

The ribbon was on its third color: yellow, then green, then this one, a deep rust that she had probably picked herself off a market stall. He worked the clasp, and the tuning-fork hum in the alloy wound down through two notes and stopped, and the band came away in his hand, warm from her.

Aki's fingers went straight up to her neck and found nothing there and stayed anyway, pressed flat, feeling for the shape of what her skin remembered.

"It's cold," she said.

"It's a Saturday in October."

"No." She was working something out; he could see her doing it, the way children do, out loud in the face. "It's cold where it was."

Ren set the band on the bench cloth. Four years of a growing neck had polished the inner face to a soft grey shine, except at the clasp, where the felt he cut himself off bolt cloth he bought himself had gone dark and slightly furred.

"Does it say goodbye?" Aki asked.

He had forty-one cards in the box from his first eleven years and a hundred and some now, and every card had a question on the back. He went through the whole box that evening looking for the card that matched hers, and reached the bottom of it, and put the lid on.

"It said hello when it woke," he said. "It's had a long shift."

"That's not an answer."

"You'll grow up terrifying," said Ren, and put the band in the reclamation tray, and then, while the mother was signing at the window and looking away, took it out again, cut the rust ribbon free with the bench shears, and gave the ribbon to the girl.

The regulation on reclamation was clear and he had read it once, in his first year, and had thereafter formed a professional opinion about which of the Bureau's papers deserved his full attention. The ribbon went into her jacket pocket. The band went into the tray. He would file the tray's weight at month's end and the arithmetic would work, because ribbon weighs nothing on the Bureau's scale, and there is a great deal of the world you can move if you are willing to move it in units the scale ignores.

They bowed. He bowed. The salt light came in off the harbor road and took them.

He pulled her card, and wrote on the back: *asked whether it says goodbye. told her it had had a long shift. she called this insufficient.* Then he sat with his tea going cold, which was a thing he did now, and had done since the rain over Honami Bay stopped and his niece Hana came home nineteen years old to parents who had spent thirty years getting there.

The letters lived in the same box as the cards, in a rubber band, and there were seven.

They had begun arriving a year and a half ago, after the papers ran the photograph and the whole archipelago spent a month looking at a girl's throat. The first had come from a woman of sixty who had worn hers from twelve to fifty-one and had, in the fourth decade, in the dark, on a night she declined to describe, run a finger up under the band and met a shape. *I want to ask,* she wrote, *whether the wave means anything, or whether it is only the mold.*

He had answered her in three words and had answered the six after her in the same three words, and had kept a copy of each answer, and the copies said:

*Yes. On purpose.*

That was the whole correspondence of Ojima Ren, regulator smith, licensed eleven years and then thirteen. Seven letters, seven answers, twenty-one words in total, and a box.

There was an eighth address. He had it from the papers and then from the trade circulars and then, embarrassingly, from a schoolchild's atlas he had bought at the Kuresaki stationer's for the purpose: a city inside a giant, on the far side of two oceans, where the sun was a machine and the mail took nine weeks. He had gone as far as the envelope twice. Hana had watched him do it the second time and said, in her thirty-year-old voice out of her nineteen-year-old face, *Uncle, she's a person. People get post.*

He checked the annex's mail every morning before opening, which took four seconds, and had taken four seconds every morning for a year and a half.

And in the drawer where the good tools lived there was a job sheet from the frontier, come to him along some chain of guild favors from a caravan town called Sette Strade, in a hand that pressed hard and underlined once: *One regulator. Kuresaki pattern, felt-lined, sized to enclosed measurements. Adjustable damping if the pattern allows. For a client who is asking.*

The last word was underlined twice.

He had built it in nine days. Felt lining, burr filed to glass, the wave stamped where the felt met the clasp. Adjustable damping had cost him a week of temper and a rebuilt escapement, and it worked, and he had tested it on his own wrist for three nights running until the hum stopped surprising him.

Then he had done the thing his master would have called a liberty.

The Bureau's pattern sealed. Every Kuresaki band ever made had sealed, at breath's end, from behind, with a pin his master's master had designed to defeat exactly one pair of hands: the wearer's own. Ren had turned that pin around. He set a catch on the outer face, thumb-sized, ridged, findable in the dark, and he sat with it finished on the bench cloth in front of him for a long time.

Then he wrote the shipping docket, and in the field for pattern he entered a name that existed nowhere in the approved chart on his wall:

*Kuresaki, second.*

## III. Twenty Years of Saying Hello

"Sixteen," Chisa said. "Monitoring Center, third floor. Storm outside. There was a man behind a desk and he asked whether I'd heard him."

"Had you?"

"I heard him perfectly. I answered his voice." She was looking at the reservoir lights, far below, breaking up in the water where a Blessed Maiden of Rinascita had gone in fully dressed at the seventh bell to settle a dispute about a fountain in another country. "His face had gone. That was the year they went. So I said *loud and clear* to the space where the face should have been, and the clasp shut, and that was the appointment."

"Who signed for you."

"The prefecture. My mother's name is on the form, and the form has a line under it that says the signature is a formality, which I have thought about for a long time." A pause with the length of a decision in it. "She read the line. She signed above it. Both of those are on the same page."

Lupa filed that where she filed everything she planned to come back to.

"It sat cold at first," Chisa said. "There's a way it takes the temperature off the front of your throat and holds it. You learn a way of standing. Chin level. Everyone in a collar stands the same. You can pick us out of a crowd from behind."

"Did it work?"

"On what?"

"On you. Did it hold anything down."

Chisa considered this with the seriousness she brought to menus and to gods.

"It made a noise every morning at six. Two seconds. You feel it in your back teeth." She drank. "Whether that was the whole apparatus or a very expensive alarm clock is a question the Bureau has never been asked in public."

"That's a no."

"That's a question the Bureau has never been asked in public."

Down the laundry line something small dropped off the end and lay in the vent-draft. Amy's, by the size. Chisa got up, retrieved it, pegged it back, and sat, and Lupa watched her do it and said nothing about a girl who could not see faces walking a line of wet cloth in the bronze dark and knowing whose was whose by the hem.

"There was a boy," Chisa said, sitting.

"A boy."

"Same year. Park, after the storm. His kite was in a tree and the branches made a lock and I cut the one on the right and it came down into his arms. His mother screamed at me from the swings." She said it flatly, as a matter of record. "Then he got loose from her and put star candy in my hand and told me the way I cut the branch was cool."

Lupa's tail moved once on the concrete, a short sweep, and stopped.

"He said his teacher told him only the brave get star candy." Chisa turned her cup. "I've been trying for twenty years to remember whether I said thank you. I don't think I did. I patted his head and left."

"You patted his head."

"I did."

"Where'd you learn that?"

"I've never known." Chisa turned to face her, which she did rarely and always on purpose. "You do it."

"I do a lot of things."

"You put your hand on the top of Lynae's head at the muster and left it there for one second and took it back before she could look up." Chisa turned back to the water. "I've been meaning to ask where you learned it."

The vents cycled. Neither of them answered the question, and the question sat down between them and got comfortable.

"Then Honami," Lupa said.

"Then Honami."

The kettle ticked as it cooled.

"There's no Bureau in a sealed city," Chisa said. "No prefecture, no Monitoring Center, no relay, no one on the other end of any wire in the world. The Sphere shut and everything outside it went to weather." She had her hands empty again, flat on her knees. "The band came on at six. Two seconds. It reported to nothing for twenty years."

Lupa said nothing, because there was a thing gathering behind her sternum that would come out wrong if she opened her mouth for it.

"Sumika collected clocks that had stopped. Whole shelves. She'd wind them and set them and put them up and they'd be wrong again by evening and she'd be delighted." Chisa's voice stayed exactly level, which Lupa had learned to read the way you read a river gone smooth over something. "I was the only clock in that city that kept time. I woke her up every morning for two months. She used to say — "

The pause went on a beat past comfortable.

"She used to say something. About the noise."

"You don't remember it."

"I remember two months out of a hundred and fifty-one entries." Chisa picked her mug back up, found it empty, put it down. "She wrote a hundred and fifty-one. The city took the rest of me while I slept, night after night, in even amounts, and left me the parts it had no use for. So I have her handwriting and her arithmetic and the shape of her sitting up at the counter with a physics magazine, and I have the fact that she said something about the noise, every morning, the same joke probably, and I have the exact place in my head where the sound of her saying it used to be."

Somewhere four floors down a door banged and a bard's voice rose briefly in a register that suggested she had discovered the table above her.

"Third August," Chisa said.

"What?"

"You asked why I kept it. Third August. Honami's summers came round every eleven days and I stopped counting them properly after the first year, so third August is a lie of convenience, but it was hot and I was arguing with the thing the way you do." She held up her left hand, index finger extended, and hooked it, once, in the air at her own throat. "You get a finger under it at the front and run it round to the clasp. And there's a wave."

Lupa waited.

"Three lines, curling. Punched into the alloy where the felt meets the catch, on the inside, against the skin, in the one place on the entire object that no inspector has ever looked at in the history of the Bureau." Chisa let her hand drop. "Somebody stood at a bench with a punch and a hammer and put it there on purpose."

The wind machines turned over. Far off, the reservoir shivered.

"That's why," Chisa said.

"That's why."

"It's the answer I have ready. It's true and it fits in a sentence and I've used it on myself for years."

Lupa turned her mug. "And the other one."

Chisa looked at her.

"You gave me a prepared answer," Lupa said. "You said so out loud, in the same breath, which is the most annoying thing about you and I'd like it on the record. So."

"So."

"So there's a second one."

Chisa was quiet for long enough that the vents cycled twice.

"It still works," she said.

"…The hum."

"Two seconds. In the back teeth. It's in a tin in my footlocker with a shirt around it and I could wake it in nine seconds from where I'm sitting." She said it to the water. "It's the last sound from that city I can still make happen. Everything else is other people's copies. Ciaccona has the frequencies, Iuno has the readings, the Archive has forty hours of survey audio. That hum is mine and it's a physical object and she heard it every morning for two months and said something about it, and I don't know what she said, and it will still make the noise."

Lupa set her mug down on the concrete with great care, because her hands had gone strange.

"Show me," she said.

"No."

It came out level and immediate and closed, and Lupa took it in the chest and inclined her head about a quarter-inch, which in the fighters' quarter is a full bow.

"Understood."

"Someday," Chisa said, "probably. When there's a reason that isn't curiosity."

"Understood."

"You could have argued."

"I've been on the other side of that door." Lupa picked her mug back up. "Somebody hauled a woman out of a room once by shouting at her and it worked, and I've been very careful ever since about what I think that entitles me to."

Chisa's mouth did something at one corner, brief, gone.

"You had it at the Dimmr Plains," Lupa said, after a while. "Departure day. I remember grey under your collar in the stills."

"I had it in the tin at the Dimmr Plains."

"Mm."

Two hundred students had watched that livestream and the college had run the stills in the refectory for a month, and Lupa had stood under them with a coffee and looked at six people walking forward, and one of them had a thin grey line at the throat above a mission collar, and Lupa had counted it as a detail about a girl she was about to jump into a void with, and had thought nothing further about it at all.

She let it stand. The pack ran on a great many things standing.

## IV. Caldara

The circuit came to Caldara on the fourth year, as it always had, three days above the Sanguis Plateaus, up the dry hill road in the last of the long heat, and Vibia rode in at the head of it with a bad knee, a satchel of Schedule forms, and a folding table she had bought herself because the Guild's table had a wobble and a wobble makes a man think he can argue.

She set up in the square by the well, because the charter said *in public view, at the hour of most traffic*, and she read the terms aloud twice, because the charter said *audibly*, and the second reading was hers, an addition of her own, on the grounds that the first time through nobody in any village on the circuit ever heard a word past the money.

Fifty-one people came out for it. Caldara held about four hundred.

"Sponsorship is a contract between the House and the athlete," she read. "Guardians are parties to the schedule of maintenance. Guardians are not parties to the athlete." A pause; she had learned where to leave the pauses. "Consideration is paid quarterly into a Guild account in the athlete's name across the term of the indenture, with the balance falling due to the athlete at majority. Guardians receive a maintenance stipend for the duration, at the rate published on the concourse board at Olymdos, which any of you can have read to you at any Guild post on the coast."

A man near the front said something about the old way, in the tone of a man performing for his neighbors, and the neighbors did their part, and Vibia let the noise pass over her the way you let weather pass over a table.

"Correspondence," she read on. "The athlete retains the right of correspondence and visit. Waiver of that right is void. Any instrument purporting to waive it is void in its entirety, and any House offering one loses its charter." She put the sheet down. "That clause is nine words long in the original and it cost somebody a year. Anyone wanting the year explained can find me after."

Fifty-one people stood in that square and every one of them waited out the clause for the number. She gave them the number, and Caldara did its arithmetic out loud, and about a third of the faces went slack in the particular way that means a plan has just died.

That was the design. She had understood it the first circuit she rode. A lump sum at the door buys a child; a stipend across fifteen years buys a very long conversation with a family about whether they meant it.

Three children were put forward in Caldara. She interviewed them the way the charter required: alone, in the tannery's back office with the door shut and a guardian within sight of the window and out of earshot of the room, one at a time, with the form face-down until she had asked.

The first two went the way they went. A boy of eight who wanted the Hill so badly he vibrated. A girl of ten whose eyes went to the window on every answer, and whom Vibia failed on medical grounds she invented on the spot and would defend to a tribunal if it came to that.

The third was seven and had her hair cut with a bowl and a strong opinion.

"Would you go to the Hill," Vibia said.

"Yes."

"If the Mark takes. Would you fight."

"Yes."

Same as all of them. They came in with the yes already made, formed at some kitchen table, worn smooth from handling.

"Third question," Vibia said. "It's on the form and it has to be filled in and I can't fill it in for you." She turned the sheet over. "Who will you write to?"

The girl looked at her.

It was the look Vibia rode the circuit for. There was a silence in it that had never been asked to hold anything before, and the child stood in it and turned around inside it, looking for a name, and Vibia watched the arithmetic happen — mother, and the sum that had been discussed at the table; father, and the same sum; the aunt in Vetta who wrote at midwinter; the boy from the ring behind the tannery who was going to forget her inside a season and whom she was going to remember for thirty years.

"Do I have to?"

"Blanks disallowed."

The girl looked at the table.

Vibia turned the form round, took out her own pen, and wrote in the box: *V. of the Guild circuit, care of the Olymdos post.* Then she signed the countersignature line, which was for the guardian, which was regulation, and blotted it, and slid the form into the satchel.

The regional audit flagged that entry every year. It had flagged eleven of hers across four circuits. Every year the flag went up to Septimont, and every year it came back down marked *reviewed, no action*, from an office on the Hill that had never once put a name on the note, and the clerks in the audit room had a running joke about it, and Vibia had a private conviction that she would rather cut her own hand off than test.

She wrote to all eleven. Twice a year. It took an evening.

---

The children of Caldara held their own Agon behind the tannery while the adults did the paperwork, because they had held one every fourth year since anyone could remember and had never once been given permission.

The ring was a scorched circle in the dust the width of two carts, and nothing had grown in it for twenty-five years, and the village explanation involved a mineral seam. Fence-post spears. Feed-lid shields. Teams of two, per the Ephor's reforms, which had reached Caldara ahead of the news that made them, as everything from the Hill did.

The local rule was the interesting one, and Vibia stood at the fence with her satchel and heard it argued into place by a nine-year-old with total authority.

"You're the wolf, so you take two."

"That's not fair."

"It's the *rule.* You're the wolf. You take two and you still win, that's the whole thing, that's *why* she's her —"

The boy playing the wolf took two opponents and a fence-post spear and lost inside a minute, and was jeered, and demanded the handicap again immediately, and got it.

Vibia noted it in her circuit book, in the column the Guild had opened for this exact purpose and had not yet decided what to do with, headed *local practice.*

---

The rest was the town's business and she wrote it down because writing things down was the trade.

A hydrologist had come up from the capital in the spring, which had never happened in the memory of the village, and had happened this year because Caldara was six letters of light on a concourse board and the survey office had received a query it could not source. She had measured the lake for four days. The lake sat flat and glassy under the plateau sun and breathed steam at dawn, and the elders told her it boiled, and the children swore to it, and she wrote in her report — Vibia read the copy pinned in the tannery, because the village had framed it — *lake: shallow, warm, saline, in slow retreat. Boils, locally attested.*

An old woman called Serica kept a shop that sold three things and had, folded in a chest under the counter, a square of oilcloth from twenty-five years back.

She brought it out for the Guild's oral-history clerk, who was riding the circuit that season collecting hometown entries for the board, and told the story the whole village told: two mornings that summer, before dawn, supplies at the village edge. Waybread, bandages, dried fish, and a paper with a warning about Discord movement in the eastern gullies. The watchman had seen the courier once, going away — a tall woman, scarred, already leaving.

The clerk photographed the oilcloth and then, because photographs lose knots, drew the knot by hand in her notebook. She had seen that knot before. She could not place where, and she wrote *seen before — check* beside the drawing and underlined it, and she would carry that underline for two years.

The paper was in the chest too, brittle at the folds. The warning was in a small hard hand. At the bottom of it, where a name would go, there was a mark.

The clerk copied it twice.

The two copies came out different, and she attributed this to the light, and put the notebook away, and asked Serica for her hometown spelling for the board, and Serica said *Caldara*, and spelled it, and asked whether the letters were really as tall as they said.

"Taller," said the clerk.

## V. Schedule B

"You said it started in a fitting room," Lupa said.

"It did."

"Mine did too." She stretched her legs out and crossed them at the boot. "Different furniture."

Chisa waited. She was good at waiting; it was the same muscle as the pathfinding, and Lupa had watched her use it on gods.

"Seven years old. One room and a curtain, my father at the table with his hands folded, my mother at the window with her back to it. Two questions, in order. Would I go to the Hill if the House sponsored me. Would I fight, if the Mark took."

"You said yes."

"I'd been saying yes since before I had the words for it. They asked twice a year for four years and got the same yes every time." Lupa turned her mug. "What I kept was their faces after. Relief. Fresh every time, like the yes might land tails one day and they'd better check."

"They were asking permission."

Lupa's ears went back a fraction and came forward again.

"They were asking permission," she said.

"Did you give it?"

"Twice a year for four years. Every time, out loud, in a level voice, at a table." A short breath through the nose. "And they never once told me what for."

Below, a tram went through the Third Ward junction and away.

"How much?" Chisa asked.

"Schedule B. I've never read it."

"You could."

"It's in the Temple registry on the second terrace, open to any citizen for the price of the copying fee. Sixteen years I walked past that building four times a week going to work." Lupa drank. "Whatever the number is, it goes in my head and stays. Then I'm a woman who knows what she cost."

"You already are."

"I'm a woman who knows the word *generous.*" She set the mug down. "That's the word in the record. The House paid generously. Enough to reopen certain doors on the Hill for a household that had been counted out in the second Tide, or to open new ones a long way off. The House was indifferent about which." She flicked one finger, dismissing a room she had been standing in for twenty years. "Guilt's cheap on the Hill. They rounded up."

Chisa put her cup down on the concrete.

"That's a wave," she said.

Lupa's hand stopped halfway.

"It's a receipt."

"It's a receipt with a wave on it."

"Take it out."

"It's load-bearing."

"I'll hold the other end."

"Three."

"Would you go. Would you fight. And who will you write to."

Chisa turned her head.

"What would you have written?"

The false stars ticked. Somewhere below, Ciaccona had found the stairwell and was singing to it, badly, with great tenderness, four floors down.

"Nothing," Lupa said. "I'd have left it blank and taken whatever the blank cost."

"Blanks disallowed."

"Yeah." Lupa looked at the washing. "I know. I wrote that part."

Chisa let that sit where it landed, for a long time, which was the correct thing to do with it.

"I checked on them once," Lupa said. "One week, the year I walked out of the House. Records clerk owed me a favor. There's a transfer of funds seventeen years old and a phrase after it. *No forwarding address.*" She shrugged with one shoulder. "So that's the whole file. I've got a ring of keys in my head and every one of them fits somebody else's door, and I make a living off it, and the joke tells itself."

"The clerk was thorough."

"Clerks are thorough about what they're asked." Chisa said it without any weight on it at all, the way you mention the time. "You asked for an address. A person who wanted to stay lost would have gone through the same office to arrange it."

Lupa's tail went absolutely still on the concrete.

"…That's a horrible thing to say."

"It's a smaller file than you've been carrying. I thought you'd want to know the size of it."

The wind machines turned over. Lupa sat with her hands around a cooling mug and the shape of a thought she had kept out of the house for twenty years, standing on the step now with its coat off.

"There was a woman in Caldara," she said, to change the subject, which was permitted. "Twice, the summer I burned. Before dawn, at the village edge — waybread, bandages, dried fish, a warning about Discords in the eastern gullies, all of it in oilcloth under a stone. The watchman saw her once. Tall. Scarred. Already leaving."

"Going away."

"…What?"

Lupa watched her light the ring.

"You do this to everyone," she said.

"Do what."

"I cut," Chisa said. "It's the tool I was handed."

## VI. The Common Table

Vopiscus had taped hands in the fighters' quarter for forty-one years and had opinions about all of them.

"Wrist's lazy," he said. "Tug. Wrap. You've been favoring it since the semifinal and you've built a whole style around the favoring. Congratulations. You've invented a limp."

Sabinia of Vetta Alta, junior bracket, second season, sat on the bench with her arm out and took it. Everyone took it from Vopiscus. He was a hundred years old by the look of him and eighty by the ledger and there were fighters on the wall of the Colosseum whose hands he had done when they were nine.

"You going to the table tonight?" he asked. Tug. Wrap.

"It's stew."

"It's stew every third night. What's your point."

"My point is it's stew."

"Your point is you'd rather eat noodles alone in your room with the schedule on your knees." Tug. "Attendance is recorded, girl."

"That's the part I don't get." Sabi flexed the wrist and got her hand slapped flat again. "It's a canteen. Why does a canteen have a register?"

Vopiscus wound gauze.

The common table sat under the arcade at the bottom of the fighters' quarter, and it was in the Guild's welfare annex at Article Nine, and it read, in nine of the plainest words on the Hill: *The Guild shall set a table. Attendance shall be recorded.* Hot food at the eighth bell, no charge, any registered athlete, any bracket, House or free. Half the Hill had called it charity when it passed. The touts had called it the wolf's soup and had, within a month, opened a side book on the nightly count, because the Hill's true faith is that anything countable is bettable, and the desk had been taking action on Article Nine attendance ever since. Vopiscus had a shell down on tonight himself. He was not proud of it and would have described the pride available on the Hill as thin.

"Because the Tide has ears," he said.

Sabi looked at him.

"You're going to tell me a story."

"I'm going to tell you a fact and you're going to file it under story, and then in fifteen years you're going to refile it." Tug. Wrap. "Every fighter I ever saw go strange in this city went quiet first. Every one. Quiet, then strange, then gone, and then somebody stamps a word on a bracket and the record runs out because nobody's paid to keep writing it." He cut the tape with his teeth, which he had been told about by four physicians. "The register isn't for the food. The register's so somebody notices the third night."

"The third night."

"One night off is a girl with a book. Two is a girl with a cold. Three is the beginning of a sentence." He turned her hand over, checked the wrap across the knuckles, and let it go. "There's a woman on the board of this Guild who has never once sat in the chamber, and every quarter the clerks send her the minutes to an address that consists of the word *north*, and every quarter the minutes come back with nothing written on them. And that clause is hers. Nine words. She fought the Ephor's own drafters for a season over the second half of it, and the second half is the half nobody understands, and the second half is the whole point."

Sabi rolled her shoulder.

"You knew her?"

"I taped her hands for a decade." Tug. Wrap. Vopiscus's face did nothing at all. "She ate alone for ten years and I watched her do it and I said one useful sentence about it and she ignored me for six years and then went and put it in a charter." He nodded at the door. "Table's at eight. Bring your schedule if you want. Read it at the table. Nobody'll talk to you if you look busy."

---

The board stood on the concourse at Olymdos where the touts used to work, public and permanent, every registered athlete's terms in daylight.

It had been running for two years and had already produced consequences nobody had drafted for. The purses were open, so a fighter now knew to the shell what the woman on her left was earning, and the fighters' quarter had discovered an entirely new denomination of resentment and had taken to it with enthusiasm. Three Houses had restructured to hide money in coaching fees. Two managers had gone into the tour business. The Hill adjusted, the way it adjusts, and the adjusting was the price, and the price was posted next to everything else.

Column nine ran down the right-hand side in the small light, and it was the one people stood and read.

VETTA ALTA. CALDARA. RAGUNNA. PORTO VENO. SETTE STRADE. VETTA ALTA again, twice, because the Plateaus had discovered that the machine could be pointed. Six letters, eleven letters, a village with four hundred people in it and a lake it lies about.

Sabi walked under it most evenings on her way to the arcade, and looked up at her own town in lights the way you look at a person you are related to and have complicated feelings about, and went in to the stew.

---

In the Guild archive on the third terrace, in the stacks where the old House match records had been transferred under the charter, a file that had held four pages for twenty years now held sixty-three.

The archivist had never met the supplier. Copying fees arrived in advance, in loose shells, in an envelope, four times a year. The material came with them: private tapes, a hospitality-desk leaf, three affidavits from retired stewards, a physician's day-book, two pages of a betting record with a hole burned in one corner. The pages were catalogued and shelved and cross-referenced as they arrived, and the stamp on the final bracket sheet stayed exactly where it was, because the archive amends nothing, ever, on principle.

The file's spine card read *M. of Ragunna, contender, junior circuit through Agon XXXI.*

The archivist had been told, by the envelope's most recent note, to expect an eleventh delivery in the spring.

Where a signature goes, the note carried a small drawing, and the archivist had tried to copy it once for the catalogue, and had given up, and had written *device, unclear* in the field, and moved on, because there were nine hundred other files and the light in the stacks was terrible.

## VII. The Appointment

"What do you actually see," Lupa said. "When it's on."

"Threads."

"That's what you tell the instructors."

"That's what there is." Chisa held her mug in both hands. "Red lines, mostly. Between people, out of people. They move when the person moves and they go slack when the person stops meaning it. In Honami they'd gone bad — they had a smell, if a thing you see can have a smell, and they went after the Left Behind and pulled them back round the loop." She paused. "In the Nihility they were the other kind. Sunk into her. Going down."

"You cut those."

"I cut those."

"Do I have one?"

"You have several. One of them is behind you at about the height of a spear and I have never once looked at where it goes, because that would be rude and because I suspect you'd feel it." Chisa drank. "You'd feel it. You feel everything."

Lupa's ears did something complicated.

"Do you?" she said.

"Do I what?"

"Have one."

Chisa put the mug down.

"I've never looked," she said.

The vents cycled. Down at the reservoir, something splashed, which was probably a laurel-headed problem for the morning.

"Why?"

"Because I'd know where it ended." Level. Flat. Exactly the voice she used to announce that the tea was ready. "And then I'd spend the rest of it looking at that instead of at the thing in front of me, and I've already had twenty years of a city where the ending was known in advance, and I found the experience instructive."

"Don't," Lupa said.

"That was my conclusion, yes."

"No. I mean — " Lupa put her mug down too hard and it rang on the concrete. "Don't. As an instruction. From me. Don't look."

"Noted."

"I'm serious."

"So am I. It's noted." Chisa tipped her head. "You've gone loud again."

Lupa breathed out.

"There was a woman in a barber's chair on Fourth Ward," Chisa said, "about six weeks ago."

"That's a sentence with a lot of places to go."

"Grey hair coming off her onto the floor, and the ends of it were gold." Chisa was looking at the reservoir. "And there were shears in her lap that she wasn't using, because the barber had her own, and those ones were grey with gold at the tips, and the woman was letting a stranger cut her hair with cheap steel while she held the other pair in her lap like a coat."

Lupa had gone entirely still, the way she went still on sand.

"I know her," Chisa said.

"From where."

"The trap. The burned dormitory, the dolls with our faces. You were somewhere else in it, being asked to do something unforgivable." She said that without any inflection at all, which was its own kind of care. "The whole thing was a lie top to bottom and then a woman walked into my part of it and cut the lie down in one stroke, the way you'd take a hedge, and she was the only true thing in that entire building. I asked her for the shears."

"You asked a — " Lupa stopped. "You asked."

"I'd just been shown a room where everyone I love was a prop. I wanted the tool that ends things." Chisa turned her mug. "She said no. She said I'd chosen the thread, and that cutting fate means cutting through your own heart to reach it. Then she left. On one word."

"Which word."

"*Eventually.*"

The false stars ticked over. Four floors down, a bard had given up on the stairwell and the stairwell had won.

"And she was getting a haircut," Lupa said.

"On Fourth Ward. Between the framer's and the place that sells the bad umbrellas." Chisa nodded slowly. "She looked at me in the mirror and put her chin down. About an inch. The way one tradesman does to another across a street."

"What did she look like?"

Chisa turned and looked at Lupa for a full three seconds, which was rare, and which meant it was deliberate.

"I don't know," she said.

Lupa laughed. It got about half a breath out of her and then stopped in the middle, because the joke turned over while it was still in the air and showed her its other side.

"Yeah," she said. "All right."

"I went back the next week."

"Of course you did."

"There's a book on the counter for appointments. Six weeks out, same chair, same hour." Chisa said it in the flat voice, the reporting one. "There's a name written in it."

"What name."

"I read it standing at the counter and I got as far as the door and it was gone."

Lupa's tail moved once and went still.

"That's happened to me twice," Chisa said. "The city took twenty years out of me a night at a time. She took four letters in nine steps. I stood on the pavement outside a barber's shop with the door still shutting behind me and I knew exactly what had been done and exactly how, and I could tell you the shape of the hole, and I could not tell you the word."

"You went back again."

"Third time I asked the barber about the tip." A small movement at the corner of her mouth. "She's an interesting woman. She keeps it in the drawer with the spare clippers, wrapped in tissue, and she's never spent it and never will and she can't tell you why. She showed me. She's been showing people for a year and a half."

"And?"

"It's a thread. It's gold. It goes somewhere."

"Chisa."

"I looked at where it goes," Chisa said, "and I put the drawer back, and I'm still deciding what I saw, and when I've decided I'll say."

Lupa sat with the whole of it. Above them the wind machines changed to their fourth-hour setting and the terrace got a degree cooler, and the washing stiffened another shade.

"She gave you a season," Lupa said.

"She gave me a word."

"A word with a season in it. That's more than anyone gets." Lupa picked her mug up, found it cold, drank it anyway. "I've been ready to die since I was seven. Luggage in the hall, packed, by the door. What I've never had is the shape of it — the how and the when, and the not-knowing sits in me like a stone in a boot and it has done for twenty-five years." She set the mug down. "And somebody handed you a date and you're standing there being *interested* about it."

"I'm interested."

"That's worse."

"It's what I've got."

"It's — " Lupa's jaw worked. "It's the worst possible thing and I'd take it off you in a heartbeat and I'd know the shape of mine at last and I'd sleep like a child, and that's a disgusting thing to want and I want it, and I've now said it out loud on a laundry roof, so."

"You didn't have to say it."

"You'd have heard it in the third syllable."

"I heard it in the first."

They sat.

"When it comes," Lupa said, "wherever it is —"

"You'll be somewhere else." Chisa said it gently, which she almost never did. "You're the wall. Walls stand where they're built."

"Then I'll move the wall."

"That's not how walls —"

"Then it'll be a *bad wall*," Lupa said, "and it'll be *there*," and her tail struck the concrete once, and the sound went out across the drying terrace and down four floors into a building full of sleeping people who did not wake, because they had all learned the sound years ago and their bodies had filed it under *safe*.

## VIII. Third Ward

Ossi ran the noodle stand on the Third Ward corner, six tables and a counter and a stove she had inherited with a curse attached, and she had opinions about her regulars, which she kept, mostly, in the way of a woman who understands that a stand is a confessional with better lighting.

The manager sat on the counter. It had come in one evening about a year and a half back out of the tram crowd, wet from nothing, entirely calm, and had climbed onto the counter and sat down at the end where the till was, and had been there since. Ossi fed it greens. Customers photographed it. The health inspector had visited twice, had a bowl each time, and written *no findings.*

The Ashinohara woman started coming on Thursdays in the late summer.

She was sixty or a little past, small, straight-backed, with her hair pinned up in a way nobody in Lahai-Roi wore it and grey coming in at the temples in two clean streaks. She ordered by pointing the first week. By the second she had the words for the pork and the words for the egg, said carefully, in the accent of somebody who had learned the sounds off a page.

She always ordered two bowls.

She ate one. The other sat across from her going cold for an hour, and at the end of the hour she paid for both in exact coins from a purse with a clasp, and bowed twice, and went out to the tram.

Ossi charged her for one, the third week. The woman came back the following Thursday and put the difference on the counter in a folded paper, and bowed, and said, in her careful sounds, *"The price is the price."* Ossi took it and never tried again.

The manager got down off the counter the first day she came in and sat by her feet for the whole hour, and had done so every Thursday since, for no one else, ever, including a Guild inspector and a woman who had once tried a fish.

She brought a box. It sat on the chair beside her, wrapped in a cloth with a knot on top, about the size of a folded jacket. She put a hand on it sometimes while she was waiting. It never opened at the counter, and Ossi never asked, because there is a way people set a thing down beside them that tells you the whole shape of the question and also tells you to leave it alone.

Some Thursdays the girl came.

She came in on the hour or a quarter past, and she was eighteen and had scissors at her hip the size of a small door, and Ossi had recognized her the first time from the news the way everyone in the city recognized all six of them, and had decided within a second and a half that recognizing people is a service the stand does not offer.

They ate. They talked about very little. Ossi caught pieces of it across four months: the tram lines, a leaking tap, whether the false sun's evening was too orange, a long negotiation about whether the girl was sleeping. Twice the woman reached over and straightened the girl's collar and the girl let her. Once the girl asked something short, and the woman went quiet for a while, and then answered something short, and they went back to the noodles.

The woman said the same thing at every parting. Three times. Ossi had it phonetically in her head and had looked it up eventually at the Ward library, and it meant *eat properly.*

The girl said *yes* three times, each time, in the same tone, the tone of a person who has been given three chances to be irritated and has declined all three.

"Your granddaughter," Ossi said once, at the till.

"My daughter," said the woman.

Ossi did not ask again, and stopped doing the arithmetic on the two faces about a week later, and put the whole thing in the drawer where a stand-keeper keeps what the customers set down beside them.

There was a notebook.

It came out on the Thursdays the girl failed to arrive. Small, cheap, bought at the corner stationer's, and the woman worked in it with a pencil for the whole hour and then put it away and paid for two bowls.

Ossi read it upside down across the counter one evening while wiping, and it was a list of names, in the local alphabet, copied out in a careful heavy hand and copied again below and again below that, down the page and onto the next, the way children do their characters in a schoolbook.

Seven names.

Ossi knew three of them from the news and two from the news and the mural on the emergency wall, and she read the whole page twice while pretending to work on a stain, and the woman turned the page and started again from the top, and got the fourth name wrong on the second stroke, and crossed it out, and did it again.

---

The other thing Ossi kept was the man.

She saw him once, in the autumn, standing at the tram shelter across the road with his hands at his sides. Sixties. Ashinohara coat, the good kind, and shoes that had been shined by somebody who did it professionally. He stood there for the better part of forty minutes and looked at the stand and made no move toward it.

At a quarter past, the girl with the scissors came down the Ward road, and the man turned and got onto the tram that had just pulled in, which was going the wrong way for anyone who lived in that direction.

Inside, the woman was sitting where she always sat. She had not looked out. Ossi would have sworn to it in any hearing.

When the tram pulled away the woman put her hand flat on the box beside her and left it there for a moment.

Then the girl came in, and the woman turned to the door, and her whole face went somewhere else entirely, and Ossi went and got the pork.

## IX. The Census

The parcel had come up from Septimont on the shuttle four days back, forwarded by a harbormaster who had held it in his office for eleven weeks with a note pinned to it saying WAIT.

Six jars of leaf, sealed in wax for sea travel, packed in straw, with a chop on the lid that Iuno had turned over twice in her one hand and identified as older than the Order, older than the Republic, older than most of the coastline's opinions about itself. And a card in the straw. Seventeen characters, in a fast square official hand, and a translation on the reverse in a junior's careful print.

*Whenever the road brings you east, the kettle is on.*

Lupa had read it out at the table because Lupa was the one who read things out. Iuno had said *ah* in a very small voice and then gone and stood at the window with her back to the room and said something about the vents. Cartethyia had proposed leaving that night, and then, twenty minutes later, having thought it through with rigor, leaving tomorrow. Ciaccona had taken the card, held it up to the light, put it down, and started tuning, which in Ciaccona was a full standing ovation. Amy had asked what the tea tasted like and Mei had said she had no idea, and Amy had said *then we should go*, and that had been that, in the way that things become that in this pack: a girl says a sentence and eight adults rearrange a year.

And Mei had said nothing for a while, which was the loudest thing in the room, because she had gone somewhere in her head where a road ran east and kept running.

They were going east. Sometime after the term, before the cold. The word *Jinzhou* stayed off the table all evening and sat in every head around it.

That was the rowdy night. That was what the eight of them had been drinking about, four floors down, while the ninth washed a pot.

---

"There's a woman in the Third Ward," Lupa said, "eating noodles on Thursdays."

Chisa's hands stopped where they were, on her knees, and stayed stopped.

"Three weeks," Lupa said. "Three weeks and four days. She takes the eleven-forty tram in from the East Terrace rooms and the one-ten back."

"You've been to the stand."

"I like the noodles."

"You hate the noodles."

"I like the manager." Lupa looked at the washing. "I count who's in this city. It's a small city. Somebody new comes in on a nine-week passage from a country that issues four exit permits a year and books a single room off the East Terrace and stays, and I hear about it inside a day, because that's the trade, and because a Guild clerk on the fourth floor of the passage office owes me for a thing I'd rather not itemize."

"Three weeks and four days," Chisa said.

"I've had it in my mouth for three weeks and four days." Lupa turned her mug. "It's been heavy."

Chisa looked out at the reservoir a long time.

"Say the thing you want to say," she said.

"I want to say I'll bring soup."

"That's the thing."

"That's the thing." Lupa's ears went flat and stayed there. "I've had a list going since the second week. Room's too small. East Terrace is a twelve-minute walk to the nearest medic and she's got a knee. There's a stove on that floor with a bad regulator and I've been composing a letter to the housing office in my head for eleven days, in a tone I would describe as *unhelpful.*"

"I know."

"And I want to come in and count her and put my hand on the table and say the word, because that's what I'm for, and the word is binding when I say it, and she'd be ours by supper and stay ours forever." Lupa's tail lay dead flat on the concrete. "That's the whole of it. That's what I've been carrying up your stairs on my heels for a week so you'd hear me coming."

Chisa turned her head.

"That's why I haven't told you," she said.

"I know."

"If you say it in the room, she stops being my mother."

"I know."

"She becomes ours. And then she's a woman the whole pack is kind to, and there'll be nine people being kind to her, expertly, all the time, and Cartethyia will learn her name in the old alphabet and Iuno will fix her housing in an afternoon and Mei will look at her the way Mei looks at people and that's the end of it — she'll be safe and warm and counted and I will never once find out what she is to me on her own." Chisa's voice stayed level the entire way through. "I have had her back for a year and two months. I have had her *here* for twenty-five days. Twenty of those were the crossing. I've had five Thursdays."

"Five Thursdays."

"Four. She was ill on the second." Chisa picked up her cold tea and drank it. "She's sixty-three. She spent twenty years keeping a room. She got a form off the prefecture with an eight-month wait on it and she stood in that line, and she got on a boat, and she came to a city inside a god where the sun is a machine, at sixty-three, alone, without telling me she was coming. She was here nine days before I found out. She'd taken the room and learned two tram lines and found a stand with a good corner seat and she was *waiting for me to have time.*"

Lupa closed her eyes for a second.

"Yeah," she said.

"So." Chisa set the mug down. "You see the problem."

The wind machines went over. Below them the whole warm apparatus of Lahai-Roi hummed on in its slow shifts, a city breathing inside a kneeling god, and four floors down eight people slept in a pile of blankets and bad decisions.

"We hunt together," Lupa said. "That's the rule."

"I know the rule."

"The rule's mine." Lupa opened her eyes. "Rules have authors. I wrote that one in a corridor in Ragunna at two in the morning because nobody ever wrote one for me and I decided somebody ought to be doing it, and I've said it over a priestess and a mercenary and a knight and you, and every single time I said it, it was because the person in front of me had run out of ways to say it about themselves." Her tail stayed flat. "You haven't run out. You've got five Thursdays and a plan and a stand with a good corner seat. So I'm holding it."

Chisa looked at her.

"You'll hear it from me first," Lupa said, "or you won't, and either way I'll hear it from you and not from a Guild clerk on the fourth floor. That's the whole of what I want."

"That cost you."

"Everything does."

---

The kettle had gone off the boil twice and neither of them noticed.

"Does he know?" Lupa said.

"She came alone."

Lupa waited about a second and a half, which for her was an eternity of tact, and then let it go, and her tail moved once against the concrete in a way that meant the letting-go had a receipt on it and the receipt was filed.

Chisa noticed. Chisa noticed everything below the waist and behind the voice and had done since she was fifteen.

"Kuresaki," Lupa said.

"What?"

"The town. On the collar. There's a stamp." Lupa gestured vaguely at her own throat. "You said the pattern's named for the town. So there's a town, and there's a bench in it, and there's a man at the bench who owns a hammer with a wave on the end of it. How far off the eastern route?"

Chisa was quiet.

"Two days," she said. "Three, with the ferry crossing at Hitosaki."

"Three days."

"Three days."

"Mm," said Lupa, and drank her cold tea, and put the mug down, and said nothing else about it at all, because she had converted the entire thing into a distance, which was the only love language she had ever been fluent in, and she had learned young to stop before the sentence that comes after.

"There's a letter in the tin," Chisa said. "With it."

"Right."

"I've written it four times."

"Right."

"I burned three."

Lupa nodded slowly at the laundry line, and made an inventory of it, and kept her mouth shut with an effort that Chisa could hear in her breathing, and Chisa let her have it, because that was also a kind of gift.

## X. Coda: Column Nine

Helios came up the way it came up: a long slow brightening from ember through amber, the Solistrees taking their hum a half-step higher across the terraces, the whole cavern remembering itself in gold. Below, the Ward woke in its usual order — the bakeries, then the trams, then the arguing.

They took the washing down together, because it was dry and because it was there.

Two forms had made these two women, and both of the forms were still in use. In Ashinohara, §4.2, the inner face: bare alloy, no lining, no applied mark, drafted at a table in a house that kept its shutters closed, on the advice of a guest in an alcove who had explained that comfort dulls the lesson and the lesson was the product. In Septimont, column nine: hometown, in the athlete's own hand, blanks disallowed, drafted in a corridor by a woman who had walked out of a fourth-terrace gate with a spear and two changes of clothes.

One country looked at a marked child and read *hazard*, and built an instrument, and put the instrument on her at sixteen in a storm.

One country looked at a marked child and read *yield*, and named a price, and fitted the price over her shoulders at seven in a scorched ring, and she stood still for it, because standing still was what every face in the village was asking.

Both instruments came with a specification. Both specifications had an inside. And on the inside, in both countries, in the places the inspectors have never once thought to check, there were people with hammers and pens making very small unauthorized additions — a felt lining bought off the bolt with a smith's own money; a wave three lines wide; a number rounded up out of a guilt so cheap it could afford to be generous; eleven audit flags a year returned unexplained from an office that has never sat in session; a blank that must be filled, filled by a stranger, in a stranger's own hand.

Call the sum of it a residue. It is a thin thing to grow up on. Both of these women grew up on it, and it is the reason there is anybody standing on this roof at all.

"You've got a thread," Chisa said.

"Where."

"Hem. Left." Chisa had the scissors out of the loop at her hip before Lupa had finished turning — the big ones, her mother's, floral-handled, the pair that had cut a tower into six hells and a god-worm down its length and a door in the middle of nothing. "Hold still."

Lupa held still.

It took about a second. The blades went in flat against the cloth, closed on maybe four millimeters of loose grey thread, and opened again, and Chisa caught the piece and dropped it over the terrace rail and watched the vent-draft take it sideways and up.

Lupa let her breath out.

"You could've bitten that off," she said.

"I have scissors."

"You have scissors."

They finished the line. Iuno's blue over one arm, folded by Chisa in the correct manner, which she had learned by watching and had never mentioned. The bard's burnt cuff. Cartethyia's ongoing municipal matter. Amy's small sleeve with the tag still on it.

Chisa stood with the basket on her hip and did the count.

She counted the way she counted everything, which was by habit and hem and wear — this collar goes up at the back because its owner sleeps in it, this cuff has a bard's rosin, this seam has been mended by a woman with one hand and a great deal of stubbornness, this sock has been through a reservoir — and she reached the end of the line and said the number out loud, the way she did, level, the truest available sentence in the flattest available voice.

"Ten," she said.

Lupa opened her mouth to correct it.

The bronze light came up over the drying terrace and the reservoir and the East Terrace rooms twelve minutes from the nearest medic, and the eleven-forty tram was four hours out, and somewhere across two oceans a man in a Bureau annex checked his morning mail, which took four seconds.

Lupa shut her mouth.

"Ten," she said.

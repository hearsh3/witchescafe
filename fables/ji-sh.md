Chapter 1: Jiā Shū

The rain had stopped before dawn, leaving the flagstones outside the third storehouse dark around the mortar-joints and smelling of wet shale.

Yangyang sat on the low stone wall at the corner of the landing with her cloak drawn over her shoulders and a mug of boiled water between her palms. Behind the timber door, the morning was already loud: Lupa was hacking through a frozen winter melon on the butcher’s block with rhythmic *thwack-thwack* blows of her cleaver, Ciaccona was arguing with Cartethyia over whose boots had tracked red clay onto the hearth-rug, and down in the lane the cooper’s apprentice was hammering iron hoops onto a pickle tub. Over all of it rushed the steady, hollow roar of the storm-drains spilling last night’s runoff down the cliff into the river gorge.

Yangyang lifted the mug to blow on the steam, and paused.

She tilted her chin a fraction toward the southern pass, tuning out the cleaver and the cooper’s mallet and the water in the pipes.

Far down in the white belly of the valley mist, where the post-road doubled back on itself along the cliff face, someone was breathing hard through a scarf—and right under that breath came the rhythmic *chink-chink* of brass mule-bells, the wet groan of wooden brake-shoes pressed hard against iron wheel-rims, and the sharp crack of a carter’s whip echoing off the wet limestone.

Yangyang set her mug down on the sill, rested her left hand briefly against the linen wrap under her ribs as she stood, and reached inside the open door to tap twice on the wood.

“The road is open,” she told the room. “There are carts on the lower switchbacks.”

By the time the column cleared the last bend below the barbican, half of Xuanfang Hold was crowded onto the wet stone risers and the terraced walls above the lower gatehouse.

Warden Liangyu stood two paces out in front of the open iron-strapped gate leaves with her manifest board clamped against her splinted forearm, her breath puffing white in the sharp morning sun.

Out of the thinning fog came the lead team: four heavy, mud-caked mountain mules leaning into their breast-straps with long ribbons of steam blowing from their nostrils. Behind them rolled a long, creaking train of six freight drays, their wheels caked to the hubs in red gorge clay. The first two carts were stacked higher than a man’s head with fat burlap sacks of Jinzhou rice and yellow millet under lashed oilskins; the third carried casks of quicklime and heavy coils of braided iron cable for the eastern breach; the fourth and fifth groaned under baskets of black stove-coal and split hickory; and lashed to the tail-board of the lead wagon sat four nailed pine crates stencilled in blue ink with the willow-and-tower mark of Huaxu Academy.

They had not come up the gorge road alone.

Marching in two mud-splattered files on either side of the wheels was a full company of Jinzhou garrison soldiers in dark oilskin road-capes, their pikes sloped at a neat, identical slant and their boots striking the wet cobbles in an unbroken rhythm that rattled the puddle-water in the ruts. Behind the coal wagons walked four men in plain Ministry grey coats with their round felt hats pulled low over their brows. They carried no staves and no weapons, and as they passed beneath the shadow of the gatehouse arch, their eyes moved methodically up the sheared stone of the curtain wall to the dark, stalled sphere of the Astrolabe Core hanging silent in its bronze calipers against the pale sky. None of them took a notebook from his sleeve.

Up by the guard-brazier at the foot of the stairs, Little Tan shifted his grip on his spear shaft until his leather cuff creaked, his eyes following the grey coats through the arch.

“That’s a whole pike company from the south barracks,” Little Tan muttered out of the side of his mouth to the young Warden beside him. “And Ministry surveyors. What’re they sending surveyors and southern pikes up here for *after* the fog’s cleared? There’s nothing left in the valley to fight.”

“Shut your mouth and unhook the tail-chain, Tan,” old Warden Ge said without slowing his stride as he walked past with an oak chock-block on his shoulder. He tapped the butt of his pike once against the back of Little Tan’s calf. “Lime gets wet on that wagon, it’ll burn a hole through the floorboards before dinner. Get your shoulder under the barrel.”

At the top of the gatehouse steps, Mei was parked squarely on an upturned salt-tub, wrapped from armpit to ankle in two grey wool blankets like a very cheerful caterpillar. Lupa had wedged a flat stone in front of the tub so Mei couldn’t rock forward onto her toes, and Iuno stood at her left elbow with her right hand resting lightly on the wool over Mei’s shoulder.

“Look at the size of those rice sacks!” Mei called out happily around a dried apricot, craning her neck to see over the pikemen’s helmets. “That’s the long-grain stuff from the south polders, I recognize the red twine! Lupa, we can make the sticky rice balls with the sesame paste inside!”

Lupa wasn’t looking at the rice. She was leaning her shoulder against the gatehouse buttress with her arms folded over her chest and her wolf ears tipped sharp forward, her gaze running down the Jinzhou vanguard captain’s iron shoulder-guards, the clean oil on his men’s spear-sockets, and the easy, unblown way the rear files took the steep stone pitch after a four-day climb.

“Hmph,” Lupa grunted, her tail giving a single slow swing against the stone. “Third file on the left’s got a decent step. Rest of ’em walk like they’ve been guarding a turnip ditch since spring.”

Beside Mei’s tub, Iuno hadn’t looked once at the rice sacks or the pikes.

Her eyes stayed on the four men in Ministry grey as they stopped beside the cistern intake, exchanged three quiet words with the Jinzhou captain, and turned up the east stair toward the administrative hall without asking a single Warden for directions.

*They already have the floor-plan, darling,* the High Priestess observed inside her head, cool and dry as chalk. *How considerate of Mingting to save us the tour.*

*Be quiet,* Iuno thought.

Down by the lead dray, where the carter was backing his mules toward the storehouse ramp, Amy had already broken every rule she had agreed to at breakfast.

She was supposed to stand by Warden Liangyu and sign the clipboard for the Huaxu boxes. Instead, she had a heavy pine crate of Academy condenser-parts hoisted against the front of her sheepskin vest with both hands, and she was standing in the middle of the wheel-rut lecturing the sixty-year-old Jinzhou driver over the lid of the box.

“I’m just saying, mister, look at your left rear shackle!” Amy called out, jerking her chin toward the grease-blackened iron leaf-spring under the wagon bed. “You’ve got a sheared cotter-pin held in with bailing wire and your axle-camber’s sagging inward! If you go back down the Hairpin empty with the spring bouncing like that, your wheel’s gonna beat you to the ferry by twenty minutes!”

“That wire’s held from the north bridge to the pass, little miss!” the carter barked back around his pipe-stem, reaching down into the straw at the front of the boot. “Mind your toes, here comes the post!”

With a heave of his shoulders, the driver swung four fat, bulging canvas mail sacks over the side-rail into the arms of two waiting Wardens.

The canvas was stained dark at the corners with river-spray and mule-sweat, and stuffed so tight with bundled paper that the seams showed white thread. Around the gathered throat of every sack, right above the hemp tie-cord, was pasted a fresh, unbroken collar of thick white mulberry paper stamped three times in bright vermilion ink with the square seal of the Mingting Internal Security Agency.

On the steps above the arch, Iuno’s gaze dropped to the red stamps on the canvas necks.

Beside her on the salt-tub, Mei stopped chewing her apricot for a second as the two Wardens heaved the four sacks onto a wooden hand-barrow, leaned into the shafts, and began wheeling the post up the broad stone risers toward the third terrace.

By the first bell after noon, the two largest canvas sacks sat slumped and open at the foot of the long pine table like moulted grain-bags, filling the warm storehouse with the smell of river-barge tar, damp wool, and cold red sealing wax.

Every single letter, bill, and packet that came out of the canvas had been neatly slit along the top fold with a steel paper-knife somewhere down the road and pasted shut again with a strip of gummed official paper bearing a small red inspection mark.

Nobody at the table commented on the strips.

Amy had appointed herself postmaster. She sat cross-legged on the end of the pine board with her newly pried-open crate of Huaxu Terminal components wedged between her boots, a pair of Lupa’s bamboo frying-tongs in her right hand for fishing into the bottom corners of the sack, and an oil-smudged courier manifest balanced on her knee.

“Next!” Amy barked, fishing a stiff yellow folded sheet out of the canvas with the tongs and slapping it down on the wood in front of Buling’s teacup. “Forwarded from South Wharf Lane, Jinzhou, via three separate bailiffs! Looks expensive!”

Buling flipped the yellow paper talisman on her forehead up onto the round brim of her *nuanmao*, leaned both elbows on the table, and squinted at the column of red brush-strokes.

“Aiya,” Buling said. “This landlord got no shame at all. Look at this. Eight hundred credits for the back room, plus forty credits ‘chimney maintenance fee’! That stove pipe was leaking smoke into the rafters before I even put my bed in! Every time the downstairs neighbour fried salted fish, my blanket smelled like a dockyard!”

“Are you going to pay it?” Cartethyia asked earnestly across the teapot.

“Pay what? Look at the date, he charged me for the whole tenth month also, I wasn’t even in the city!” Buling pulled a flat carpenter’s charcoal stub out of her royal-blue silk sleeve, licked the tip, and drew two fierce black strokes straight through the landlord’s total, writing *THREE HUNDRED MAX, PIPE SPOIL ALREADY* across the bottom margin as if the paper were going to walk back down the mountain and argue for her.

Amy reached back into the sack without looking and hauled out a heavy, two-foot cylinder wrapped in brown butcher’s wax-paper and bound in greasy hemp twine. There was no envelope and no stamp—only *THE WOLF* scrawled across the wax in thumb-thick black charcoal.

Lupa caught it one-handed before it hit the board, snapped the twine with her side teeth, and peeled back the paper to reveal a massive coil of dark, garlic-studded Jinzhou blood-sausage smoked black as bog-oak.

She sniffed it once, her wolf ears pricking straight up, and hacked a two-inch hunk off the end with her belt knife.

“Ha!” Lupa grinned around the mouthful, shoving the rest of the coil toward the bread-board. “Old Butcher Ma behind the south shambles. Bet me ten silvers in the summer I couldn’t carry his scalding-vat across the yard without spilling the lard. Still owes me the silvers, but the old bastard knows how to cure pig.”

“Two from the foreign mail packet!” Amy sang out, tossing a crumpled buff envelope covered in purple harbour-stamps toward the window-seat, and handing a smaller, flour-dusted square of folded bakery paper across the salt-jar to Cartethyia.

Ciaccona slit the buff envelope with her thumb-nail, pulled out a single sheet of Ragunnesi notary paper, and let out a tragic groan, dropping the back of her head against the window-frame.

“My homeland remembers me,” Ciaccona told the rafters. “The harbour-master of Portunnus wishes to inform me that my cousin’s lute license expired in August, and the tavern by the fish-pier is still billing me twelve shells for a brass chandelier that fell down while I was playing *The Doge’s Breeches*.”

“You stood on the table during the chorus, Cia,” Cartethyia pointed out mildly, before turning her attention to her own square of bakery paper.

Out of the fold dropped two curled strips of sugared dried lemon peel from Egla Town. Cartethyia picked one up, placed it carefully on her tongue, and read the large, round, slightly crooked handwriting from top to bottom with her chin propped in both palms. When she reached the floury thumbprint at the bottom corner, she moved her eyes straight back to the first line and read the whole page through a second time, her lips moving silently over the names of the ovens.

Amy peered into the bottom of the first sack, shook the canvas once, and looked down the bench toward Chisa, who sat quietly beside Lupa’s elbow with her hands around her plain porcelain cup.

“Nothing from Lahai-Roi in this batch, Chisa,” Amy said, her voice dropping out of its carnival bark. “I checked the bottom seam twice.”

Chisa nodded once, her face completely calm above the pale blue silk tie tucked into her collar. “My mother posts on Thursdays,” she said. “The sea-packet goes round the south cape, not through Jinzhou. It will come when we reach the river.”

“Here’s the heavy brick for the legal department,” Amy said, lifting a thick bundle of official dispatches bound in red tape out of the second sack and setting it by Iuno’s right wrist.

On top of the Ministry circulars and garrison quartermaster receipts lay a single cream-colored envelope of heavy bamboo paper, addressed in Jinhsi’s clear, upright hand to Iuno and the household. Across its back flap, slicing right through the pale wax impression of the Jinzhou yamen, ran the pasted white strip and red ink-stamp of the Mingting censors.

Iuno turned the cream envelope over once with her living hand, looked at the sliced wax, and set it neatly beside her saucer without opening it.

“And one for Ma!” Amy announced, pulling a single folded sheet of lined account-book paper out of the sack and holding it up by the corner. “Smells like green onions!”

Mei practically bounced on her woollen seat-cushion before remembering her stitches and catching her breath with a flat palm against her ribs. She snatched the lined sheet out of the air, unfolded it across her knees, and read the first three lines with her mouth falling open in pure, radiant delight.

“It’s from Mrs. Ren!” Mei crowed, holding the onion-scented paper up with both hands as if she had just been awarded a dukedom. “Look! She wrote to me herself! She says the yellow marigolds in the cracked pot by the cistern made it through the frost, and she’s keeping my winter quilt in the cedar chest with the camphor balls, and Chixia’s been eating three bowls of lotus-root and pork-rib soup a day in the front room!”

Mei lowered the paper an inch, beaming straight at Iuno.

“And she says the window latch is still broken, and the carpenter’s definitely coming on Thursday!”

“The carpenter has been coming on Thursday since the sixth month,” Iuno said dryly, though she reached across the corner of the table and tucked the slipping edge of the grey blanket back around Mei’s knee.

At the foot of the board, Amy didn’t call out the last name.

She laid the bamboo frying-tongs down on the pine plank, reached both hands into the bottom of the second sack, and lifted out what was left.

She walked it up the side of the bench and set it down in front of Yangyang.

There were three things.

The first was a fat, lopsided wad of eleven envelopes tied together in a fierce bundle with a length of frayed red patrol cord. No two envelopes were the same size or colour: one had been folded out of the back of a harbour-ward parking summons, one was a torn sheet of brown noodle-shop wrapping paper with a dark ring of soy sauce dried into the corner, and the top one had a little round rubber crutch-tip drawn in black ink beside Yangyang’s name.

The second was a single, crisp, square white envelope bearing the embossed seal of Huaxu Academy, dated on the back flap in small, exact characters.

The third was a soft, lumpy parcel the size of a turnip, wrapped in grey dispensary paper and tied up in six tremendous, lopsided granny-knots of red twine, with a neat rectangular label in Baizhi’s handwriting pasted squarely over the middle knot so it wouldn’t come undone in the mail-cart.

For a beat nobody said anything.

Yangyang set her teacup down on the board. Slowly, she drew the fat bundle, the square white envelope, and the lumpy parcel off the wood and held them together in her lap. She didn’t untie the patrol cord. Along the line of her jaw, the small cerulean feathers of her collar rose all the way up and stayed there.

Over by the brick hearth, Lupa flipped the iron steamer lid open with a clatter, plucked two fat roast-pork buns out of the steam with her bare fingers, wrapped them in a clean linen napkin, and walked past the bench to drop the warm bundle right on top of the brown-paper parcel in Yangyang’s lap.

“Sun’s still hitting the stone wall behind the upper cistern,” Lupa said roughly, already turning back toward the stove to stir the soup pot. “Go eat those before the lard sets. Too much damn paper in here anyway, smells like a tax office.”

Beside Iuno, Mei looked across the pine plank and gave Yangyang a small, warm nod.

Yangyang gathered the letters and the buns against her chest with her right arm, stood up carefully from the bench, and stepped out into the afternoon sun.

The low stone parapet behind the upper cistern was out of the wind, sheltered by the broad timber gable of the third storehouse and facing west over the lower yards. Down below the terrace wall, the six Jinzhou wagons stood unhitched in neat rows along the gravel, their shafts resting on wooden trestles while the mules rolled on their backs in the dry dust.

Yangyang sat on the sun-warmed slate step at the angle of the wall, her cloak tucked beneath her hips and the fat bundle of envelopes resting on her knees.

She hadn’t even finished working the first knot of Chixia’s red patrol cord over the corner of the bundle when the scullery door creaked behind the cistern, and Mei came shuffling around the corner at a slow, flat-backed walk. She had her grey wool blanket belted around her waist with a length of clothesline like a heavy quilted barrel-skirt, and she was carrying two earthenware mugs of hot ginger water at chest height.

“Permission to sit on the warm rock,” Mei said, stopping two paces off. “I didn’t climb anything, there were zero steps between the scullery and the cistern, and Lupa watched me the whole way.”

“The step is wide enough,” Yangyang said, shifting her cloak six inches to the left to clear the sunniest slab of slate.

Mei lowered herself down in two careful stages—bracing one hand on the cistern rim so her abdomen stayed flat—set the second mug down by Yangyang’s boot, and leaned her back against the warm stone gable with a contented sigh.

Yangyang slipped the frayed red patrol cord off the stack of eleven envelopes without cutting it, winding the red cord twice around her left wrist so the loose ends dangled against her sheer sky-blue cuff.

The envelopes weren’t numbered, and the Mingting censors had clearly given up trying to put them back in the order they were written before pasting their white inspection strips across the flaps. Yangyang slid her thumb under the top flap—the one folded out of a yellow harbour-ward parking ticket—and unfolded the cramped, scrawling pages.

She read three lines. A sudden, quiet laugh escaped her nose, and she had to press her left palm flat against her ribs as her shoulders shook.

“Oh, come on, you can’t laugh at a parking ticket and not read it,” Mei said, leaning her shoulder lightly against Yangyang’s uninjured right arm. “Did she arrest the tax-office goose again?”

“This one is from the morning the fog came back,” Yangyang said, her mouth curving up at the corner as she angled the yellow sheet into the sun and read aloud:

> *yangyang the stupid screen just went grey right when i was gonna tell you about the pepper oil. i kept typing for ten minutes before baizhi came in and took the terminal off me because she said the mountain ate the signal again. she says i have to write on paper now like an old magistrate in an opera. my handwriting looks like a crab fell in the inkstone and walked across the table. if you cant read the bottom line it says EAT YOUR GREENS AND DONT LET LUPA DARE YOU INTO ANYTHING*

Mei grinned into her ginger water. “She knows Lupa.”

Yangyang shuffled to the third envelope in the pile—a sheet of Mrs. Ren’s coarse brown butcher paper with a wide, dried crescent of red chili-oil soaked into the bottom margin—and read on:

> *mrs ren made lotus root and pork ribs again and she wont let me hop to the kitchen on the crutch because the rubber foot squeaks on her tiles and wakes the cat. i told her i named the crutch yangyang and now every time supper's ready she yells PUT YANGYANG IN THE CORNER BEFORE YOU SIT DOWN with a totally straight face. also jinhsi came down the lane yesterday on her way back from the harbour!! she didn't even bring the big guard cohort, just sanhua, and she stood right outside the window gap in the drizzle and asked if we'd heard anything from the pass. i told her you've got three cracked ribs and blue feathers and you're probably trying to carry boxes anyway. DONT CARRY BOXES.*

“I haven’t carried any boxes,” Yangyang told the lower yard, mildly offended.

“You tried to lift the water-kettle with your left hand yesterday morning and Wen hit your knuckles with a wooden spoon,” Mei pointed out cheerfully. “What else has she got?”

Yangyang pulled out a long, narrow strip torn from the back of a Huaxu dispensary diet chart. Half the lines were scored through with such ferocious black ink that the pen-nib had torn little holes in the paper:

> *i hate this bench. i hate this pillow. i know every crack in mrs rens ceiling plaster by heart. everyone is up there on that stupid mountain and im down here eating soft noodles with medium spice like a baby. ~~if the road doesnt open by friday im borrowing the coal-mans mule~~ baizhi just read that over my shoulder while she was changing the gauze and she says if i go within twenty yards of a mule she will tape my good leg to the bedpost. open the brown parcel last. it's for her, not you. don't wear it on the parapet, your head is the wrong shape now with the bird stuff and i'll know if you stretched the wool.*

Mei laughed out loud, one hand pressed carefully over her belly-wrap. “She’s going to inspect the ear-flaps with a ruler the second we walk into Jinzhou.”

Yangyang’s fingers moved to the bottom of the bundle.

Wedged between the last two big envelopes was a tiny, square packet folded out of thin blue note-paper, creased down until it was no bigger than a walnut shell. Even the Mingting censor had barely been able to paste his white strip across the corner without covering the whole thing.

Yangyang unfolded the blue slip in her lap.

There were no grease stains on it, and no crossed-out words. The pencil lead was pale and uneven, written in the dark with the paper resting against a bent knee.

Yangyang didn’t read this one aloud. Her voice stayed quiet inside her chest as her eyes moved down the four short lines:

> *it's past the third watch and the rain is blowing in through the window gap onto the floorboards. when the line went dead i kept waking up thinking the gorge had shut again and i'd made up the phone call in my head. i know i didn't make it up because amy's map is still sitting on the chair by the door. just come back down the road soon, okay. the room gets too quiet before mrs ren gets up.*

Beside her on the slate step, Mei didn’t lean over to look at the blue paper, and she didn’t ask what was on it. She simply took a slow sip of her ginger water and watched two sparrows down in the yard tug at a loose straw on the first Jinzhou wagon.

Yangyang read the four pencil lines a second time. Then she folded the blue slip back along its small, tight creases and slid it deep into the inner pocket of her tunic, right against the silk-wrapped square of Hsin’s Prism and the folded letters she had carried back across the dark.

She picked up the crisp, square white envelope from Huaxu Academy next, broke the censor’s tape with her fingernail, and drew out a single sheet of heavy grid-ruled paper.

She read it to herself in the quiet afternoon sun. Every character was small, upright, and written without a single blot from top to bottom:

> *Eighteenth day, tenth month. Huaxu Academy, Remnant Ecoacoustics.*
>
> *Watcher,*
>
> *The patient’s flank incision was cleared of sutures on the fourteenth. She will have informed you in her correspondence that she walked to the corner noodle stall unassisted on Wednesday. In point of fact, she leaned against Mrs. Ren’s garden wall four times on the outward journey and required the porter’s hand-barrow to return. Both the leg and the side are healing cleanly.*
>
> *Do not remove the linen strapping from your seventh, eighth, and ninth ribs until you can draw a full breath in frost without a catch at the breastbone. If Luoqing attempts to test your vocal resonance before the bone has knit, refer him to my memorandum of the ninth.*
>
> *The narrow column beside the sixth entry of my incident book remains open for the document you described. Send ahead, in writing, with a date, before you descend the pass.*
>
> *The front room at Mrs. Ren’s is considerably louder than the dispensary, and Jinzhou is quieter than I prefer while you are away. Return when the road allows.*
>
> *— Baizhi.*

Along Yangyang’s jaw, the small cerulean feathers of her collar lifted once against her cheek and settled softly back down. She folded the white sheet neatly into its envelope and laid it on top of Chixia’s bundle.

Then she picked up the lumpy grey parcel and worked the last of Chixia’s six atrocious granny-knots loose with her thumbnails.

Inside the dispensary paper lay a winter cap.

It was a regulation Jinzhou harbour-ward patrol cap of thick crimson wool with long, fold-down ear-flaps—except someone had attempted to line the inside of the crown and the flaps with squares of soft grey rabbit-fur. Around the right ear-flap and the back of the neck, the fur was held on with enormous, crooked, looping basting-stitches of red thread that bunch-pleated the wool like a drawstring sack; then, halfway up the left ear-flap, a second needle with neat, tiny, indestructible stitches of dark blue thread had clearly confiscated the cap and finished the seam properly so the rabbit-fur wouldn’t fall off in the wind.

Pinned to the red visor with a brass dispensary safety-pin was a torn corner of card:

*FOR THE WIND. Tell her the fur is so she stops complaining about her ears. — C.*

Yangyang held the small crimson cap in both hands, her right thumb tracing the exact spot behind the left ear where the frantic red stitches gave way to Mrs. Ren’s tidy blue ones.

Mei looked sideways over the rim of her mug at the grey rabbit-fur sticking out around the brim.

“She tried to sew the fur on by herself in bed,” Mei said softly.

“Yes,” Yangyang said. “She always pulls the thread too tight when she reaches the corner.”

Mei rested her chin on her drawn-up knees, looking out over the terraced roofs of the Hold toward the south pass, where the gorge road dropped away into the bright, cold afternoon haze.

“First thing when we get back to Jinzhou,” Mei said plainly, “before we even unpack the bags at Mrs. Ren’s, we’re walking down the canal by the bird-bridge while the vinegar stall’s still open.”

Yangyang looked from the southern pass back down to the crimson wool in her hands.

“The man by the bridge closes his shutters at the fifth bell in winter,” Yangyang said. “We’ll have to go before supper.”

“Before supper,” Mei agreed, and leaned her temple lightly against Yangyang’s right shoulder.

Yangyang didn’t put the cap on her head. She folded the rabbit-fur ear-flaps inward so the wool wouldn’t crush, tucked the cap carefully inside the dry breast of her cloak beside the letters waiting for the winter solstice, and drew her patrol log and short cedar pencil out of her sleeve.

She opened the log to a clean page near the back, laid it flat on top of Chixia’s envelopes in her lap, and wrote a single line across the top in her small, level hand:

*The road is open, and the hat fits the pocket.*

She stayed there on the warm slate step with the red patrol cord wound loosely around her left wrist and the pencil resting still against the page, while down in the lower courtyard the late sun dropped behind the unhitched shafts of the Jinzhou carts.

Chapter 2: Luò Yuè

Three mornings later, the air in Huizhen Plaza smelled of steamed glutinous rice, wet quarry-stone, and coal smoke.

The Waking Moon Festival was two nights away, and the market terraces of Xuanfang Hold were fuller than they had been since the gates were unbarred—and tighter. Down on the lower tiers, two rows of Jinzhou garrison pikes stood racked in oiled-wood frames outside the old granary barracks, their polished iron points catching the flat grey light between the eaves. Up by the quartermaster’s shed at the head of the water-stair, two of the men in plain Ministry grey coats stood under the dry overhang of the tile roof with their hands tucked into their cuffs, asking the assistant tally-clerk quiet, unhurried questions about the depth of the main cistern piers and how many winters the eastern retaining wall had needed shoring.

Neither of the grey coats raised his voice, and fifty paces away in the crowd, the rumour moved from basket to basket without ever needing a speech.

By the salt-fish tubs, a bow-legged porter shifting sacks of Jinzhou millet onto a hand-barrow leaned in past the cooper’s ear and muttered that his brother-in-law on the baggage wagons had heard it straight from a Mingting courier at the ferry: once the Censure Courts finished clearing the last of the Tacet Discords out of the gorge seams, the Ministry was pulling the southern pikes back down the pass and letting Xuanfang Hold come down into the canyon for good.

Halfway up the broad stone risers beside the bell-frame, Little Tan and a freckled young Warden from the third watch stood with their backs to the balustrade and their thumbs hooked tight inside their spear-slings, staring across the roofs at the two Ministry clerks by the quartermaster’s door.

“Look at the sky, I’m telling you,” the freckled Warden whispered fast through his teeth, jerking his chin toward the dark, unmoving sphere of the Astrolabe Core hanging over the mist. “It hasn’t turned a hair since the rain. The blind investigator from Mingting took the Helmsmaster’s iron south in leaded boxes on Monday, the Sentinel’s pagoda’s still bolted from the inside, and now they’ve got Ministry clerks measuring the foundation piers. Nobody measures foundation piers unless they’re writing off the masonry.”

“Shut up, somebody’ll hear you—” Little Tan started, glancing nervously at a file of Jinzhou soldiers coming up from the bathhouse.

Old Warden Ge trudged up the middle of the stair between them carrying a bucket of black axle-grease in one hand and an iron crowbar in the other. He didn’t turn his head by a fraction of an inch as he passed their elbows.

“West gate hinges still take grease on Friday, boys,” Ge said to the steps ahead of him. “Move your feet before the sergeant finds you a broom.”

Down in the narrow cobbled court below the Cloud-Sleeve theatre, where the puddles from yesterday’s squall still sat dark between the flagstones, three small children in padded vests were jumping in turn over a length of hemp rope tied to a copper down-spout, slapping their wooden soles on the stone in time to the swing:

*“Moon Fox bolted up the door,*
*Left her shoes upon the floor,*
*When the censors clear the stone,*
*Down comes Xuanfang all alone—”*

Before they could start the second turn, their grandmother marched out of the turnip stall with a bunch of muddy white radishes in her fist, untied the hemp rope from the down-spout with one sharp jerk, and shoved it into her apron pocket.

“Go wash your hands at the trough before I box your ears, all three of you!” she snapped, and turned straight back to the stall-keeper to pinch a turnip. “Two coppers is highway robbery for frost-nipped greens, Old Liu, give me the ones from the back basket.”

And ten paces across the plaza, festival business refused to budge an inch for anybody.

The Moon Fox Rice Cake stall had set up its trestles on the exact corner by the well-head where it had stood every tenth month for sixty years, four tall cedar steamer-tiers stacked over a roaring charcoal drum and throwing out thick, sweet clouds of white rice-steam. Beside it, right outside the open doors of the eating-house, the Fillet Fairmaiden was standing on an upturned beer keg chalking *WAKING MOON DOUBLE-CARP POT — HALF PRICE IF YOU DRINK THE BROTH WITHOUT CRYING* across her slate signboard in letters big enough to read from the watchtower, while Ember and Stoke banged an iron frying-pan with a ladle and shoved wooden discount tallies into the hands of passing Jinzhou pikemen before they could cross the gutter.

In the queue at the rice-cake steamer, Buling had both hands planted on the hips of her royal-blue silk cheongsam, her yellow paper talisman flipped halfway up her nose as she leaned over the counter to peer into the open top tray.

Inside the cedar ring sat two dozen plump white glutinous-rice cakes, each one stamped on top in dark red bean-paste with the curled emblem of a nine-tailed fox.

“Hey, boss,” Buling said, pointing one fingernail at the left side of the steamer. “Look at those four by the rim. The steam dripped off the lid, the red paste ran together—now the fox tail is stuck to the ear, looks like a fat turnip with legs. Defective stamp. Cannot sell a turnip cake for Waking Moon price. Give me the four ugly ones for five coppers.”

“It’s sticky rice, miss, it puffs up when the fire’s hot!” the baker protested, waving his bamboo tongs. “The bean-paste inside is the exact same weight!”

“Outside appearance is half the festival luck,” Buling said briskly. “I eat a smudged fox, my whole winter goes crooked. Four for five coppers, I take them off your hands right now so the Jinzhou soldiers don’t see your quality control.”

Before the baker could draw breath to yell, Lupa stepped up behind Buling’s shoulder, dropped a handful of Jinzhou silver and copper onto the wooden board with a heavy *clink*, and jerked her thumb at the top two steamer tiers.

“Give me both trays, grandpa,” Lupa barked, “and wrap the four ugly ones in a separate leaf so the magistrate shuts her trap before the rice gets cold.”

A few yards away by the well-curb, Ciaccona leaned her hip against the carved stone parapet with her lute case slung across her back, one boot hooked over the other as she listened to the echo of the children’s interrupted skipping rhyme still bouncing around the theatre alley. She pulled a face as if she had just bitten into an unripe lemon.

“A flat fourth,” Ciaccona muttered under her breath. “Every single time a garrison town gets the shakes, the street kids drop the third and chant in a flat fourth. You’d think just once a rumour would arrive in a major key.”

Beside her, Cartethyia stood very straight under her crystal laurel, looking with grave concern from the dark Astrolabe Core overhead to the baker wrapping Lupa’s lotus-leaf bundles.

“Excuse me, sir!” Cartethyia called out earnestly as the baker tied the hemp string. “If the people in the lane believe the foundation stones under the Hold are going to give way once the gorges are cleared, why has the guild not sent for the quarry-masons? In Egla Town, when the sea-wall cracked behind the chapel, the whole parish carried mortar baskets after Sunday service!”

The baker’s bamboo tongs froze in mid-air. Two Jinzhou soldiers standing behind Lupa looked at each other, cleared their throats, and quietly stepped out of the queue toward the noodle shed.

“The stones under this plaza have been here since my great-grandfather’s time, little miss,” the baker said quickly to his steamer lid, keeping his voice low. “Here’s your lotus parcels, take them while they’re hot.”

Buling didn’t wait for Lupa to hand them out. She plucked one steaming white rice cake straight out of the top leaf, peeled the wet bamboo paper off the bottom, and bit the smudged red fox-head clean in half.

While she chewed the sweet, chewy rice, Buling’s bright red eyes shifted once toward the sheared eastern wall above the roofs—where the newer grey garrison masonry sat mortared into the huge, square-cut black basalt blocks of the mountain’s old bones—and then dropped to the worn culvert stone under her flat black shoe. She didn’t answer Cartethyia, and she didn’t look at the Ministry clerks up by the water-stair. She simply swallowed, licked a smear of red bean-paste off the corner of her thumb, and popped the rest of the cake into her mouth whole.

Down at the edge of the terrace, Amy walked slowly along the top of the drainage kerb with her newly soldered copper-loop Terminal held high above her pink head, turning the antenna toward the southern gap, then toward the west watchtower. The glass stayed flat and dark—not a single flicker on the relay bar. With a disgusted huff, Amy shoved the slate back into the deep pocket of Lupa’s sheepskin vest and kicked a loose pebble into the drain.

At the foot of the water-stair, Little Tan was still standing at his post beside the stone bollard. His knuckles were white around the ash shaft of his spear, and his eyes were fixed on the two Ministry grey coats fifty feet up the steps as one of them knelt to tap a small iron sounding-hammer once against the base of the cistern arch.

Chisa came down the steps beside Lupa with one of the warm lotus-leaf packets in her left hand.

When she reached the bottom riser, Chisa stopped two feet from Little Tan’s elbow. She didn’t look up at the Ministry clerks, and she didn’t look at the dark sky. She opened the corner of the lotus leaf, took out a single hot, fox-stamped rice cake on its square of bamboo paper, and set it down on the flat stone cap of the bollard right beside Little Tan’s hand.

“Your left greave-strap is twisted at the buckle,” Chisa told him in her plain, level voice. “If you stand on it until the watch changes, it will chafe your tendon.”

Little Tan blinked, his gaze dropping from the grey coats to the steaming white cake on the stone, and then down to his own shin. He let out a long, shaky breath through his nose, shifted his spear into his crook, and bent down to straighten the leather strap as Chisa walked on across the plaza.

***

By the seventh bell of the evening, the wooden shutters of the third storehouse were latched tight against the mountain mist, the brick stove was roaring with split birch and Jinzhou coal, and a wide stoneware platter of steamed Moon Fox Rice Cakes sat in the center of the pine table beside the teapot, their red bean-paste foxes shining in the yellow oil-lamp light.

Supper had been cleared to the scullery. At the hearth-end of the bench, Mei sat propped comfortably against her feather bolster with her knees tucked under her grey wool blanket and her hands wrapped around a mug of hot milk-coffee.

She nudged Iuno’s shin under the table with her toe.

“Come on, Iuiu,” Mei said. “You’ve had Jinhsi’s letter sitting in your coat since the carts came up, and everyone else already read theirs. Read it out! Did she say if the ducks are back under the willow bridge?”

Down the table, Cartethyia looked up hopefully from folding a square of bamboo steamer-paper, and Amy propped her chin in both palms next to the salt-jar.

Iuno set her teacup down on its saucer.

With her right hand, she reached into the right side of her heavy wool coat—the pocket on her living side, away from the small square velvet box resting flat against her left ribs—and drew out the cream-colored bamboo envelope. The sliced back flap, held together by the white gummed strip and red ink-stamp of the Mingting Internal Security Agency, showed clearly in the lamplight as she slid the folded mulberry sheet out with her thumb and forefinger.

She laid the envelope beside her saucer, angled the page toward the hanging oil-lamp, and read aloud in her clear, measured voice:

> *To the Lady Iuno and the household at Xuanfang Hold,*
>
> *We received your message with great relief when the line briefly cleared, and we have entered the names you spoke into the yamen’s fair copy with our own hand. Though the mountain relay has fallen silent again, the south road is now passable, and I trust the Huaxu supplies and the garrison escort have reached Warden Liangyu in good order.*
>
> *Jinzhou prepares for the Waking Moon as it does each autumn. The wisteria arbour over the north bird-bridge has come into flower again with the warm rain, and the petals are thick on the canal. Moon loaves from Panhua’s bakery have been waiting on my desk every noon, and I have been putting the dockets aside to eat them hot and sleep through the afternoon.*
>
> *Please tell the Outrider that her room at the barracks has been aired, and that we have asked no one else to lengthen her line on the roll. Is it not strange that Sanhua has gone down to the coast for a holiday and left her sword on the rack in the guardroom? Setting a coal brazier beside Baizhi’s desk in the Remnant wing has made her office much too warm for her record work, so she spends her mornings at Mrs. Ren’s instead. We keep the lamps lit by the water, and we wish you a quiet season until the road brings you south.*
>
> *— Jinhsi.*

Iuno lowered the cream mulberry sheet to the pine plank.

“A courteous letter,” Iuno said mildly, smoothing the fold with her thumb. “And phrased plainly enough that the censors in Mingting had no excuse to hold it back at the pass.”

Across the board, Yangyang sat with both hands resting around her porcelain teacup. She hadn’t reached for the rice-cake platter. Her dark eyes were fixed on the mulberry paper under Iuno’s wrist.

“There is no wisteria on the bird-bridge,” Yangyang said in her quiet, exact voice.

The storehouse went still. At the stove, Lupa paused with the iron kettle in her hand; down the bench, Amy took her chin out of her palms.

“Wait—what?” Mei asked, turning her head on the bolster.

“The north bird-bridge on the Jinzhou canal has two willow trees by the water-steps and open stone rails,” Yangyang said, looking straight across the teapot at Iuno. “The wisteria arbour is on the east canal lane in Mingting. And wisteria flowers in the spring, not before the Waking Moon.”

Mei blinked twice, and then sat up an inch straighter against her pillows.

“Hold on—and Panhua doesn’t have a bakery!” Mei said, pointing a finger at the letter. “Panhua runs a restaurant! She makes spicy beef noodles and twice-cooked pork! If you walked into Panhua’s kitchen at noon and asked her for a sweet moon loaf, she’d chase you into the street with a ladle!”

Iuno’s hand had gone completely still on the pine plank.

Slowly, her gaze dropped from Yangyang’s face to the sliced red Internal Security Agency seal on the back of the envelope, and then back to the neat, upright columns of ink on the mulberry sheet.

“And Jinhsi has never slept through an afternoon in her life,” Iuno said, very quietly. “Nor would Sanhua leave her sword on a guardroom rack to go to the coast.”

Down at the door-end of the bench, Chisa tilted her head a fraction toward the hanging lamp.

“Baizhi does not allow coal braziers in the Ecoacoustics wing,” Chisa said in her plain, level voice. “The smoke alters the dampness of the recording membranes.”

On the bench gap beside her, Buling stopped reaching for a rice cake, pulled her hand back into her blue sleeve, and looked once at the shuttered window.

“Wait a second,” Amy said, scrambling up onto both knees on the bench and leaning halfway across the pickled-radish saucer to stare upside down at the cream paper. “Why would Jinhsi put four huge lies right in the middle of a letter that had to go through the Mingting inspection bags? A censor sitting in an office in Mingting doesn’t know what trees grow on the Jinzhou bird-bridge or what Panhua cooks for lunch!”

Ciaccona set her tin mug down with a tiny click, rose from the window-seat, and stepped behind Iuno’s shoulder, looking down at the brushwork with her head tilted.

“Read the four lies again, princess,” Ciaccona said softly. “Just the four sentences that aren’t true. Leave the true ones out.”

Iuno looked down at the page.

Her right forefinger moved to the first untrue sentence in the middle paragraph, then to the one beside it, then down to the two in the final paragraph.

She didn’t need to read the whole lines out. She read only the single word standing at the head of each false sentence, where Jinhsi’s upright brush had begun the clause:

“‘*The*,’” Iuno read aloud into the quiet room. “‘*Moon*.’ ‘*Is*.’ ‘*Setting*.’”

Inside the brick firebox, a pocket of pine resin gave a single sharp *pop* against the grate. Nobody at the table moved.

“The moon is setting,” Amy repeated under her breath, her hands resting flat on the scarred pine board.

Over by the hearth, Lupa lowered the iron kettle onto the hob with a dull thud, her wolf ears flattening slightly as she frowned at the latched wooden shutter.

“The Waking Moon’s on Friday,” Lupa said roughly. “It’s coming full. How the hell does a waxing moon set?”

*She did not write that for the weather clerk, darling,* the High Priestess murmured inside Iuno’s head, without a trace of theatre left in her voice.

*I know,* Iuno thought back.

Out loud, Iuno did not guess, and she did not look up toward the storehouse rafters above which the Astrolabe Core hung in the cold. With her right hand, she folded the cream mulberry sheet cleanly along its original creases, slid it back into the slit bamboo envelope, and tucked it away inside the right-hand pocket of her wool coat—the pocket without the velvet box.

On the stoneware platter in the middle of the table, the last three white rice cakes sat untouched, their red bean-paste foxes cooling in the lamplight.

***

Long after the tenth bell, the main room of the storehouse was dark except for the thin red seam of coal-embers glowing beneath the iron stove-door.

Yangyang woke on her pallet by the inner wall before the middle watch, her strapped left ribs aching dull and tight from the mountain damp. Around her in the dark, the household’s breathing rose and fell in its familiar rhythm—Lupa’s deep, heavy draw near the pantry door, Cartethyia curled warm beside Ciaccona’s lute case under the window, Chisa and Buling still and quiet at the far end of the floor.

Yangyang pushed her quilt back without making a sound, slipped her cloak over her sheer cloud-worked sleeves, and stepped barefoot into her boots. She lifted the wooden door-latch with two fingers, stepped out onto the frosted landing, and walked up the short stone flight to the northeast parapet where the rampart looked out over the open canyon.

She stopped at the top riser.

Iuno was already standing at the crenelation, her heavy wool coat buttoned to the throat and the bare crystal at her left shoulder catching a faint, cold gleam against the stone. Two paces behind her, under the overhang of the timber watch-roof, Mei sat tucked on the dry stone bench with two grey wool blankets wrapped around her shoulders and her boots drawn up out of the rime.

Nobody told Yangyang to go back inside. Mei shifted over a hand’s breadth on the bench and patted the dry corner of the wool; Yangyang stayed standing beside the parapet coping two paces from Iuno’s right elbow, resting her palm on the freezing limestone.

The rain clouds from the week had torn apart into long, motionless ribs of black vapour across the gorge. Below the wall, the wet slate roofs of the Hold glistened like dark iron in the night air, and along the lower market tiers only three or four yellow horn-lanterns still burned small and solitary outside the guard-posts.

High overhead, framed between the black karst needles of the pass, Sentinel Hsin’s Astrolabe Core hung dead still inside its crooked bronze caliper-rings, its great celadon sphere opaque and unlit against the stars.

And the moon was in the wrong place.

Two nights before the Waking Moon Festival, it should have been swelling nearly round, riding high and white above the southern pass toward the middle of the sky.

Instead, it hung low and heavy in the west, already dropping down toward the saw-toothed ridge of the karst spires hours before midnight. Its upper rim was tarnished the dull, smoky yellow of old horn, tilted backward on its curve as if it were sliding down the wrong side of the valley roof, and even as the three of them stood on the wall, the lower edge of the disc touched the black rock shoulder of the ninth spire.

The thin silver light on the storehouse slates slipped backward into shadow.

On the stone bench under the watch-roof, Mei pulled the grey wool blanket a little tighter under her chin.

“It’s too early,” Mei whispered into the frost.

Nobody answered her. Along Yangyang’s collarbone, the small cerulean feathers lifted once in the cold draft coming up off the gorge and settled back against her throat as the pale rim of the moon sank down behind the black stone of the ninth spire, leaving the dark sphere of the Core hanging alone above the mountain.

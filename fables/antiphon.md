A chancel has two sides, and both of them are named after men who are usually elsewhere. The south belongs to the Dean and is called *decani*. The north belongs to the Precentor, the one who sings first, and is called *cantoris*. The choir sits in two banks facing across the gap, and the psalm goes over that gap verse by verse, one side and then the other, all the way to the end. The gap is the instrument. Push the two halves together into one comfortable block and the form dies on the spot, because an answer requires a distance to arrive from.

The antiphon proper is the short verse hung at the front of the psalm and again at the back. Its entire job is to tell you how to hear the hundred and fifty lines in between. It is a label made out of music. Sung first, it is a promise. Sung last, it is a receipt.

A Roman governor in a wet northern province once wrote to his emperor about a sect he had been obliged to investigate, and listed among the charges that they met before dawn on a fixed day and sang to one another, turn and turn about. He filed it. The province is gone, the sect is gone, the emperor is gone, the crime has been gone for eighteen centuries, and what survives of that entire coastal administration is one sentence about people singing at each other in the dark.

The rubrics allow a single voice to sing an antiphon. It is done constantly, in cold buildings, by one person with a candle. The versicle and the response both, decani and cantoris, out of one throat, across a gap of nothing at all. It is still called an antiphon. The books have never thought this worth a footnote.

Under a glacier, in a city that hangs its sun on a schedule and lights it by committee, two women share a bed built for one student with modest ambitions. Each of them keeps a room the other has never been shown. One of those rooms has velvet in it and a fire and a framed insult. The other has weather coming.

## I. Decani

She came up the east stairwell at ten to five with the tray held level and her whole night filed and none of it settled.

The building was doing its small early business. The vents on the change of shift. Ciaccona's door standing open a hand's width the way it always stood, because a woman who spent her childhood on boats sleeps better with an exit. Down in the kitchen, on the twelfth hook, a plain white cup that had been on that rack for one hour and had already been looked at four times by a nineteen-year-old pretending to consider pastry.

The tall cup steamed on the tray with the cardamom beside it, because Iuno had spent nine months learning that Mei will not ask for a thing twice and has therefore stopped asking for anything at all, and had responded to this the way she responded to every structural problem, which was by building an institution.

The bedroom held one sleeper and the wreck of three.

Amy had gone down to the kitchen half an hour ago and had left behind a diagonal, an annexed pillow, and a blanket arranged by a girl who fights in her sleep. Mei lay in the middle of it with one arm flung across the vacancy where her daughter had been, mouth slightly open, hair doing something criminal, four Tuesdays deep into the longest run of unbroken sleep in her recorded life.

Iuno set the tray on the sill.

Four minutes. She knew it the way she knew the vent cycle, in the body, in the place where timers had always lived: the leaves were in, the water had gone in just off the boil, and the tall cup had four minutes to become something worth handing over.

She sat down on the edge of the bed to spend them.

Mei's hand came across the sheet without waking, unerring, the way it had crossed the whole dark in the fourth watch on the night of the vigil, and closed around the crystal wrist.

The stump took it in as bearings. It always did. Wood at eleven inches reports as a low grain; a wolf two doors down reports as a drum with the skin slack; two heartbeats three floors up had been the meridian she navigated by since the sync, and she had said as much out loud once, to a woman across a kitchen table, at three in the morning, and had watched the woman understand exactly what instrument she had been reading from.

Tonight the bearing pulled.

*Ah,* said the High Priestess of Septimont, from the far side of the sternum, in the tone of a woman who has been expecting a delivery. *There it is.*

Iuno closed her eyes, which in that house is how you open the door.

⁂

The way down had not changed. The lounge below the stair had changed, in the manner of a room that has been holding something in for a week and has decided to stop.

The candles stood. The fire in the courtyard beyond the window went on with its long confident business, and the imported moon hung over the roofless pillars where it always hung, low and unclouded and stolen outright from the best night of their life. On the wall in its gilt frame the strip of prop-paper kept its flat void font: CHARACTER ARC: LOVES MEI. CONCLUDED. Two armchairs. One mahogany table. A deck of cards squared to the table's edge by a hand that squares things.

The High Priestess was standing.

She stood in the middle of her own small warm room with a full glass in her hand and her back to the stair, facing the wall behind the piano, and the twin tails of blue silk hung dead still, and that was the whole of the report.

"How long."

"It came up while you were reading her." The Priestess did not turn. "Though I will say, in the interest of a clean audit, that I have sat with my back to that wall for two years, and a woman who sits with her back to a thing is a poor witness to its history."

Iuno came down the last step.

In the wall behind the piano, where thirty years of load-bearing masonry had ended the world, there was a door.

It was very large. It went up past the ceiling line and kept going, in the way of things in that country, and the ceiling had made an arrangement with it. Oak, or the memory of oak, black with the kind of age that gets into the grain rather than sitting on it, and across the whole of it a single band of iron with a lamp-hook riveted at the centre. The lock plate was there for show. Six inches of clearance at the foot, worn out of the stone by centuries of a door doing what doors do.

Under it, along the flagstones, in a room with a fire and thick walls and no exterior whatsoever, a draught was coming through.

Iuno crouched and put the flesh hand flat in it.

Cold. Moving. Steady, with a long slow pulse in it that arrived about every nine seconds and went away again.

"Don't," said the High Priestess.

"You've had a week."

"I have had a week of looking at a door in my own drawing room and I have spent it *drinking*, thank you, and I would like the minutes to reflect that this is the first thing in fifty years I have kept from you and that I am not enjoying it."

"Why."

The Priestess turned round at last, and the mockery had gone out of her posture entirely, which is how the session begins.

"Because we have opened a great many doors in the line of duty," she said, "and every single one of them was somebody else's."

The draught pulsed. Nine seconds. Nine seconds.

Iuno stood up and put her palm on the oak and felt the whole vast weight of it decline to resist her, the way a door does when somebody has been oiling the hinges of it for thirty years without ever once being thanked; and she pushed, and the door went.

⁂

The chapel was cold, and the cold had a floor to it, the flat unbothered cold of stone that has never once been asked to be anything else.

It ran long and narrow, north from the doorway, six bays of it, and the roof went up into a dark the candles behind her declined to enter. Flagstones. A rail. Two banks of stalls facing each other across the gap, worn smooth on the arms, the wood gone the colour of tea. Down the whole east side, from the rail to the far end, from her waist to somewhere well above the reach of any ladder that country had ever produced, there was glass.

Metres of it.

She had built this room out of a temple she resigned from, and she had never been in it.

The glass was lit. That was the first fact, and Iuno stood in the doorway with her hand still on the oak and took the fact apart, because there was no outside. The lounge had walls. The courtyard had a moon she had stolen and a sky she had engineered, and both of them ended at the psyche's masonry. Behind that whole east wall the masonry should have gone on being masonry. Instead there was a source: faint, cold, high, with no warmth in it anywhere, coming through the panes from the far side of the boundary she had spent her life calling the edge of the world.

She walked the length of it slowly, with her hands behind her back, the way she had walked a Senate colonnade.

Fourteen pictures.

They were beautifully made. That was the offence of them. Whoever had cut them understood the trade completely: the lead came doing the drawing, since every black line in a stained window is the thing holding the picture apart, so that the picture is made out of its own joins; flesh in the pale pinks that go grey at a distance; the good expensive blue kept back for the one place in each panel where blue would land.

In the first light a woman in a coat lay on a shingle beach with her hair in the water and another woman knelt over her, and the kneeling one had two arms.

In the second, the same, with the light coming from the other side.

In the third the beach was a battlefield three-quarters glass, and the kneeling one had one arm, and she was down.

She went along them. Nine of them had her in the ground before the middle of the story, in nine different postures, on nine grounds, under nine skies, and the golden-eyed woman went on walking out of all nine into an empty right-hand margin. In two of them they went down together, which the glaziers had rendered with a tenderness she found professionally offensive. In one there was no beach at all: a woman with grey-blue eyes in a temple office at sixty, signing something, well-fed, unaccompanied, and it was the most peaceful window in the room and Iuno looked at it for a long time and then moved on.

The fourteenth was a house at night with one square of yellow in it.

She counted them four times, in threes, the way she counts anything. She got fourteen three times.

*You have a hobby,* said the High Priestess, from the doorway, declining to come in.

"So does she."

*Yes. And she buys them so they can't be set.* A pause. *Darling, look at the last one.*

The fifteenth light stood at the north end, nearest the far door, and it was clear.

Plain glass, hand-blown, imperfect the way the east tower's used to be, held on all four sides in lead that had been cut and worked and soldered at the joints with exactly as much care as the fourteen. Somebody had spent a day on the came alone. It carried no picture and never had, and the lead held it precisely as hard as it held the other fourteen.

Iuno put her hand in her coat pocket, and found it, and stood there in a cold chapel with a smooth piece of Mengzhou lead between her finger and her thumb, a quad, cast on purpose to leave the paper alone, bought that same night by a four-hundred-year-old woman in transit coveralls who kept the customs of every street she worked.

*It holds,* she thought. *That's the entire job.*

The fifteenth light was hinged.

She found the hinges by touch and then found the little iron latch by knowing where such a latch goes, and it came up under her thumb with a click she recognised in her spine before her mind had it, and the casement moved a quarter of an inch and stopped.

There was a wedge in the frame.

Felt, folded three times, gone hard and dark as a shoe sole, driven in at the top corner where nobody looks and trimmed off flush with somebody's thumbnail. Iuno crouched with her fingertips on it and did not pull.

In front of the casement stood a harp.

It was tall and plain and entirely without ornament, temple issue, the sort of object a treasury buys forty of. The frame had been oiled once, a long administration ago, by somebody working through a list. She ran her hand down the strings without sounding them and counted twenty-nine, and stopped, and did the thing she had been doing since she was eleven years old, which was that she checked.

Every string on it was tuned to the same note.

*Well,* said the Priestess, very quietly, from the doorway.

Iuno crouched there with her fingers on the wedge for some while.

They had told the girls it was the god's instrument. They had told them at eleven, on the Capitoline, in the east tower with the sky coming up green over the aqueduct: *when the augury is true, the harp will speak.* Twenty-two girls in a cold room and a harp that never once spoke, in six years, through three hundred readings, including the good ones, including the two everybody knew were good.

And a girl of eleven with a professional turn of mind had gone up alone one morning to find out whether the god was silent or absent, and had found instead a wedge of grey felt in the top corner of the east window where nobody looks, cut flush with a thumbnail, put there by senior girls who had worked out, sensibly, at some point in the previous four centuries, that an instrument which sounds on its own in front of a paying client is a liability the temple could not carry.

She had gone back down the stairs and had not said anything to anyone, and had grown up to keep doors and accounts.

*It came with the furniture,* the Priestess said.

"Mm."

*You built a chapel in the back of us and you shipped the gag in with it.*

"I built a chapel and put it behind a wall and never came in. Let's have the whole invoice." Iuno stood. Her knees reported the cold. "Something is on the other side of that glass and it is providing my lighting."

*Yes.*

"And there's a door."

There was. In the north wall, past the harp, low and small and altogether unlike the great one at her back: a plain plank door, silvered, warped at the foot, of the kind you find at the sea end of anything. It sat in its frame the way a door sits when the building has settled around it for a long time and the two of them have come to terms.

The draught came from under it in that nine-second pulse.

Iuno stood with her hand on the latch. Behind her, thirty feet down a cold chapel, in the lit doorway of a warm room, a woman in blue silk stood exactly at the threshold and did not cross it.

*I have no chair in there,* said the High Priestess.

"I know."

*Take a bearing every ninety seconds and come back.*

"I'll come back at four minutes."

*You will come back at four minutes,* the Priestess agreed, *because if you do not, I shall have to go and get you, and I have nothing to wear.*

Iuno opened the door in the north wall.

*℣. Aperi — Open it.*
*℟. Intra — Come in.*

## II. Cantoris

There was no wind. That was the first thing, and it took her four seconds to understand what she was noticing, because a woman who has spent her life reading weather has no category for a place with none.

The air stood. It had been standing long enough that when the door came open behind her, the draught went out into it in a single visible sheet, low along the ground, like something poured.

Rock. Dark, close-grained, worn down rather than broken, running away flat in every direction with a long shallow tilt to it that the eye took a while to find. Across the rock, in sheets and tongues and long thin fingers, water. Ankle-deep at the most. It lay perfectly still and perfectly clear, and it held the sky, and it went on holding it, tremorless, for as long as she cared to stand there watching — which is a trick of glass rather than of water.

And above, thick dark. It had depth in it — the true kind, the kind an engineered ceiling spends a fortune imitating — and it went up and kept going, and in it, low and faint and small, hung stars.

They were coloured.

Iuno stood on the threshold of another woman's country and looked up at a sky full of blue and pink and red and gold, faint as pilot lights, none of them bright enough to read by, all of them steady, and she was a temple girl again for one entire second, eleven years old on the Capitoline in the cold, waiting on a paring of moon with the count already in her mouth.

Then she looked down, and saw the stones.

They stood out of the rock in a long shallow arc that followed the water's edge, and they went on further than the light allowed her to follow. Some were her own height. Some came to her knee. They were cut from the ground they stood in, which she could see from the grain running through, and they had been set upright with the honest slight wrongness of things put up by one person working alone with no tools worth the name.

Every one of them had a name on it.

She read the nearest without meaning to, because it was there and she reads.

SUMIKA.

Then the next.

MYA. AVIDIUS. Then one in a script she did not know at all, then PHROLOVA, then two more in the unknown script, worn nearly out, then a stone with three characters of Huanglong hand that she took by their bones, then one that said THE SHAPER in somebody’s blocky capitals, and then her eye went one further along the row, ahead of her, the way a reader’s eye always goes.

She stopped reading.

She stood on cold rock at the edge of a still sheet of water in a place with no wind, and she put her real hand over her mouth, briefly, in a country where nobody could see her do it, and then she took it down and put both hands behind her back and began to walk the row properly, from the top, the way you walk a colonnade.

The path was worn.

That was the thing she read first, because it was the thing she was built to read. Between the stones and the water's edge, the rock had a groove in it, hollowed a good centimetre down at the middle, polished along the bottom, wider at the turns where a person shifts weight. She crouched and set her palm in it and it fit the way a stair fits a foot.

She was still crouched when she heard the water.

A hundred yards down the arc, a woman was standing shin-deep in a sheet of it with her back turned, and she straightened as Iuno watched, and lifted two cupped hands out of the water, and carried them to a stone, and poured.

The water went over the top of it and down the face, and the name came up out of the rock dark and legible, and Mei stood looking at it with her hands empty and dripping until it dried.

Then she went back to the water for the next one.

⁂

Iuno watched her do four.

The fourth one she felt in the chest, because by the fourth one the procedure was legible: down to the water, both hands, up, pour, stand, read, dry, next. No pause anywhere in it for feeling. It had the economy of a thing done nightly for a very long time by somebody who has stopped noticing they are doing it, and Iuno had watched a wolf receive a cup at four minutes on two hundred and eleven mornings and knew a liturgy on sight.

She came down the row.

Mei heard the boots on the rock somewhere around the sixth stone and turned with her hands still full, and the water ran out between her fingers and went back into the sheet at her ankles.

"Hi."

"Hello."

Mei looked at her for a moment. Then she looked at the row, and at the water, and at the dark above it, in the manner of a woman running an inspection at speed on a house that has received a visitor two hours early.

"Sorry about the — " She stopped, and appeared to discover in real time that there was nothing here to apologise for, and finished it anyway. "Yeah."

"There's a door."

"Since when?"

"Tonight."

"Huh," Mei said.

And that was the whole of it, apparently, because she went back down to the water and filled her hands again.

Iuno stood on the rock and did the arithmetic on that, and the arithmetic came out immediately and unhappily. Mei had a woman appear out of a wall in the middle of the most private hour of her life and had spent one syllable on it. Which meant either that Mei had never once believed this place was private, or that Mei had spent thirty-odd years having things come out of walls at her and had budgeted accordingly.

*Both,* said the High Priestess, from a long way off, in the voice of somebody speaking through a floor.

"Can I see?" Iuno said.

Mei paused with her hands full. "See what."

"The round."

⁂

The round took as long as it took.

Mei showed her the water first, which Iuno had expected, because Mei teaches the way she does everything, from the ground up and out of order.

"It's clean. You'd think it wouldn't be." Mei crouched and put her hand flat in the sheet. "It doesn't go anywhere. It's just here. I used to think it came in and out but I sat and watched it for — a while. It doesn't."

"Where does it come from?"

"Same place as the rock."

Then she showed her the hands, because Iuno tried it one-handed and lost most of it before she got there, and Mei laughed once, warmly, at her, and came over.

"Two."

"I have one."

"You've got two. They're different." Mei took the crystal wrist and set the palm up alongside the other, and pressed the two of them together at the heel, and closed the fingers a fraction. "There. It's a bowl. It doesn't care what it's made of."

"It leaks at the joint."

"Yeah, they all do. Go faster."

Iuno went faster, and carried water in a bowl made of one hand of flesh and one of instrument-grade crystal, and the crystal reported the temperature the way it reported everything, as a bearing, and the water in it threw a small cold constellation across her sleeve all the way to the stone.

She poured. The name came up dark: a woman's name, in the unknown script, three strokes and a long tail.

"Who is this?"

"I don't know."

Iuno looked at her.

"There's about forty I don't know," Mei said. She was already going back down. "They were here first. Some of them are in a — I can't read it. Some of them I can read fine and it doesn't do anything." She scooped. "That's just how the place came."

"You wash them anyway."

"They're on the round."

⁂

It went on. Iuno filed nothing and counted everything, because counting is involuntary in her the way blinking is involuntary in other people, and because a woman who counts in a graveyard at least has something to do with her hands.

Some things the count produced:

The stones nearest the top of the arc were the driest and the palest, and were washed last, and Mei's route through them was quicker.

Six of them had the top left corner scoured smooth and slightly hollow, in the exact place a thumb goes.

One stone, low, close to the water, had been re-cut. The old name was still under the new one at the depth of a fingernail, and Iuno read it sideways with her cheek nearly on the rock and got most of it and put it away in the place where she puts things she has decided to be slow about.

The track in the rock, at its deepest point, was a centimetre and a half down. She did the sum on rock of that hardness and a soft-soled boot and got a figure that she declined to hold in her head for longer than it took to arrive.

And there was a stone Mei walked round.

She did it well. She did it the way a person walks round a chair that has been in the same place for eleven years: no adjustment visible, the path simply going out and coming back, and the groove in the rock proving it, because the groove went out and came back too.

Iuno let her do it twice.

"Third from the water," she said. "Dry."

Mei kept walking.

"Mei."

"It's Amy's."

The stars held. There was no wind to move anything, so nothing moved.

"She's on the third floor of this building eating a pastry she believes she stole."

"I know where she is."

"Then take it down."

Mei stopped at the next stone with her hands full and poured, and the name came up, and she watched it dry all the way out before she said anything, which she had not done once in the entire round.

"Yeah." She shook her hands off. "I'll get to it."

⁂

Iuno went down to the water.

She filled the bowl, one flesh, one glass, and carried it back up the row, and stood in front of a pale dry stone with AEMEATH cut into it in a fast careless hand by somebody who does everything fast, and she poured.

The name came up dark.

Behind her, some distance off, the sound of a woman standing very still in shallow water.

Iuno waited until the stone had dried out completely, which took a little under a minute in that dead air, and then she went back down and filled her hands and did it again.

"You don't have to do that," Mei said.

"It's on the round."

The third time she did it, Mei came and stood beside her, and they watched the letters go dark and pale and dark again, and neither of them said anything, and the water in the sheet at their boots held the whole coloured sky and did not tremble.

⁂

"How often do you do this?"

Mei was up to her ankles, filling her hands. She answered without looking round, in the flat pleasant voice of a man on a cutter at half past four in the morning who has never in eleven years been spoken to by a member of the public.

"Every night."

Iuno waited.

"You lose it if you leave it." She came up with the water. "There was a stretch where I stopped. I wasn't sleeping, and when I did I went somewhere else." She poured. "Came back and I could read about half. They go pale, and then they go under, and then it's a rock."

"How long was the stretch?"

"Dunno. Couple of years."

"How long did it take to get them back?"

Mei thought about it, and it was clear from her face that nobody had ever asked her a question of this shape, and that she was enjoying it in the way she enjoys any problem with a procedure at the bottom of it.

"Longer," she said.

⁂

Somewhere in the second half of the round, Iuno looked up and counted the sky.

She did it the way she was taught in the east tower: quadrant by quadrant, low to high, with the count kept in the fingers and the eyes kept moving. It took a while. They were faint, and faint is what the training was for.

Blue, out to the east, low, steady, unremarkable, and she looked at it for slightly too long and put her hands behind her back.

Pink, high, small, and nearer than the rest by some trick of the depth.

Red, well down toward the horizon such as it was, in the part of the sky you would look at if you were checking exits.

Teal. Sky. Coral. A pale straw gold. A green with a bad attitude.

And a great many others, further out, faint to the edge of nothing, in every colour the eye keeps in reserve.

And one directly overhead, gold, larger than the rest by a wide margin, with real weight to it, and Mei worked underneath it for twenty minutes and kept her head down the whole time.

"The big one."

"Mm."

"Overhead."

"That one's mine," Mei said, and carried her water up the bank.

Iuno counted them all, and then counted the stones, and then stood on cold rock with two numbers in her head that were one apart.

"There's one more up there than down here."

"Yeah."

"You know that."

"I've counted." Mei was crouched at a stone with her hands empty, wiping the base of it with her sleeve. "Every time I do it I get the same gap. One."

"Which one is it?"

"Can't hold it." Mei stood up and pointed, roughly, at nothing, high, west of the pink one. "It's around there. If you look right at it, it goes. If you look at something else, it's there."

*Averted vision,* said the High Priestess, from very far away, sounding exactly as she had sounded at eleven. *Rods at the edge, cones at the centre. They taught us that in the second year.*

Iuno turned her head a few degrees off and held it, and let the corner of her eye do the work.

There was something there. It had no colour she could put a name to, because the part of the eye that finds faint things does not do colour, and that is the whole cost of the technique and always has been.

"How long has it been there?"

"Since I got here."

"When did you get here?"

Mei laughed, one breath, and went back down to the water.

⁂

"Say the number," Mei said, later, at the far end of the arc.

Iuno had known it was coming for about ten minutes and had spent the ten minutes deciding.

"No."

Mei looked over her shoulder. Her hands were full and the water was going out of them.

"You counted them. I watched you do it. You do the thing with your fingers."

"I counted them."

"So say it."

"You've never said it out loud," Iuno said. "In your life. To anyone. And I've been down here forty minutes." She stood with her hands behind her back and her chin level. "If I say the number, you'll know it forever. It'll be in the room every time you come down here, in my voice, and you'll never get it out again, and I will have handed you a thing you did not ask for and called it help." She let that sit for exactly as long as it needed. "So: no. Ask me a different day and I'll tell you."

Mei stood in the water for a moment.

"Okay," she said.

Then she poured, and the name came up, and she read it, and it dried.

⁂

The arc ended.

It ended without ceremony, in a stretch of unmarked rock forty paces long, and then the rock ended too, and gave out into something Iuno's eye refused for a second and a half to accept.

Grass.

A square of it. Ten feet by ten, maybe twelve, lying on that dark rock like a rug in a warehouse. It was cut. It had been cut recently and cut badly, all the way down, past the point where anybody would want it, so that the whole square had gone yellow underneath and showed it.

Iuno stopped walking.

"What is this."

"Grass."

"Yes."

"It comes up if you leave it." Mei crouched and ran her palm flat over the top of it, the way you check a table for dust. "So I do it. It's the only thing here that grows."

"With what?"

"There's a thing." Mei waved vaguely at nothing in particular, which in that country meant it would be there when she wanted it. "It's old. The blade's off."

"It's yellow underneath."

"Yeah." One shoulder. "I've never been good at it."

Iuno stood on a square of badly cut grass in a country that had never had a season, one hour and a half after telling a four-hundred-year-old woman across a kitchen table that grass at that height means a schedule and a blade and a person who came round on a day and did it wrong, and that she had spent four centuries in that afternoon staring at a hand and had never once turned round.

She said nothing at all.

*Darling.*

*Not tonight.*

*Darling —*

*I said not tonight.*

The Priestess went quiet, thirty feet and one wall and an entire order of reality away, and Iuno crouched down and put her flesh hand flat on the cut grass, which was cold, and dry, and real.

"Mei."

"Mm."

"Where's the light coming from, when you cut it?"

Mei looked at her with an expression of perfectly open confusion, and it was the most honest thing that had happened all night.

"There isn't any," she said. "I just do it."

⁂

Far out, past the grass, past where the rock came back and went on, there was a doorframe.

Iuno saw it because she looks at doors. It stood by itself on the flat with nothing on either side of it, and it had a door in it, and there was snow on the ground around the foot of it for about a metre in every direction and nowhere else, and even at that distance the shape of the thing said *cold* the way certain buildings say *money*.

"That one's mine," Mei said.

"Yes."

"It doesn't open."

Iuno looked at it a while longer. From here she could see the hinges. They were on this side.

"Mm," she said, and turned back toward the row.

## III. In Choro

"Come and see mine," Iuno said.

Mei was drying her hands on her thighs. She stopped.

"…Am I allowed?"

Iuno had built a career on faces, had read a Senate from forty feet, had once got a man's entire family history off the way he handled a coin. She read this one and it cost her something she declined to itemise.

"Yes."

"Because it's — I mean it's *yours*, it's the — " Mei made a gesture that took in her own head, and then the whole horizon, and then gave up. "You've got a whole — "

"Mei."

"Yeah."

"There are two of us in there and one of us has been asking to meet you for two years."

Mei's face did about four things.

"…She *what*?"

Mei took her hand to be led, because Mei takes hands, and she took the crystal one because it was nearer.

Her thumb went across the palm and up the back and over the little finger, where a facet had been cut out of the inside of that forearm eleven days ago along the grain, so the loss was structural, and had gone to a jeweller on the ninth terrace who asked no questions and took four days over it, and had come back as a plain band that sat on a glass finger looking exactly like part of the arm, which is how a thing gets carried in plain sight for a week and a half in a house full of professional observers.

Her thumb stopped.

It stayed there for the space of one breath.

Then it moved on, and closed, and Mei pulled her toward the door in the north wall at her usual speed, which is the speed of someone who has never in her life walked toward anything at a sensible pace.

⁂

The chapel from the outside was a grey wall.

Iuno had a moment of not understanding it, standing on the threshold with her hand still in Mei's, looking at forty feet of flat lead-veined slate where fifteen lights should have been. The glass had gone the colour of a wet pavement. The lead ran through it in a black tangle that resolved, when she made herself look properly, into the outlines of fourteen pictures, all of them turned away.

A window keeps the whole of itself for the side that is already inside. She had known that since she was a girl kneeling under other people's glass, and had never once been on the wrong side of her own.

Mei went past her into the chapel and stopped dead about six feet in.

⁂

Iuno gave her the room.

She stood at the low door with the draught going past her ankles and let a woman with no memory and a graveyard walk the length of her interior chapel by herself, and she watched her go along the fourteen lights from the north end backwards, which is the wrong direction and produced the correct order for the first time all night: a house at night with one square of yellow in it; a woman at sixty, well-fed, signing something; two of them going down together; and then nine, and nine, and nine.

Mei stopped at the ninth-from-last for a long time.

"This is you."

"Yes."

"You're — " Mei put two fingers on the glass, low, where a shingle beach came up out of the lead into a margin of nothing. "You're on the *floor* here."

"Yes."

"And she's just walking."

"She's got somewhere to be."

Mei turned round.

She had the look on her face that came out approximately twice a year and that four separate people in that household had learned to get out of the way of, and Iuno, who had built the room and hung the glass and never once come in, stood in the doorway of her own chapel and received it.

"How long have these been in here?"

"They were here when I came in."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have. I built the room and shut the door and I have been sitting on the other side of that wall for two years drinking with a woman in silk." Iuno kept her hands behind her back. "Somebody put fourteen windows in the back of my head, Mei, and I could not tell you whether it was the void, or the woman who reads me as a hobby, or my own hands at two in the morning while I was busy doing sums about mornings."

"Which do you think?"

"My hands."

Mei looked at her for a while.

Then she put her palm flat on the lead of the nearest light, at the height of her own shoulder, the way she had been putting it flat on wet stone all night.

"You've got one too."

"One what."

"A row," Mei said.

The chapel took that and kept it. Down at the south end, thirty feet away in the lit doorway of a warm room, a woman in blue silk turned her glass a quarter-turn by the stem and said absolutely nothing, which from her is a verdict.

"Yes," Iuno said.

"How often do you come in?"

"Tonight."

Mei nodded slowly, and took her hand off the glass, and left the arithmetic where it lay, which is the single most generous thing anybody did for anybody in either house that morning.

⁂

Then she went back along the row, at speed, and stopped at the north end in front of the clear one, and Iuno saw exactly what was about to happen and had no instrument on earth to stop it.

"Fifteen."

"Fourteen."

"There's fifteen in this room and this is one of them."

"It has nothing in it."

"It's got *lead* in it." Mei had her hand flat on the came, doing what she does, which is arrive at the true thing by the shortest available road while everybody else is still getting their coats. "Somebody cut all of that. Look at the corners. You don't do corners like that for a gap."

The cold chapel held it.

Iuno stood there with a smooth piece of Mengzhou lead in her coat pocket, given to her an hour earlier by a woman who had said, *it holds the space, that's the entire job, ask any compositor which sort they'd rather lose.*

"Yes," she said.

⁂

Mei came into the lounge like weather, by pressure and rearrangement, and stopped on the third step down with her mouth already open.

The High Priestess of Septimont was standing in the middle of her own drawing room with a glass in her hand, dressed to the last pin, and had been standing there in that exact attitude for some considerable time, arranging her face.

"You've tracked salt in."

"Sorry."

"Do not be sorry, be *careful.* The velvet was a war."

Mei looked down at her boots, which were wet, and then at the deep blue velvet and the moon-silver piping, and then round the whole small warm room — the candles, the mahogany, the cards squared to the edge, the courtyard window with a fire going in it under a moon she had watched come up herself, once, on a night she had never known anybody else had kept — and Iuno came down the steps behind her and watched a woman with nothing in her own country stand in a furnished one.

"You've got a *fire*."

"I have a courtyard. The fire is in the courtyard. There are standards."

"You've got *cake.*"

"There is always cake," the High Priestess said, with enormous dignity. "Somebody in this administration has to feed the management. I have been at it alone for two years and the position is thankless and I intend to say so at length."

Mei ate the cake with her fingers, standing up, and got sugar on the arm of the good chair, and the High Priestess watched her do it with the expression of a woman having a religious experience she intends to deny under oath.

⁂

The room declined to make a third chair.

It was noticed. It was noticed by all three of them at approximately the same moment, when Mei looked round for somewhere to sit and the room, which had produced a window out of a blind wall and a courtyard out of nothing and a ledger out of a deck of cards, offered her two occupied armchairs and a piano stool.

Mei sat on the floor with her back against Iuno's chair and her boots to the fire that was in another room, and put her head back, and appeared entirely content.

*It won't make one,* the High Priestess said, in the private register, over the top of her.

*No.*

*I want that logged.*

*Logged.*

⁂

"What's that?"

Mei was looking up at the wall where the plot board used to hang.

The High Priestess turned her glass a quarter-turn by the stem, guest-handle-out, and said nothing at all for a full second, which from her is a confession.

"It's a prop," Iuno said. "Off the floor of the Nihility. She framed it."

Mei read it. It took her a while, because it was upside down from where she was and because she read it three times.

CHARACTER ARC: LOVES MEI. CONCLUDED.

"…Huh."

"It is," the High Priestess said, "the single funniest thing the abyss has ever said about anybody, and I intend to be buried with it."

"It's wrong, though."

"Obviously it's wrong. That is the *joke*, darling, keep up — "

"The other bit." Mei was still looking at it. She had one arm over her knee and her head back against the chair, doing the thing she does about twice a year, which is stop performing. "*Arc.* That's a place. It's got a fire in it."

The candles stood very straight.

The High Priestess of Septimont, who had held a Senate for thirty years and had written the framing joke herself and had defended it at length on four separate occasions, sat down in her chair rather suddenly.

"…Yes," she said. "Well."

⁂

They played a hand for nothing, because the Priestess ruled that a guest who arrives with wet boots must be given something to do with her hands.

Mei played badly and with enormous confidence and won, and the Priestess reviewed the outcome with the air of a woman auditing a bank that has just been robbed by a child.

"You drew *four.*"

"Yeah."

"You held one card. In a house game, against me, you kept a single ace and threw the rest in the water."

"It was a nice ace."

"It was — " The Priestess stopped. She looked at Iuno. "Is it like this all the time?"

"Yes."

"And you *chose* it."

"Repeatedly."

⁂

Mei got up at some point and went to the piano and put one finger on a key and left it there without pressing, and took it back, and came and sat down again on the floor.

Nobody in the room said anything about it.

⁂

"May I ask you something?" the High Priestess said.

Mei tipped her head back against the chair to look at her upside down. "Sure."

"It is unkind and I have been holding it for two years and I would like it understood that I am asking it here, in a room with a fire and a door, rather than at a table in front of eight people who love you."

"…Okay."

"What happens if you win?"

The fire in the courtyard beyond the window went on with its business. In the sky above the pillars, the imported moon held.

Mei's face did something.

It did it quickly and it stopped, and what replaced it was the good one, the warm one, the one that has walked out of forty years of rooms leaving everybody in them reassured — and the High Priestess watched it arrive with the flat professional attention of a woman timing an eclipse, and Iuno, three feet away in her own chair above the crown of Mei's head, watched the same event with entirely the wrong instrument and read it clean anyway.

"I'll let you know," Mei said.

"Mm."

"Sorry, that came out slick." Mei rubbed the back of her neck. "I don't have one. Everybody keeps asking me things that have an answer, and I keep going *yeah, definitely,* and then it's four in the morning."

"You are in excellent company," said the High Priestess, "and the wine is dreadful and expensive. My turn to be asked."

Mei thought about it for a long moment, sitting on the floor of somebody else's head with her boots to a fire in another room.

"What do I call you?"

The candles moved.

"I have six," the High Priestess said, and she said it lightly, the way she says everything expensive. "Regina, which we abdicated in public with paperwork. Moneta, who warns; the office is closed, and every warning was delivered at cost, including the ones nobody wanted. Sospita, the savior, reassigned to a committee with a wolf-chaired subcommittee. Covella, the crescent, retained in perpetuity. Lucina, who brings the child into the light — open, currently, with years of work in it. And Pronuba." She turned the glass. "Every one of them is a job."

"That's not what I asked."

"I am aware."

"You've got the same name as her."

"I have the same *three.* Iuno Caecilia Severa, of the temple, of the second cohort of the year the aqueduct froze, and there are two of us wearing all of it, and one of us is real in the sense you mean, and I have made my peace with the arithmetic and I do not require it recited at me on a Tuesday by a woman with sugar on her fingers."

Mei sat with that for a while.

"Okay," she said.

And then she reached over to the mahogany table, and turned the deck ninety degrees so it was square to the edge the way it had been before she'd knocked it, and said, entirely in passing, to the room, in the middle of doing something else:

"Iuno, are you going to eat the rest of that?"

The High Priestess of Septimont sat perfectly still in her chair.

She had been addressed by an office for fifty years. She had been addressed by a girl of eleven in the dark, and by a woman on a cliff, and by a fool with golden eyes shouting through a firestorm at somebody standing behind her. She had never once, in the whole of her existence, been called by her own name by a person who was not confused about which of them she was talking to.

Iuno sat three feet away in the right-hand chair and watched it happen to her and said nothing, and made no move to clarify anything at all, and the cost of that was considerable and she paid it in one instalment without discussion.

"…It's lemon," the High Priestess said, at last, in a voice with something wrong in the bottom of it. "You may have it. There is more."

"There's always more, isn't there," Mei said, pleased, taking it.

"Yes," said the High Priestess of Septimont. "There is."

## IV. Ad Laudes

Mei went back through at the end, because a woman who has spent forty minutes in a warm room with cake does not stop being a woman with a round to finish.

They walked the chapel together, and at the north end Iuno stopped in front of the harp.

"What's that for?"

"Weather."

Mei looked at the twenty-nine strings, and then at the clear light behind them with its hinge and its latch, and then at the felt wedge driven flush into the top corner where nobody looks.

"Someone's jammed it."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because an instrument that plays on its own in front of a paying client is a liability," Iuno said, "and because the girls who put it in were sensible, and because it has been in there for four hundred years and I found it when I was eleven and went back down the stairs and never told anybody."

Mei absorbed that in the way she absorbs everything, which is completely and at once and without commentary.

"You want me to do it?"

"No."

Iuno got her thumbnail under the corner of it.

It had been in that frame a long time. It came out slowly, with the small grudging complaint of felt that has become part of a building, and she stood there for a moment holding four hundred years of institutional caution between her finger and her thumb, an object about the size of a coin.

She opened her hand and let it drop on the flagstones.

Then she pushed the casement, and the fifteenth light swung out a finger's width, and stopped where the lead let it.

⁂

Nothing happened for four seconds.

Then the room found out about itself.

The lounge behind them had a fire in it and had been warm for two years. The chapel was stone and had never been anything but cold. The country past the north door had no weather at all and had been standing still since before anybody could say. Three rooms at three pressures with two doors open between them, and the air did what air does, which is that it went.

It came through the great door and down the length of the chapel in one long push, and it went out under the north door into a place that had never had a wind in it, and on the way past it crossed twenty-nine strings tuned to the one note.

The harp took it.

It came up out of the silence the way that instrument does, from underneath, arriving already in progress: one note swelling into the room with its beginning somewhere behind it, and then the harmonics coming in over the top in the order the physics allows, the octave, the fifth, the double octave, the third, until the whole cold chapel was full of one chord that nobody had played.

Mei put her hand out and grabbed the back of Iuno's coat, which is a thing she does.

The chord swelled, and held, and thinned, and swelled again.

And then it played four bars.

They came out of the drone in sequence, clean, in time, with a lift at the end of the second bar and a fall through the fourth, and they were a tune, and a harp tuned to one note in unison cannot produce a tune, and Iuno had counted the strings herself.

She knew it before the second bar finished.

Three acts, a god who dies before the interval, a structural error the Mengzhou season had adored; a teahouse off Bookbinders' Row where everybody weeps into their cups on schedule; four bars hummed under the breath by a woman in transit coveralls at half past three in the morning while she filed something behind her own eyes; and, for two years, badly, on purpose, over the shuffling of cards, by a woman in blue silk who has never been able to say where she got it.

Behind them, thirty feet down the chapel in the lit doorway of a warm room, the High Priestess of Septimont made no sound whatsoever.

The four bars finished. The drone came back up underneath and went on.

"That's pretty," Mei said.

"Mm."

"Do you know it?"

The chord stood in the cold air, sounding, with nobody's hands anywhere near it.

"No," Iuno said.

⁂

They went out through the north door into a country with no wind, and the wind went with them.

It came out low, the way it had come out the first time, a sheet of moving air spilling over the threshold and going away downhill across the rock. Mei was two steps ahead and stopped so suddenly that Iuno walked into the back of her.

The water was moving.

All of it. Every sheet and tongue and finger of it, out to the edge of the light and past it, ruffling in long slow travelling bands from the doorway outward, and where it ruffled the coloured stars broke up in it and scattered and came back together and broke again, so that the whole floor of that country had gone from a mirror to a thing alive.

Mei stood in the middle of it with her arms slightly out from her sides.

Iuno looked at the water and could not speak for a moment, because she was eleven years old on the Capitoline with her nose an inch off a basin, and she was twenty-two in the east tower doing the long reading, clear sky in every direction and wind on the water for one hour, leaning east, and one line written underneath it in a young woman's careful hand:

*Weather.*

Thirty years. The single worst piece of filing of her professional life, and it had cost a city its warning, and she had crossed it out on the second page of a blue ledger forty minutes ago with a pencil and written *people* over the top of it, and here it was again, in front of her, in someone else's head, doing it in real time.

She did not file it.

She stood on cold rock beside a woman with her arms out and let the wind out of her own chapel go past both of them and out across the whole length of that arc, and she declined, in full, to write anything down.

⁂

She left the door open.

That was the whole of the works programme. She got to the low silvered door with the warped foot, and she stopped with her hand on it, and she took her hand off it and stepped through and left it standing wide.

The light came out.

It came out low, because the door was low, and it lay down long across the rock and went on going, past the near stones, past the track worn a centimetre and a half into stone that hard, past the arc and the water and the last of the marked ground, and it reached the square of badly cut grass at the end and lay down over that too, gold, at an angle, so that every blade in it stood up with its own shadow behind it and the yellow underneath showed.

A bar of light, low, coming in from the direction of the door.

It made a west out of the doorway.

Mei stood in it for a moment with her hands loose at her sides, in the first light that country had ever had, looking down a long row of stones that had all at once acquired edges.

"That's going to stay on?"

"For as long as there's a fire in the other room."

"And if there isn't?"

"Then somebody will have let it go out," Iuno said, "and there will be a conversation about it."

Mei laughed, one breath, and went down to the water.

⁂

Iuno came up out of it the way you come up a step you had forgotten was there.

She was sitting on the edge of a bed in a dormitory on the third floor of the east wing with her crystal wrist in a sleeping woman's hand, and the false sky beyond the window was running its slow dawn program, engineers' rose-gold coming up the eastern rim on schedule, because in this city even the light keeps appointments.

She looked at the tray on the sill.

The tall cup had stopped steaming and had gone to the temperature at which a thing is worth handing over.

Four minutes.

She lifted it and turned it handle-out, and set the cardamom beside it, and sat with it in her hands a moment; and behind her sternum, in a velvet lounge with a great door standing open at the back of it and a chapel beyond that with a chord still sounding in it, somebody had stopped shuffling the cards.

*Are we going to be alright?* the High Priestess asked.

*Ask me in Jinzhou.*

*…Darling.*

*Sorry.* Iuno turned the cup. *Yes.*

Mei surfaced the way she always surfaced, all at once, from the bottom, with her eyes still shut and one hand already out for the cup because thirty-odd years of being woken by strangers has taught her body to be useful before it is conscious.

<font color=moccasin>"…thanks,"</font> she said.

Iuno put the cup in her hand.

She had spent the night as two women in two houses, and she sat on the edge of a bed built for one student with modest ambitions and could not for the life of her tell which of them had just been thanked, and declined to ask, and let it stand.

<font color=moccasin>"Mm,"</font> Mei said, into the cup. Then, without opening her eyes: <font color=moccasin>"Your hands are freezing."</font>

<font color=royalblue>"It's cold in there."</font>

<font color=moccasin>"Yeah."</font>

⁂

Iuno went down at twenty past five and took the little blue ledger out from under the bread tin, and opened it to the third page, and wrote in the hand that had trembled on the first page and stood anyway:

*A door, north wall of the chapel. Great door on the south. Both left open. No rider on either.*
*Her round: nightly, both hands, water off the sheet. The names go pale in about a minute. She has been doing it long enough to cut a centimetre and a half into rock.*
*Forty she cannot read. She washes them.*
*One more up than down, and it will not hold still. Averted vision. Unnamed.*
*Amy's stands. Dry. Washed three times tonight by me, without asking, and she let me.*
*The count: withheld — by me.*
*A cold door standing on the open flat, snow at the foot of it and nowhere else. Hinges on her side. She knows. Say nothing.*
*Harp: gag out. Four bars, in a room where four bars are impossible. The same four. Ask —*
*The grass is cut. She says she cuts it. She says the blade is off. It is yellow und*

The pen stopped.

Because three floors up, on the roof, a nineteen-year-old in fuzzy socks who had gone out with a stolen cup to watch the light come up the eastern rim had stopped with the cup halfway to her mouth; and a wolf's door went down the corridor, hard, all at once, the way it goes when the nose gets there first; and somewhere above the Riseway passage, coming down the long air through a sealed sky on a schedule nobody in that building had been told about, there was something red.

Iuno left the sentence where it was.

She capped the pen, and put the ledger back under the bread tin, and took her coat off the back of the door — the good one, with the reinforced seams, the one with a smooth piece of Mengzhou lead in the inside pocket holding a space the width of one word.

At the door she turned round and went back for the pot, and for two cups off the rack, because whatever was coming down through a sealed sky at that hour, the people standing under it were going to want tea.

Then she went up the east stairwell two at a time.

And behind her sternum, in a small warm room where two armchairs had faced each other at the same conversational distance for two years, one of them stood turned a few degrees out of true toward the hearth-side floor, with sugar on the arm of it that nobody had brushed off.

And past that, through a great door, in a cold chapel with a fifteenth light standing open a finger's width, the harp went on sounding to an empty room: all twenty-nine strings on the one note, holding the chord across the gap between two houses the way the form requires — the versicle put out from the south side, the response arriving from the north, turn and turn about, before light, on a fixed day.

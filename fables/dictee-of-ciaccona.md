*SHORT TALK ON FIDDLES*

In a river town I will keep to myself I found my uncle's fiddle in a pawnbroker's window. Four of its eight strings gone. The varnish — his recipe, the whole house reeking of it every spring — still legible on the lower bout. Priced under what the case cost new. I bought it. Three days of meals. I played it in the market square every week for seven, our family songs, the ones only a Toccata would know, so if he was anywhere in that town he would hear his own strings calling. On the seventh day a carter said: *shipped out, coastal freight, works under another name now.* I restrung nothing. I left the fiddle with the broker's daughter — ten years old, wanted to learn — with the varnish recipe written out fair. I walked out of that town in the wrong direction, on purpose, and I have never looked for my uncle since. My gift to him was silence. *Me.* Silence. The bard's most expensive instrument is the rest.

&nbsp;

*SHORT TALK ON CHALK*

The acolytes walked straight past me. To Brigida's door. Where they chalked a fool's cap. A little drawing, like a child's. The fish-buyers stopped coming. Her daughter's betrothal went quiet. The customs clerk found a discrepancy. The chalk did the whole day's work of a garrison. Brigida came to me with bread in good cloth and said, kind the whole way through: *You sing beautifully, girl, and every word is true, and I believe you, and you have to go sing it somewhere I'm not.*

&nbsp;

*SHORT TALK ON MADNESS*

When I sang the truth to strangers — the documented, provable truth — do you know what they heard? A young woman alone on the road, shouting that the god is a horse and the saints were murdered. The Order had spent a generation making that speech the recognized sound of a cracked mind. They composed my *genre* in advance. An acolyte pressed a coin into my hand and told me the Order ran a charitable house for women afflicted as I was. He went home feeling *holy.* I stood there holding his coin and understood I could sing from every seawall in Rinascita, and the net effect on the world would be a rise in charitable donations.

## TERPSICHORE &nbsp; &nbsp; CHORIC DANCE

&nbsp;

&nbsp;

*A study in the body that plays.*

&nbsp;

The lute is strung with gut. The gut is from an animal. The animal was alive. The strings remember being alive in the way that all material remembers: when you draw a bow across gut, the sound is a body remembering its body.

The lute's neck has been broken three times. The seam runs along the fingerboard, raised, so that the left hand, playing, reads the scar with every shift. The thumb traces it. Back and forth. A braille of damage.

The player's hands crack in cold weather. The knuckles split along the joints. She plays through it; the keys turn pink at the edges; the audience attends to the sound, which is the point: what the body spends so the ear can receive.

*SHORT TALK ON DOSAGE*

Song is medicine, and medicine is dosage. I watched fifty thousand people witness a saint at once. All that love, poured onto one girl in one instant. She nearly died of being *seen.* So when the saga-books say witness saves — and it does, I have staked my life on it — remember there is a bard keeping the other ledger too. Who gets sung. How loud. When. And who gets the mercy of the dark.

&nbsp;

*SHORT TALK ON THE COPPER BELL*

In the collapsing orrery, when they pushed Lupa out alone into the next page of the story, what was lying in the dust beside her — placed, aimed — was my bell. The instrument that rings the past up out of the air. A bard, going down with a sealed fiction, spent her last free motion leaving her torch where the wolf would land. Lupa rang it against the dark and it gave her: everything. The bell is why anyone alive knows what happened to Mya of Ragunna. The bell was a gift. The bell was the creed made copper.

The sacred, she has learned, lives in the places the auditors skip.

A lullaby survives because it is beneath confiscation.
A lamplighter's ledger matters because it is tedious.

The sacred is: a registrar in a country town, writing a child's birthdate as the previous day's, because a registrar's first duty is to the truth and his second duty is to know when the truth needs help.

The sacred is: an acolyte watching a dead saint walk through her own hometown. *I didn't see anything.* And walking away.

## i. Channel Nine

Channel nine had been classified as equipment testing for thirteen years, which meant that at midnight on a Tuesday, legally, there was nobody on it.

"Evening, Lahai-Roi. It's the hour, you know the voice, and the management would like it noted that this station does not exist and has never existed and any resemblance to a broadcast is a fault in your set." Amy had her boots on the desk and the fader under two fingers. "We're taking calls, we're taking complaints, and we're taking one specific kind of complaint at a reduced rate. Line one, you're on. Where am I finding you."

*"Sublevel four, ma'am. Grid maintenance."*

"You're the welder."

*"I'm the welder."*

"How's the arm."

*"Doctor says six weeks."*

"The doctor says six weeks to *everybody*, that's how you know it's a real profession. What do you want to hear."

*"Something with a bit of speed in it. There's four hours left and the conduit's winning."*

"Four hours. Right. Sublevel four, this is a fast one, and I want it on the record that when the conduit files its report it should mention that you had *help*."

She ran it. She talked over the intro the way she had at fourteen, riding the last eight bars, landing the final syllable a half-beat ahead of the vocal, which was a thing she could do and knew she could do and had never once got tired of.

Line two was a girl who could not sleep before an examination. Amy gave her a song and a lie about her own results, which had been excellent, and which she described as catastrophic in a way that got a laugh out of a stranger at nineteen minutes past midnight, which was the entire product.

Line three was somebody's grandmother who wanted to complain about the tram timetable.

"Ma'am, this is a pirate radio station."

*"You're the only one who answers."*

"...That's fair. Give me the route number."

Line four had no registry.

She saw it come up on the little board — the strip that should have carried an exchange prefix sitting blank, the way a blank looks when a machine has tried three times and given up — and she looked at it for about a second and a half, and put her boots down, and keyed it, because she was her mother's daughter and had never once in her life left a door unopened.

"Caller, you're on. Where am I finding you."

*"In a tree."*

The voice came through with weather in it. Wind across a bad microphone, a long way off, and under the wind a laziness that had nothing to do with being tired.

"...Bold," said Amy. "Which tree."

*"Yours."*

She kept the fader steady, because that was the job.

"We've got about nine hundred trees."

*"You've got one with a light in the third-floor window at four in the morning."*

"That's the drying room."

*"It's not."*

"Caller, this is a music programme," said Amy, cheerfully, cool as lake ice, "and you are a *situation*, and I've had situations before and every one of them ended with somebody's uncle apologising. Do you want a song or do you want the number for campus security? I can do both. I'm extremely versatile—"

*"Song."*

"Which one."

*"The one you won't finish."*

The room went on being a storage cupboard with a rig in it. The needle sat where it sat. Amy's hand stayed on the fader.

"We don't take requests for that one."

*"Who's 'we'?"*

"It's a station. There's a policy."

*"You're a nineteen-year-old in a cupboard."*

"I'm a *legend* in a cupboard, and I'd like that distinction preserved for the historical record." She was smiling. Her face was running the whole professional apparatus and the rest of her had gone somewhere cold. "Caller. Serious question. Who is this."

The wind went across the microphone.

*"Somebody who used to be very good at finding you."*

"That's not a—"

*"You had a hat with ears on it,"* said the voice. *"You wore it indoors, and you were extremely unpleasant on the subject."*

Amy sat very still in a storage cupboard in the Phosphor District with thirty thousand people in the dark on the other side of a fader.

"Caller—"

And the voice hummed four bars.

Four bars of a kitchen tune, the kind a person makes up with flour on her hands, going up at the third the way a question goes up, hanging on the fourth, waiting — and stopping, in the same place, at the same silence, at the exact door where it had always stopped, for thirteen years, in the head of a girl who had never once been able to make it go further.

The line cut.

Amy sat in eleven seconds of dead air, which on radio is a catastrophe, and which two hundred separate listeners would afterward describe in two hundred separate ways.

Then she brought the fader up and said, in the coolest voice available to her, "That was a prank call, everybody. That was a *prank call*, and it was rude, and I want you all to know that I *definitely* have her number."

She played something loud.

Under the desk her hands would not do what she asked them.

## ii. Terms

The call came back the next night at seventeen minutes past midnight.

Amy had the board watched. She had also, in the intervening twenty hours, told nobody, which she noticed herself doing, and filed, and did nothing about.

"Caller, you're on."

*"You've had a day."*

"I've had a day."

*"And?"*

"And I've got about four hundred questions and I've put them in order and the first one's mostly swearing," said Amy. "Where are you."

*"Frostlands. Top of your Riseway. There's a woman up here selling the worst coffee I have ever had in a very long career of eating things off the ground."*

"Sonya."

*"Sonya."*

"Sonya's coffee is a *civic institution*—"

*"Sonya's coffee is a war crime, and I told her so, and she agreed, and she charged me the same."* A pause with a grin in it. *"Come up."*

Amy leaned back in the chair.

"Come down," she said.

The wind went across the microphone for a while.

*"No."*

"There's a whole—" Amy stopped, and started again, and got it flatter. "There's a house down here. There's nine of us. There's a spare bunk, and there's a woman who'd take one look at you and put a plate in front of you before you finished saying no, and one of them is four hundred years old and she'd *cry*, and at this point she cries at everything, it's just her weather—"

*"No."*

"Why."

*"Come up and I'll tell you."*

"Tell me now or I'm not coming."

*"You're coming,"* said the voice. *"You've been coming since I hummed. You've probably got the shuttle times up on another screen."*

Amy looked at the shuttle times on the other screen.

"...I hate you," she said, with total sincerity.

*"Mm."*

"I don't even know your name."

The silence on the line had a different texture from the others.

*"Camellya."*

Amy put her hand flat over the microphone, which is an amateur's move, which she had not made since she was fifteen, and sat in her own storage cupboard with her eyes shut for the length of one held note.

Then she took her hand off.

"Five-fifteen," she said. "There's a bench at the top by the fuel shed. If you're not on it I'm going home and I'm going to be *extremely* unpleasant about you on air for a month."

*"Bring a coat."*

"I'm *from* here—"

*"Bring a coat, weed."*

The line cut.

Amy sat there for a while.

Then she got up and left the rig running on the dead band, because she had done that every night for a year when she was fourteen and the habit had outlived its reason and she had never once audited it, and she went and wrote a note and put it on the fridge where the household would find it at six.

The note said: *gone up the mountain. back thursday. don't worry about me — actually, worry a bit, that's the whole reason I'm telling you. love you. A.*

She read it twice, and left the second half in, and went to get her coat.

## iii. The Prime Minister

She was on the bench.

Amy came off the shuttle into a wind that took the first four things she had planned to say, and there was a woman sitting on a fuel-shed bench in the minus-twenty with bare arms, wine-dark hair going sideways, holding a paper cup of Sonya's coffee at arm's length like evidence.

She stood up.

They looked at each other across nine meters of packed ice, and neither of them moved, and behind them the wind combed the drifts into long parallel sentences.

"You're short," said Camellya.

"You're *rude*."

"I'm accurate."

"You're rude *and* accurate—"

"You were going to be tall," said Camellya. "You told me so. You were extremely confident about it and I remember being persuaded."

Amy's throat did something. She put a stop to it.

"Yeah, well. The market moved."

Camellya crossed the nine meters.

Up close she was exactly the height Amy's whole body had been expecting, which was the first thing that went wrong. The second was the flower at her sternum. The third was that she smelled of pine and snow and, faintly, of the green of a cut lawn, and Amy had spent twenty-two years telling herself she had invented that smell.

"Right," said Camellya. She looked her over, top to bottom, with the frank appraisal of a woman inspecting a horse she had once owned. "You did the whole thing, then."

"I did the whole thing."

"Ten years in a machine over a city."

"Who told you."

"A woman with a lot of fog and forty-seven robots that dust." Camellya threw the coffee into a drift. "She's got you on file. She's got everybody on file. It's a personality flaw, she's aware of it, she has decided to keep it, and I have never once been able to work out whether to congratulate her or throw a rock at her."

"What'd you settle on."

"Both. Rotating." She turned and started walking, downhill, off the pad, out toward the white. "Lake first."

"The lake's frozen."

"That's the *point*, weed."

---

The Snowfluff Seals came up through the ice at eleven in the morning, all of them, the whole squishy government of them, in a slow parliamentary procession out of a hole they maintained by the simple method of sitting in it.

Amy walked out onto the ice and sat down cross-legged at the edge of it, and the biggest one hauled itself out and put its enormous wet head on her knee and honked at a volume that carried to the far shore.

"Hello, Prime Minister," said Amy.

Camellya stopped ten paces back.

"That's a different animal."

"That's him."

"Weed. That is a completely different animal."

"That's the *Prime Minister*—"

"The one you're thinking of was about half that size and had a notch in the—" Camellya stopped walking and stood in the wind with the sentence continuing without her, her mouth about a second and a half ahead of the rest of her, the way it had gone on the radio.

Amy watched her stop.

"Yeah," she said, quietly. "He had a notch in the left flipper. Ma said he got it arguing."

Camellya put the heel of her hand against her sternum and pressed.

"That's a different animal," she said again, from further away.

"It's an *office*," said Amy. "Not a seal. Whoever's biggest is Prime Minister. Ma set it up that way on purpose so I wouldn't work out that they die."

"Did it work."

"For about four years. Then I got good at counting."

The wind came across the lake. The government redistributed itself with a great deal of complaint. Camellya came the last ten paces and sat down on the ice beside her, and after a moment a smaller seal came and put its chin on her boot, and she looked at it with the expression of a woman receiving a summons.

"It's an office," she said.

"Yeah."

"Mm." She scratched the seal between the eyes. "That's most people, isn't it. Whoever's standing there gets the title. The one before hands it over, and everybody goes on saying the same name at whoever's in the chair."

"Are we talking about seals."

"We're talking about seals," said Camellya, and they were not.

---

Amy's handheld went off in her coat.

She read it and typed back one-handed, and it went off again eleven seconds later, and she typed again, and it went off a third time.

"That's the house," she said. "That's — hang on. That's Cartethyia asking if I ate. That's the wolf asking where exactly. And that's the seer, who hasn't asked me anything, who has sent me a photograph of the fridge with my own note on it and nothing else, which is *psychological warfare*—"

"What'd you tell them."

"That I'm fine."

"Are you."

"I'm *fine*," said Amy, typing.

Camellya watched her thumbs.

"You do the voice on the radio," she said.

"I do a *lot* of voices on the radio, that's the—"

"You do your mother's."

Amy's thumbs stopped.

"That's what I heard," said Camellya. "I was a long way south on a set I stole off a fisherman, and somebody said *there's a girl on channel nine again*, and I sat down on a crate and listened to three hours of a stranger being extremely funny about seals. And about forty minutes in you did the bright one. The one for the room." She kept scratching the seal. "I have listened to somebody play that instrument for longer than this city has had a name. I know every string on it. I never once got underneath it, and I had two thousand years and unlimited access, and I would like you to understand what it did to me to hear it come out of a nineteen-year-old at one in the morning over a dead channel."

Amy put the handheld in her pocket.

"So you came."

"So I came."

"You came a thousand kilometers and you won't come two hundred more." Amy turned round on the ice. "That's the thing I want. That's the four hundred questions, all of them, that's the only one. She's *down there.* She's asleep in a bunk with her foot out from under the blanket, and there's a flower on her windowsill in a glass of water she's been changing the water in for two months, and *I know where that flower came from*, and I haven't told a single person, and you won't come and *look at her.*"

The seals moved about. The lake ice made its long sound underneath them, geological and unbothered.

"How long have I got," said Camellya. "That's your question. You've just worked it out and you're about to be very rude to me about it, so let's save the ten minutes."

Amy stared at her.

"...What."

"Four days," said Camellya. "That's the budget. Near her I get about a week before the walls start talking, and I spent three of it on a magnolia courtyard and a flower and a very good exit, and I've been paying that off up here ever since." She went on scratching the seal. Her flower had gone a shade darker at the edges and had stayed there. "Near *you* I get four days."

"Near me."

"Four days."

"Why is it *less*—"

"Because it was never her in the room," said Camellya.

## iv. Eleven Days

They walked.

The cabin was two hours across the drifts, and Camellya walked it barefoot to the ankle in the snow, and declined to discuss this, and Amy gave up on the subject somewhere in the first twenty minutes.

"Explain the room."

"There's a room in me and it's locked."

"That's not an explanation, that's a *diagram*—"

"It's the best I've got and I've had a long time to improve it." Camellya walked with her hands loose at her sides, reading the ground the way other people read a page. "I was made to do one thing. I got out of the job. And getting out cost me, because the thing they built to make me do the job is still in here, and it has very strong opinions about where I go and who I go toward, and the only way I've ever found to disagree with it is to take the reason out of my own head."

"You wipe yourself."

"I wipe myself. It's an old habit, it's kept me alive, it's the single most cowardly thing anybody in this world does routinely, and I'd like credit for saying that first." She stepped over a drift. "But some of it declines to wipe. Some of it goes into a room and the room gets a lock, and the lock is the only reason the rest of me is walking around, and behind the door there's a kitchen and a lake and a blue door and a small extremely loud person in a hat with ears on it who used to—"

She stopped walking.

Amy stopped four paces on and turned round.

"Who used to what."

"That's the door," said Camellya. "That's it exactly. Every time. Four bars and a door."

They walked.

---

She came back to it at the top of the last rise, with the hollow below them and the lake behind and the roof of a cabin showing through snow.

"I stood here," said Camellya.

Amy stopped.

"Eleven days before the thing you did. I've counted it back four times since your fisherman's radio and I get eleven every time." She had her hands in her pockets and her eyes on the hollow. "I don't remember why I came. I never do. I get a pull and the legs go and the head follows about a week later asking questions nobody answers. I came up this road and stood right about here, and there was a cabin with snow to the sills and a blue door and no smoke, and I went down and stood outside it for an hour."

"Camellya—"

"And I looked in the window," she said, "and there was a fire laid in the hearth. Small sticks first. Done properly. And one match on the stone beside it."

The wind came down off the ridge.

"And I looked at it, and it meant nothing to me. It was a fire somebody hadn't lit. That was the whole of what I had." She started down the slope. "I ate soup off a man two valleys over and slept in a pine and went south in the morning."

Amy stood on the rise with her hands in her pockets.

"A laid fire and one match is a *goodbye*," said Camellya, from below her, without turning round. "Anybody who has ever kept a house in her life reads that off the glass in four seconds. I had kept a house. I *had* one. And I'd put it in a room and locked the room, because keeping it was killing me, and I chose to live." She reached the bottom and looked up. "So that's my file. That's what's in it. I was eleven days early and I couldn't read the window."

Amy came down the slope in six strides, badly, sliding the last of it.

"That wasn't your fault."

"Mm."

"You didn't *know*—"

"No."

"You *couldn't* have known, the room was locked, that's not a — that's a *medical thing*, that's an *injury*, and you don't get to stand there and file yourself under—" Amy's hands had come up on their own. "And if you'd known you'd have gone in. You'd have walked through that door and sat down in my kitchen and made me look at you and said *what is this fire for*, and I'd have had to say it out loud, and if I'd had to say it out loud—"

She stopped.

Camellya waited. She was very good at waiting.

"Say that again," she said. "Slower."

"No."

"Say it again and put your mother's name where mine is."

"*No.*"

"Then put yours."

Amy stood in the snow outside a cabin she had left ready thirteen years ago and said nothing for a long time, and the wind took the whole of it, fairly, at the same speed it takes everything.

"It's a different situation," she said.

"Mm," said Camellya. "Open the door, weed. I'm cold, and I'd like you to appreciate how rare that sentence is."

## v. A Fire, Laid

The blue door was a blue door. Some colours keep their promises.

Amy took the key off the cord around her neck and stood at the lock for eleven seconds, and Camellya stood behind her and said nothing, and then the key went in and the cold came out and they went inside.

It smelled of cold wood and old paper and, thirteen years down under everything, of cocoa.

Camellya walked into the middle of the room and stopped.

Her flower went dark. She put a hand flat on the table and stood breathing through her mouth like somebody at altitude, and Amy — with a hand half out and no plan for it — watched a kitchen do to an indestructible woman what two thousand years of the world had failed to manage.

"Do you want—"

"Give me a minute."

Amy gave her a minute.

At the end of it Camellya took her hand off the table, and both of them looked at the four fingerprints pressed into the wood, which was oak, and eighty years old.

"Sorry about the table."

"It's fine."

"It's not fine, I've been in the house forty seconds—"

"It's *fine*," said Amy, "because it means you were here, and there are about four things in this house that prove anybody was ever here, and now there's five. Sit down before you fall down."

Camellya sat down.

"That's a hell of a thing to say to somebody about a table."

"I've had a long time to think about tables."

---

The fire was laid.

She had known it would be. She had known for thirteen years, the way you know a thing you have declined to check, and she knelt at the hearth and looked at her own nineteen-year-old handiwork — small sticks first, done properly, the way she had been taught by a woman in a coat — and at the single match on the stone beside it.

Camellya came and looked over her shoulder.

"That's a good fire."

"Yeah."

"Somebody taught you."

"Somebody taught me."

"So what's it for," said Camellya.

Amy sat back on her heels.

"It's for when she comes home."

The wind did something in the chimney.

"She came home."

"I *know* she—"

"She's asleep in a bunk two hundred kilometers south with her mouth open and a foot out from under the blanket. You told me that yourself, on the ice, and you said it like a woman reading a weather report." Camellya crouched down beside her. "So what's the fire for."

Amy knelt in front of a hearth in a house she had left ready for a woman who had already arrived, and had no answer, and heard the shape of the question keep going after the question stopped, the way the four bars kept going after they stopped.

She picked up the match.

Her hand shook, which annoyed her enormously, and she struck it on the stone and it broke, and she said a word she had learned from a woman out of another world entirely, and Camellya laughed out loud — a bark, delighted, the sound of somebody genuinely surprised — and took a second match out of the tin on the mantel and put it in her hand without comment.

The fire took.

It went up the way a fire goes up when it has been laid by somebody who was paying attention, all at once and with no drama at all, and the room came out of the dark in pieces. A record player. A shelf of game cartridges lined up by hand. A mug on the left side of the shelf, handle out, ready.

Amy sat down on the floor with her back to the couch and watched it burn.

Camellya sat down beside her.

Outside, the snow went past the window at a little under one meter per second, every flake finishing a decision it had made forty minutes earlier, high up, in the dark, where nobody was watching.

"It's warm," Amy said, eventually.

"Mm."

"I want to be very clear that this isn't a *breakthrough*. I'm not having a *moment*."

"You're having a moment."

"I'm having a *small* moment."

"You're having a whole one. I've seen a great many of them. I'd know."

"You'd forget."

"Reliably," said Camellya. "Which is why I intend to be extremely obnoxious about it while I've still got it."

---

They played, because there was a machine and there were cartridges and because the alternative was talking.

Camellya won eleven games in a row.

"That's *cheating*."

"It's a controller. I'm holding it the way you're holding it."

"You've never *seen* this—"

"I've never seen anything," said Camellya, thumbs moving. "That's the whole trick."

"That's not a trick, that's a *diagnosis*—"

"You're playing against a memory of the last time you played." She took the corner without slowing. "You've got a whole library in there. Every match you lost. Every thing that worked once. Every clever move some kid pulled on you in a dormitory. You consult it. It's very impressive and it costs you about a fifth of a second." On the screen something of Amy's came apart. "I've got a body. The body's read the room already. There's nothing in the way."

"That's *horrifying*."

"It's restful."

"Play left-handed."

Camellya swapped hands without looking and won the twelfth.

Amy threw the controller into the cushions and lay flat on the floor and looked at the ceiling of her mother's house.

"I've been asking for a rematch for twenty years," she said, to the ceiling. "Out loud. To people. I said it to a whole lunch table once. I said *I wish we could gather for a rematch someday* like it was a joke, and somebody asked who with, and I said *my big sister*, and then I had to spend forty minutes explaining that I don't have a—"

She stopped.

The fire moved in the grate.

"Twenty years is a long time to hold a rematch open," said Camellya.

"Yeah, well." Amy put an arm over her eyes. "You were busy."

---

Later, with the fire down to its working heat and the kettle on, she showed her the doorframe.

It stood between the kitchen and the front room and went up like a ladder, and Camellya put her thumb at the bottom and read her way up.

"Six. Six and a half—"

"She insisted."

"—she *insisted*. Seven. That's an adult's hand."

"That's Ma."

"And then—" Camellya's thumb moved. "—that's a kid trying to write like an adult."

"That's me. Every year. Ruler flat on the head, pencil in the other hand." Amy leaned on the frame with her arms folded. "You can't measure yourself. That's the whole thing about it. Somebody else is supposed to hold the pencil."

"Is that why they're crooked."

"They're crooked *on purpose*, thank you. After the third one I stopped correcting them." She said it flat, the way she said everything that mattered. "It's evidence."

"Of?"

"That nobody was holding it."

Camellya said nothing. Her thumb went up past twelve, past fifteen, past eighteen, into the blank wood above — all that empty frame somebody had left room for.

Then she stopped.

"What's this one."

"That's the weird one," said Amy. "Don't ask me, I've never worked it out. There's no name on it, it's about nine centimeters above eighteen, and it's *straight*, which means it isn't mine. I think Ma did it as a joke about how tall I was going to get and then forgot to write on it." She shrugged. "It's the only line up there that's dead level. It used to really annoy me."

Camellya looked at the mark for a long moment.

"Mm," she said, and went and got the kettle.

## vi. House Rules

They had the hot chocolate at the table, made to the correct recipe, which is a crime.

"There's salt in this."

"There's supposed to be salt in it."

"There's *salt* in it."

"It's a *secret ingredient*—"

"It's a *crime*, weed, it's a documented—" Camellya stopped with the mug halfway down.

Amy watched her stop.

"Yeah," she said. "You said that too. Every time. You said it was a crime and then you drank the whole thing and asked for more, and Ma used to make an extra cup and put it by your elbow without saying anything about it, and one night you fell asleep at this table with your face in your arms and I drew on the back of your hand and you had it on you for two days before you noticed."

The mug went down.

"Tell me," said Camellya.

"No."

"Amy."

"*No.*" Amy put both hands flat on the table. "You've got four days and you've spent one and a half, and you burned six hours of it on a *lake*, and you did that on purpose so I couldn't say no to you later, and I've seen the technique. My *mother* invented the technique."

"That's very good," said Camellya. "That's completely correct, I'm proud of you, and I'm going to keep asking."

"It'll hurt you."

"Yes."

"It might do worse than hurt you."

"Also yes."

"Then *why*—"

"Because I have never in my entire life wanted to remember anything," said Camellya.

The wind did something in the chimney.

"Two thousand years," she said. "Longer. I've thrown all of it away and I'd throw it away again tomorrow and I have never once lain awake about it. I've watched empires go under and thought about lunch. And I sat on a fuel-shed bench for two days drinking the worst coffee on this continent, waiting for a shuttle, so a girl could come up a mountain and be rude to me — and I *wanted* to be there. It's new. I've got very little that's new." She looked up. "And I'd like to spend some of it on you. Because whatever I was to you, I stood in your snow eleven days early and read the window wrong, and I would like to file an appeal."

Amy sat in her mother's kitchen with her hands around a cup of hot chocolate she had made wrong on purpose.

"Then we do it my way," she said.

"Meaning?"

"Meaning there are rules." She got up and went to the shelf and came back with the wind-up clock that had lived on it since before she was born, and set it on the table between them, and turned it to face Camellya. "Six of them. They're on the back of a lab printout and they're on a fridge two hundred kilometers south, and nine people spent four hours at a kitchen table arguing them into existence, and every one of them was written for me, and I'm going to lend them to you."

"Read them out."

"One. I say *going in* before I go. When I come back I say a number, and it's a different number every time, so nobody has to guess whether they've got me or something wearing me." Amy sat down. "That's Chisa's. She's a woman with scissors and she thinks about things nobody else has the stomach to think about."

"Good rule."

"Two." She held her hand out, palm up, and waited. "Somebody's got a hand on you the whole time. That one's the wolf's. She made me put it in the list out loud, and she said—" Amy's voice went somewhere for a second and came back "—she said, *that's not for you. That's for me. I'll take the shift.*"

Camellya put her hand in Amy's.

It was too hot. Something under the skin of the palm moved when Amy closed her fingers. Amy closed her fingers.

"Three. Somebody listens. Chisa again — she can hear a frequency go from *out* to *gone*. Out here that's me, and I'm the best ear in this hemisphere, and I'll know before you do."

"Four?"

"Four's the clock." Amy wound it. "Eleven minutes and I'm coming to get you."

"Eleven's arbitrary."

"Eleven's the number she can tolerate," said Amy, and let it sit, and declined to explain it, and Camellya looked at the clock for a while and then let it go.

"Five?"

Amy hesitated.

Then she reached into her coat and brought out nine inches of felt with black yarn hair and two small gold buttons and a coat sewn out of a year she had missed, which she had taken off a shelf at eleven o'clock the previous night without asking anybody, and put it in Camellya's other hand.

"Five. Ma goes in the hands. Fold your fingers over her before you go, so they're shut, so nobody in the room has to sit there looking at them open."

Camellya looked down at it for a long time.

"Who made this."

"Cartethyia. During."

"During what."

"Eleven weeks," said Amy. "Nine of them were the wolf, on nights. Cartethyia had mornings. My body was in a bed with its eyes open and its hands like that, and this is what she carried around, and she says it did absolutely nothing at all, and she gave it to me anyway, because now I'm in there and I can hold things."

Camellya folded her fingers over it.

"And six."

"Six is you do it where people can see you. No sneaking off at two in the morning to spare anybody the sight." Amy squared her shoulders. "That one's Ma's. She said she'd made every mistake on the list personally and she'd like one generation to skip a step."

"Is she right."

"She's *insufferably* right, and she got there by being wrong for two lifetimes, which is the only method anybody in my family respects." Amy set the clock down. "Ready?"

"Give me the thing."

"You taught me to fall," said Amy.

Camellya's hand tightened.

"I was six," said Amy, "and I went up the wisteria over the porch because the seals were coming through the ice and I wanted the high ground, and I got stuck, and Ma was two hours away at the trading post. And you came round the corner of the house with a dead something over your shoulder, and you looked up at me for about a second and a half, and you put the dead thing down, and you said—"

She stopped. Her voice had gone somewhere and she waited for it.

"—you said, *don't stiffen up. It's the stiffening that breaks you. Go loose and let the ground have you.* And I said I'd die. And you said, *you'll die eventually, that's not today's problem, let go.*"

The clock ticked.

"And I let go," said Amy. "And you caught me. And then you made me do it eleven more times off the porch rail, and you counted them out loud, and on the eleventh you stepped back and let me hit the snow, and I rolled, and I got up, and you said—" she was smiling; it was a terrible smile and there was nothing she could do about it "—you said, *there. Now you can jump at anything.*"

Camellya's flower had gone black.

"Going in," she said.

"Number when you come back."

"Going *in*, weed."

"Say it properly—"

"Going in," said Camellya, and the lights in her went out.

---

It went wrong at ninety seconds.

There was a sound out of her that Amy had heard exactly once before, out at the rim of everything, off a stratum of drifting testimony where ten thousand voices lay preserved at the moment of their subtraction, and Amy held on to her hand.

At two minutes the vines came.

They came out of both forearms at once and went into the floor and the floor gave, and they ran along the boards and up the wall and around the doorframe, and the doorframe held. The room filled with the smell of crushed green and something under it like frost cracking. The mug came off the table and broke. The fire went sideways in the grate and stood back up.

At three the record player started by itself, played four seconds of nothing, and stopped.

Amy stayed where she was.

"Four minutes," she said, to a woman who was not there. "You're at four. That's *fine*. She gave me eleven because eleven was what she could stand, and I've been in a room where nine people picked a number for me, and you've got the whole eleven, you can have all of it, it's *paid for*—"

At five the vines went slack.

At six they drew back in, everywhere at once, and the boards came back down more or less flat, and the wall let go of what it had been holding, and the doorframe stood there covered in green and went on standing.

At six-forty Camellya's fingers moved on the felt.

"Ninety-six," she said.

Amy's eyes shut.

"That's a lie," she said. "You never gave me a first number, so ninety-six is a lie, and you said it to find out whether I was paying attention."

"Were you."

"*Yes*, you absolute—"

"Good," said Camellya, and opened her eyes, and Amy watched them come back on one after another, like a building.

They sat there.

Camellya's flower came up off black, through purple, and settled at rose. She looked at the wreckage of a room she had crossed without moving — the boards, the crockery, the green up the walls — and then down at nine inches of felt in her fist, and unfolded her fingers one at a time, carefully, as though the doll might have been damaged by her.

"I held a pencil," she said.

## vii. The Pencil

"What?"

"I held a pencil in this house." Camellya was frowning at the middle distance, her mouth running slightly ahead of her, the way it had on the ice with the notch in the flipper. "You'd had a bad day about something. You were small, and you were *furious* about being small, and you'd been standing at that frame stamping your feet, and I came in with snow on me and you turned round and you were absolutely—" she made a shape with one hand, a small vertical enraged shape "—*incandescent*."

Amy had stopped breathing.

"And I said: how tall do you want to be. And you said, *taller than everybody.* And I said that's greedy and unrealistic and I respect it." Camellya got up. She went to the doorframe with her hand out in front of her like a woman walking in the dark. "And then you asked me how tall you were going to *get*. Which nobody can answer. And you were seven, and you needed a number."

She stopped in front of the frame, in front of the blank wood above the last mark, and put her thumb on the straight, level, unlabelled line nine centimeters above it.

"So I put the pencil where my own head was," she said, "and I drew a line, and I said *there*."

Amy came off the chair.

"That's your—"

"Stand me against it."

"Camellya—"

"*Stand me against it*," said Camellya, and turned round and put her heels to the frame and her shoulders flat and her chin level, the way you do, the way everybody does, the way children have done at doorframes in every house in every world since somebody first thought of it.

Amy got the ruler out of the drawer.

Her hands were unsteady. She laid the ruler flat on the top of Camellya's head and slid it in against the wood, and looked, and had to look again, and then had to put a hand on the frame.

The line crossed the edge of the ruler dead centre. It went through it the way a line goes through the thing it was drawn against — same height, same wood, same body, thirteen years apart.

"You're the same."

"Told you."

"You *didn't* tell me. You don't remember telling me. You've *never* told me—"

"I'm telling you now," said Camellya.

Amy put her forehead against the doorframe.

She stayed there a while. There was green all over the wood from the vines, drying, going brown at the edges, and under it thirteen years of pencil, and under her forehead a mark a woman had made as an answer on an ordinary afternoon and then lost — and that a girl had looked at ten thousand times, alone, without once knowing it was a record of somebody having been in the room.

"Everything moved," she said, into the wood. "Everything. Ma's face moved. Her *name* moved. My body moved and then it stopped moving and then I didn't have one. The city moved. They built a whole new *sky*." She turned her head against the frame. "And this stupid line's been sitting here the entire time being exactly the right height for you."

"Mm."

"Do you know how insulting that is."

"It's extremely insulting," Camellya agreed. "I'm reliable in the least useful possible way. Get a pencil."

"Why."

"Because it's got no name on it," said Camellya, "and I'd like one."

Amy wrote it.

She wrote it in her own hand, which had been trained at eight to copy an adult's and had never entirely stopped, and it came out crooked, and she left it, and the frame between the kitchen and the front room of a cabin in the Roya Frostlands acquired, thirteen years late, the word **CAMELLYA** beside a straight level line.

"There's no number on it."

"You don't have one."

"Everybody's got a number."

"You're *immortal*—"

"Put one," said Camellya. "Any of them. It's a frame. It's supposed to say a year."

Amy thought about it, and wrote a small *?*, and under it, smaller, *(she wouldn't say)* — and Camellya read it and laughed so hard she had to sit down on the floor.

---

"Right," she said, from the floor. "You."

"Me what."

"You stopped at eighteen."

"I stopped *growing* at—"

"You stopped at eighteen *on the frame*," said Camellya, "and it's been eighteen for thirteen years, and there's a nineteen missing, and I'm the one holding the pencil. Get over here."

Amy stood in the middle of the room.

"That's—" she said. "That's a lot, actually."

"Mm."

"I'd like it noted that that's a lot—"

"It's noted. Come here."

She came.

She put her heels to the frame and her shoulders flat and her chin level, and it was the most exposed she had felt since a technician opened a pod and found the word gone out of the clay, and Camellya stood up and laid the ruler on her head and made no remark whatsoever about the fact that Amy's eyes were shut.

The pencil went against the wood.

"Hold still."

"I'm *holding*—"

"You're vibrating, weed."

The line went on.

Amy stepped away and turned round and looked at it, and it lay on top of eighteen. Same wood. Same height. A girl who had stopped growing at twelve and had gone on climbing a ladder that stayed exactly where it was, for six years, out of pure spite.

"That's the same line."

"Mm."

"It's *straight*, as well."

"Mm."

"Put a wobble in it."

"No."

"Camellya—"

"No," said Camellya, and wrote *19* beside it.

And then, without asking, underneath it, she wrote *29*, and put a bracket down the side joining the two.

Amy's mouth opened.

"You can't—"

"You did the ten," said Camellya, still writing, small, with her nose close to the wood, the way people write when they intend a thing to last. "Ten years on your own in a machine over a city, with the eye on you, keeping a calendar nobody was ever going to check and observing Tuesdays for an audience of nobody. And there are nine people in a dormitory who'd take a knife for you, and not one of them counts it, because you don't let them, because you only ever bring the ten out when it wins you an argument."

"That's not real time, that's—"

"It's on the frame now." Camellya stepped back and looked at her work: two numbers and a bracket and a crooked name and thirteen years of a child's handwriting going up a piece of eighty-year-old oak in a house nobody had lived in. "That's what a frame's *for*. It's a list of afternoons when somebody was standing there with a pencil. The height's an excuse. It's always been an excuse."

## viii. Attendance

She left on the fourth morning, which Amy had known she would do since the fuel-shed bench.

They walked up to the shuttle pad through snow that had come in overnight and taken the path Amy had salted, fairly, at the same speed it takes everything.

"You're going to go without saying goodbye."

"I'm saying it now."

"You're saying it *forty minutes early*, which in your family counts as—"

"My *family*," said Camellya, and stopped in the middle of a white field with the wind coming off the ridge, and looked at a girl in a good scarf with a house key on a string under her collar, and something crossed her face that had a great deal of weather in it and resolved into nothing a person could use.

"What."

"Nothing. It's a good word. I've heard it four times in four days and every time it's been about me, and I keep waiting for somebody to correct it." She started walking. "You're going to tell her."

Amy walked eleven steps.

"Spring," she said.

"Why spring."

"Because the wisteria comes up in spring." Her hands were in her pockets and her eyes were down the valley. "She's better at hard things when there's something to look at. She goes and stands at a window and then she can say it. If I give her a window she'll get through it."

"Mm," said Camellya. "That's a good plan."

"It's a *stalling* plan."

"It's both. Most good plans are." They came round the last turn and the pad lay below them with its one light on. "Who'll be in the room."

"The seer."

"Not the wolf?"

"She'd break something."

"Then she'd fix it badly and pretend she meant the crack." Camellya nodded. "Take the seer. Seers are good in a room. They've already had the conversation nine times in their heads and they've got the boring parts pre-solved."

"You've met a lot of seers."

"I've met two and I've been unbearable to both. It's a small field and I'm well known in it."

The shuttle came up out of the white with its lamps swinging.

"Hey," said Amy.

"Mm."

"When are you coming back."

Camellya stopped with one boot on the step.

Two thousand years of a woman who followed a pull and had never in all of it made an appointment stood in the door of a shuttle in the Roya Frostlands and did the arithmetic, and it took her four seconds, which at her age constitutes a life's work.

"Spring," she said.

"You don't do that."

"I'm aware."

"You've never *once*—"

"I'm aware, weed, and I'd like a receipt." Camellya put her hand out. "Say it back so it's binding."

"Spring."

"Spring." She stepped up into the car. "And if the walls start talking early, assume weather, and start without me, and tell me the whole thing afterwards at that table with the salt crime, and I'll go through a wall about it and you can hold my hand and read me the list."

"That's a *terrible* arrangement—"

"It's the arrangement."

"How long do you have to be away," said Amy. "Before it refills. The four days. How long."

The door started across.

Camellya looked at her through the closing gap with an expression Amy would spend a good part of the following season failing to decode.

"Play me out," she said.

And the shuttle went down the mountain into the dark with its lamps swinging, and Amy stood on the pad in the wind, holding a question she had asked properly, out loud, in time, and had not been answered — which was a new experience, and which she disliked enormously.

## ix. One Listener

She went on air the next night at midnight, because she had promised the welder.

She did the hour straight. She did the timetable complaint for the grandmother, who had been telling people, so that three separate callers now phoned in about trams and the station had accidentally become a service. She played something fast for sublevel four. She was, by every measure available, excellent.

At forty past she said: "Right. Housekeeping. Some of you heard a prank call on Tuesday."

She let it sit.

"I lied," said Amy. "It was somebody I've known since I was four, who I hadn't seen in twenty-two years, who turned up out of the frostlands with no manners whatsoever and ate my breakfast, metaphorically. And I'm going to tell you about it in a way that gets me into no trouble at all, because I've got a family who listen to this show and about six of them are extremely good at reading between things."

She took the fader down a hair, which was a thing she did when she meant something, and which four hundred people had noticed over the years and written to her about.

"Here's the shape of it. There's a person in your life. You've got something of theirs. Something they lost — a year, a name, a house, doesn't matter — and you're the only one left carrying it, and every single day you decide to go on holding it, because handing it over is going to hurt them, and you love them, and you've done the sums, and the sums say keep it."

Somewhere in a sublevel a welder put a torch down.

"And you get very, very good at carrying it," said Amy. "You get *virtuosic*. You build your whole day around it. You get up at four. You pour tea out of windows. And then somebody sits down next to you on some ice and says: check which one of you decides where you go."

She let eleven seconds run, which on radio is a catastrophe, and which she did on purpose.

"That's the item," she said. "Nobody call in about it, I'll cut you off. Here's a song."

She played the one she plays.

And at four minutes past one, with the board dark and the city down and the rig running on nothing the way it had every night since she was fourteen, the strip with no registry on it came up.

Amy looked at it for about a second and a half.

Then she keyed it.

"Caller, you're on. Where am I finding you."

*"Long way south. There's a coast down here doing something interesting."* Wind across a bad microphone. *"That was a very good hour."*

"You listened to the whole thing?"

*"I've listened to every one since Tuesday. I've got a stolen radio, a bad conscience, and nothing else in the world to do."* A pause with a grin in it. *"You said twenty-two years."*

"I did the sums on the shuttle."

*"Mm."*

"You'd have got it wrong."

*"I'd have got it *badly* wrong. I once told a woman I'd known her four hundred years and it turned out to be nine hundred, and she has never let it go."* The wind moved. *"Play me something."*

"You're a *caller*, you don't get to—"

*"Play me something, weed."*

So she did.

And when it was running, and the cupboard was warm, and the whole Phosphor District was breathing its blue-violet breath through a window she had calibrated herself at fifteen because she'd thought it was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen underground, Amy leaned back in the chair with the headphones half off and said, into a live microphone, to one listener:

"Hum it for me."

The line stayed open.

Then, small and low and unhurried, across a dead channel, into a city of thirty thousand sleeping people who would never know what they were hearing:

Four bars. Going up at the third. Hanging on the fourth, the way a question hangs.

And then a fifth.

One note. Nothing much. The kind of note that costs nothing at all to sing, and eleven days to earn — and it went past the door where the song had stopped for thirteen years, and came down on the other side of it, and sat there, plain as a step.

Amy came forward in the chair fast enough to take the headphone lead with her.

"Where did you get that."

*"Mm?"*

"The fifth bar. *Camellya.* Where did you get the fifth bar—"

*"I've had it since Tuesday,"* said Camellya, sounding mildly interested. *"It arrived somewhere on the shuttle. I've been assuming it was always there."*

"It *wasn't*—"

*"Then it's new."* A pause. The wind went over the microphone and came back. *"I'll lose it, you know. That's the arrangement. Somewhere between here and spring it'll go, and I'll be sitting in a tree humming four bars and stopping at a door with no idea there was ever anything behind it."*

Amy had her hand flat on the desk.

"Then say it again."

*"Why."*

"Because I'm *recording*," said Amy. "I've been recording since you keyed. I record everything. I've recorded everything since I was fourteen. It's the single most annoying thing about me and four separate people have told me so in writing."

The line was quiet a moment.

*"Ah,"* said Camellya.

"Yeah."

*"So if I turn up in spring with four bars and a wall—"*

"Then I play it back to you," said Amy, "and you get it again. And if you lose it again I play it again. And you can lose it as many times as you like, and I'll be here every time, because that's the *job*, that's—" Her voice went, and she let it, and carried on around it. "Somebody's got to hold the pencil. You said."

The wind went across a bad microphone a long way south.

*"Mm,"* said Camellya. *"Well. Keep it somewhere safe, then."*

"I've got it," said Amy.

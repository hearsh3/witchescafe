Old Royan was cut before it was written, which is why the alphabet has no curves in it. A blade in wood goes where the grain allows. So the letters lean, all of them, the same way, and the lean is the hand of every man and woman who ever needed to say *this is the edge of my field* to somebody who would arrive after they were dead.

Boundary stones came in pairs. This is the first thing the faculty teaches and the last thing anyone remembers. A field has a near stone and a far stone, and a formula longer than one face of one rock runs across both, and the reader is expected to walk. At the break the cutter leaves a tick — two strokes, shallow, angled down. The grammars gloss it *continuation*. The road crews call it the go-on. It carries no sound and no meaning of its own, and it will ruin a translation if you drop it, because it is the whole difference between a sentence that has finished and a sentence that is somewhere else.

Where the stone is broken, the reading is broken. The convention is nine hundred years old and it is the same in every faculty on the continent: you set a bracket, you count what is missing if you can count it, and you leave the bracket standing. A scholar who fills the bracket has produced an emendation. An emendation that is loved becomes a reading. A reading that is loved long enough becomes the stone, and then there is no way back to the rock, and the field has a new edge that nobody ever cut.

Eleven words in the Royan corpus occur exactly once. There is no second attestation to check them against, no cousin text, no glossary, no bilingual anywhere on the shelf. The grammars print all eleven and pass them on and decline to be embarrassed about it. Students learn them by heart the way you learn the names of people who died before you were born.

There is a twelfth. It has never been found on stone, so the corpus has no room for it. It is laid at the wrist, in soot and fat, with a needle bound to a shaft of birch, by a grandmother in a kitchen, on a child small enough to be held still by her mother. It is the last mark of the first spiral. Nobody in the Roya can read it. Every one of them carries it. The standard grammar leaves the entry blank and prints the number twelve above the blank, and the compiler has been dead sixty years and cannot be asked why he bothered.

⁂

Under the Dimmr Plains, in a fissure the road crews had always stepped around, there is a hole full of black water.

The survey office numbered it before anyone went down. Boreholes get numbered on the day they are logged and named on the Friday, and in the year the Plains were opened the clerk with the Friday deadline had ninety-four to name and a book at home he read on Sundays. He worked down the list in it. Marah, which is bitter. Elim, which had twelve springs and seventy palm trees. Nephtoah, which is the spring of the water of Nephtoah, and about which the book says nothing else at all.

Number four he called Lahai-Roi, because the city above had been named off the same list a generation earlier by the same office, and reuse was permitted, and he liked the gloss. The gazetteer prints it in small type at the foot of the page: *the well of the Living One who sees me.*

He went home at six and had his dinner. He is in this account exactly this long.

A hundred and some years later a girl came up out of it with half her ink gone and a facility to-do list in her pocket, and the water came up behind her and filled the basin and made a lake, and nobody has been down since, because there is no down.

## I. Autopsy

The Registry opens at eight and the queue starts at ten to.

Sigrika was fourth. She had a document sleeve she had made herself out of two sheets of acetate and a strip of binding tape, and on the back of it, in her own hand, in red: found by, found at, found when, carried by, handled by, condition on receipt. Chain of custody. She had looked up the form and there was no form, so she had built one out of the evidentiary protocol in a second-year methods textbook and her own idea of what a serious person does.

Window three was three windows down from a poster about influenza.

"Number."

She gave the number. The deputy registrar typed it with two fingers and the confidence of twenty-two years, and her reading glasses hung on a beaded chain, and her name plate said I. FENN.

"Right." Fenn looked up. "You're the person of contact."

"I'm sorry?"

Fenn turned the screen a few degrees, which was against policy, and did it anyway.

"Enrolment record. Section four. It's a free-text field, we don't prompt for it, most of them fill it in at induction and never touch it again." A nail tapped the glass. "Amended in second year. That's her hand, I take it. Slants backward."

The whole runework faculty had complained about that slant for four years. Halvard had once held a paper up to the class as an example of a hand that made itself harder to read on purpose, and had been genuinely delighted when Denia agreed with him.

The field said Sigrika's name. It had said Sigrika's name for two years and eight months.

"Most of them say *mother*," Fenn said.

Sigrika put the document sleeve on the counter because her hands wanted somewhere to be.

"What's this."

"It was in the facility. Under the Plains. It's a duty list — rations, waste, a locked cabinet, an audit — and the name at the top of the column is Subject D, and the ink was thirteen months dry when I found it, which means somebody wrote it and then went home and had dinner."

Fenn drew the sleeve toward her without opening it.

"You want it lodged."

"I want an inquiry."

"You want the Collective."

"They have a committee."

"Then you want the committee."

"It has been sitting since the eleventh and has produced a schedule for producing a schedule." Sigrika kept her chin at the angle. "The room is at the bottom of a hole with a lake on top of it. Nobody is going to survey it. Nobody is going to autopsy anything. This is the last object in the world that was in that room, and I would like it to exist somewhere that isn't my coat."

Fenn opened the sleeve with a paper knife, read the list all the way through, turned it over, read the back, and set it down flat.

"Copy or original."

"Which do you keep?"

"Copy. Original belongs to the finder until somebody with standing asks for it." Fenn's mouth did something small and tired. "Nobody's going to ask."

"No."

"So you'll be carrying it."

"Yes."

Fenn went to the machine behind the counter and copied it and the machine made its long grey noise, and the copy came out, and it was the same eleven lines in a font the machine had chosen, and it looked like a photocopy of a duty list, which is what it was, and Sigrika understood for the first time in her life that a true thing can be photographed into meaninglessness.

The original came back across the counter. Fenn slid a second sheet after it.

"While you're here."

CLOSURE OF ACADEMIC RECORD — DISPOSITION. Three boxes down the left margin.

□ WITHDRAWN (voluntary — student signature or letter)
□ AWARDED
□ DECEASED (certificate or coroner's order attached)

And below them, six ruled lines under one word: REMARKS.

Sigrika read it twice. The queue behind her had grown to nine and one of them was eating an apple.

"There's no certificate."

"No."

"There's no order."

"There's a lake." Fenn took her glasses off. "I'm going to be straight with you, because you'll have read worse forms than this one. It sits open till end of term. If nothing comes in by then, the system closes it itself, and the system's default is administrative withdrawal."

"On what ground."

"Non-attendance."

Sigrika stood very still.

"Say it again."

"Administrative withdrawal. Non-attendance."

"Write it down."

Fenn looked at her.

"Please write it down. That you said it. With the date."

There was a moment where it could have gone the other way — a rule about correspondence, a form for requesting a written statement, a fee. Fenn reached into the drawer and took out a compliment slip, the small ones with the crest at the top that go out with returned coursework, and wrote nine words on it in a bureaucrat's small immaculate hand, and dated it, and signed it, and pushed it over.

Sigrika folded it once and put it in her breast pocket.

"Thank you."

"Take the form. It can come back another day." Fenn put her glasses on again and squared the copy against the desk edge, and then, to the desk: "I knew the face. She came in about the fees, twice. Stood at that window like she had all afternoon and I was the one running late." A short breath. "Made me laugh, and I don't."

Sigrika got as far as the door.

She came back.

"Was she polite?"

Fenn considered the question with real care, the way you do when somebody has asked you the only question that matters and you can tell.

"Terribly," she said. "It was very rude."

## II. Sigla

The long-distance annex sells you the surface in quarter-hours.

You book at the desk, you take a booth, you close the accordion door, and a counter above the handset runs down in green. The relay goes up through the Riseway and out along the root network and comes ashore in a shed in the Roya with a lightning rod and a padlock, and the lag is a second and a half each way, so everybody talks over everybody, and every family that uses it develops a dialect of pauses within a month.

Sigrika booked two quarters. She had never in her life booked two.

"—rika? Is that— Bergr, it's *her*, get off the—"

"Hi, Mam."

Three seconds of a kitchen. A door. A dog that had not existed in spring.

"You've a dog."

"That's the Rennirs' dog, he's got a route now, he does the whole road and he expects a biscuit at every house and Vigga gives him two, which is why—" Halla was already going, the way she went, the valley arriving in one long line. Sturla's roof. The wedding at Midsummer that had been rained into a barn and had been better for it. The co-op's new freezer. And then, sideways, pleased and pretending not to be: "Halvard's letter came."

"He writes home?"

"Every spring. *Remarkable aptitude.* I read it out at the co-op."

"Mam."

"I read it out at the co-op and Ásta said it must be a form letter, and I said fetch me one of yours then, Ásta."

Sigrika put her forehead against the cool of the booth's side wall.

"Are you eating," Halla said.

"Every day."

"That's a lawyer's answer."

"Twice a day. Sometimes three."

"Mm."

The counter said 21:40. She had planned a sentence in the corridor. She had planned three, in three registers, the way she plans everything, and the corridor version had been calm and the corridor version had had a preamble.

"Mam. Some of the ink's gone."

A second and a half.

"Gone where."

There it was — the flat literalism of a woman who has never once in her life heard of ink going anywhere. Sigrika laughed, and it came out wrong, and she pressed her wrist against her mouth until it stopped.

"There was water," she said. "It wasn't water. It took what it touched. My hands came back. The braids came off at the jaw. The ink at the wrists went up to about the middle of the forearm, and it stopped there, and it's stayed stopped eleven days."

The line held empty for two lags, which meant her mother had a hand over the receiver.

"Did it hurt?"

"It went the way frost goes off a window."

"Which ones."

"The first spiral."

Halla said her name once. Just the name, at full length, the whole thing, the way it is said by exactly two people in the world.

"Amma's here," she said. "She's at the table. Hang on."

There was the sound of a chair, and a receiver set down on wood, and a conversation in a kitchen at a distance, and Sigrika stood in a booth in a city under a glacier with her ear against a shed in the Roya and heard her grandmother's voice for the first time in fourteen months and could not make out one word of it.

The chair again.

"She won't take it."

"I know."

"She's never taken it. She thinks it's ridiculous that I talk into it." Halla's voice had gone flat, which is what it does when she is carrying something. "She says to tell you—"

A pause while she got it right.

"*Húðin man þik. Kom.*"

Sigrika had the pen out before the second word landed.

"Say it again."

"Sigrika—"

"Say it again, please. Exactly. Don't tidy it."

Halla said it again. Sigrika wrote it in blue, which is the ink for cross-references, because there was nothing yet to cross-reference it against.

"Once more. The vowel in the second word."

"It's just what she *said*, love, I'm not a—"

"Once more."

Halla said it a third time, with an edge on it now, and the edge did not change the vowel, and the vowel was the one Sigrika had heard the first time and had hoped to be wrong about.

*Man.* The skin remembers you. Present tense, indicative, plain as bread — the same verb that had stood on the erased mark at her wrist, the mark that meant *the valley remembers you*, gone off her arm and put back into her ear by the woman who laid it there in the first place.

And the vowel was long. A long vowel there belongs to a mood the grammars call the *stefnu*: the meeting-mood, attested on grave-markers and boundary stones and nowhere else, deprecated by the year the corpus was closed, reserved for a thing that is not yet and is not doubted.

*The skin is going to remember you. Come.*

Both readings sat on the page in blue, and neither of them would move over for the other, and a philologist who has been told since the age of nine that every question has one correct answer stood in a soundproofed booth and could not conjugate her own grandmother.

"Sigrika."

"I'm here."

"She's put the jar by the door."

The counter said 09:12.

"...The jar."

"The soot jar."

Sigrika put her hand flat against the wall of the booth.

"Don't make me say what it means," Halla said. "I'll cry and I've the milk on." A clatter, a rush of kitchen, and then, brisk, saving them both: "Here — your brother's about to burst—"

"Is the sky fake?"

"Hello, Uni."

"Mam says it's fake. Is it fake?"

"It's fake."

"Is it any good?"

Sigrika looked up through the booth's little window at the annex ceiling and the strip lights and the water stain.

"It's all right in the mornings," she said. "It gets the blues wrong after four."

"Then come home," said Uni, with the total logic of nine, and dropped the receiver, and was gone at a run.

Halla came back on and started a sentence about the freezer, and the counter went to zero, and the line cut the way institutional lines cut — no warning tone, no fade, just the immediate flat air of a dead handset. Booked time ends when booked time ends.

Sigrika sat down on the fold-out seat in a booth she was no longer entitled to and looked at four words in blue ink for a long time, until a boy knocked on the glass because the desk had given him the booth.

That night she went to the library and pulled Thorbergsen's grammar off the reference shelf and turned to §414. *The stefnu-mood. Extinct as a productive form by the closing of the corpus. Survives in fixed inscriptional formulae only. Not to be constructed.*

Thorbergsen had been dead sixty years and could not be asked.

Her grandmother was seventy-nine, would not take the phone, and had constructed it on a Tuesday, in a kitchen, to a woman standing there holding a receiver.

Sigrika wrote *§414 — see also: Amma* under the four words. She looked at what she had written for a while. Then she closed the book and put it back on the shelf face out, spine to the wall, which is what you do when you intend to come back.

## III. Collation

Halvard's Tuesday runs ninety minutes and he has not stopped in twenty-six years.

She took her usual seat, second row, aisle. At minute six a first-year came in late, hunched, apologising with her shoulders, and took the seat on Sigrika's left, and put her bag down, and settled, and stayed awake through the entire lecture, taking notes in one colour.

Sigrika let her.

At minute forty Halvard put a plate up on the projector — a boundary pair out of the Mingting corridor, near stone and far stone photographed in the same frame — and asked the room what the tick at the join was for, and the room did what rooms do, and Sigrika answered because the silence had run to nine seconds and she has never once in her life been able to let a silence do that.

"It marks continuation."

"And what does the reader do about it?"

"Walks."

"*Precisely*," Halvard said, delighted, and drew an arrow on the glass with his finger and smudged the plate. "Which is the whole of the discipline, and you may put it in an essay and I will mark it generously."

He caught her at the door afterward. He has a way of standing in doorways that he believes is casual.

"I've spoken to the office. There's an incomplete available to you. Nobody would think anything of it."

"Everybody would think something of it."

"Well. Yes." He had the grace to look caught. "You'd survive it."

"Professor, the fourteenth item in the corpus. The Sarnvik near stone. There's a far stone in the field notes and no plate."

Halvard blinked. And then, because he is a man who only ever learned one way to love anybody, and it works, and it had worked on her at seventeen when nothing else did:

"There is *no* plate. There has never *been* a plate. Sørensen saw it in 'ninety-one, described it in four lines, and did not photograph it, and every reconstruction of that formula since is built on four lines by a man with famously poor eyesight — sit down, this will take a moment—"

They stood in the doorway for twenty minutes while the next lecture filed around them. He gave her the Sørensen. He gave her a reading list. He talked about the man's eyesight the entire time, with enormous scorn and enormous love, and she went down the stairs afterward carrying four books and feeling, for the length of the stairwell, like a person with a Thursday.

The cafeteria at half past twelve smells of oil and steam and the particular scalded sweetness of institutional custard.

She got to the fry station and put her tray on the rail and her hand went toward the number pad, and Marga said, before Sigrika's mouth opened:

"Double, love?"

Marga has run the fry station for twenty-six years. She has a badge and forearms and she uses the tongs the way a conductor uses a stick.

Sigrika stood with her hand on the rail.

"...Yes."

Two portions went into the tray, side by side, in the two paper sleeves, and Marga tapped the tongs twice on the guard to shake them off, which is a thing she has done ten thousand times and will do ten thousand more.

"Never seen her eat anything else," Marga said. "Four years. Not once. I used to think she was on a wager."

"She wasn't."

"No. She was just like that." Marga was already reaching for the next tray. "Said ta, though. Every time. There's students been here three years never said a word to me in their lives."

Sigrika carried the tray to a table by the window and sat down, and the two orders of fries went cold in front of her over the next forty minutes, and at some point in the middle of it she took out her pen and wrote, in red, which is the ink for data:

*She said ta. Every time. (M. — fry station, 26 yrs. Unprompted.)*

A boy she had never seen stopped at the end of the table with his own tray.

"Are those going spare?"

"Take them."

He took them, and sat down across from her uninvited, and talked for six minutes about a lab practical that had gone wrong in a way he found extremely funny, and ate both portions, and said thanks, and left.

The next day she ordered double again.

Then she pinned the memorial thread to the top of the board, where the emergency notices go, and left the reply standing, because it was true, and because a thread that starts deleting the true things it dislikes has quietly become a directory.

She wrote the handle in her journal. In blue. Cross-reference.

Under it: *wrong friend.*

## IV. Reservoir

The variety shop on the arcade's second level sells kites, joke teeth, three kinds of glue, and the good pens.

"Reservoir solution," Sigrika said. "Three-eighty series."

The woman behind the counter — Ossa, thirty-one years on that counter, spectacles pushed up into grey hair — went on fixing the till roll.

"Discontinued."

"I know."

"Company folded. They reclassified the frequency-hold after the Tide, whole product line went in a skip." The roll clicked home. "There's a generic. Film's thinner, pops sooner, kids don't care."

"Does the solution touch the recording?"

Ossa looked up.

"...The what?"

"The reservoir holds a recording."

"Oh — the chip, love. Solution's soap." Ossa put her hand out. "Give it here."

Sigrika gave it to her, which took a moment, and which Ossa noticed and had the decency to work around.

It sat in a woman's palm on a counter under a strip light: a fat little wand, a ring, a bulb of tinted plastic, and a rocker switch in two colours on the underside, near the grip.

"Right. Amber's record, blue's play." Ossa turned it over. "Thumb goes here for the trigger, and amber's *right there*, which is a design a grown man was paid for. How long have you had it?"

"Eleven weeks."

"And you've been carrying it in a pocket."

Sigrika went cold from the sternum outward.

Eleven weeks of a coat pocket. Eleven weeks of a bag with a journal in it and three pens and a document sleeve. Eleven weeks of sitting down hard on terraces and slate shelves and the floor of a phone booth with her hand closed round it.

"Can you lock it?"

"Voids the warranty."

"The company's gone."

Ossa considered this for about a second and a half.

"Fair."

She took it into the back. Sigrika stood at the counter among the kites and heard, through the bead curtain, the small domestic sounds of a soldering iron warming, and a woman humming something with no tune to it, and a drawer.

It took ninety seconds.

"There." Ossa set it down. "Blob of solder under the rocker. It'll play. It will never record again as long as it exists."

Sigrika put her hand flat on the counter beside it.

"What do I owe you."

"Two for the generic and nothing for the other; I'd have to invent a line for it." Ossa rang up the two. "Run it now and then, though."

"...Sorry?"

"The reservoirs go stiff if they're never worked. It's a membrane. It wants moving." She put the little bottle in a paper bag and folded the top over twice, because she folds the top over twice. "I've had three of these in over the years, all the same, all from people who kept them in a drawer for safety. Safest thing in the world, a drawer. Ruins everything you put in it."

The piano annex had been half-emptied in the spring and had stayed half-empty, which is the worst state a building can be in.

Nastasha was in the third-floor room with a stack of parts on the lid and a metronome she was ignoring. Crates still stood along the wall. The upright wore its shipping tag: DONATED: SHELTER SCHOOL 4.

"You stayed," Sigrika said, from the door.

"I got a post. Forty children in a converted canteen and a conductor's stand somebody's father made out of a music stand and a lot of hope." Nastasha turned around. Her hands were doing what they do. "They think I'm brilliant. I keep time with my whole arm and they think it's a *style*."

"Is it?"

"It's now."

Sigrika came in and set the Dream Maker on the piano lid.

"I need you to count something."

Nastasha looked at the toy on the lid.

"Ask me for anything else."

"I need the tempo."

"Sigrika."

"Please."

Nastasha put the metronome away in the bench, which is what a musician does when she has decided to take a thing seriously. Then she sat on the stool and put both palms flat on the lid on either side of the little machine, and put her chin down, and closed her eyes, and her hands shook against the wood and her whole right arm came up and began to move.

The room did what rooms do.

The heartbeat went on under her palm — patient, unhurried, close, recorded through cloth or years or glass — and Nastasha's arm went with it, and the tremor rode on top of the beat the way a small weather rides a large one, and neither of them was in charge.

"Fifty-eight," she said, at length. "Give or take. It wanders."

"Wanders how."

"It slows at the end of a phrase and picks up again." Nastasha's eyes opened. "That's an organ with a person over the top of it, breathing, taking their time about it, doing it for somebody."

Sigrika stood with her arms folded across her ribs.

"Is it hers?"

The tremor went on. Nastasha looked at her hands a while, and then at the girl standing in a doorway full of crates.

"You're asking me to be a witness."

"Yes."

"I'll tell you what I heard. I'll stop short of telling you what it was." She said it gently and it was granite. "Because you'd take it and file it, and in six months you'd have a document, and the document would be doing your believing for you, and then somebody would find a fault in the document."

"That's what documents are *for*—"

"That is exactly what they're for," Nastasha agreed, "which is why you're getting one from me over my dead body."

Sigrika stayed where she was. She had built an argument for this conversation across eleven days and she watched it go over the side.

Nastasha pulled a sheet of manuscript paper off the stack and uncapped a pencil with her teeth, which is what you do when your hands are the way hers are, and drew one note on the middle line. Then, over the top of it, a shallow arc with a dot beneath.

"That's a hold."

"For how long?"

"That's the joke." Nastasha turned the paper round. "It's the only mark in four hundred years of the trade that refuses to say. There's a number under every other thing we write. Under that one, in any edition, in any hand, in any language: nothing — because the instant you write a number under it you've written a different mark."

She added one word beneath. *lunga.*

"*Lunga.*"

"Long. Which is an opinion, and we print it anyway." Nastasha held the sheet out, and her hand shook, and the paper shook with it, and she waited without a flicker until Sigrika took the other corner. "Somebody has to stand up there with their arm in the air while the room holds its breath, and the whole orchestra watches the arm and forgets the page. That's the job. It's a real job. They pay me for it now."

Sigrika folded it along the crease it wanted.

At the door she stopped, because there was a thing she had come with and had set down when the first thing got heavy.

"I'm sorry about your hands."

"Everybody is."

"I'm saying it's yours."

Nastasha's face changed — one degree, the way weather changes over a valley, and the valley grows things anyway.

"Nobody's managed that one yet either," she said. "Shut the door, it sticks."

## V. Crux

The ridge path takes forty minutes if you take it properly.

Sigrika came up it on a Friday evening with a broom over her shoulder.

It was an ordinary broom out of the maintenance store on the east side, requisitioned on a slip she had filled in truthfully and signed with her own name under the heading PURPOSE, where she had written *sweeping*.

The shrine sits where the ridge flattens. Below the terrace the glacier runs out grey-blue under the false dusk, and on it the god kneels in his century of snow with his shoulders bearing what shoulders bear. The lattice stands open to the air. Ten thousand crystals hang in it, each one holding its small salt-white light at the exact brightness of being remembered.

Hiyuki was at the far end with a trowel and a flat of something that would probably die.

"Candidate."

"There's needles all over the step."

"There are."

"There have been needles all over that step since the day it opened." Sigrika set the broom against the gatepost and looked at it, and then at nothing in particular. "It's been bothering me since spring."

"Mm," said Hiyuki, and went back to the flat, and the matter was closed in the way things are closed in that house.

Sigrika swept the terrace. It took eleven minutes and produced a pile the size of a hat, and she was extremely thorough about the corners, and when she had finished she stood in the middle of a clean stone floor with a broom in her hands and no further plan.

The bell hangs at the head of the steps, low, on a beam cut short so that children can reach it.

She looked at it for a long time. Her hand stayed at her side.

"The bell is for remembering the dead," Hiyuki said, from the flat of seedlings, without turning round.

"Yes."

"Mm."

Sigrika sat down on the step.

"The lattice. How does it take one."

Hiyuki put the trowel down and came and sat, in her deliberate way, at the correct distance.

"You bring a reach that stayed on the near shore. A thing you put a hand out toward and never touched. The frost finds it and holds it, and it keeps." She turned her palm over and looked at it — at a hand that was cold for a century and is warm now. "I carried ten thousand of them in my chest. That was a filing error, and it took a wolf and a girl with scissors to correct it."

"Take mine."

Hiyuki's eyes came round.

"You can do that? You can just — take it."

"I can offer it. The frost decides." A pause. "Sit still."

Sigrika sat still. Hiyuki put two fingers against the back of her neck, at the temperature of a held railing in winter, exact and kind, and the terrace went very quiet, and the crystals in the lattice hung there and went on being crystals.

The cold went in, and found what it was looking for, and came back with its hands empty.

Hiyuki left her fingers where they were for four seconds longer than the office requires. Then she took them away.

"Is it broken."

"The lattice is a thousand years old and has misfiled nothing in all of it."

"Then why—"

"Because it takes what stayed on the near shore." Hiyuki looked out at the glacier. "Yours went across."

Sigrika stopped breathing for a moment.

"What does that mean."

"Mm."

"Hiyuki."

"It means the frost declined to hold it. Which is a fact about frost." Hiyuki turned her cup a quarter-turn on her palm, an old gesture, worn smooth. "The girl's whereabouts are exactly as unknown to me tonight as they were this morning, and the shrine's opinion on that question is worth what a shrine's opinion is worth, which on this specific matter is very little, and I intend to say so out loud rather than let you build a cathedral on it before breakfast."

"You could have let me have that."

"I could have."

"Why didn't you."

"Because you would have gone down the ridge with it and read it eleven times before you slept," Hiyuki said, "and in three months, on a bad Tuesday, you would have decided it was a trick, and you would have lost it, and you would have lost the evening as well. I am a hundred and some years old. I have watched a great many people spend a mercy like that at a very poor exchange rate."

Sigrika put her elbows on her knees.

"I want you to write it down."

"Which."

"The room. The water. The journal. What I did." It came out at exam speed, ink before the bell. "You were there. You were the only person there. You're a shrine keeper of the Left Behind with a hundred and six years of standing and there isn't an office on this continent that would query your hand, and I need it on a page with a date on it, and I know exactly what that sounds like, and I want it anyway."

Hiyuki got up and went inside.

She came back in ninety seconds with a sheet of the shrine's grey paper, a brush, and an ink stone, and she knelt at the step and wrote three lines in a hand like architecture, and dated it, and sealed it, and gave it over.

Sigrika read it.

It was correct. It was complete. It gave the day and the place and what she had carried and what she had let go of, and it said *witnessed by*, and then a name that means something in four countries.

She read it again.

And on the third pass the thing she had been braced against for eleven days arrived from an unexpected direction and knocked her clean over, which is that she was holding precisely what she had asked for, and it had cost ninety seconds and good paper, and it did nothing whatsoever.

Her hands started shaking. She put the sheet down on the step and set a stone on the corner of it so it would stay, because even then, even in the middle of it, she is a girl who weights paper.

"It doesn't work," she said.

"No."

"Why doesn't it work."

"Because you already saw it," Hiyuki said. "There is no instrument in the world that will tell you a thing you already know. Every one of them goes silent about the thing you want, which is different, and further away, and has no form."

She sat down again.

"You may have a hundred of these. I will write you one a week for the rest of my life and I will never once ask why. It costs me ninety seconds and good paper, and I have both."

"That's—"

"Mm."

Sigrika sat on a swept step above a glacier and cried for about four minutes, badly, the way she does, with her chin still up, which is the only angle her chin has ever learned.

Hiyuki sat beside her and let it be the size it needed to be.

⁂

Afterward, when the false dusk had gone to its lowest setting and the crystals had come up in the lattice like a very quiet city, Hiyuki said:

"I have an errand for you."

Sigrika wiped her face with the heel of her hand.

"Now?"

"It has waited a year." Hiyuki nodded down at the gate. "The threshold stone. Teach it to me."

"...The step?"

"I have stood on it approximately four thousand times. I would like to know what I have been standing on. I am told there are eleven people on the continent who can read it and one of them has been sweeping my terrace."

So Sigrika went down to the gate with the lamp and knelt, and put her cheek almost to the stone, and ran her fingers along it the way you do when the light is bad and the wear is worse, and after a minute she said, "Oh, for—" and stopped, and then started laughing, and the laugh ran on slightly too long.

"It's a boundary stone."

"Mm."

"It's a *field* stone. It's been re-used. They've laid it face up as a doorstep — somebody wanted a flat rock and there was a flat rock." She sat back on her heels. "It's a ward. It's the standard perimeter formula. It's on every stone in the Roya. It's the first thing they teach you to read, because it's the same on all of them."

"And it says."

"*Halt. This far.*" Sigrika looked up. "You built a shrine with no lock on the door and no hours on the gate and a broom for whoever's nearest, and the thing everybody walks over to get in says *stop*."

Hiyuki considered the stone at some length.

"What does the rest of it say?"

"There is no rest."

"You said it was a formula."

"It's a formula that runs long." Sigrika's thumb had found the join at the stone's worn edge, where two shallow strokes stood, angled down, half eaten by a century of boots. "This is a near stone. There's a tick at the end. It means the sentence continues on the far stone, and the far stone is wherever the far stone is, and you're supposed to walk to it."

"And when nobody finds it."

"Then you write the bracket and you leave the bracket standing, and you print the near stone alone, and every edition for nine hundred years prints the tick beside it so no one mistakes it for the whole thing." She heard herself. She stopped. "They came in pairs."

The lamp guttered and stood.

"Mm," said Hiyuki, and it was a different *mm* than the others, and both of them heard it be different.

Sigrika went down the ridge at the deep-night setting with a sealed sheet of grey paper in her bag that had done nothing at all for her and that she will keep for the rest of her life.

The broom stayed at the gate.

By the end of the week three separate people had used it and put it back, and none of them asked whose it was, and it never once occurred to Hiyuki to explain.

## VI. Conjecture

Wednesdays belonged to Lynae. Lynae had gone eleven weeks without saying so out loud and had been at the door at seven every one of them.

The rooms above the noodle place on the fourth terrace had a stove, a window that opened, and a rent book on the table.

"You've *moved*."

"I've rented." Lynae hung her jacket on the back of the door. "There's a difference and I'm still working out which one this is."

Sigrika turned in the middle of the floor. Two rooms. A bed frame with the mattress still to come. A crate for a table, and on the wall above the crate, in a frame, behind glass, a single sheet of Academy letterhead.

*We are pleased to confirm your enrolment—*

"That's the forgery."

"Mm-hm."

"It's very good."

"It's the second-best thing in this room." Lynae was going through the cupboard, which contained a kettle and one plate. "Cards or food."

"What's the first?"

Lynae nodded at the crate.

"Rent book. It's in my own name."

Sigrika opened her mouth. She has, since the age of nine, been three steps ahead of every argument in any room she stands in, and she stood there for a moment being one step behind this one and entirely unable to locate the step.

"Cards," said Lynae. "Sit down."

⁂

The game is called Bid Nine and Lynae learned it in barracks where the stakes were shifts.

"Nine hands. Each hand, before you look, you say how many tricks you're taking. Then you play it. Score's the difference between what you said and what you got, and the low score wins."

"Before I look."

"That's the game."

"How can I possibly—"

"Bid."

"...Four."

Lynae dealt. Sigrika took six, which is more than four, and lost two points, and stared at the score sheet.

"I did better than I said."

"You did worse than you knew." Lynae was already shuffling, the deck crossing her knuckles and dividing into four clean streams and coming back together like water finding a channel. "Over is worse than under. Over means you had it in your hand the whole time and had no idea what you were holding."

"That's a terrible rule."

"It's the only rule. Bid."

She bid five and took eight. She bid seven and took nine. She bid two, out of spite, and took seven, because the cards had decided to be kind and she has never in her life been able to fail on purpose.

By the sixth hand she was thirty-one points down and Lynae was on four, having bid zero twice and made it both times, sitting there through whole hands playing her lowest card into every trick with an expression of enormous peace.

"How do you *do* that."

"Bid."

"How do you—"

"You're asking me how to win by trying harder." Lynae dealt. "You cannot win this by trying harder. That is the entire design. Somebody in a barracks invented it specifically to torture people like you and I have thought about them fondly for nine years."

Sigrika picked up her hand and put it down.

"Are you going?"

The shuffle went on.

"Bid."

"They're going to Jinzhou. Iuno's had the maps out a week and Lupa's been counting the pot up since Sunday and everybody's being extremely normal about the corridor, which is how I know." Her chin was at the angle. "Are you going?"

"*Bid,* Sigrika."

Sigrika looked at the crate, and the frame on the wall, and the rent book, and the bed with the mattress still to come, and the second-best thing in the room.

"Zero."

Lynae's hands stopped.

"Zero," Sigrika said again.

She played it out. She played every card she had into the ground, one after another, and gave away four tricks that were hers and did it on purpose, and it was the hardest thing she had done since a room with water in it, and the last card went down and she had taken nothing at all.

Lynae looked at the score sheet for a moment.

"Huh," she said.

Later, at the door, with her jacket already on, Sigrika said: "You didn't answer."

"I answered."

"You said *bid.*"

"Yeah," said Lynae, and shut the door, and stood behind it a while with her hand flat on the wood.

⁂

Rebecca had got hold of the emergency-procedures wall in the east stairwell, which has been legally a mural since Lynae's spray-paint incident and has therefore been legally anything ever since.

She was working with her tongue between her teeth and a row of markers standing on the ledge, capped, in colour order, which was the only orderly thing about her.

"You gonna draw or you gonna audit?"

"I'm looking."

"You've been looking four minutes with your arms crossed. That's auditing." Rebecca held out a marker without turning round. "Take it."

"I can't draw."

"Nobody can draw." Rebecca gestured at the wall with the marker still extended. "Look at it. That's a man. That is supposedly a man."

Sigrika looked at the wall.

It ran from the fire door to the second landing, acid green on institutional cream, dozens of them, some with names and some without, in a hand that had clearly started confident and got better and never once got good. A woman with a shotgun and a halo of hair. A man with a jaw like a shovel. A dog. Four figures round a table with the table drawn wrong. And low down, near the skirting, in a different colour, small: a girl in white, outline only, and above her a moon the size of a coin.

"Who's that one."

"That one's alive," Rebecca said.

Sigrika took the marker.

She stood in front of the wall a long time, and then she drew, badly, at the height of her own shoulder, two rectangles side by side with a scatter of lines in each, and a small square between them.

Rebecca came over and looked.

"...What is that."

"Fries."

"That's hands."

"There's two orders there."

"Why's there two."

"Because there's two," Sigrika said, and her voice did something on the last word, and Rebecca capped her marker with her thumb, which is what she does when she has decided to stop talking for a second.

"Okay," Rebecca said. "Yeah. Okay." She looked at it a while longer. "That's the best one on here."

"It's the worst thing on this wall."

"Yeah." Rebecca went back to her man with the jaw. "Both."

At the fire door Sigrika stopped, because there was a marker in her hand she had forgotten to give back, and Rebecca said, without looking up:

"Keep the green. And here—"

She underarmed a second one across the landing. Sigrika caught it. White.

"Why?"

"'Cause you'll want it."

"For what?"

Rebecca drew a line. She got it wrong. She drew over it.

"White's for the ones that aren't," she said.

"Aren't what?"

"Yeah," said Rebecca, and kept drawing, and that is the whole of the answer she has ever been willing to give anybody, and she had now given it twice in one year, which for her constitutes an outpouring.

⁂

Thursdays are the greenhouse, because nobody talks to you in a greenhouse.

The tomatoes were in their fourth truss and doing what tomatoes do under a sun run by engineers, which is grow anyway. Sigrika sat on the upturned crate at the end of the row with her coursework on her knees and the smell of the leaves at the back of her throat like a room from years ago.

Amy came in at ten past and sat on the next crate down and said nothing for eleven minutes, which was the single most considerate thing anybody had done for Sigrika all month.

"I was harsh at you," Amy said, eventually. "In the common room."

"You were right."

"Both, probably." Amy pulled a leaf off and rolled it between her fingers and smelled her hand and pulled a face. "I'm going to skip the proper apology because Lupa says apologising properly is a way of making the other person look after you, and I've decided she's right about that, so."

"So."

"So I'll tell you a thing instead and you can do what you like with it." Amy put her hands on her knees. "I don't remember most of it. Ten years. It's worse than blank, it's like a room you've walked through a lot and can't describe. And the part I do have is three notes."

Sigrika waited.

"And I could never tell whether she was still singing them or whether I was remembering them," Amy said. "The whole time. Ten years. I never worked it out, and I couldn't have, and the answer turned out to be both, and it would have made no difference to what I did either way, because either way what I did was hold on to them."

"That isn't evidence."

"No."

"You know that isn't evidence."

"Yeah, I know," Amy said. "I'm just saying it worked."

She got up and brushed the compost off the back of her legs. At the door she took something out of her pocket and put it on the crate beside Sigrika's coursework: a key with a strip of tape on it, and on the tape, in marker, **BOOTH 2 — DON'T TOUCH THE FADER**.

"What's this."

"Twenty minutes, Tuesdays. Dead slot, nobody's on, the transmitter's warm anyway." Amy shrugged with one shoulder. "You can put a record on and sit there. The light just needs to be on."

"I'm not a broadcaster."

"You run a board of four hundred people at two in the morning and hand out sour sweets as a punishment," said Amy. "Ma's given Iuno five days to do honest in. Then the pot goes."

And she went out into the too-warm afternoon, and Sigrika sat in a greenhouse with a key on a crate beside her and left it lying there for nine days.

## VII. Apparatus

She did it on a Sunday night, at her desk, with the lamp low and her sleeves pushed to the shoulder and the shaving mirror propped against a stack of Sørensen.

Right arm first, because the right arm is easier and you never start with the hard one.

The transcription took four hours.

She did it properly. She did it the way the faculty does it, the way she has done it forty times for other people's stones: the drawn field first, one to one, every stroke recorded whether she liked it or not; then the diplomatic text, letter by letter, with no word division; then the normalised text; then the reading; then the apparatus at the foot, in the smallest hand she has, where the doubts go.

Her forearms are a text. She had known this the way you know a thing you have never once said aloud. She had gone twenty years without reading them.

The right arm's surviving spirals were legible from the middle of the forearm up, and they said what she had been told they said since she was seventeen: *the road ahead*. Below the middle of the forearm, skin. Clean, unmarked, as though the needle had never touched her.

She set the bracket, and counted the missing strokes off the curve of what was left, and could not count them, and wrote *[lac. — extent uncertain]*, and hated it, and left it.

The left arm took three of the four hours.

She got the diplomatic text down, and normalised it, and then sat looking at the normalised text a very long time, because the surviving formula parsed as a tail with no head — a verb inflected to agree with a subject that was no longer anywhere on her body, sitting there in the small hours making no sense whatsoever, the way the back half of a sentence makes no sense when you have torn the page.

And at the break, in the last place before the skin went clean — set into the very bottom of the surviving spiral, so small she had run her thumb across it a thousand times in twenty years and never once looked at it — were two shallow strokes, angled down.

Sigrika put the pen down.

She sat with her arm turned over under the lamp and her heart going, and she thought about a step above a glacier and a flat rock somebody had wanted for a doorway, and about a plate on a projector in lecture hall two, and about a man saying *precisely* and drawing an arrow with his finger.

Her grandmother had laid the first spiral at the wrist when she was six, in a kitchen, with her mother holding her still and humming. And she had finished it, as every grandmother in the Roya finishes it, at the top edge of the first spiral, where the second spiral would arrive thirteen years later — with the go-on.

*This continues.*

*The sentence is not over. It is somewhere else.*

She had been carrying a continuation mark on her arm since the age of six, and the void had eaten everything below it and stopped at the join, and what was left of her began with an instruction to go and find the rest.

She wrote it up. She wrote it up in full, in the apparatus, in the smallest hand she has, and she left the bracket unemended and the extent uncounted, and at the foot of the page, where the editor's notes go, she wrote:

*Near stone. Far stone not located. Formula printed as it stands, per convention.*

And then she stopped, because she had come to the twelfth word.

The last mark of the first spiral. The one the corpus has no room for because it has never been found on stone. The one nobody in the Roya can read and every one of them carries. It had sat at her wrist, three fingers up from the heel of the hand, laid there by a woman who learned it from a woman, and it was gone. She had been six years old when she got it and unable to read at all, let alone read that.

She had no image of it. She had never photographed her own arms. Nobody photographs their own arms.

Sigrika sat at her desk at four in the morning with a pen in her hand and understood, entirely and all at once, that exactly one image of that mark had existed anywhere in the world, and it had been in a navy journal with a Fleet Snowfluff sticker and a silver-marker cat with too many legs, inside four years of photographs taken from under tables and across rooms by a girl who was never meant to have preferences —

— and that she had held it in both hands, in the water, and opened her fingers.

She stayed where she was. There is a lake over it now.

She put her head down on the desk on her folded arms for a while.

Then she sat up and finished the apparatus, because the apparatus is indifferent to how you feel, which is the single kindest thing about it.

⁂

The Heliogyre residual came back from the lab on the Tuesday with a note clipped to it.

*Fig. 4 — instrument drift. Smooth and resubmit.*

She ran it a fourth time.

The residual was eleven counts and a rest, eleven counts and a rest, and it had been sitting in every set since autumn, and it moved: each repetition sourced fractionally further along the root network, east, and east, and east, like a word walking.

Drift keeps no beat.

And underneath the eleven, at the very floor of the instrument's range, where the array reports noise and the manual instructs you to disregard it, there was something else. One beat. Slow. Alone. It held no relation to the eleven whatsoever, and it stayed exactly where it was, and it came from a bearing that ran south and slightly down, out under the plains, toward a basin full of black glass.

Sigrika looked at it a long time.

She wrote down what she had.

**APPENDIX C (retained).** *The residual at Fig. 4 is periodic. Period stable across four runs at three separate stations; source displaces east between repetitions at a consistent rate. Smoothing removes a signal. Retained on the candidate's authority; appendices are permitted.*

**APPENDIX D (retained).** *A second periodicity is present at instrument floor. Period approx. 0.95 Hz, non-travelling, bearing 194° and below the horizontal. Source: [ ]. Retained without interpretation.*

She left the bracket standing.

Then she initialled both appendices and stamped them with the little seal she carries everywhere, because of course she does, and put them in the internal post at eleven at night when the corridor was empty and there was nobody to ask what she was doing.

⁂

The Registry opens at eight and the queue starts at ten to.

She was second. Fenn read the number off the sheet without looking up, and then looked up.

"Candidate."

Sigrika put the disposition form on the counter, face up. All three boxes empty.

"You can't lodge that."

"It's a remarks field."

"On a form that does nothing at all until a box is ticked."

"I know."

Fenn turned the form round with one finger and read what stood in the six ruled lines, in a small immaculate hand, in black.

*STATUS: absent, field. DURATION: until the weather turns. Lodged as a remark, for the record, under her own seal by S., candidate, Heliodic track. Witnesses constitute record.*

And beneath it the seal, pressed hard enough to emboss the sheet.

Fenn read it twice.

"The system will kick it back."

"Then I'll file it again."

"It'll kick it back at end of term. Every term."

"Then I'll file it every term."

"For how long?"

Sigrika stood at window three with the queue behind her and thought about a shallow arc with a dot under it, and a word in pencil beneath that, and a woman standing in front of forty children in a converted canteen with her arm in the air and her hand shaking and the whole room watching the arm.

"There's no number," she said. "That's the mark."

Fenn took her glasses off.

She got up, which she does perhaps four times a day, and went to the cabinet behind the counter — the physical one, the one nobody has used since the migration, which the Registry keeps because a building that throws out its old cabinet is a building with no memory — and pulled a drawer and put the form in a folder and put the folder in the drawer and closed it.

"The system does what the system does," she said, sitting back down. "The drawer's mine."

"Thank you."

"I'll put a note on the account. It'll stop nothing." Fenn was already reaching for the next slip. "But somebody will have to read it before they close it. And then they'll have read it, and there's nothing I can do about that, and neither can they."

## Colophon

The dormitory kitchen ran a five-day argument that week, and nobody explained it to her, and nobody had to.

She could hear the shape of it through a floor: nine voices, and a pot, and long silences with weight in them, and Iuno's flat register laying rules the way you lay planks. Twice a day somebody came out and put food in front of her at the long table without comment and went back in. On the third evening Lupa set down a bowl, looked at the coursework, moved the bowl four inches onto the page where Sigrika would have to lift it, and left.

There are eleven cups on that rack and twelve hooks. The twelfth has a plain white cup on it now that nobody has claimed and nobody discusses, and by the second day Sigrika had learned to reach past it for the one she wanted, the same as everybody else in that house.

On the Thursday Lynae came into the kitchen, picked up the marker by the shopping list, and wrote a number on the whiteboard. Four figures. She drew no box round it and put no word beside it, and she capped the marker and set it back on the ledge and went out, and nobody rubbed it out, and it was still there weeks later at the height where everyone had to look at it.

Sigrika ran it against everything she had. Days, kilometres, a roster, a serial, a date in four calendars. It came to nothing in all of them.

She wrote it in her journal in blue, under *§414 — see also: Amma*, and under the four-character handle, and under *wrong friend*, in the growing column of things that were true and would not sit down.

⁂

The pen was in her pigeonhole on the Friday.

It was her own — the heavy one, the good one, given to her at seventeen for a prize and lost in second year and hunted for with real distress for a fortnight. The nib was hers. The small dent by the clip was hers. The ink window was hers.

There was a new cartridge in it.

No note. No wrapping. Nothing on the pigeonhole slip, and the porter's desk had no record, and the porter, who was helpful, was extremely certain that nothing had come through the desk, and offered to check the book, and checked the book, and there was nothing in the book.

Sigrika stood in the lodge holding a pen and thought about a girl with three colours of ink who had sat beside her for two years and borrowed her pens and eaten her lunch and asked her seven hundred small questions and never once an important one.

The pen went into her bag. It has stayed in her bag, capped, with the new cartridge in it.

⁂

That night she took Rebecca's white marker and turned to the back of her journal, where the endpaper is black card, and set the bracket.

Inside the bracket, where the twelfth word had been for twenty years and where the only image of it in the world had been for four, she wrote what the convention requires and nothing else:

*[1 word, illegible]*

And after it, at the edge, two shallow strokes, angled down.

The page came out invisible. White on black takes a minute to dry and gives itself up only at a low angle, and you have to tilt it to the lamp to read it at all, which she did, twice, and then closed the cover.

Then she put the Dream Maker on the pillow beside her ear, where a heartbeat goes, and pressed the blue side, and ran it — because the membranes go stiff if they are never worked, a woman had said so over a till roll, for nothing, in a shop that sells kites — and lay in the dark listening to somebody breathing over a beat and taking their time about it and doing it for somebody.

It slowed at the end of every phrase.

It picked up again.

She slept six hours, a record for the term, and woke without once checking the time.

⁂

Above the ridge, a broom stood against a gatepost where it had been left, and in the course of that week three people used it and put it back, and none of them asked whose it was.

In a kitchen in the Roya a clay jar stood by the door with its lid on and its rim wiped, and the woman who set it there went past it eleven times a day and left it exactly where it was; and when her daughter suggested it might live in the pantry, out of the way, she said it was fine where it was, which was a lie, and was the point.

And under the Dimmr Plains, at the bottom of a hole that a clerk with a Friday deadline had named out of a book he kept at home — after a well in a desert where a woman nobody was keeping a record of found out she had been seen, and said so, and gave the place its name, and the name outlived her by three thousand years and every empire in between —

— something in the black water kept its own slow time.

One beat.

Nobody counted it.

It went on anyway.

## The Candidate's Cherished Item

**A compliment slip.**

*Academy Registry, Window Three; crest at the head; the small kind that go out clipped to returned coursework, on which the standard printed line reads* With the compliments of the Registrar. *Nine words stand below it in a deputy registrar's hand, dated and signed:*

> *"I said: if nobody signs, it closes as non-attendance. — I. Fenn, Dep. Reg."*

*It is kept folded once and laid inside the back cover of a field journal, flat against the black endpaper. It is in the way. It has to be lifted and set aside before the endpaper can be read, which is the reason it lives there, and which the owner has explained to nobody.*

*Later hands have added to the same journal, on the census pages: a heron with a hairpin; a white bird, twice; an orange bird, loud at dawn.*

*The endpaper itself photographs blank.*

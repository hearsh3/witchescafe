# Playing Elsewhere

### 脫先

*A Changli story. Jinzhou, the seventh month, after Mt. Firmament.*

---

## I. 虛手 — THE EMPTY HAND

The second card came up with the ordinary post, between a complaint about a drain and a request for the loan of a ladder.

Wen brought the tray at the hour he always brought it, set it on the corner of the table where the sun would reach it by noon, and took his own seat behind the partition. His brush had begun catching on the same stroke again. Changli listened to him work the bristles back into shape against the dish, twice, and made a note on the edge of her blotter to have the Bureau of Stores send up a proper box of them and to say nothing about it.

The morning had been good. The road estimates from Hongzhen had come in at four figures, two of which were guesses and both of which were honest about being guesses. The Plum Rain Troupe had written to ask whether the mountain road would take a property wagon before the eighth month, and had enclosed, uninvited, a list of the seven things that could go wrong with a property wagon. The resettlement register had closed on its last entry eleven days ago and she had read the closing page four times since, which was three times more than the work required. Luo Zemin's hearing had a date. His daughter's protection order had been renewed without anybody having to ask her to renew it.

Changli signed forty-one counterfoils, initialled the drain, wrote *granted, with the ward's ladder, and tell them to bring it back* beside the ladder, and opened the next envelope.

She knew the stock before she had the card out. Fractsidus paper has a weight to it that carries through the envelope into the pads of the fingers, and she had held one of those for four months in a book, and had spent some of that time deliberately touching the corner of it while thinking about something else, the way a person keeps returning her tongue to a tooth.

The card was blank. Both sides. No lamb, no slash, no mark of any kind.

There was a second thing in the envelope.

It was a receipt. The east post office's retained copy, the office half, the one that stays in the file against a query — printed form, ruled columns, the fourth month, her own name entered in a clerk's careful downhill hand, the fee, the counter number, and the date on which a single card of unusual stock had been accepted for delivery to the Counselor's office at City Hall.

She laid the two items on the blotter and squared them with her forefinger.

Beneath the window, somebody in the yard was explaining to somebody else that the cart would in fact fit, and that the problem was the man holding the gate.

Changli read the receipt again. Then she turned it over, found the back blank, and turned it back.

Four months ago she had been given a stone and had not answered. Whoever had given it had gone into the retained file of a public office, taken out the proof that she had received it, and posted it to her.

Her left forearm had begun to ache. It did that in the heat, and it did it before weather, and it did it now for a third reason, and she noted the third reason the way she noted everything, and moved her sleeve so the cuff sat clear of the ink.

"Counselor," said Wen, from behind the partition. "The ladder request. Do they want it for the well or the roof? The clerk has written both."

"Ask them. If it is the roof, they will want the long one, and the long one has a split foot that Ward Three has been promising to mend since the fifth month." Her voice came out entirely ordinary. She listened to it come out that way with a sort of distant professional respect. "Add a line reminding Ward Three about the foot. Put it in the body of the letter where they cannot miss it and then put it in the margin as well."

"They will write back complaining that we have said it twice."

"They will. And then they will mend it."

Wen wrote. Changli put the card and the receipt into the breast of her coat, finished the remaining eleven counterfoils, and left the room at the ordinary time.

She was halfway along the gallery before she understood that she was walking toward the lower stair, and that the lower stair went to the yard, and that the yard went to the east gate and the post office and four hours of entirely necessary work she could do alone.

She stopped beside a window with a view of two pigeons and the top of a cypress.

Then she turned round and went up.

***

Jinhsi was in the little room, on the half-day Baizhi had ruled her, with the shutters angled to keep the sun off the desk and her feet up on a stool she had disliked all her life and had recently made peace with.

The Loong Scale lay in its open case beside a folded page.

She had a cup. She turned it a quarter-turn by the rim as Changli came in, which she did when she was about to be interested in something, and which she did about ninety other times a day, and Changli had long ago stopped trying to tell the two apart.

"You are early," Jinhsi said. "Wen usually brings the second tray before you come up, and I have been sitting here composing the sentence in which I ask you whether you have eaten."

"I am going to do this badly," Changli said. "I would like that on the record first."

Jinhsi took her feet off the stool.

"Sit down."

"I am frightened, and I am going to do it while I am still frightened, because I said I would, and I have discovered that the frightened version of me is a poorer speaker than I expected. I have thought of four ways to open and all four of them are arrangements."

"Then use the fifth one."

"There is no fifth one. There is only the thing."

Changli took the card and the receipt out of her coat and put them on the desk, and put her hand flat beside them for a moment before taking it away.

Jinhsi read the receipt first. She was very still while she did it. Then she picked up the card, held it to the window, turned it over, and set it down on the desk exactly where it had been.

"Someone has been in the retained file at the east office."

"Yes."

"How long do those stay?"

"Two years, then they go to the prefectural store. The fourth month is still in the office. It is in a drawer under the counter in a building with one lock and a night bar, and the clerks are three women and a boy, and they are none of them a suspect, and I am going to say that out loud now so that nobody starts there."

Jinhsi's hand went to the brush before she had finished. She wrote three lines, read them, and wrote a fourth.

"Sanhua takes two people down at the second hour and secures the fourth month and the fifth. Everything retained, under seal, with the counter clerks present and their names on it, and they are to hear from her before she opens the drawer that the file is the subject of the order. Wen makes the copy. My signature goes on it." She looked up. "The hearing requires that. I want you to have the reason in the same breath as the order."

"I know why. Thank you for saying it anyway."

"I am going to keep saying things. You will find it tiresome long before I do." Jinhsi blotted the page. Then she put the brush down, and sat back, and looked at the two pieces of paper on her desk with an expression Changli had seen her turn on a warehouse scale that had come apart in somebody's hand. "Now tell me the other part. You did not come up here about a post office."

Changli looked at the shutters.

"Four months ago somebody sent me a pass," she said. "I read it as an invitation, or as a joke, and I chose, over four months, to treat it as a thing I would deal with when I had a free week. I have not had a free week since the third month and I was not going to have one, and I knew that, and the not-having was convenient."

"And this?"

"This is the same person telling me that a turn I did not take is a turn I took."

Jinhsi was quiet for a moment. The cup went round another quarter.

"You are saying that you have been playing for four months."

"I am saying that I have been playing since the day my teacher died, and that I have made exactly one move in thirty years, and the move is that I have not made one." Changli heard her own voice go thin at the end and let it. "And somebody has now served me with it. Correctly. Through a public office. With a receipt."

Jinhsi looked at her for a long time.

"Go and find the Registrar," she said.

***

Iuno was on the guesthouse step with the tea things on her knee, which was where Iuno went at that hour to be inside and outside a house at once.

The market below was in the flat part of the afternoon. A boy was stacking crates in an order his mother kept revising. The dressing over Iuno's crystal shoulder had shrunk to something narrow and businesslike, and she had her sleeve rolled off it entirely, which Baizhi had ruled against and Lupa had ruled on.

Changli sat down on the step without asking.

Iuno noticed. She poured a second cup without comment, and the not-commenting was itself a comment, and both of them let it stand.

"You are two steps closer than the last time," Iuno said.

"I am aware."

"Good. The High Priestess has opinions about people who conduct themselves from a distance, and I have spent several weeks declining to relay them."

"Relay one."

*She likes you, darling. Tell her nothing.*

"She says you take the worse cup on purpose and she finds it ostentatious."

"It is ostentatious." Changli turned the smaller cup in her fingers. "It is also the only reliable way I have found to make a room stop watching my hands."

She put the card on the step between them, and the receipt beside it.

Iuno set the pot down and read the receipt with the flat, quick attention of a woman going through a column, and Changli watched her reach the end of it and go back to the top and do it again, and she understood from the second reading that Iuno had already seen the whole thing and was checking the arithmetic out of professional courtesy.

"You did not answer the first one."

"No."

"For four months."

"For four months, and for thirty years before that, and I would like to hear you say the next part, because I have already said it to the Magistrate and it came out of my mouth like an excuse."

Iuno put the receipt down.

"A pass hands the turn back," she said. "Both players pass in succession, and the game is over, and the only work left on the board is counting it. I told you that in a road under some willows with a cart going past. What I did not say, because I did not know you well enough to be rude, is that a turn you decline to take is still a turn. You have been sitting at a board for thirty years with your hand in your lap. That is a pass. It is the politest move on the board and you have been making it since before I was in this country."

"And the second card."

"The second card is a copy of your own move, posted to your own office, with a stamp on it. Somebody has entered your pass into the record, because you would not, and now there are two in a row." Iuno lifted her cup. "Your game is over, Counselor. Whatever the book contains, it contains it finished. All that is left is to count the board, and you cannot count it, because you have never once put it on a board."

Changli looked at her own hands for a while.

"He gave it to me in a corridor eleven days before he died," she said. "He put it in my arms and he said, *in this unfinished game, you shall find the ultimate truth,* and he said it in the voice he used for the funeral hats, which was the same voice he used for everything, and then he asked me whether I had eaten. I have carried that sentence for thirty years like a lamp I was told to keep lit." Her thumb moved once along her cuff. "It has never once occurred to me that it might have been an instruction with a verb in it."

*Oh, that landed. Look at her.*

*I know. Be quiet.*

Changli discovered that she had set her cup down on the step at some point without drinking from it.

"I have read that record for thirty years."

"I am aware. You have read it the way a bishop reads a psalm. You know every page and you have never once stood the thing up in a room and looked at it from the far side." Iuno's voice had no unkindness in it, which made it worse. "Four hundred and twelve moves. It is not a text. It is a set of instructions for building a position, and until somebody builds it, nobody in this city knows what it says, including you."

The boy below finally got the crates the way his mother wanted them. She said something, and he said something, and both of them laughed, and he took two off the top and started again.

"I need a room," Changli said. "A table long enough for a board and a second board, and hands enough to place four hundred stones, and somebody to read the coordinates who will not get bored at move ninety."

"You need considerably more than that, and you know it, and you have come to a step in the east market to ask for it, which I am choosing to find encouraging."

Behind them the door opened.

Mei came out with her hair on one side of her head and a biscuit in her bandaged hand, blinked at the light, and sat down on the step above them with her knees apart and her elbows on them, entirely at home in the arrangement.

"There's a wolf asleep across the whole stairhead," she said. "I had to go over her. I don't think she noticed, which is a lie, because she moved her tail."

"You are supposed to be lying down."

"I lay down. It took forty minutes and I'm proud of it." Mei broke the biscuit and offered the larger piece to Iuno, who took it without looking. Then she leaned forward past Iuno's shoulder and looked at the two pieces of paper on the step, and her face changed in the way a face changes when a person recognises a weight her hands already know. "That's the card. The one from the book."

"There are two now," Changli said.

Mei thought about it, chewing.

"Is it bad?"

"I would have said yes an hour ago."

"And now?"

Changli looked at the market, and the crates, and the woman at the end of the lane who sold persimmons and had known her face for two months and had never once been given anything to say about it.

"Now I think somebody has taken my turn for me," she said, "and that I have needed somebody to do that for a very long time, and that the person who has done it is almost certainly not a friend, and I would like all three of those sentences to be true at once for a while before I decide which one to act on."

Mei nodded slowly.

"Okay," she said. "So you'll need the whole house."

"I was working up to asking."

"You didn't have to. Amy's going to want a table." Mei stood, brushed crumbs off herself onto the step, and put her head back through the door. Her voice, going up the stairs, arrived back out to them with the words already half worn off it. "Amy! Are you doing anything Thursday? — No, she's not."

Iuno drank her tea.

"She will now spend an hour arranging the furniture," she said, "and it will be the correct furniture, and she will have decided on it before she reaches the landing. There is no defence. I have stopped looking for one."

## II. 棋譜 — THE RECORD

Mrs Ren charged for the lamp oil.

She did it on a slip of paper, in advance, with the amount written twice, and she did it standing in the doorway of her own front room while eleven people rearranged the furniture in it, and she was entirely pleasant about it, and everybody understood that what was being purchased was permission.

"Four lamps until the eleventh hour," she said. "After that I want the shutters closed, because the family in the room above pays for quiet and gets it. The table is mine. The table's legs are not mine, they were made by a man in the harbour ward who is dead, and if you break one I will want the name of somebody who can do the joint."

"Chisa can do the joint," said Lupa, from under the table, where she was taking out the fourth leaf.

"Then we have an understanding."

The front room at Mrs Ren's had been built for a bed, a washstand, and a window that would not latch. By the fourth hour it held the extended table, both benches off the landing, Chixia's chair and Chixia's footstool and Chixia, three lamps with a fourth in reserve, four bowls of stones borrowed from three separate households, a stack of Wen's ruled paper, and, on the sill, a bowl of water into which Abby had already fallen once.

The board itself came up the stairs on Sanhua's shoulder.

It was City Hall's, out of the old committee room: a proper table board, four fingers thick, with legs, with a bowl-and-lid for each side, the wood gone the colour of weak tea along the edges where four generations of prefectural officials had rested their sleeves while failing to reach a decision. Sanhua brought it up alone, turned it in the doorway without touching either frame, and set it down on the table with a sound so small that Ciaccona looked up from the far end.

"That is a nineteen-line board," Ciaccona said. "It is going to want a nineteen-line room."

"It has a nineteen-line room. It has a nineteen-line table with a person at every side of it." Sanhua straightened the board with two fingers until its lines ran square to the table's edge. "The Counselor will want to be able to walk round it. Please keep the benches out from the wall."

Jinhsi came at the sixth hour, up the stairs like a person, with Deng behind her carrying nothing and looking as though he had been forbidden to carry anything, which he had. She stopped on the fourth step from the top because the fourth step announces you, and everybody in the front room heard it and nobody got up, and she stood there for a second with her hand on the rail and something moving across her face before she came the rest of the way.

"You have taken Mrs Ren's table apart," she said.

"We put a leaf in," said Amy. "That's the opposite of apart. Sit at the end. You're the only person here who can read a game record at speed and I'm going to make you do it until your voice goes."

"I am to be reading for two hours, Baizhi says, and then walking for ten minutes, and then reading again."

"Then you'll read for two hours and walk for ten minutes." Amy pushed a stool out with her foot. "I've allowed for it. There's a break at move a hundred and forty because that's where the pages change hand."

Changli had brought the book in both hands and had put it down in the middle of the table at the beginning and had left it there.

It sat with its oiled boards and its new spine and the great sheaf of blank pages sewn in at the back, and nobody touched it for a while, and the room went on assembling itself around it, and Changli found that she preferred this to anything she could have arranged.

Ciaccona came past at some point in the first hour and stopped at the book's corner, where the card lay on the cover with the receipt squared beside it.

"I wrote on mine," she said.

"I heard. A whole bar, with its value."

"On a road in the rain, for a man walking north with a hole in his hand, and it took me a month and a half and it was the hardest four strokes of my life." Ciaccona put her hands behind her back, deliberately, like a woman in a shop. "I am not going to touch yours. I have been thinking about it since the invitation and I have decided out loud so that nobody has to wonder. Somebody sent you a blank and it is yours to answer, and if a bard gets at it first it becomes a piece about a bard."

"Thank you."

"Do not thank me, I would love to. It has a beautiful margin." Ciaccona went back to the bench. "Ask me again in a year and I will probably have weakened."

"Before we begin," Changli said.

The room came round to her, unevenly, the way a room does.

"I have shown this page to eleven people in thirty years. Every one of them was a person I chose, in a room I chose, at an hour I chose, and I told each of them exactly as much as I wanted them to have." She put her hand flat on the cover. "This evening I am going to be told things. I would like everybody here to understand that I have never once done this, that I will probably be unpleasant about it somewhere around the ninth hour, and that when I am, the correct response is to carry on."

"We'll carry on," said Lupa.

"You may find—"

"Counselor." Lupa did not look up from the stone bowls she was distributing. "You've explained the rule. Nobody here needs the second explanation. That's the part where you're being unpleasant already, and it's four hours early."

Chixia made a noise into her cup.

Changli stood with her hand on the book for a moment longer.

"Very well," she said, and opened it.

***

They assigned the jobs the way that household assigns anything, which is loudly, out of order, and with three people volunteering for the same thing before anyone has established what the thing is.

Jinhsi read. She had the record open on a slope of folded cloth so she could keep her hands free, and she read the entries in the flat, even, slightly-too-quick cadence of a person who has read four hundred pages of assessment aloud to a committee and has learned that the only way through is to stop performing.

"One. Sixteen, four. Two. Four, sixteen. Three. Sixteen, sixteen. Four. Four, four."

"Corners," said Ciaccona. "Both of them going round the outside like people arriving at a party and choosing the wall."

Sanhua placed. She had taken the seat nearest the board and she set the stones with two fingers, without hurry, and she did not once put a hand down to steady herself on the table. By move sixty the sound had become the room's clock.

Amy kept the numbering. She had ruled a page into columns before anybody had suggested it, and she wrote the move number and the coordinate and, in a third column that nobody had asked for, the page and line of the record it came from, so that the position could be unbuilt as fast as it was built.

Iuno sat at the far end with a second sheet and wrote nothing at all for a long time.

Chisa sat beside Sanhua with her hands loose on the table's edge and her head slightly tilted, and after about twenty moves she began to say the coordinates a half-beat before Jinhsi read them, quietly, to herself, and then stopped doing it out loud and went on doing it.

Lupa poured. Cartethyia fetched. Chixia held the lamp at the angle Sanhua's hands wanted and complained about being useful in a chair, and then went on doing it for three hours. Yangyang stood at the window with her patrol log shut on her forearm and her hat pushed back, and did not write.

Abby lay along the length of the windowsill with his ears spread and narrated.

"That one is white. That one is black. That one is black also. I am observing a pattern, and I will inform you when I have it."

"You have it," said Cartethyia. "That's the pattern."

"Then it is an extremely poor pattern and whoever designed this should be ashamed. *Two* colours." His jewelry turned one notch on its own schedule. "I have seen shells with nine."

"Do you want to place some?"

"ABSOLUTELY NO! I have been watching the tall one's fingers and I do not have those fingers. I have magnificent ears and a considered opinion, and I am contributing the opinion."

By move ninety the corners had filled and the sides had begun. By move a hundred and forty, when Jinhsi stopped to walk her ten minutes up and down the landing with Sanhua a pace behind her, the board had the look a board gets when two people who know exactly what they are doing have been doing it for an hour: crowded, and calm, and nothing anywhere in it that a person could point to and call a mistake.

Changli walked round the table while they were out.

"He never played the corner enclosure on the left," she said. "Forty years I have watched him not play it. I used to think it was a preference."

"What do you think now?" said Ciaccona.

"I think he was leaving it to be argued about later, and then he ran out of later."

At move one hundred and eighty-one, Sanhua's hand stopped over the board.

"That point is occupied," she said.

Jinhsi checked the page. "One hundred and eighty-one. Three, seventeen."

"There is a white stone on three, seventeen. It has been there since move ninety-four."

The room went quiet in the particular way it goes quiet when a thing everybody has been told about turns up in front of them.

Amy pushed her chair back.

"Right," she said. "Second board. Auntie, you were correct in a field a year ago and I'm going to need you to be correct again on a shorter schedule."

"I was correct on a road, not in a field, and the distinction matters to me because the road had shade." Iuno was already turning her sheet over. "We have no second board."

"We have paper and we have a straight edge and we have a clerk." Amy looked at the door. "Wen, are you still on the stairs?"

Wen had been on the stairs for two hours with a bowl on his knee and his coat folded beside him, in the posture of a man who has been told he is welcome and has decided that the doorway is welcome enough.

He came in, looked at the table, and asked for the straight edge.

He ruled nineteen lines one way and nineteen the other on a full sheet of office paper, counting aloud, in a voice that carried no drama whatever. It took him nine minutes. He got to the end, checked the count both ways, found the vertical short by one, said nothing at all about it, turned the sheet over and did the whole thing again.

"Three hundred and sixty-one," he said, and set it down beside the wooden board. "It will take the stones if nobody leans on it."

"Nobody will lean on it," said Lupa. "I'll stand behind whoever tries."

"Thank you." Wen looked at his own ruling for a moment. "My daughter has started asking what I do. I have been finding it difficult to answer. I am going to tell her about this."

He went back to the stairs.

They restarted from move ninety-four, and by the eighth hour there were two positions on the table, and the second one was going up on ruled office paper with borrowed stones, and it was smaller, and stranger, and nobody in the room could look away from it.

***

They lost an hour to the interleaving.

Two games written in one numbering do not come apart cleanly. A move belongs to the second board if it is illegal on the first, and only some of them are illegal; the rest have to be argued. Amy and Iuno argued them. Jinhsi ruled on the ones that would not settle, and ruled fast, and marked each of her rulings with a small circle so they could be reversed later, which is a habit she has and which Changli had taught her and which neither of them mentioned.

Somewhere in the middle of it, Ciaccona stopped playing with the edge of her sleeve and said, "Read me the dates."

"There aren't dates on every move," said Amy.

"There are dates on some of them, and there are dates at the head of every page, and I want the ones you have, in order, with the move numbers attached." Ciaccona had turned round on the bench so her back was against the table. "Just read them. I am going to be irritating for about four minutes."

Amy read them. Eighty-odd dates over thirty-one years, in a flat list, with move numbers.

Ciaccona listened to the whole thing with her eyes shut.

Then she said: "The second board only gets played when the first board is fighting."

"Say that in a way I can write down," said Amy.

"I can do better. Read me the first board's moves around the twelfth of the second month, the year he has as the year of the flood."

Amy found them. A sequence of four moves in the lower right, then a break, then the same four points again in the same order.

"That is a repetition," said Jinhsi. "Somebody is taking a stone back that they have just lost. It is a ko. You cannot retake it straight away, so you go elsewhere and play something the other player has to answer, and then you come back and take it."

"Elsewhere," said Ciaccona. "And where's *elsewhere*, on this board?"

Amy checked her third column and said nothing for about three seconds.

"The second board," she said. "Every time. Every single time the first board goes into one of those repeats, there's a move on the second board inside the same week."

Ciaccona turned back round.

"Then it is not a second game," she said. "It is the room he went into when he could not sit still."

Chisa's hands had not moved from the table's edge. She said, in her level voice, the one that delivers findings as items on a list:

"Or somebody else was playing the second one, and he had only the one book, and they came to see him when he was stuck."

Nobody answered that.

Jinhsi wrote both readings on the sheet in front of her, side by side, with the same weight of brush, and did not strike either of them.

***

At the eleventh hour Mrs Ren came up, looked at the shutters, and left them open, and went down again without saying anything, which cost her something and which everybody noticed.

They were at move three hundred and ninety.

The first board was full and dense and had the last third of its life happening in one corner. The second board — the one on ruled office paper, which had by now acquired a crease and a tea ring and a small burn where a lamp had been moved badly — had seventy stones on it and looked like a conversation between two people who kept being interrupted.

And on the table's third quarter, in the space between the two boards, Amy had begun making a small separate pile of the moves that fitted nowhere.

"Eleven," she said, at the end. "Four hundred and twelve entries. Three hundred and thirty-one on the big board. Seventy on the paper. Eleven left over."

"Leftovers," said Abby, with immediate professional interest.

"Not that kind."

"Everything is that kind eventually."

Iuno was looking at the pile.

"Eleven is odd," she said.

"Yeah."

"On a road last year I told your Counselor to count the parity, and I said that an even number meant two games and an odd number meant three, and I said it to be clever, and I have thought about it perhaps twice since." Iuno's voice had gone quiet and quick. "Read me the eleven coordinates."

Amy read them.

Chisa's head came up before Amy reached the fourth.

"None of them is larger than nine," she said.

The room stopped.

"Say it again," said Changli.

"Not one of the eleven has a number in it above nine. Every coordinate on the big board goes up to nineteen. Every coordinate on the paper board goes up to nineteen." Chisa's fingers found the edge of the ruled sheet and rested there. "These eleven fit in a corner, or they fit on a board with nine lines."

Jinhsi had gone perfectly still.

"There are nine-line boards," she said. "They are what you put in front of a child."

## III. 手割 — TAKING THE MOVES APART

They drew the third board on the back of Wen's spoiled sheet, the one with the short column, because it was the only paper in the room nobody would want afterward.

Nine lines each way. Eighty-one points. It took two minutes.

Amy read the eleven moves. Sanhua placed them. They fit.

It was not a good game. Anybody could see that, including the three people in the room who did not play. Black opened in the middle, which on a nine-line board is a reasonable and slightly showy thing to do, and then Black chased White's stones round the board with the grim single-mindedness of somebody who has decided that the way to win is to be next to the opponent at all times. White gave ground carefully in three places and then, at move ten, did something small and quiet on the lower edge that took the whole bottom of the board away.

Move eleven was Black's. It was a long way from anything. It was the move of a player who has just understood what happened at the bottom of the board and is having a feeling about it.

And after move eleven the record stopped.

Changli had come round the table and was standing at the corner nearest the paper, with one hand on the table and the other at her side.

"There is a line under it," said Jinhsi. "In the record. Under move eleven, in his hand."

"Read it."

"You have read this."

"I have read it four hundred times and I have read it as the end of a page." Changli's voice had gone somewhere very level. "Read it out loud in this room."

Jinhsi read it.

"*Unfinished. She had to go and open the shop.*"

The lamp on the sill guttered where Abby had shifted, and Cartethyia reached over and steadied it without being asked, and for a while the only sound in the front room was the market below finishing for the night.

Changli sat down on the bench.

She did it slowly, and she did it properly, and she put both hands on her knees, and she looked at eleven stones on the back of a spoiled sheet of office paper.

"I was ten," she said.

Nobody moved.

"I had the key to a bookshop and I opened it at the fifth hour and the old man who owned it slept until seven and had done for years and had no intention of changing for me. The key is in my coat. It has been in whatever coat I owned for forty-one years and the shop is still there and a boy of about sixteen has the other one and I have never once introduced myself to him. There was a man who came in on the second day of every month to collect a bound set that never arrived on time, and he would stand at the counter and complain about the binder, and one morning he asked me what I had been reading, and I told him, and he said it was rubbish, and I said it was not, and he came back the following week with a board." Her thumb had begun moving along the inside of her left forearm, wrist to elbow, and stopped. "He set me a problem. He never explained a single thing to me in twelve years. He set problems and he sat there and he waited and when I got one wrong he took the stones off and set it again. I thought that was the most interesting thing that had ever happened to me and I still do."

"He kept your game," said Mei.

"He kept eleven moves of one, in the same book as the thing he thought was important, because he only ever had the one book." Changli was looking at the board. "I do not remember it. I have no memory of this at all. I remember the board and the shop and his hands and I do not remember this game, and it is here, and I played it, and I lost it, and then I got up and went and did my job."

She stopped.

"He wrote *she had to go*," she said. "As though it were an explanation he owed somebody."

Iuno poured tea and put it in front of her and did not say anything, which was a considerable exercise of restraint and which she performed with the slightly strained air of a woman charging herself interest.

Lupa came round the end of the table with a plate.

"Eat that," she said.

"I am not—"

"You're going to be. That's the same thing on a delay." Lupa put the plate down beside the eleven stones, where it would be in the way. "You can hold the feeling and the bun simultaneously. People do."

Changli looked at the bun for a moment. Then she picked it up.

***

They came back to the big board at the wrong hour, past midnight, with the lamps low and Mrs Ren's family upstairs asleep and everybody in the room tired enough to be honest.

Three hundred and thirty-one moves. Thirty-one years. The last quarter of it all happening in one corner.

"Show me the end," Changli said.

Jinhsi read the last eleven moves of the first board. Sanhua placed them.

And then the room watched an extraordinary thing happen, which was that Sanhua, having placed the last one, reached out and took a stone off the board, and put another one down, and took that one off, and put one back, and stopped, and set her hands on the table.

"It comes round," she said.

"Say it plainly," said Amy.

"I have put four stones down and taken three off and the board is in the position it was in four moves ago. If I continue, it will be in the same position again in four more." Sanhua looked at the pattern. "Neither side can stop. The moment either one of them plays anywhere else, the corner falls."

Chixia, who had been awake for nineteen hours and was propped on one elbow with her bad leg on the stool, said what everybody was thinking, in the voice of somebody who has never once been embarrassed about not knowing a thing:

"So they just — what. Go round forever?"

"Yes," said Jinhsi.

"That can't be a *rule*."

"It is a position. Rules have to have something to say about it." Jinhsi was leaning forward now, with her hand flat on the folded cloth, and her voice had changed. "It is very rare. I have seen two of them and both were in books. It has a name."

She stopped.

The stopping was so abrupt that Mei looked up from the floor, where she had been sitting with her back against the bench for an hour.

"Go on," said Changli.

Jinhsi looked at her.

"You know the name."

"I know the shape. I have known the shape since I was twenty-one." Changli's hands were still on her knees. "He hated jargon. He never gave me a technical word for anything in twelve years. He thought naming a thing gave a person somewhere to stop." A breath. "So no. I do not know the name. And you do, because you learned this out of books like a sensible person, and I would like you to say it."

Jinhsi did not want to say it. It was on her face.

"長生," she said.

And then heard it.

The room heard her hear it.

"Eternal life," she said, after a moment, in a voice that had gone completely flat. "That is the name of the shape. 長生. It is the word for the cycle that will not end."

Changli sat very still.

"Say my name," she said.

"Counselor—"

"Say the whole of it. You have said it perhaps forty thousand times."

"長離."

"長," said Changli. "The same character. He named me with half of it."

Ciaccona put her hand over her mouth.

"When," said Iuno.

"When I was ten, or eleven. He had been writing this record for twenty years by then and he was somewhere past move two hundred, and the corner had already begun to have him, and a child with no name turned up in a bookshop and beat him at nothing and lost at everything and had to leave in the mornings to open a shop." Changli's voice was thin and perfectly controlled and the control was audible. "And he gave her the first character of the position he could not get out of."

Amy said, into the quiet: "That's either the cruellest thing I've ever heard or it's a joke."

"He liked a joke," said Changli.

"Which one is it?"

"I have been deciding for thirty years." Changli looked up, and her eyes were wet, and she made no attempt whatever to do anything about that. "And I think tonight is the first night I have understood that he did not know either, and that he wrote it down anyway, because he had the one book."

Mei got up off the floor.

She came over and stood next to Changli's bench and did not touch her, and after a second Changli leaned sideways about four inches and put her shoulder against Mei's hip, which was the whole of it, and Mei put one hand on the back of her head for exactly as long as it took Lupa to start clearing cups.

"Right," said Lupa. "Shutters. Everybody out of Mrs Ren's front room. Tall one, you're taking the Magistrate down the stairs on the rail side. Wolf's going first because the fourth step announces you and I'd rather it announced me."

"I can walk down a—"

"You can. You're going to do it with somebody on the rail side anyway, because Baizhi wrote it down and she wrote it down in a way she expects me to enforce, and I enjoy enforcing it." Lupa stacked four bowls in one hand. "Board stays. Paper stays. Nobody touches the small one."

Abby was already asleep in the empty stone bowl with one ear over the rim.

Changli stood at the door with the book in her hands.

"Thank you," she said, to the room, and then, because she could hear how that had come out, "I am going to have to learn how to say that better. It came out like a receipt."

"It came out fine," said Chisa, from the bench, without turning. "You said it at the door, which is when people mean it."

## IV. 長生 — ETERNAL LIFE

Shen's table stands on the north side of the east arch, out of the traffic, with the schedule of charges on a board repainted so many times that the numbers stand proud of the wood.

Changli got there at half past nine, which was late. There were two people ahead of her. The first wanted a birthday greeting for a brother in the salt country and stood over the writing with his lips moving. The second was a girl of about fourteen with a letter she had written herself, which she wanted checked, and Shen checked it, and changed two characters, and charged her half.

Then it was Changli's turn, and she put a book on the table.

Shen looked at it without touching it.

"I write letters."

"I know."

"People bring me contracts. People bring me a deed with a crease through the seal and a cousin they want removed from it. I have sent a prefectural surveyor away from this table." Shen squared a fresh sheet without needing to look at it. "I write letters, and I read what I have written, and I read the boards on the sixth. What is that?"

"A game record. Thirty-one years of one, kept by a man who has been dead for thirty." Changli put her hand flat on the cover. "The game is my own business. There is one line in it I would like read by somebody who learned before the reform, and there is a name, and the name is mine, and I would rather not be the person who tells you what either of them says."

Shen considered her for a moment.

"You're the Counselor."

"I am."

"You've walked past this table perhaps two hundred times."

"Two hundred and more. I know your charges by heart and I have never once had anything to send."

"Hm," said Shen, which from Shen is a long speech, and put out her hand for the book.

She turned the boards with the care of somebody who has handled a great deal of old paper and has a professional grudge against how most of it has been treated. She read the first page. Her eyes went over the name at its head without pausing.

"Qiao Zhi," she said. "Board of Rites. The Second Secretary who wrote the dispute about the funeral hats."

Changli's hand stopped halfway to her sleeve.

"You know that name."

"I copied out four hundred lines of it when I was nineteen and my master would not let me sleep until the strokes were even. Everybody in this trade past a certain age has copied out the funeral-hat dispute, because it is nine pages long and there is not one single character in it worth looking at, and that is why it is good practice." Shen turned to the last written page and back. "He wrote a very clean hand and he pressed too hard. You can feel the sixth page on the eighth."

"I could not have told you that."

"You are not paid to. Which characters."

Changli found the third game and turned the book round.

Shen read the line under move eleven. It did not take her long.

"That's plain," she said. "'Unfinished. She had to go and open the shop.' There's nothing in it. The shop is a shop. The hand shakes on the last two strokes and it shakes because he was writing fast and the brush was old, and I would not read a feeling into it if you paid me the full rate, which you are going to."

"I am. Now the name."

"Your own?"

"My own."

Shen wrote it. She did it upside down, out of long habit, so that it faced the customer, and she did it slowly, which she has to.

長離.

"Everybody has told you the same two things," she said. "The first character is long, or lasting, and it is what people put in a name when they want a child to still be here in forty years. The second is fire. It is the trigram, it is the south, and it is the bird in the old books, and somebody at some point has told you it is the yellow bird, and they were showing off, and they were also right."

"I have been told all of that, yes."

"Then here is the rest of it, and it costs the same." Shen put the brush down across the rest and turned the sheet a half-turn toward her own side, and put one finger beside the second character. "Look at the left of it. That is a bird in a net. That is the whole of the picture: a small bird, and the thing that has hold of it. The old books use it for the oriole because the oriole is what they were catching."

"And?"

"And it is also the ordinary word for going away." Shen said it in exactly the voice she had used about the funeral hats. "It is what a person writes to say that a ship has left, or that two people have parted, or that somebody has resigned an office. Two hundred years ago every clerk in this country wrote it four times a day in that sense and gave the trigram no thought at all."

She picked the brush up again.

"Also, he wrote it wrong. Or he wrote it the way his own school wrote it, which by now is the same thing." Shen inked, and set the character down a second time, beside the first, and the two of them looked identical and were not. "He puts that stroke in third. Everybody under forty puts it in last. They changed the schools in the fourth year of the reform and the change went all one way and I am the only person at this gate who still argues about it."

Changli looked at the two characters for a long moment.

"So it says clinging," she said, "and it says leaving, and there is no way to decide from the page which one he meant."

"There's no way to decide from the page whether anybody ever means anything." Shen capped the ink. "I have written eleven thousand letters at this table. I have written *I am well* for a man with three months to live, and I wrote it down because he said it, and it went south, and his sister has it in a box. What he meant is his own business and it went into the ground with him."

She wrote out the charge and turned it round.

Changli read the figure and paid it, and then, because she had been standing at a counter in front of a person who had given her something she had wanted for forty years, she paid it again.

Shen looked at the second lot of money on the table.

"That's the second time in my life somebody's done that," she said.

"Who was the first?"

"Next," said Shen, to the queue, which had three people in it.

***

Master Qu came up to City Hall at eleven about a property wagon.

He was eighty-one and he arrived on his own feet and he took the stairs one at a time with his hat in his hand and refused the chair Wen brought until he had established, at some length, that he had refused it.

The Troupe had been asked to take a play up to Hongzhen for the reopening of the road. Somebody in the Bureau of Rites had suggested it. The town had written back enthusiastically. Everybody involved had assumed the play, because there is only one play you take to Mt. Firmament.

"They want 爛柯," said Master Qu. "Of course they want 爛柯. They have just had eleven weeks of their own clocks lying to them and a woman on the east terrace came in from her washing line with her hair down to her waist, and the Bureau has decided that what that town requires is a nice evening about a woodcutter who loses a hundred years."

"You would prefer to take them something else."

"I would prefer to take them the *Maiden*, which is a good play, which we can stage in a barn, and which contains four jokes about a customs officer that will go extremely well in a place with a customs officer." He put his hat on his knee. "But they will get 爛柯, because they have asked for it, and because I have staged it forty-one times, and I have never once been able to make the last scene work."

Changli put her brush down.

"Tell me about the last scene."

"You know the story."

"I know a version. I have never seen it played."

"Then I shall enjoy this." Qu shifted on the chair he had refused. "A woodcutter goes up the mountain for firewood. He comes on two old men playing a game on a rock. He stops to watch. One of them gives him something to eat, a date, a stone to suck, it varies with the province and we use a plum because a plum reads from the back of the house. He watches. It is a good game and he is absorbed. After what he takes for an hour, he reaches for his axe to go home, and the handle has rotted to powder in his hand. He goes down. The village is strange. His house belongs to somebody with his own face and four generations between them. Everybody he loved is under the hill. Curtain."

"And it does not work."

"It works beautifully. Audiences weep. I hate it." Qu's hand came down flat on the arm of the chair. "In forty-one stagings that man does not have a single line after he picks up the axe. He watches, he loses a century, he comes down, he cries, and the whole of the last scene is other people explaining to him what has happened. I have had four actors of real quality up there with nothing to do but be told things, and every one of them has come to me afterward and asked me for a line, and I have never had one to give them."

"And now?"

"Now I have one, and the Troupe will not play it, and they are correct, and I am going to keep asking." He looked at her from under his eyebrows with a great deal of enjoyment. "Would you like it?"

"Very much."

"He says it at the top of the mountain, before he goes down. He has been watching for a hundred years, and he looks at the board, and he asks the two old men: *whose turn is it?*"

Changli started to laugh.

Then she stopped laughing, with the sound still in her throat, and Master Qu watched her do it with the frank interest of a man who has spent sixty years learning to read the fourth row.

"There," he said. "You have got there faster than my leading actor, and he has had it explained to him twice."

"They have not moved."

"They have not moved in a hundred years. That is why the handle had time to rot. The boy comes down that mountain believing he has watched two immortals play a game of the highest quality, and what he has actually watched is two old men who are stuck, and who have been stuck since before his grandfather, and who have been sitting there so long that a young man with a job to go to has been thrown away on the sight of it." Qu retrieved his hat. "It is a farce. It is the funniest thing in the repertoire and nobody will let me stage it as one, because the town will want to cry, and crying is easier to sell than being made to feel foolish, and I have a wagon to get up a mountain road."

Changli signed the wagon permit. Then she signed the second one, for the second wagon Qu had not asked for and had been working round to for twenty minutes, and wrote in the margin that the Bureau of Rites was to be shown both.

At the door he stopped.

"Counselor. A question of no importance."

"Ask it."

"When the two of them are stuck — my two old men, on the rock." Qu turned his hat round once. "Has anybody ever written what it is they are stuck *over*? Because I have read four versions and not one of them will say. It is always a great game. Nobody will say what is in the corner."

"I can tell you what it would be worth," said Changli. "In my experience it is usually small."

"Ha," said Qu, with real satisfaction, and went down the stairs one at a time.

***

That evening they went back to Mrs Ren's, and the room smelled of lamp oil and other people's supper, and Chixia had got her chair to the good end of the table before anyone else arrived.

Jinhsi came with two books under her arm.

"I have been in the Library since the second hour," she said, "which Baizhi has ruled is sitting down, and I put that to her in writing."

"She'll have that framed," said Lupa.

"She wrote *sitting is not resting* on the bottom and sent it back, and I have it in my pocket." Jinhsi set the books on the table. One was a printed collection of records, thick, with a broken corner. The other was thin and old and had a Ministry stamp on the cover. "There are two rules for the position in that corner. That is what I went to check. They do not agree and they have not agreed for four hundred years, and every generation the Board of Rites reissues one of them and the amateurs go on using the other."

Amy pulled the thin book toward her and opened it at the ribbon.

"Give me the short version."

"The first rule says the game is void." Jinhsi sat down. "A position that will repeat forever has no result. Nobody wins, nobody loses, and the game did not happen. You take the stones off, you put the record in the drawer, and if the two players want a game they begin again from an empty board."

The room took that in.

"And the other one?"

"The other one forbids the repetition. You are permitted to go round the cycle, and then you are required to do something else, and whichever player arrives at that requirement first has to play somewhere the corner cannot reach, and when they do, the corner dies, and they carry the loss for the rest of the game." Jinhsi turned the thin book round so Amy could see the clause. "One rule says *nothing happened*. The other says *somebody pays and the game continues*."

Iuno had been leaning back with her cup on her knee.

"Which was he under?" said Amy.

The question dropped into the middle of the table and sat there.

"Say that again," said Changli.

"Which set of rules was your teacher playing by?" Amy said it slowly, because she had seen the face. "Because if he was under the first one, that corner means the game's void and he had thirty-one years of nothing, which is a sad story about a man with a hobby. And if he was under the second one, then somebody had to pay, and he knew it, and he sat there."

"Nobody has asked me that."

"Eleven people, you said."

"Eleven people over thirty years, and every one of them asked me who the opponent was." Changli had already pulled the book toward her. "Every one. Including me. That is the only question I have ever put to this record. I have spent thirty years asking a document who was in the room."

She found the margin.

He had kept a running count. Every fiftieth move, in the left margin, in his hard small brown hand: a number for one side and a number for the other, with a stroke between them. She had read those counts a thousand times as a man keeping score.

"He counts his own stones," she said.

"Meaning?"

"There are two ways to count a board." Jinhsi's voice was careful. "Under one of them you count the empty ground you have surrounded, and you count the stones you have taken off your opponent, and you do not count your own men at all. Under the other you count the ground and the men standing on it together." She looked at the margin. "He counts the men."

"And in thirty-one years," said Changli, "he never once wrote down a prisoner."

She turned four pages, and four more, checking, with the speed of a woman who knows exactly where everything in a book is.

"There is not a single prisoner count in this record," she said. "Which means he was under the rule where the repetition is forbidden. Which means the void was never available to him. Which means that for something between eleven and nineteen years, depending on where you say the cycle begins, my teacher sat at a board knowing that the position required one of the two of them to go and play somewhere else and lose the corner, and he went round, and round, and round, and he recorded every single circuit."

Nobody said anything.

"How much," said Chisa.

"What?"

"How much is the corner worth." Chisa's hands were flat on the table. "You've all been saying the corner. Nobody's said a number."

Amy and Jinhsi got there together, from opposite ends, with a stone bowl between them and Sanhua counting the ground twice because the first count came out disputed.

"Eleven," said Amy. "Give or take one, depending how the edge resolves. Eleven points."

"Out of?"

"Three hundred and sixty-one."

Chisa nodded once, slowly.

"Then it's less than a line," she said.

Cartethyia had been quiet for most of an hour, sitting sideways on the bench with her feet drawn up and her crystal laurel turning above her hair at its own pace.

"Can I say the big one," she said.

"You are going to anyway," said Ciaccona. "You have been holding it since the word *void* and your ears have gone pink."

"They have not."

"Darling."

"They have." Cartethyia put her chin on her knee. "It's the war, isn't it. That's the same shape. The Threnodians come out of us. They come out of cities and armies and the fact that people build things worth taking, and every time we get better at fighting them there's more of us and more to come out of, and every time somebody wins something the board just — comes back round." She looked up. "That's the corner. That's a hundred years of two old men on a rock."

"It is a very tidy analogy," said Iuno, "and I would distrust it for that reason, and I cannot make it stop being true."

"So which rule do we get?"

"There is a woman," Amy said, "who has read our whole story fourteen times. She goes back to the beginning and runs it again and looks for a better result. Every time it comes out wrong she takes the stones off." She turned her pencil over on the table. "That's rule one. She's been using rule one for a very long time and she will tell you, at length, and quite persuasively, that she is doing it out of love."

"And rule two?"

"Rule two is that somebody plays somewhere else and eats eleven points."

"Which eleven?" said Cartethyia.

Amy opened her mouth and shut it again.

"That's the whole question, isn't it," she said. "Everybody at this table knows the shape and not one of us can tell you what the eleven points are made of. That's where every clever answer I've ever heard goes to die. Somebody will always tell you what has to be given up and they will always be describing other people."

Mei was on the floor again, with her back against the bench and her bandaged hand open on her knee.

"I'm a corner," she said.

Iuno's cup came down.

"Sweetheart."

"No, it's — I'm not being tragic about it, I'm being accurate." Mei turned her hand over and looked at it. "Whatever it is that puts me back together, it doesn't get tired and it doesn't get better and it doesn't get worse. I go round. I've gone round nine times that Lupa's counted. Nobody can finish it, including me, and the only way anything on that board ever changes is if somebody decides that the corner isn't the game."

"That is not the same thing at all," said Jinhsi.

"It's a bit the same." Mei tipped her head back against the bench and looked at the ceiling. "I'm just saying I know what it's like from inside one. You stop being a place where things happen and you start being a place where things repeat, and everyone gets very careful about you, and the game goes on somewhere else." A beat. "It's fine. I'm doing something about it. It's taken a couple of lifetimes and a wolf."

"Two wolves," said Lupa, from the door. "The first one was nicer."

"The first one was *not* nicer."

"The first one was me at twenty-two and she was extremely nice and it did you no good at all." Lupa came in with a pot in both hands and put it down in the middle of the table on top of Amy's third column, which was deliberate. "That's enough of the eternity. Everybody's had four hours of it and nobody's eaten since the second bell."

"There are stones on the table," said Sanhua.

"There are stones under the pot, and they'll survive, and if they don't we'll draw them on paper like the last lot." Lupa began handing out bowls. "Counselor. You get the one with the chip."

"I would have taken the one with the chip."

"I know. That's why you're getting it and not choosing it."

Yangyang came away from the window, where she had been standing for most of the evening with her arms folded.

"She will not come near it," she said.

Everybody looked up.

"The board." Yangyang's chin went a fraction toward the table. "She has been at the other end of the room since the first night. She went past it once, on the first evening, at about the ninetieth move, and then she went to the window and she has stayed there."

"Is she—" Cartethyia stopped. "Sorry. You have to do all of it now and I keep making you do the easy half."

"I have words to spare." Yangyang's ears had gone a little pink under the hat and she carried on at exactly the same pace. "A wind over a board moves the stones. I think she worked that out on the first evening, and she took herself out of the question so that nobody in here would have to ask her. She was an Outrider. It is the sort of thing an Outrider does and then declines to discuss."

"That's manners," said Chixia.

"It is manners," Yangyang agreed, "and I want it written down somewhere that she is being unbearably polite at me, and I cannot argue with her about it, because she has no words left and I have all of them."

The curtain at the window lifted about four inches and settled.

"She heard that," said Mei.

"She hears everything. She reads my correspondence."

Abby, who had been asleep in a stone bowl for two hours, woke up at the smell of the pot, walked the length of the table through three separate pieces of paper, and stopped at the eleven stones on the spoiled sheet.

He picked up two of them in his mouth.

He carried them four steps, put one down beside Chixia's bowl, and went back to the other end of the table, where he set the second one down on the empty bench by the wall.

Then he came and sat in front of the pot and shouted at it.

Nobody at the table said anything about the bench.

Sanhua got up, took the two stones back, and set them down on the nine-line board in their places without being asked, and Changli watched her do it and had to look at the ceiling for a moment.

## V. 定式 — THE SETTLED SHAPE

Sanhua asked for her hour on the fourth day, in writing, through Wen, which she did not have to do.

The note said: *Counselor. I would like an hour of your time on a personal matter, at whatever hour suits your office. I have told the Magistrate that I am asking. I have not told her what for.*

Changli read it twice and wrote back: *Tomorrow, the ninth hour, in the garden room. I will have nothing else after it. Bring whatever you need to bring and take as long as you require.*

She got up at five and went over the whole thing in her head while she dressed, and caught herself doing it at the third repetition, and stopped, and sat on the end of the bed with her hands in her lap and let the morning happen.

Sanhua came at the ninth hour exactly. She had a folder.

She also had a covered plate, which she put on the side table with the air of a woman discharging an entirely separate obligation, and neither of them mentioned it for fifty minutes.

"Thank you for the room," Sanhua said. "I asked for this in the garden room and I did not say why. The Magistrate sat in that chair four days after the column came home and told me where she wanted me to be, and it was the first time anybody had asked me that in eleven years, and I wanted the conversation to happen where it happened."

"Then take that chair."

"I will take the other one. That one is hers."

Sanhua sat down and opened the folder and did not look at it.

"I have nine questions," she said. "I wrote them out three weeks ago and I have changed four of them since. Some of them are unkind. I would like to ask all nine, and I would like you to answer them in the order I ask them, because I have arranged the order and the arrangement is most of my preparation."

"Ask them."

"The first one is the whole of it, and then the other eight are how I check the answer." Sanhua's hands were flat on the folder. "Between the fourth month of the year I came into this office and the twenty-first of last month, how many of the maps I planned the Magistrate's movements from had been through your hands before they reached me?"

Changli did not reach for anything.

"All of them," she said.

"Say the number."

"I do not have a number. I have a practice. Every runner's map that crossed my desk went to a copyist and was copied fair before it went up the stair, and that was done for the lake material and for the prophetic chronology, and the copying was honest in every respect except the dating." Changli kept her hands where Sanhua could see them. "I never altered a road. I never altered a distance, a bridge, a gate hour, a garrison, or a water level. I want to tell you that plainly and I want you to hear that I understand it is the smallest possible mitigation."

"It is not the smallest. If you had altered a road I would be having a different conversation and it would be shorter." Sanhua's eyes had not moved. "Second question. When you moved the Magistrate's three-day absence eleven days forward in the sixth month, did you consider that I would be planning her protection around the new date?"

"Yes."

"What did you conclude?"

"That you would do it well, that you would do it with materials I had shaped, and that if anything went wrong you would believe you had failed."

The snow came then. Two flakes, then five, drifting into existence above the table and going out before they reached the wood.

Sanhua looked at them.

"That is the one I could not write down," she said. "Thank you for saying it without being asked twice."

She went through the other seven.

They took forty minutes. They were exact, and several of them were worse than the first, and one of them was about a particular Tuesday in the third month on which Sanhua had stood in a doorway for six hours on the strength of a schedule she now knew Changli had set. Changli answered them all. Twice she had to stop and go and find a paper to be accurate, and both times Sanhua waited without filling the silence, and the second time she moved the lamp so that Changli could read.

At the end Sanhua closed the folder.

"That is all nine," she said. "I want to tell you what I have decided, because otherwise you will spend a month working it out from my face, and I have watched you do that to people and I would rather not be a project."

"Go on."

"I am going to keep planning around your material. There is nobody else who can produce it, the Magistrate has ruled you back into the work, and my refusing would be a gesture that cost her and not you." Sanhua set the folder aside. "So I will do the work, and I will check it, and I will check it in a way you can see, and you will find it insulting for about a year. I am telling you in advance so that when you feel the insult you will know it is scheduled."

Changli laughed before she could stop herself.

"That is the most Jinzhou sentence anybody has said to me in eleven years."

"I learned it here."

"You learned it from me."

"I learned the shape from you." Sanhua said it quite evenly. "That is the other thing I came to say, and I have been deciding all week whether to say it, and I am going to, because I have been sitting at the end of a corridor for eleven years and I have started to suspect that nobody has ever said anything to you."

***

Changli got up and poured the water.

It was a thing to do with her hands and both of them knew it, and Sanhua let her, and took the cup, and held it without drinking.

"When I came into this office," Sanhua said, "I was told where to stand by a woman who had been doing the job for four years without a title. You said: half a step behind, on the side the light comes from, and never in a doorway she might want to leave through. I have stood that way in eleven hundred rooms. I have taught it to two people. I have corrected a captain of the guard on it in front of his own men, and I used your words."

"It is good practice."

"It is excellent practice. It is also a shape, and I did not invent it, and I have never once asked whether it was the right one for me." Sanhua turned the cup a little. "In the old books there are settled patterns for the corners. Somebody worked them out four hundred years ago, the strong players agreed on them, and now every child learns them and they are correct. And then you play one in a game where the rest of the board has changed, and it is still correct, and you lose."

"You have been reading Jinhsi's collection."

"I read the first forty pages and I stopped, because the second corner in the book was mine." Sanhua put the cup down. "Half a step behind, on the light side, never in the doorway. It is a correct pattern for the board I was handed at nineteen. Four days ago the Magistrate pointed at a chair and asked me to sit in it while a dead man's parents told her about a shirt, and I sat in it, and I had no idea what to do with my hands."

Changli was very quiet.

"What did you do with them?"

"She put hers on the table," said Sanhua. "So I put mine beside it."

The garden door stood open. Out on the path, the gardener went past with a basket against her hip and looked in, and lifted a hand, and went on.

"I am not going to ask you for a new pattern," Sanhua said. "That is the point. If I ask you for one I will have it by Thursday and it will be excellent and I will stand in it for another eleven years."

"You would," said Changli. "I would give you one. I would enjoy giving you one. I have been sitting here for the last minute constructing it."

"I know. I watched you start."

"That is fair." Changli looked at her own hands. "Then let me give you the only thing I am still entitled to give, which is a piece of information about myself. When I taught you where to stand, I was teaching you where *I* stand. I have spent forty years half a step behind people on the side the light comes from. It is the best seat in any room. You can see every face and nobody has to look at yours." She turned her left wrist over on her knee, deliberately, so the scars lay uppermost. "And last week a woman with one arm told me I have been sitting at a board for thirty years with my hand in my lap, and I have not slept properly since, and I am fifty-one."

Sanhua looked at the scars for a moment.

"May I say the thing I came to say."

"You have been circling it for an hour and you have been extremely graceful about it and yes."

"Your frequency has changed."

The room went still.

"I have looked at you since I was nineteen," Sanhua said. "You are the second-clearest thing I have ever had to look at, and you have been the clearest for weeks at a time when the Magistrate was away, and I know what you sound like the way I know a road. In the third month there was a sag under it. It came and went and I put it down to the season. Since the mountain it has stopped coming and going."

Changli did not move.

"Say it in a way I can act on."

"You are burning out of the reserve and not out of the day." Sanhua's voice did not change at all. "I do not know the medicine. I know the sound. When a person is tired, the whole thing goes quiet and comes back in the morning. Yours does not come back in the morning any more. It comes back to a lower place and then it holds there all day, and it has been holding at that lower place since we came down off the mountain, and the place is lower than it was in the spring."

"How much lower?"

"I am not an instrument. I will not give you a number I have made up." Sanhua leaned forward slightly. "I will give you this. You should go to Baizhi, today, before the fifth hour, and you should ask her for the whole of it out loud, and you should ask her in a room with a person in it. And if you would prefer not to do that, then I am going to tell the Magistrate what I have just told you, and I am going to do it this evening, and I am telling you that in advance so it is not a threat."

"That has a wrapper on it."

"It has a wrapper on it," Sanhua agreed. "Underneath, it is a notice, and I have learned the difference from a Registrar with one arm, and I like it very much."

***

Baizhi kept her waiting eleven minutes, which was an eleven-minute conversation with a man about a splint, and Changli sat on the bench outside the dispensary among four other people and was recognised by two of them and talked about seed prices with one.

Then the door opened and Baizhi looked out and said, "Counselor. Come in and take the left chair, the right one wobbles and I have been meaning to speak to somebody about it for a month."

"I will have somebody about it tomorrow."

"I am aware that you will. That is why I said it in front of you."

Baizhi measured for a long time.

She did it in the order she always did it in, and she did not narrate, and twice she went back and did a thing again, and the second time she wrote a number down and looked at it and drew a line under it.

"Sit up. Breathe out and keep breathing out past where you want to stop."

Changli did.

"Again."

She did.

Baizhi put the instrument down and washed her hands and sat on the stool with the notes on her knee.

"You have been carrying two old Overclocks and a great deal of ordinary work," she said, "and the physicians who saw you at twenty-three wrote that the first one might have cost you years, and they were being careful, and I am going to be less careful because you have come in here and asked me out loud."

"Please."

"Your reserve reads at a woman between eleven and fourteen years older than your record says. That gap has been widening since the spring. It widened faster in the fifth and sixth months, which had a siege in them, and it has not narrowed since, which is the part I mind." Baizhi turned the page round so Changli could see the figures. "I will not give you a date. Anybody who gives you a date is guessing in a robe. I will give you a shape, and the shape is this: if you go on at the rate of the last six months, you will have perhaps a decade in which you can do this job the way you do it now, and then a period after that in which you will be able to do rather less of it than you will want to, and the second period will be longer than you are imagining and it will not be the part that kills you."

"And if I do not go on at that rate?"

"Then the gap stops widening, and you get the ordinary allotment of a woman of fifty-one with a bad arm, which in this country is a long time, and most of it is good." Baizhi looked at her steadily. "You will also have to spend some of it sitting down while other people do things badly, and I want to be honest that in my experience that is the part people find intolerable."

Changli looked at the figures for a while.

"What happens if I Overclock again?"

"Then we are having a different conversation and it will be the last one." Baizhi did not soften it at all. "One more at full extension takes the decade. Possibly all of it. I am telling you that in a room, in advance, with my own mouth, while you have a mouth to answer with."

Changli's hand went still on her knee.

"You know the clause."

"The whole city knows the clause. A wolf made a foreign country write it down in a field and my colleague read it out loud to a flying cat with his forehead against her palm." Baizhi capped her pen. "You are not household four-one-one. You undertook it for them and nobody has ever undertaken it for you, and I have been waiting four months for you to walk in here, and you have made me wait longer than was decent, and I am cross about it and I will be over it by supper."

"I will be committing myself," said Changli. "That is the thing. The clause covers a person being spent by somebody else. I have been spending myself in quarter-hours since I was nineteen and there is nobody standing in front of me to say the words."

"Then somebody is going to have to do it," said Baizhi, "and it had better be a person you cannot dismiss."

## VI. 脫先 — PLAYING ELSEWHERE

The demonstration game happened on the sixth evening, and it happened because Chixia would not stop asking.

She had spent four nights watching other people put down stones on behalf of a dead man and had arrived at the reasonable position that she had no idea what any of it was for.

"I can follow the shape of it," she said. "I can see the corner and I can see the middle and I can see that when the tall one puts a stone down everybody makes a noise. I want to watch two people actually play. Fast. Badly. With opinions."

"You want a floor show," said Ciaccona.

"I want to be able to tell the dispatch desk what I've been doing for a week. At the moment I have *there was a book* and *they cried about a corner*, and Little Guo is going to ask follow-up questions."

Jinhsi, who had spent the day at the Bureau of Waterways and had come to the guesthouse at seven with her coat over her arm, said, "I will play. I am poor at it and I play constantly and I will not be embarrassed in front of this room."

"Against who?"

Jinhsi looked round the table.

"Against Mei," she said.

Mei, who was lying along the bench with one arm over her eyes, said, "I don't play."

"You have watched four hundred moves go down in the last five days."

"Watching a thing isn't the same as—" Mei stopped, and took her arm off her eyes, and sat up slowly. "Okay. That's not true. I don't know whether I play. That's the honest sentence. Nobody's ever put a board in front of me."

"Then somebody is about to," said Amy, and cleared the end of the table.

***

They played on the wooden board with the game record pushed to one side.

Mei picked up a stone.

She did it without looking at her hand. The stone came up out of the bowl on the tips of two fingers, the index laid over the middle, and went down on the board with the middle finger, and it made the small flat crack that a stone makes when it is placed by somebody who has done it enough times that the sound is no longer an accident.

Sanhua's chin came up.

Changli, at the far end of the table with a cup in her hands, said nothing whatsoever.

"Huh," said Mei, looking at her own fingers. "That's — yeah. Okay. Hands again."

"Do you know where to put it?" said Jinhsi.

"No. Not a clue. I know how to hold it." Mei turned her hand over. "It's like the regulator. The bit that knows isn't the bit that remembers."

"Then we will begin with you putting it wherever you like and me telling you what it does."

They began.

Jinhsi played the corners. Mei played next to her.

It took about six moves for the room to see it, and it took Chixia, who had never held a stone in her life, about eight.

"She's following you around," Chixia said.

"She is."

"Like — every time. You put one down, she puts one down next to it."

"Every time." Jinhsi placed a stone on the far side of the board, in an empty quarter, and Mei's hand went out and put one beside it within four seconds. "There. I have gone to a completely different part of the board, which is a thing you are allowed to do, and which is the single hardest thing to learn, and she has come with me."

"Is that bad?"

"It is very friendly and it is losing her the game."

Mei looked at the board.

"Explain it to me like I'm a person who's good at other things."

"Every stone I put down is a small threat. Some of them matter and some of them are rubbish." Jinhsi tapped the board twice, in two different places. "That one, you have to answer. If you ignore that one, I take nine of your stones and you will be able to see me doing it. And that one there is worth about two points and I played it to see what you would do, and you answered it, and while you were answering it I got to play first somewhere that was worth twenty."

"So what should I have done?"

"Ignored me, and gone and played where you wanted to play."

"That feels rude."

"It is the entire game," said Jinhsi.

Iuno, on the bench, put her cup down very carefully and did not look at anybody.

*Darling.*

*I am aware.*

*You are not going to say it.*

*I am going to let a nineteen-year-old say it, because she is saying it about a board, and if I say it it becomes about a hallway.*

They played on.

Jinhsi took the bottom of the board, and then the left side, and then a sizeable and entirely undeserved chunk of the middle, and she did it by making forty small noises in forty places and walking away from each one while Mei came to see what it was. At move thirty-eight she stopped, looked at the position, and put her stone back in the bowl.

"That is enough for a demonstration."

"Am I dead?"

"You are extremely alive. You have thirty-eight living stones and every one of them is next to one of mine." Jinhsi's mouth was doing something complicated. "You have built a beautiful fence around everything I did and you own none of it. I have about sixty points and you have about eleven."

Chixia whistled.

"So she loses by being nice at you."

"She loses by answering," said Jinhsi.

Lupa, leaning on the wall by the door with her arms folded, said: "Yeah."

That was all she said. She said it in the flat voice she uses for weather, and she did not elaborate, and she did not look at Mei, and Mei sat with her hand on the edge of the board and went a slow, deep red from the collar up.

"Oh, come *on*," Mei said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You said *yeah*."

"I did say yeah." Lupa pushed off the wall and came to look at the board over Jinhsi's shoulder. "Eleven points. That's funny. That's funny and I'm allowed to think it's funny, because I've been the stone at forty-nine, fifty and fifty-one for four years and I've got a right to a bit of comedy."

Cartethyia had both hands over her mouth.

"Nobody is to write this down," said Mei.

"Auntie wrote it down," said Amy.

"I have made two notes and one of them is a diagram," said Iuno.

***

Afterward, when the bowls had been put away and Chixia had extracted the promise of a second demonstration from Jinhsi on a different evening, Changli took the record over to the lamp and turned to the pages near the end of the first board, and stood there with her thumb against the margin.

Amy came and stood beside her.

"You've gone somewhere," Amy said.

"I have been somewhere for about forty minutes. I am deciding whether to come back with anything."

"Is it the note?"

Changli looked at her.

"You have seen it."

"I've been reading his margins for five days, and yours." Amy folded her arms. "You argue with him in a lighter ink in a hand that gets bigger when you're annoyed, and there are three hundred of them, and they're mostly good. And then there's one that isn't yours and isn't his."

"It is mine. It is twenty-nine years old and it is mine." Changli turned the book so the lamp fell on it. "I wrote it about eight months after he died, when I had decided to work the whole record through from the beginning so that I could find the truth in it, the way an idiot of twenty-two decides things. And I wrote three words in the margin and then I stopped working through it and I have never done it again."

Amy read them.

Three words, in a young hand, pressed hard.

*answers everything.*

"That's your reading of the opponent."

"That is my reading of the opponent. I made it at twenty-two, on the basis of about a hundred moves, and I have never revised it, and I have never once in twenty-nine years wondered whether it was a compliment or a diagnosis." Changli shut the book with her finger in it. "Tonight I watched a woman lose sixty points to a nineteen-year-old by going to look at everything she was shown."

Amy did not answer that straight away.

Across the room Mei was on the floor with Abby on her lap and her head tipped back against the bench, saying something to Cartethyia that was making Cartethyia laugh into her sleeve.

"There's a window," Amy said. "I looked it up on the third night because I'm me. We can account for her from the beach forward and we can account for a chunk of the old records from the founding forward, and between the two there's a stretch nobody can put a person in, and it's about four hundred years long, and thirty-one of them are your teacher's thirty-one."

"I am aware."

"And it doesn't prove anything. It's a gap. Gaps aren't evidence, they're just where the evidence isn't."

"I am aware of that too, and I have been aware of it for five days, and I want you to know that I have not asked her."

"Why not?"

Changli looked at the book under her hand.

"Because I would be asking her to be the answer to my teacher's joke," she said. "She has spent a year in this country being the answer to other people's questions about their own histories. There is a whole wall of the Library about her. A man built a machine to tell her who she was. If I take this book across the room and put it in her lap, I will be handing her one more document that requires her to be somebody, and she will try, because she tries, and she will be sorry when she cannot, and she will apologise to *me*." Her thumb moved once along the inside of her forearm. "I have spent my whole life arranging the hour at which people are asked things. I have decided to be very careful about this one."

"Are you going to ask her at all?"

"I asked her on the second morning," said Changli. "Plainly, once, over the breakfast table, with everybody there. I asked her whether she had ever played, and she said she did not know, and she said it without flinching, and then she said she would like to be able to give me an answer and that she had a beach and about four hundred years of nothing. And then Lupa put a bowl in front of her." Changli's mouth moved. "That is the whole of my investigation and it is going to stay that way."

Amy looked across the room for a while.

"I'll tell you the thing I'd write in the remarks column," she said. "If I were doing it properly."

"Do."

"Somebody who answers everything isn't rare." Amy shrugged. "It's the commonest fault there is. It's what people do when they've decided the worst thing that could happen is that somebody asks for them and they're not there. My mother does it. Auntie did the opposite version for thirty years, which is the same disease wearing a better coat. I did it for ten years inside a machine with a very small number of people to do it at." She pushed off the table. "So your dead Secretary sat down thirty-one years running against a person who could not leave anything alone. That narrows it to about half the population of Huanglong and every single person in this room."

Changli let out a breath that was almost a laugh.

"Put that in the remarks column," she said.

"I'm not writing it in the remarks column. It's a rubbish finding."

"Put it in anyway. It is the only one anybody has produced in thirty years that does not require a miracle."

Amy got out her pencil.

Behind them, Mei said, "Did somebody say my name?" and nobody answered her, and Lupa said "No," in a voice that meant yes, and the room moved on, and the three words stayed in the margin where they had been since Changli was twenty-two.

## VII. 打入 — THE INVASION

The old committee room at City Hall has a board in it because four generations of prefectural officials have needed something to look at.

It stands under the west window with its legs on the bare boards where the carpet stops, and its edges have gone the colour of weak tea, and there is a worn patch on the near side at the height of a resting sleeve. Changli had carried the board down to Mrs Ren's and Sanhua had carried it back, and the room around it had gone on holding its committees the whole time, some of them beside a bare table and none of them noticing.

Jinhsi came up at the seventh hour with her hair still damp at the temples.

"I have walked to the second gate and back and Sanhua has certified it," she said. "Baizhi wants eleven minutes twice a day and I have decided to do nineteen once, and I am aware that this is the same argument I have with the Bureau of Waterways every spring and that I lose it."

"Sit down. Black."

"You always give me Black."

"I always give you Black."

They played.

They had played, by Changli's count, one thousand two hundred and forty-one games, beginning in a room above a snow-blocked road when Jinhsi was nine and had lost her second tooth that week and had been unable to talk about anything else. Changli knew the opening Jinhsi would choose before her hand reached the bowl. Jinhsi knew that she knew. They played it anyway, because the first twenty moves of a game between two people who have played twelve hundred is a conversation about whether anything has changed since Tuesday.

At move twenty-two, Jinhsi said:

"How many have you given me?"

Changli's hand did not stop. She placed the stone, and sat back, and put her hands in her lap.

"None," she said.

"Counselor."

"I am answering you. I have never in twelve hundred games played a bad move against you on purpose. Not one. I have beaten you one thousand one hundred and four times." Changli's voice was perfectly level and she had gone slightly grey around the mouth. "You have beaten me one hundred and thirty-seven, and every one of those was yours, and I have the dates of the first eleven because I wrote them down."

Jinhsi looked at the board for a while.

"Then why did I ask," she said.

"Because you are extremely good at reading a room, and you have been reading this one for five days, and you have worked out that a woman who forges a date for seven years does not stop being that woman when she sits down to a game." Changli turned her cup a little on the table and left it. "So you asked the question you had the words for. The one you actually want is a different question and I am going to give you the answer to it, and then you are going to be angry, and I would rather we did it here than at the fourth hour in a corridor."

"Say it."

"In eleven years I have never once invaded you."

Jinhsi did not move.

"There is a thing you may do in this game," Changli said, "which is to walk into a place your opponent has made and put a stone down in the middle of it and try to live there. It is rude. It is frightening the first eleven times it is done to you. Everybody good has had it done to them so often that they stopped noticing around the age of fourteen." Her hand went out and touched the edge of Jinhsi's left-hand shape, the wide, calm, beautifully-built thing that had been growing since move nine. "I have never done it. Not in a teaching game, not in a real one, not once, in twelve hundred. I took the edges. I took what you left. I beat you by building faster than you and never once by coming inside."

"Why?"

"You were nine. Then you were ten." Changli's jaw moved. "And then it became a thing I had not done, which is a different sort of reason and a much worse one."

Jinhsi put her hand flat on the table.

"Say the consequence out loud," she said. "You know what it is. You worked it out before you sat down."

"I would rather—"

"You told me you would tell me what you see while you were still frightened. You are frightened now. I can see the vein in your neck from here." Jinhsi's voice had gone very quiet and very exact, which is what it does. "Say the consequence."

Changli looked at the board.

"You build," she said. "You build better than anyone I have ever taught. You take a difficulty and you put a structure round it and the structure works and the structure is generous. And when somebody walks into the middle of something you have made, you go absolutely still, because in eleven years of the best training available in this country nobody has ever taught you what to do with your hands."

"The draft."

"The draft."

"They came inside." Jinhsi was looking at her own left-hand shape. "Fourteen pages. A meal served late. A request I sent back. They came inside the thing I had made and I read it four times and I could not think of a single move, and I sat in that chair with the pages in my hands for an hour, and then I went downstairs and asked the steward about the kitchens, because the kitchens were something I knew how to build."

"Yes."

"And you went upstairs and killed it with a forgery."

"Yes."

Jinhsi's hand closed slowly on the table's edge.

"I am very angry," she said.

"I know."

"I do not want to be talked out of it, I do not want it explained, and I am going to be angry about this one for a long time, and I want to tell you now that it is going to come back at bad hours, and that some of the times it comes back I am going to be unfair." Her eyes had gone bright and her voice had not moved at all. "I am telling you in advance so that it is not a threat."

"That is Sanhua's phrasing."

"She got it from the Registrar and I got it from her and it is going round this city like a cold."

Changli's mouth trembled and held.

"Then here is my part," she said. "Give me the board."

"What?"

"Give me the board back. I want to show you something and then you are going to be angry about that too, and it is worth it." Changli reached across, picked up the stone Jinhsi had just placed, and set it back in its bowl. "Your move twenty-two. Play it again."

Jinhsi played it again.

And Changli reached into her own bowl and put a white stone down in the exact middle of the wide, calm, beautifully-built shape on the left.

Jinhsi jerked back in her chair.

She actually jerked back. Her shoulder hit the chair rail. She stared at the stone, and then at Changli, and then at the stone, and her whole face went through four things in about a second and a half.

"That is *inside*."

"It is."

"It is — you cannot — it has nowhere to go, it is surrounded, it is—" Jinhsi's hands had come up off the table. "It is going to live, isn't it."

"I do not know. That is the honest answer and it is the first honest answer of this kind I have given you in eleven years." Changli sat back. "It might live. It might die and cost me nine points and ruin the outside while it dies. You are going to have to work it out with your own hands while somebody is sitting opposite you, and I am going to sit here and be entirely unhelpful, and that is the lesson."

Jinhsi looked at the board.

She looked at it for four minutes. Changli watched the whole of it: the first response discarded, the second discarded, the count begun and abandoned, the moment where she stopped trying to kill the stone and started trying to work out what she would still own if it lived.

Then she played.

It was a poor move. It was a poor move in an interesting direction, made by somebody who had found her own reason for it, and Changli felt something go through her chest that she did not have a name for and would be thinking about for a month.

"That is bad," said Jinhsi.

"It is."

"Do it again tomorrow."

"I intend to do it every single game for the rest of my life," said Changli, "and you are going to hate me for about a year, and then one day you are going to kill one of them, and I am going to be extremely unpleasant about having taught you."

Jinhsi laughed. It came out wet.

Then she reached across the board and took Changli's sleeve, which is a thing she has done since she was small, and which requires the person whose sleeve it is to stop.

***

The lamps came round at the eighth hour. Somebody in the yard was taking a delivery at the wrong gate and being very good-natured about walking it round.

"There is one more thing tonight," Changli said, "and it is mine, and I have been holding it for two days because I wanted to bring you the board first."

"Then bring it."

"I went to Baizhi. I went because Sanhua said she would go to you if I did not, and she was right to say that, and I want it on the record that the first thing I did was resent her for it." Changli put both hands flat on her knees. "My reserve reads at a woman between eleven and fourteen years older than my record says. The gap has been widening since the spring. If I go on at the rate of the last six months I have perhaps a decade of doing this job the way I do it, and a longer period afterward of not being able to, which she says is the part people find intolerable, and she is a good physician and she is correct about that."

Jinhsi had gone very white.

"And if you do not go on at that rate?"

"Then it stops widening, and I get the ordinary allotment of a woman of fifty-one with a bad arm, and most of it is good." Changli made herself say the rest of it without any arrangement at all. "And if I Overclock again at full extension, it takes the decade. Possibly all of it. She told me that in a room, in advance, with her own mouth, while I had a mouth to answer with."

The room was quiet.

"You said that phrase deliberately."

"I did."

"You wrote that clause in a field for a foreign household."

"I wrote it in a field for a foreign household, and I meant every word, and I have watched a physician and a wolf and a Registrar use it on each other all summer, and it has never once occurred to me until four days ago that the reason nobody has ever used it on me is that I am the one who spends me." Changli looked at her hands. "There is no clause for that. I have looked. I drafted three in my head on the stairs and all three of them were arrangements."

Jinhsi's chin had gone down.

"I would have said the words to your face."

"You would. And I would have listened to you and thanked you and gone and done it anyway, because I have been deciding what you can bear since you were eight, and that muscle does not stop working merely because we have had an honest morning." Changli's voice cracked slightly and she carried on through it. "I need somebody I cannot manage. You are the person I love most in the world and you are also the person I have been managing the longest, and those two facts are in the same hand."

Jinhsi put her forehead down on her wrist for a moment.

Then she lifted it.

"Then get one," she said. "Tonight. Not next week. And bring me the name so that I can be furious that it is not me."

"I have a name."

"Of course you have a name."

"I have had it since Tuesday afternoon," said Changli, "and I have spent two days finding urgent work between myself and the asking, and I noticed myself doing it yesterday at about the fourth hour, and I put the paper down and came here instead, which is an improvement of about thirty years."

***

They sat with the board between them and the invaded shape still on it.

"May I ask you mine," Jinhsi said.

"You may ask me anything. It is going to take me a while to stop being pleased about that."

"How long am I going to be here?"

Changli went still.

"I do not know," Jinhsi said. "That is the state of it. Baizhi cannot measure it. Amy says the apparatus can tell her what my rhythm is doing this week and nothing at all about the shape of the whole. Jué might know, or might have an opinion he would present as knowledge, which with him amounts to the same thing and he would say so himself." She turned her cup a quarter-turn by the rim and stopped it with her thumb. "I have the question written on the page beside the scale. Sanhua keeps the page. I have not asked it."

"Why?"

"Because of two things and I am only proud of one of them." Jinhsi looked at the board. "The first is that I have eleven weeks of work in front of me and a mountain town holding a meeting about whether it wants ordinary winters, and I do not have room in my head this month for a number that large.

"The second is that I am afraid the answer is *a long time*." Her voice stayed flat. "And I think if I hear that, then every conversation I have from that afternoon onward becomes a rehearsal. I will sit at supper with Panhua and I will be measuring. I will look at Chixia arguing about a cart and I will be doing arithmetic. Mei told me once that she stopped being a place where things happened and became a place where things repeated, and everybody went carefully around her. I have watched it happen to her and I do not want to find out what it is like to do it to a whole city from the inside."

Changli did not reach for the cup, or the pot, or the board.

"I can tell you the short side," she said. "That is the one I know. I am the woodcutter."

"You are what?"

"There is a play. Master Qu is taking it up the mountain and complaining about it." Changli's mouth moved. "A man watches two old men at a board and comes down to find a century gone and everybody he loved under the hill. He is the figure everybody weeps at, and the Troupe has never given him a line."

"And you are him."

"I am him going up," said Changli. "I stood in front of you when you were eight years old with a book you were too small for and I said I would help you get what you wanted, and I have been on that mountain for eleven years, and it has been the best of my life, and in a decade or so I am going to have to go down it." She turned her left hand over on her knee. "And you are going to stay. And whatever the number is, it is going to be larger than mine, and neither of us is permitted to be sorry about that in front of the other, and I am tired of the rule and I am proposing that we throw it out tonight."

Jinhsi's eyes had spilled over. She did not do anything about it.

"I have nobody to ask," she said. "That is the whole of it. Everybody who could tell me is either four hundred years old and cannot remember, or is a dragon on a mountain who has spent his whole life arranging a good handover and cannot picture the afternoon afterward, and I asked him what he would do with one and it took him a full minute to think of the sea."

"Then here is a Counselor's answer, and it is the only kind I have."

"Give me a Counselor's answer. I would like one very much."

"Learn the work now," said Changli. "Not the grief. The work. There is a body of practice for being the one who stays, and nobody writes it down because it is embarrassing and domestic, and I have done all of it badly, and I can teach you every one of my errors in about a decade if you will let me use them."

"Name one."

"Keep the small things. Not the seals and the appointment letters. The pear stones, the bad handwriting, the argument about the ventilation, the diagram you were too shy to ask about." Changli's voice had gone rough. "I have thirty years of a man and what I kept was a book, because the book was important, and I have spent this week discovering that the only page in it I would not sell is eleven moves he lost patience with in a shop."

Jinhsi wiped her face with the back of her wrist.

"What else?"

"Say the number out loud to people while they are alive," said Changli. "And when you cannot bear to, say that instead, which is also a number."

## VIII. 收官 — THE ENDGAME

They finished the count on the eighth day, in the afternoon, with the shutters half over and the lamps unlit and Mrs Ren's front room hot enough that Chixia had her cap off.

The corner came off the board in eleven minutes, which after nineteen years seemed insulting.

Sanhua resolved it both ways under Jinhsi's direction: first with Black walking away, then with White. Amy took the count each time. Chisa counted the second one independently, out loud, by touch, moving her hand across the ruled sheet in rows and saying the running total at the end of each row, and got the same figures as Amy to within one point, and the one point was Amy's.

"Eleven both ways," said Amy, at the end. "Same as the first count. Half a point in it on the edge, and I'm calling the half for White, because the record has him filling there twice and I'd like to believe he had a reason."

"Then the corner is where we left it, and we have never done the rest." Changli had come round to stand at Amy's shoulder. "Count the whole board. Both resolutions, all four quarters, out loud. I have wanted somebody to do that since I was twenty-two and I have never once had thirteen people and a table."

It took forty minutes.

At the end of it Amy stopped with her pencil above the column and did not say anything.

"Do it again," said Jinhsi.

Amy did it again.

"Black wins," she said. "Either way."

The room heard the shape of it before it had the words.

"Say the margin."

"If Black gives up the corner, Black wins by twenty-seven. If White gives up the corner, Black wins by thirty-eight." Amy put the pencil down. "It isn't close. It stopped being close a long time before he died."

Changli had both hands on the table.

"How long before."

"That's the bit I've been chasing for an hour and I've got it." Amy turned two pages of her own working round. "Move two hundred and sixty-eight. Up until then the corner's the game — whoever loses it loses. After two hundred and sixty-eight, White's group on the upper side is settled and Black's centre has joined up, and from that point the corner is worth eleven points and the game is worth thirty-odd, and the corner never matters again. Never. Not for one move."

"And the record runs to?"

"Three hundred and thirty-one on the big board."

"Sixty-three moves," said Jinhsi.

"Sixty-three moves after it stopped mattering." Amy looked up. "And on the dates, two hundred and sixty-eight is about nine years before he died. So: nine years. He went round that corner for nine more years in a game he was winning by twenty-seven, and all he had to do at any point in those nine years was play somewhere else and take the eleven."

Nobody said anything.

Out in the market a woman was calling the end-of-day price on something and getting a reply from two streets away.

"Which one was he?" said Cartethyia.

"Black," said Changli. "He was always Black. He said it was because he liked going first and because he had no patience, and both of those were true and neither of them was the reason."

"So he *won*."

"He won by twenty-seven points, which is a very great deal," said Changli. "And he did not know, because he never looked up from the corner, and there was nobody at his elbow to tell him, because he was a Second Secretary of the Board of Rites who did not care to be explained to."

She sat down on the bench with rather less grace than she usually manages.

"That is the ultimate truth in the unfinished game," she said. "Thirty years. There it is. A man who could not afford to lose eleven points, in a game he had already won, for nine years, and then died in a corridor."

Ciaccona, from the end of the bench, said, "That's a good joke."

"It is a magnificent joke and I would like to hit him with the book."

"No, I mean it's a *good* one. It's got a proper turn in it." Ciaccona had her chin on her hand. "He'd have loved it. Everybody who reads that record spends thirty years asking who he was playing, which is the wrong question, and the punchline is that it never mattered who, because he wasn't losing to them. He was losing to the corner."

"He was not losing at all."

"He was losing nine years," said Chisa.

***

They put it away.

Sanhua took the stones off the big board in handfuls and sorted them back into their bowls by colour, and Lupa did the paper board, and Cartethyia was allowed to do the last row because she asked.

Changli wrote at the table with the record open.

She did it in her own ink, in her own hand, on the last written page, beneath his final entry, dated, in the format the Board of Rites uses for an entry made out of time:

*Move 332. Black plays elsewhere. The lower right corner is given up, eleven points.*

*Resolved on the eighth day of the seventh month, by a table of thirteen persons at Mrs Ren's guesthouse, east market, ward four, Jinzhou.*

*Result: Black wins by twenty-seven points.*

*Entered by Changli, Counselor to the Magistrate of Jinzhou, thirty years and four months after the last move, being the student of the recorder and holding his book.*

She blotted it. Then she sat and looked at it for a while.

"You could have resigned it," said Amy, from across the table. "I thought you were going to. I had that in my head all week."

"So did I. I had the sentence." Changli capped the ink. "And then I sat down to write it and discovered that resigning is what he would have wanted, because resigning is a kind of tidiness, and it lets a person leave a board with their reputation on. He did not deserve to be tidy. He deserved to have somebody come along thirty years later, take the eleven points, and hand him the win in front of witnesses while calling him an idiot."

"That's affection," said Iuno.

"That is the whole of my religion, Registrar."

Jinhsi reached over and turned the record so she could read the entry.

"You have put thirteen persons," she said.

"There were thirteen in the room."

"There were twelve people and a cat."

"There were thirteen persons," said Changli, "and one of them ate a stone on the second night and returned it, and I am not going back to alter a public entry."

Abby raised his head off the windowsill.

"I returned it *clean*," he said, with enormous dignity. "I washed it in the bowl. Nobody thanked me."

"Thank you, Abby."

"You are welcome, SPARK LADY."

***

At the door, when the rest of them had gone down to see whether Panhua had any of the crispy thing left at that hour, Changli put her hand on Lupa's arm.

Lupa stopped. She did not ask. She stood in the doorway with her ears half back and waited, which is a thing she does, and which fewer people notice than ought to.

"I need something from you," Changli said.

"Figured."

"I have a physician's figures. I will give you the paper; you may have a copy and I would rather you had the original, because I will read the original at two in the morning and you will not." Changli's voice was quite steady and her hand was not. "There is a clause I wrote in a field for your household. I cannot write myself into it. The office cannot bind the person and the person is the problem."

"Say what you want. Plain."

"If I am ever going to spend myself again — a full extension, or the slow version, which is a schedule with no evenings in it for a season — I want somebody standing in front of me who says the words out loud while I still have a mouth to answer with. Not to stop me. I may still do it. I want it said." She made herself finish. "And I want it to be somebody I have no way of managing."

Lupa considered her.

"You've got a magistrate."

"I have a magistrate who was nine years old in a room with me and who will be standing in front of me with her whole face, and I will do what I have always done, which is decide what she can bear and then hand her the version I have decided on." Changli's jaw was tight. "You I cannot do anything with. I have tried. I attempted it in a field with tea and an argument and you took the argument and told me you would be insulted later on your own time."

"Yeah, that was good. I've told people about that." Lupa scratched the back of her neck. "Terms."

"Name them."

"Three." Lupa held up a hand and used it. "One: I get the physician, direct. Not through you, not through your clerk. I want to be able to walk into her room and ask and get the answer without you hearing about it first.

"Two: when I say it to your face, you hear the whole thing standing still. No papers in your hand, no doorway, no walking me out. You stand there till I'm finished and then you can do whatever you want.

"Three: I'll do it in front of somebody. Not in a corridor, not in a nice quiet room where you can be gracious about it. Somebody else in the room, every time. You're better at that than you think and worse at it than you'd like."

Changli let out a breath.

"That third one is going to be unbearable."

"Yeah. That's why it's in there." Lupa's ears came forward. "And a fourth, free: you tell the magistrate as well. Same day, her own face, out loud. I'm the extra one."

"Agreed. All four."

"Then we're done. Wen's on the stairs, he's got a bowl, he'll be delighted." Lupa started down and then stopped two steps below and looked back up, and said it flatly, in the weather voice, without any weight on it at all: "For the record, I don't think you're going to need me much. You've been out of practice at asking for about forty years and you've asked four people in eight days. That's not somebody heading for a cliff. That's somebody who's noticed the road."

"You are attempting to be kind."

"I'm attempting to be accurate. Kind's Baizhi's job." Lupa went down two more steps. "Come on. Panhua shuts the fryer at ten and I've got an arrangement with her about the oil."

Wen entered it in the day-book that night, under the day, out of sequence, and marked it *out of sequence* so the auditor would be spared a hunt: the terms, verbatim, then Changli's name, then Lupa's. It took him three minutes. His brush caught twice.

Changli signed it. Then she stood there for a moment with the pen still in her hand and read her own name at the bottom of a clause designed to protect her from herself, and Wen, who has held that job for a while now and has learned some things, turned the book round, blotted it, and went and got her a chair.

## IX. 天元 — THE CENTRE POINT

The persimmons cost less than she expected, which annoyed her in a way she recognised as entirely her own.

The woman at the corner stall had known Changli's face for two months and had never once had anything to sell her, and when Changli stopped and asked for six she wrapped them in a cone of newspaper, and then, without being asked, took the top one out again and swapped it for a better one from the back, and said something about the second week being when they come right.

"How long do they keep?"

"Four days on a shelf. Two if there's a wolf in the house." The woman tucked the cone. "You're the Counselor."

"I am."

"My brother-in-law had a well argued about for eleven months and it went his way and he has been insufferable since the fourth month." She handed the cone over. "I've been meaning to say. He was in the wrong."

Changli went down the lane with the persimmons under her arm, past the lantern seller on the corner, who had his oil out and the day's last lamp on the edge of the table.

She had watched him do this on three evenings and had never spoken to him. He set the lamp down at the edge, and walked south, and the flame burned until it burned through, and in the morning he came back and trimmed the wick and poured fresh oil and the lamp was ready.

She stopped.

"Does it go out on its own?"

He looked up. He had a rag over one shoulder and the guarded pleasantness of a man who is asked about his stock four hundred times a season.

"Burns through about the third hour. Wick's cheap." He tipped the can. "Trim it in the morning, fill it, it goes again. Done it for nine years."

"Have you ever taken it in at night?"

"Tried it the first winter. Nobody came down the lane." He set the can under the table. "You leave it out, people know the corner's here. Costs me a thumb of oil."

"What is your name?"

He told her.

She did not write it down. She stood there for a second longer working out what to do with her hands, which was a new problem, and then she said thank you and went up the guesthouse stairs with six persimmons and a book.

***

The household was eating on the landing and the stairs and in the front room because Mrs Ren's front room had been full of a dead man's game for eight days and the furniture had not gone back.

Mei had brought the duck down.

It stood on the table on its green box with the three frogs in front of it, and it had already performed twice, and Chixia had formed a strong attachment to the smallest frog and had begun defending its professional reputation.

"It's not sulking. It's *waiting*. There's a difference and you can hear it in the croak."

"It is sulking," said Cartethyia. "Look at its face."

"It has one face. It has had one face since it was painted."

"Then it was painted sulking."

Changli put the persimmons on the table and they were gone in ninety seconds, which is what happens.

She took the record and the spoiled sheet with the nine-line board on it and the eleven stones still in their places, because nobody had touched them since the third night, and she went and sat on the step outside the door.

After a while Jinhsi came out and sat down beside her, one step up, with a bowl.

"They are arguing about the frog," she said.

"I heard. Is anybody winning?"

"Chixia has produced an argument from the mechanism, which she got from Mei, who got it from the shopkeeper, and which she is delivering as though she had taken it apart herself." Jinhsi settled the bowl on her knee. "It is a very good argument and she has no idea what any of the words mean and the room is losing."

Changli laid the sheet across her lap.

Eleven stones on nine lines. Black in the middle, then the chase, and White giving ground three times, and then the small quiet move at the bottom that took the bottom, and then Black's move eleven, a long way from anything, the move of a person who has just understood.

"You are going to finish it," said Jinhsi.

"I am going to play one move."

"That is finishing it, for you."

Changli turned the sheet a little so the lamp from the doorway fell across it.

"He beat me by about forty points on eighty-one intersections," she said, "which is the equivalent of beating a child at running, and he wrote it down and kept it for thirty years in the same book as the thing he thought would outlive him."

"What are you going to play?"

"The middle."

Jinhsi stopped with the bowl halfway up.

"On nineteen lines," Changli said, "the middle is romance. You told me that in a wrecked room on the fourth level of the Library to a woman sitting on the floor. You cannot make territory in the middle, you cannot run to a side from it, and a beginner plays it because it is beautiful and loses."

"I did say that."

"You did, and you were right, and I have been playing on nineteen lines since I was ten." Changli took a stone out of the cup she had brought up with her. "This is a nine-line board. On nine lines the middle is the whole thing. Everything touches it. There is nowhere on this board that is far away."

She put her fingers on the stone the way he had taught her, index over middle, and set it down on the centre point, and it made a small flat sound against the paper and the table beneath it.

Move twelve.

The two of them looked at it.

"That is a good move," said Jinhsi.

"It is an adequate move. White will answer on the left and I will be a little better off than I was."

"It is a good move and you know it is, because you have spent a week telling this household that nobody ever says what a corner is worth, and you have just put a stone in the middle of the smallest board in the house." Jinhsi put her bowl down on the step. "May I say the thing I have been carrying since the committee room? You will want to arrange it and there is no arranging it."

"Say it."

"I do not want the decade," said Jinhsi. "I want the part afterward. The part Baizhi says people find intolerable. I want you sitting down while other people do things badly, in a chair, in a room I can get to, being extremely rude about the drainage." Her voice had gone unsteady and she carried straight on. "And I am aware that I am asking you for the worst years, and I am asking for them anyway, and I would like that entered."

Changli did not answer for a while.

Down the lane the lantern seller's lamp was going in the dark, alone, at the corner where the ward changes.

"Entered," she said.

***

Amy put her head round the doorframe.

"Are you two going to eat, or are you going to sit on a step being profound while there's fish."

"We are being profound," said Jinhsi. "We are nearly finished."

"You're on the step where Auntie was profound at the Counselor a fortnight ago, and before that Lupa was profound at Auntie on it, and at this rate Mrs Ren is going to start charging for it like the lamp oil." Amy jerked her head back at the room. "Four minutes. Then it's cold and I'm not reheating it for a magistrate."

She went in.

Changli took out her pen.

She opened the record at the back first, where the great sheaf of blank pages stood sewn in behind the last written leaf. She had paid a binder in the fourth month to put them there, and she had specified the number, and she had stood in his shop and told him it was for a continuation, and had gone home pleased with herself.

Forty pages. Enough for another thirty-one years.

She turned them through under her thumb, all of them, one by one, and they stayed as they were, and she found that the sight of them had stopped frightening her at some point in the last week.

"Those will end up with shopping on them," she said.

"They will," said Jinhsi. "Somebody will use the third one for a well."

Then she turned forward to the page with the eleven moves on it, and his line under them in the hard small brown hand that pressed too much on the down-strokes, thirty-one years old.

*Unfinished. She had to go and open the shop.*

She wrote beneath it, in her own ink, and did not rule a line first:

*Move twelve, at the centre. The eighth day of the seventh month, on a step in the east market, thirty-one years later.*

*Unfinished. She had to go in to supper.*

She looked at it for a moment.

Then she closed the book, and put the sheet with the twelve stones on it on top, and weighted the corner with the cup so the evening would not take it, and got up off the step.

Inside, somebody had wound the duck again. The bells started. The baton went up. Chixia was already explaining the mechanism to Wen, who had been given the good chair and a bowl and had stopped protesting about both, and Lupa was holding the pot at the door to see who still wanted any, and Mei was sitting on the floor with her back against the bench and Abby asleep across her feet.

The two frogs played their part.

The smallest one waited until they had finished. Then it opened its mouth and delivered its opinion, and the whole room made a noise, and the duck took a bow, and went up on its toes.

# Local Time

The frost on the long table had retreated an inch around the bottom of the porridge pot.

Mei sat with her back to the storehouse wall and her right boot hooked around the bench leg so the cold off the flagstones wouldn't creep up her shins. Above them, Wen's kitchen shutter stood propped open on its stick, letting out a steady drift of ginger steam and woodsmoke.

Down the terrace steps came Warden Liangyu with the morning dispatch bag slung flat against her hip. Everyone at the table looked up at the bag out of habit.

Liangyu stopped by the gate place at the end of the board, reached into the bag, and brought out an apple. She bit into it, nodded to Iuno, took a scallion roll off the tray Wen held out the window, and walked on down toward the well without leaving a single piece of paper behind.

Lupa watched her go, a chopstick held suspended over the pickle dish.

“Did she miss us?”

“The reliefs changed at dawn,” Yangyang said from across the board. “There's nothing on the clip.”

Mei turned her head to look at Iuno.

Iuno's small ruled pad lay beside her saucer. The page was white from top to bottom. Her pencil rested in the groove down the spine, unsharpened since yesterday afternoon.

“I have no official calls,” Iuno said.

Nobody spoke. At the far end of the bench, Ciaccona lifted her teacup with her three good fingers, her splinted left forefinger sticking straight out toward the mountains, and looked over the rim at Iuno's pad.

“None,” Ciaccona said.

“The yamen has the lists,” Iuno said, her voice altogether even. “The garrison quartermaster has the receipts. The road crews have their own foremen. If anybody in Jinzhou requires my opinion before noon, they are mistaken.”

“You've got the pencil out,” Amy pointed out around a mouthful of fried egg. She had traded the crisp lacy edge of hers onto Chisa's plate in return for Chisa's yolk, and was mopping the yellow up with a corner of flatbread.

“The pencil lives with the pad,” Iuno said. “Wait until you see how little I do with it.”

“I'll give you till the mid-morning bell, princess,” Lupa said, pushing the chili crock toward Mei with her knuckle. “Then you'll start reorganising the salt jars.”

Mei laughed, felt her side pull in the cold air, and pressed the heel of her palm lazily against her coat over the closed scar while she reached for the spoons with the other hand. “What time is it everywhere else?”

Amy tapped her Terminal where it lay beside the soy cruet. “Mid-morning at Startorch. They'll be out of first lecture in twenty minutes. Ragunna's still dark unless somebody's up with the bakers, and Septimont's barely got the kettle on.”

“I am calling Uncle Cosimo after lunch,” Cartethyia announced, straightening the crystal laurel on her head so it tilted over her left eyebrow instead of her right. “Wait—Cia, you'll come too, won't you?”

“At the first afternoon bell I am a working musician,” Ciaccona said, inspecting her splint. “The man who owns the Golden Reed down on the second terrace has offered me silver coin and all the jasmine tea I can survive to play two sets by his brazier. I'm going to see whether three fingers are enough for a pastoral.”

Suisui was sitting with her back straight, working her horn comb through the lower length of her hair before pinning it up. On the rim of the porridge tureen, Dumpling gave a small clockwork whirr and pecked twice at a grain of rice stuck to the porcelain.

“Ba will be finishing his turn round the pond about now,” Suisui said to Yangyang without looking round. “If we wait another half-hour, Ma will have taken the ledger away from the steward and sat down with her tea.”

Yangyang nodded once.

Her own Terminal lay face up on the scarred pine two inches from her cuff. Every other Terminal down the table was out too—Amy's propped against the vinegar bottle, Chisa's laid neat and dark beside her chopsticks, Lupa's shoved half under a napkin where the grease wouldn't reach it.

“Jinzhou's on the same sun we are,” Mei said softly, nudging Yangyang's knee under the table with her boot.

“Chixia had an appointment with Baizhi at the sixth bell,” Yangyang said. “For the leg.”

“So she's either being lectured,” Amy said, “or—”

Yangyang's hand went out toward the glass. Before her fingertip touched the call key, the screen blazed bright white and the little speaker let out a shrill, impatient ring that rattled the spoon in her saucer.

***

Yangyang carried the Terminal down three steps to the low parapet wall where the sun came clear through the gap in the eastern spires.

She pressed the green key before she sat down.

“—LOOK!”

The screen showed nothing but grey flagstones blurring past at speed, a flash of red rubber boot-sole, a puddle jumping sideways, and then a wild swing up into blue sky and the red brim of a patrol cap worn backwards.

“Yangyang! Are you looking? Look down!”

The camera pitched toward the ground again. Two boots in heavy patrol socks were hitting the harbour wall pavers at a flat-out jog—left, right, left, right—with no third point between them and no rubber squeak following behind.

“You're running,” Yangyang said.

“I'm not running!” Chixia shouted over the slap of her soles and a stiff wind coming off the water. The camera bounced back up to her face, flushed bright pink to the roots of her hair, grinning so wide her eyes were crescents. “This is brisk! The paper says *brisk walking on even surfaces*! The harbour wall's the flattest thing in the ward!”

“Your feet are both off the ground at the same time.”

“That's momentum!”

Behind Chixia's head, the bamboo awnings of the wet-fish market went bobbing past in a streak of blue canvas and hanging scales. A man gutting mackerel at a tub looked up, saw the red blur, and bellowed, “Chixia!” by way of a polite good morning.

“Morning, Old Ma!” Chixia yelled back without breaking stride, swinging round the corner onto the canal towpath. “Tell your brother the permit's at the desk!”

Yangyang braced her elbow on her knee to steady her end of the picture. “Where is the crutch?”

“Behind Mrs Ren's umbrella stand! She tried to make me take it to the door and I left it while she was salting the turnips!” Chixia panted once, loud in the microphone, and angled the screen out over the water so the wind wouldn't hit the speaker directly. “Look. The canal's up since the rain.”

On the screen, the green water of the Jinzhou canal slid by under the stone embankment, and ahead of Chixia's bobbing shoulder rose the high, humped arch of the bird-bridge. Three grey herons stood in a row along the middle of the parapet, hunched into their necks against the river breeze.

Behind Yangyang on the terrace, footsteps on the frost slowed and stopped.

Mei stood there with two stacked porridge bowls in one hand and the empty pickle dish in the other, looking down over Yangyang's shoulder at the bridge on the glass.

Chixia caught the shadow of Mei's head at the top of Yangyang's frame.

“Hey, Mei!” Chixia hollered, the camera jerking as she pointed up the path toward the arch. “See the fat one in the middle? He just took a whole loach off the noodle boat while the boy was poling! If you two don't hurry up and get back here for your walk, there won't be a fish left in the ward!”

Mei's mouth curved. She rested two fingers briefly against the cold stone of the parapet behind Yangyang's hip.

“Tell him to leave the shrimp alone,” Mei called down to the screen. “We're coming as soon as we can.”

She carried the bowls on toward Wen's window. Yangyang's collar lifted a little along her jaw in the sun, the brown scorched tips at the front catching the light beside the new short sheaths of blue.

On the screen, the bird-bridge fell behind, and the path tilted upward toward the willow avenue of Huaxu Academy. Chixia's breathing had acquired a sharp little hitch at the top of every second breath. The lower edge of the picture dipped as her left hand came up and pressed hard against her jacket, just above the hip.

“Stop on the wall,” Yangyang said.

“I'm at the gate!” Chixia wheezed. “Look, there's the porter's box—morning, Uncle!—I'm already here, there's no point stopping when I'm already here!”

“You're holding your side.”

“I'm holding my pocket! My keys are jingling!”

She took the broad stone steps of the Academy entrance at a lurching, stubborn trot, her boots scuffing loud on the risers, shoved through the double swing-doors of the south wing with her shoulder, and went straight down the tiled corridor without slowing for the corners.

“Chixia,” Yangyang said. “Knock.”

Chixia hit the third door on the left with the flat of her palm and pushed it open in the same motion.

“Baizhi! Look who's on the—”

The picture swung wildly across a chart of acoustic frequencies, a willow branch outside a clean pane, and settled on a desk squared to the floorboards.

Baizhi sat behind it with a glass vial in her left hand and a pen in her right. The saucer sat on top of her teacup. For two seconds she looked at Chixia standing in the doorway, then at Chixia's boots, then at the flush across Chixia's cheekbones.

“You ran from the canal,” Baizhi said.

“I walked briskly!” Chixia gasped, leaning one shoulder against the doorframe while she held the Terminal out between them. “You wrote *brisk* on the discharge slip! I read it!”

“Brisk walking does not produce an audible expiratory wheeze at four metres, nor does it bring a patient up the south steps with her hand over the sixth suture.” Baizhi set the pen down parallel to the spine of her ledger, rose from her chair, and walked round the desk. She took the Terminal cleanly out of Chixia's hand. “Sit down before you pull the fascia.”

“I'm fine, I didn't pull anything—ow, okay, sitting.”

The camera tilted as Baizhi stepped back, showing Chixia dropping her hip squarely onto the front right corner of Baizhi's polished desk, knocking the parallel pencil an inch out of line. Chixia braced both hands on her knees and blew her bangs out of her eyes with a huge, triumphant puff of air.

Baizhi looked down at her desk corner. Her fingers tightened once on the casing of the Terminal; then she turned the lens toward herself and held it steady at chest height.

“Outrider,” Baizhi said.

“Morning, Baizhi.”

“Hold the camera four inches lower and turn toward the sun.”

Yangyang lowered the Terminal to her breastbone. At the front of her collar, where the burning stone had singed the feathers brown, seven or eight tiny cerulean pin-feathers had pushed through the ash-coloured stubs.

Baizhi studied them through the glass without speaking.

“Does the new growth itch?” she asked.

“In the morning,” Yangyang said. “When the air is dry.”

“Do not scratch the sheaths. If the skin tightens, apply plain boiled water on a clean cloth. No ointment.” Baizhi's eyes rose to Yangyang's face. “Are you finishing your meals?”

“Lupa watches the bowls.”

“Good. Have you a date for your return to Jinzhou?”

“Not yet,” Yangyang said. “We don't know yet.”

Baizhi nodded once, a very small movement. Behind her elbow, on the back left corner of the desk, the thick black incident ledger lay closed and squared to the wood; the edge of a folded white sheet showed flush against the pages near the middle. Baizhi's eyes went to it and came back.

On the front corner of the desk, Chixia had got most of her wind back. She leaned sideways into the edge of the frame until her red hair brushed the shoulder of Baizhi's white coat, peering at the screen.

“Tell Suisui I saw her grain carts on the news-sheet!” Chixia said, her voice down to half-roar now that she was sitting. “And tell Wen that Mrs Ren tried her peppercorn brine on Tuesday and the whole courtyard smelled like Xuanfang for two days!”

“You are leaning on my arm,” Baizhi told her, without shifting her shoulder away.

“I'm sharing the screen. You've got narrow lenses in here.” Chixia hooked one heel over the rung of the visitor's chair she wasn't sitting in, her boot swinging an inch from the desk drawer. “Call me tomorrow, Yangyang. I'm walking the north market tomorrow. Slowly.”

“At a walk,” Baizhi corrected, her hand reaching out to set the displaced pencil straight again right beside Chixia's thigh, leaving Chixia where she was.

***

Twenty minutes later Suisui came out of the storehouse with her hair pinned up to her satisfaction and her own Terminal already open.

She sat down on the sunny bench beside the door and patted the plank at her left. Yangyang came up from the parapet and sat hip to hip with her, tucking her sheer sleeve in so it wouldn't catch on Suisui's fan.

Suisui propped the Terminal across one knee from each of them.

The call connected on the first ring, to the sound of bamboo bristles scraping stone and a mynah bird whistling two notes of an opera aria from an unseen cage.

On the screen appeared the south veranda of the house in Mingting. A young manservant's grey cuff lingered at the corner of the lens, adjusting the brass stand on the low table, and then withdrew. Behind the railing, old Lao Zhou was sweeping dry lotus leaves along the edge of the pond toward his basket.

Their mother sat on the left in the straight-backed rosewood chair, a porcelain cup in her hand and the household account book closed on her lap. Their father sat on the right, leaning so far forward over his knees that his beard nearly brushed the screen.

For three seconds nobody in Mingting said a word.

Ma's cup stopped halfway to her saucer. Ba squinted, leaned an inch closer, and then sat all the way back in his chair, slapping both palms down on the carved armrests.

“Little Two!” Ba boomed, pitching his voice as if he were calling across the canal during a boat race. “Turn your head! Let your father see! Look at the colour of that—I had the clerk pull the third volume of the *Imperial Avian Compendium* from the south library yesterday morning, and the woodcut has the throat feathers stopping at the collarbone! Wait till I write to the archivist! Tell me directly, does the plumage undergo a grey winter moult when the frost sets in, or is the blue fixed in the barb?”

Suisui came in right under him, dropping her voice half an octave so the microphone stopped rattling. “Ba, good morning, you're shouting into the brass horn again, and she isn't a woodcut in the south library.”

“I am speaking clearly for the mountain relay!” Ba protested, though he came down two notches at once.

Ma set her cup in its saucer without a click. Her eyes went from the blue mark on Yangyang's forehead—identical to the one between Suisui's brows—down through the blue-and-white length of her hair to the cerulean feathers resting against her throat.

“Do they get wet when it rains?” Ma asked.

“It rolls off,” Yangyang said, tilting the right edge of the Terminal half an inch toward herself so her face was in the middle. “Like a duck's back.”

“Not a duck,” Ba objected. “A Xuanling! The third volume specifically distinguishes water-fowl oil from—”

“When you wash your hair,” Ma went on seamlessly over him, “does the soap dry them out? I asked your Auntie Shen, and she said her cousin's hunting falcon had to be misted with warm water from a brass sprayer in the winter or it plucked its own chest.”

“Plain water is fine,” Yangyang said. “Baizhi said no ointment.”

“And no horn comb,” Suisui added, nudging the left side of the Terminal back toward her own knee. “I offered her mine on the salt-house kang and she nearly took my wrist off.”

“You started at the top,” Yangyang said, nudging the screen back to the centre. “You don't comb feathers from the top.”

“I was neatening the side where you slept on your ear!” Suisui tipped the screen five degrees her way again and leaned in toward their parents. “Ma, since she won't tell you until next month: she was lying to us by omission on the first page of that letter. When we were at the shooting stall in the plaza, the Xuan Paragon herself walked up in front of six people and called her *Disciple*.”

Yangyang's collar rose straight up to her ears. “It was on the second page.”

“A Sword Transcendent!” Ba started up again at festival volume, half-rising from his rosewood chair. “Of the old Xuanfang line! I told your grandfather when you were eight years old and picked the wooden broadsword off the carpet instead of the abacus—”

“Does this master have a kitchen?” Ma asked Yangyang. “Or is she one of those mountain immortals who lives on pine needles and cold water?”

“She ate three bowls of fish soup on the second terrace,” Yangyang said, her ears bright pink under her blue hair. “And she has a toothache. Ruoyun looks after the bills.”

“Good,” said Ma. “Nobody learns swordwork properly on an empty stomach. Suisui, what are you doing about the Ministry of War?”

Suisui touched her closed fan to her chin. “I have sent four pages of inquiries to the Registry Desk regarding the ink used on her Watcher transfer, and I am waiting for the third clerk to realize the Zhaoming Guild holds the shipping bond on their winter coal.”

“Give me his name,” Ba said immediately. “His uncle sits on the Canal Board with Old Fang. I'll go down to the teahouse before lunch and—”

“Ba, if you go near the teahouse, he'll claim it's a family favour instead of a procedural error,” Suisui said firmly. “Stay by the pond. Let me ruin his Thursday properly.”

Ma smoothed the blue cloth cover of her account book with her palm. “I have told Cook to hang four strips of cured pork in the smoke-shed and order the double-sifted glutinous flour from the south mill. You will both be through the front gate by the twenty-eighth of the twelfth month, and if that bird in your hair, Suisui, drops oil on the good cushions again, it sleeps in the hall.”

Dumpling gave an offended click from Suisui's shoulder.

Then Ma looked straight at Yangyang, her hands coming to rest one over the other on the ledger.

“And the other child,” Ma said, her voice dropping into the register she used when someone had come in late from the rain. “In the other house. Is she warm enough?”

Yangyang looked at her mother's hands on the screen.

“Her Suisui bought two thermometers because she didn't trust the first one,” Yangyang said. “And Chixia sent a red patrol cap lined with rabbit fur up the gorge for me to take her at the solstice.”

Ma nodded once, slowly. “Tell her other sister to put a ginger peel in the foot-brazier. It keeps the damp out of the ankles.”

Behind the veranda rail, there was a sudden crash of terracotta and a frantic fluttering of wings. Lao Zhou yelled; the family's fat yellow spaniel bolted across the flagstones behind their chairs with Ba's best felt garden slipper clamped in its jaws and a crushed chrysanthemum stem trailing from its collar.

“Drop that!” Ba roared, lunging out of his chair and vanishing off the right side of the screen. “Lao Zhou, head him off by the moon-gate! That's the lambskin lining!”

Ma didn't even turn her head toward the commotion. She picked up her teacup, blew across the top, and looked back at her daughters.

“Button your collars when the sun goes behind the ridge,” she said, and reached out to tap the red key before Ba could come back shouting.

***

At the fifth bell Amy commandeered the middle of the long table, propped her Terminal against the big tin tea caddy, and switched the lens to wide.

“Move your horn, Carty, you're stabbing Cia's eyebrow in the preview,” Amy said, backing onto the bench between Mei and Chisa. “Lupa, stop breathing on the microphone.”

“I'm standing behind you,” Lupa said, leaning both forearms on the back of Amy's shoulders so her chin was level with the top of Amy's head. “How else am I supposed to see?”

In Lahai-Roi, the call connected to the clatter of a tin lid hitting a floorboards and Rebecca's voice swearing at it before the picture cleared.

The attic kitchen at the Startorch dormitory came into focus, bright with morning Helios light slanting through the dormer window. Rebecca stood by the two-ring stove in an oversized black vest with a spatula in her chrome left hand and a smudge of dried batter on her jaw. Lynae sat backward on a wooden chair at the kitchen table, her arms folded along the top rail and an enormous, dog-eared *Applied Materials & Structural Loads* binder open in front of her.

“Look what finally showed up in the pigeonhole,” Rebecca said by way of greeting, and shoved a postcard right up against the camera lens until it blurred.

It was a garishly coloured woodblock print of Xuanfang Hold's sky-rings that Cartethyia had bought from a barrow by the south gate, with three extra clouds drawn in purple crayon. Rebecca flipped it over with her metal thumb to show the back, where seven people had crammed their handwriting sideways into the margins around the stamp.

“Wait,” Cartethyia said eagerly, leaning past Ciaccona's lute case. “Did the pressed radish flower stay stuck to the corner? I used rice paste!”

“It fell off in the envelope and I thought it was a moth,” Rebecca said. “And who let the wolf write the bottom bit? It just says *SALT FIRST, THEN OIL* in letters an inch high.”

“Because Lynae said you tore the skin off the last bream!” Lupa shouted at the screen. “If you don't salt the pan before the oil smokes, the belly sticks every time!”

“The pan was warped! I bought a new pan!” Rebecca pointed the spatula at Ciaccona. “And your lute hates me, bard. I played the four notes for the woman downstairs and her cat left the room.”

“Cats are critics, darling,” Ciaccona called, lifting her splinted finger lazily in greeting. “Play it for a dog next time. They're kinder.”

“The cat lives with you,” Chisa told Rebecca calmly. “It will come back for the fish.”

Lynae lifted a sheet of ruled examination paper off her binder and held it up beside her cheek. At the top right corner, in thick red marker, was a circled **91**.

“Mock paper two,” Lynae said, trying and failing to keep a grin off her face. “The cantilever truss question on page four tried to murder me, and I murdered it back.”

“Let me see the truss diagram!” Amy yelled, standing up on the bench-rail to squint at the small print. As she leaned in, her eyes flicked for half a beat past Lynae's elbow to the shadowed gap under the bedframe in the bedroom doorway, where the canvas strap of Lynae's packed travel bag still lay tucked against the skirting board; then Amy's gaze snapped back to the red ink. “Wait, did you use the three-pin shortcut I showed you or the textbook way?”

“Textbook way on the sheet, your way in the margin so the marker couldn't dock me for skipping steps,” Lynae said proudly.

Behind them, the dormitory stair door banged open against the wall.

“Becca, the postman had two boxes from home and my mother packed the whole smoked—”

Sigrika squeezed sideways into the narrow kitchen with a brown-paper parcel tied in hairy twine under one arm, a jar of dark red berry jam in her hand, and half a round of rye flatbread already clamped between her teeth.

She turned toward the table, saw nine faces crowded into the Terminal screen, and saw Yangyang sitting on the bench end between Mei and Suisui.

Sigrika stopped dead.

She took the flatbread out of her mouth. Slowly, without taking her eyes off the screen, she set the jam jar down on Lynae's truss calculations, reached into her canvas shoulder bag, and pulled out a thick black-covered notebook with three pens aligned flush to the edge and a brass tally-clicker on a cord.

She shoved her face in between Lynae's shoulder and Rebecca's chrome elbow until her hair brushed the desk lamp.

“Hello,” Sigrika said, very fast and extremely polite. “Excuse me. Are those contour feathers around the cervical tract or modified semiplumes? When you turn your head—could you turn your head slightly to the left? Thank you—do the interlocking barbules hold all the way to the vane tip, or do they fray like an owl's for silent flight?”

Yangyang blinked once. The cerulean feathers along her collarbones lifted a fraction of an inch.

“They hold to the tip,” Yangyang said. “Except the small ones under the chin.”

Sigrika uncapped her red pen with her teeth and wrote across the top margin of an open page without looking down at the paper. “And the waterproof oil—do you have an active preen gland, or is the hydrophobic property structural in the keratin? When the wind drops before a squall in the gorges, do you feel the pressure change in the hollow of the shaft before your inner ear registers it?”

“Siggy, breathe,” Lynae said, rescuing her exam paper from under the jam jar. “Say hi to Mei first.”

“Hello, Mei! I brought flatbread!” Sigrika stuffed another huge bite of rye bread into her cheek and kept her pen poised over the page, looking straight at Yangyang with shining eyes. “Is the call two-toned? The northern gorge records have a high descending whistle with a catch on the fourth beat, like *kree-eh-ul*, only higher.”

“Yes,” Yangyang said, the corner of her mouth lifting. “And I feel the rain in the collar about ten minutes before the clouds come over the ridge. They stand up when the air gets heavy.”

“Ten minutes,” Sigrika repeated reverently, underlining it twice in red, then swapping to the blue pen with a practiced flick of her forefinger. “If you ever shed a secondary feather during autumn moult, please don't throw it in the stove, put it in a dry envelope with the date and the altitude—”

Down in the Startorch courtyard, the brass lecture bell gave its five-minute warning clang.

Sigrika jumped, aligned the blue pen flush to the red and green ones along the notebook edge, and shoved the book back into her bag. As she tugged her coat straight, her right hand went once to the outside of her left breast pocket, pressed the stiff shape inside it for half a second, and came away to grab a second wedge of flatbread off the wrapper.

“I have a seminar on the third floor!” she called out, already backing toward the landing with her mouth full. “Becca, don't let Lynae eat all the mutton fat, save the rind for the bean soup! Nice to meet you!”

The door banged shut behind her, followed by the rapid clatter of boots taking the attic stairs two at a time.

Suisui looked at the screen over her fan. “She has very thorough questions.”

“She likes birds,” Yangyang said mildly.

At the stove, Rebecca reached into the sink basin with her chrome hand and hauled a raw, goggle-eyed bream head up into the middle of the camera frame by one gill.

“Right, wolf,” Rebecca said to Lupa. “Chiyo's at the market till noon and I'm making stock out of this bastard right now. Do I crack the skull down the middle with the cleaver first, or roast it whole?”

“If you don't pull the bitter bone out of the throat first, I'm coming through the relay to beat you with the ladle,” Lupa said, shoving Amy half off the bench to get closer to the glass, and the rest of the household scattered to leave them to it.

***

Iuno took her Terminal down to the south bastion wall, where the wind couldn't reach and a stack of squared cedar timbers held the midday sun.

She sat down on the lowest timber, rested her left side carefully so the cracked crystal at her shoulder stayed clear of the wood, and dialled Septimont.

Augusta answered on the third ring.

The screen showed the bedroom of Augusta's house above the old forum. On the braided rug in the middle of the floor sat an iron-bound sea trunk with its lid thrown back against the wardrobe. Inside the bottom half of the trunk, directly on top of four neatly folded linen tunics, sat Cub, his massive brindled paws tucked under his chest and his chin resting heavily on the brass hasp.

Augusta stood in her shirtsleeves by the window table, scowling down at a printed harbour bill.

“The port authority moved the tide,” Augusta said without looking up. “Six hours earlier on the Thursday sailing. And the purser has put us in the forward deck-cabin over the anchor chain because the grain commissioners booked the stern.”

“You are the Ephor,” Iuno said, resting her living elbow on her knee. “Tell the grain commissioners to sleep over the chain.”

“Angel booked the berths under her own name so the harbourmaster wouldn't bring the municipal brass band down to the jetty at five in the morning.” Augusta set the shipping bill down. Her gaze came up to the screen, went past Iuno's face, and fixed on the pale, jagged frost-line running across Iuno's bare left shoulder crystal.

Beside the Terminal on Augusta's table lay a six-inch offcut of copper garden wire from the rose trellis. Without Augusta touching it, one end of the wire lifted a fraction of an inch off the oak and began to curl slowly around her left thumb.

Angel came through the bedroom doorway carrying a folded stack of woollen stockings. She stopped behind Augusta's chair, looked at the screen, and looked straight at Iuno's shoulder.

“How much does the cold get into that crack?” Angel asked.

“When the mist comes up from the gorge,” Iuno said honestly. “Wen heats a brick in the bread oven and wraps it in flannel for me, and Mei sleeps on that side.”

Angel nodded once. She set the stockings on the corner of the dresser and bent to take Cub by the leather collar. “Out, boy. Those are the clean shirts.”

Cub closed his eyes and went dense as an anvil. Angel tugged once, laughed under her breath, and left him in the trunk.

Augusta looked down at her own thumb. She pressed the curled copper ring flat against the tabletop until the metal gave a tiny click, then looked back at Iuno.

“And the box?” Augusta asked.

Iuno's fingers brushed the outside of her coat pocket, where the small velvet square rested against the dented flank of her tea flask.

“Unopened,” Iuno said.

“You told me Tuesday.”

“Tuesday is whatever day I open it.”

*A masterstroke of calendar administration, darling,* the High Priestess observed from the back of her mind. *The Pontiffs would have formed a subcommittee.*

Augusta let out one long, dry breath through her nose. “If Tuesday hasn't turned up on your calendar by the time I finish countersigning your retirement papers, I am standing up at the breakfast table and making the toast anyway.”

“I will have Lupa hide the spoons.”

Gravel crunched rapidly on the bastion steps behind Iuno.

Amy came wandering round the corner of the timber stack with her head down, squinting at a grease-smudged schematic from the Hold's Skyworks crew, and hooked her chin companionably toward Iuno's right shoulder.

“Hey, Am—”

Amy looked up, saw the Ephor of Septimont staring at her out of the screen from two feet away, and bit the second syllable clean in half.

A single tiny spark of white static popped at the tip of her pink fringe.

“—bassadorial maritime schedules!” Amy finished at top volume, snapping upright with her hands behind her back. “Morning, Ephor! Morning, Angel! Cub looks very comfortable!”

She backed away three paces, gave Iuno an enormous, silent thumbs-up from behind the cedar timbers, and vanished back up the steps faster than she had come down.

Augusta's iron eyebrow rose a quarter of an inch.

“Ambassadorial,” Augusta repeated.

“She is taking an interest in foreign trade,” Iuno said, her face perfectly smooth. “Suisui has been lending her pamphlets.”

Behind Augusta, Angel reached behind the bedroom door and lifted down a heavy, fleece-lined sea-cloak—the one with the tarnished bronze clasps that Augusta had worn through two northern campaigns and had left hanging on the back peg claiming the southern crossing would be mild.

Angel laid it across the top of the trunk directly in front of Cub's nose.

“Pack the cloak before you shut the lid,” Angel told her, and Augusta looked at it, looked at Iuno on the screen, and ended the call before Iuno could smile at her.

***

By the middle of the afternoon the winter sun had slid behind the western rim of the Hold, and the long shadow of the outer ring lay blue across the colonnade.

Iuno sat at a small square table outside the closed shutter of the salt-house with her flask of Earl Grey, her ruled pad, and the pencil she had finally sharpened with Chisa's small pocket-knife.

When the Ragunna line clicked open, it was the lunch-hour closing at the Guild.

The screen showed the long scrubbed table in the Guild kitchen. Phoebe sat near the window with three sheets of squared paper spread out around her teacup, each one covered in neat ink circles representing herb pots and oblongs representing benches. Zani sat beside her, her shoulder resting against Phoebe's, a coiled cloth measuring tape sitting between her elbow and a plate of bread.

She was wearing her new blue coat indoors; the tailor had taken the scratchy wool off the neck and replaced it with a band of dark grey velvet, which Zani touched once with two fingers as she leaned toward the camera.

From the open doorway at the top of the cellar stairs came a loud, deliberate splash of water, followed by a resonant baritone voice echoing up the stone stairwell:

“Inform the northern delegation that if the lute is tuned to Ragunnese pitch, the basin must be set within three yards of the well-head!”

“Gerald has been helping with the seating plan,” Phoebe said happily. “He wants to be near Cia and Cartethyia during the supper.”

“We measured the courtyard before opening,” Zani said, tapping the cloth tape on the wood. “From the foot of the outside stair to the washhouse wall is fourteen paces. If we take down the lower washing line and move the rosemary tubs under the kitchen window, we can set two long tables and keep a three-foot lane clear to the street door.”

“And the school has twenty-four folding chairs we can borrow on the Friday,” Phoebe added, turning one of her squared sheets toward the lens so Iuno could see the little labelled boxes. “I've put Lupa at the corner nearest the kitchen hatch so she doesn't have to walk around the pillars when she wants to look at the pans. Have you worked out which month you'll be coming back across the sea?”

Iuno looked past the edge of the colonnade. Fifty yards away, Mei was sitting flat on the flagstones in front of the well-head trestle, holding a skein of twine between her wrists while Old Chen wound it into a ball and told her a long story about his brother's mule.

“We don't know yet,” Iuno said. “Nobody has bought a ferry ticket.”

Phoebe's face didn't fall at all; she picked up her pen and drew a small dotted square at the end of the table nearest the fig tree. “Then we'll wait to send the printer's cards until you know which boat you're on. The courtyard isn't going anywhere.”

Iuno drew her own ruled pad an inch closer across the pine table. Her pencil point touched the blank white paper.

“Fourteen paces,” Iuno said, in the measured, dry tone she had once used to audit the granaries of three provinces. “Does the outside stair have a landing at the turn, or does it come straight down into the flags?”

“There's a square landing three steps from the bottom,” Zani answered promptly. “Four feet wide. The autumn drain sits directly underneath the bottom riser.”

“Clear the drain the Wednesday before,” Iuno said. “If it rains in Ragunna, the water backs up across the lowest step within twenty minutes, and anyone coming down the stair in a long hem will soak her cuffs before she reaches the chairs.”

“I told you about that drain,” Zani said mildly to Phoebe.

“You did,” Phoebe agreed, writing *Wednesday — drain* in the margin of her plan.

Behind them, sitting up on the dry-goods counter with her boots swinging against the cupboard doors, Laevatain reached for a dark cloth bag with a one-handed brass snap. At her waist, her stabilizer spheres turned with a low, steady hum.

“Hold on,” Laevatain said, pulling the little wooden thumb piano out of the bag. “And tell the bard if she takes her splint off early I'll hear about it. Now be quiet a second. I've got the middle bit.”

She braced her back against the pantry cupboard, wedged the wooden box between her palms, and played.

It was a whole tune—sixteen bars of a ferryman's hornpipe, taken at the cautious, heavy-thumbed pace of a woman clearing a minefield. On the seventh bar her left thumbnail caught two tines at once with a sour *clink*; she frowned at the metal, backed up two notes without apologising, and marched straight through the turn to the final chord.

Phoebe hummed the last four bars along with her, softly, so the harmony landed warm under the tinny steel.

From the bottom of the cellar stairs came another splash. “The tempo remains pedestrian,” Gerald called up, “but the phrasing has acquired spine!”

Laevatain slid the instrument back into its dark bag and snapped the flap shut with her thumb.

“Tell the wolf the viburnum on the cliff held all its flowers through the Tuesday blow,” she said to Iuno. “I sent a photograph to her machine, if she ever remembers to look at it. And tell her if she tries to drive a yellow truck through the harbour ward, the arches are six feet wide and the municipality will send her the bill.”

“I will convey the dimensions,” Iuno said.

When the screen went dark, the colonnade was quiet again. Down by the well, Old Chen had finished winding his twine, and Mei was holding her hand up for him to pull her off the cold stones.

Iuno looked down at her pad. The page was still blank.

With her living hand, she wrote two lines across the top in small, upright script:

*Courtyard: 14 paces. Landing 3 steps from bottom.*
*Check drain Wednesday.*

She looked at the words for a moment, closed the cover over them without tearing the leaf out, and slid the pad into her pocket beside the unopened box.

Inside the storehouse doorway, someone had stacked four sacks of husked millet against the post where the afternoon wind didn't reach.

Mei sat on the cedar threshold with her back propped against the lowest sack and her legs stretched out in the sun. Amy sat wedged between her knees, the tin fox-whistle hanging outside her jacket on its red string, holding her Terminal in both hands.

Room 304, Engineering South, answered on the second ring.

The camera was set lower than desk height, angled up from the sawed-down tea table beside Mornye's wheelchair. Behind Mornye's shoulders, the wide glass window showed the Solistrees along the Academy avenue turning gold-white as Helios began its afternoon shift. Her long bangs were pinned back with two plain steel clips, and on the hovering shelf of her Starstack sat Buddy—round as a dumpling, glowing a slow, warm amber, his two stubby legs tucked under his belly and his two short stubby arms waving solemnly at the lens.

“Hi, Buddy!” Mei said immediately, waving back with all five fingers.

Buddy gave a delighted little bounce that made the Starstack dip an inch, nearly sliding a stack of index cards off the edge before Mornye caught them with one hand.

There were dark smudges under Mornye's eyes, and the corners of her mouth lifted the instant she looked at the two of them wedged in the doorway.

“You're sitting against a grain sack,” Mornye said to Mei, her lecture-hall voice nowhere in evidence. “Did you sleep through?”

“Seven hours,” Mei said, leaning her chin on the top of Amy's pink head. “I only woke up once because Lupa was kicking the wall in her sleep.”

“She was fighting a badger,” Amy said, tipping her head back to glare up at her mother's chin before looking back at the screen. “Professor, tell Ma what you found in the basement.”

Mornye reached down beside her chair brake and lifted a flat grey archival cassette box with a faded blue Startorch Academy crest on the spine.

“You asked for the primary record,” Mornye told Mei gravely. “The Junior Open Day files were misboxed with the Facilities plumbing schematics in sub-basement two. I had Halvard pull the crate yesterday afternoon.”

She slotted the cassette into the reader on her desk and swung her secondary monitor round on its articulated arm until it filled the left half of the camera view.

Grainy, slightly washed-out video flickered onto the glass.

It showed the indoor athletics hall at Startorch twenty-one years ago, hung with paper bunting and handwritten cardboard signs reading **PRIMARY FLIGHT TRIAL — UNDER 10s**. Five small, ungainly quad-rotors hovered in a wobbling pack around a circuit of orange traffic cones.

On the screen, a tiny white-and-pink drone with mismatched landing skids and oversized twin props dived inside the third pylon, clipped the prop-wash of a much heavier blue drone painted with lightning bolts, dropped four feet until its skids brushed the gym mat, and clawed its way back into the air to cross the chalk finish line first—while in the lower right corner of the shot, the blue drone sailed clean over the boundary tape, across the refreshments trestle, and nose-first into the punch bowl.

At the judge's table on the recording, a woman in a dark coat with golden eyes stood up, clapped both hands over her head, and handed the first-place blue ribbon to the boy with the lightning bolts, who was still wringing punch out of his drone.

In the storehouse doorway, Mei went rigid behind Amy's back.

“Play that pylon turn back,” Mei demanded, leaning so far over Amy's shoulder that her nose nearly touched the Terminal glass. “Half speed.”

Mornye tapped a key and ran the four seconds back at half speed.

“Look at the boundary tape!” Amy shouted, pointing her finger at the little white-and-pink craft on the monitor. “He cut the gate by two yards, dumped his wake right on my port rotor, crashed into the punch, and Ma gave him the big ribbon with the gold fringe because it had *heart*!”

Mei stared at the frozen frame on Mornye's monitor—at the woman at the judge's table laughing and bending down to shake the wrong kid's hand, and, twelve feet behind her by the bleachers, a very small girl with pink pigtails standing with her controller held against her stomach and her chin wobbling.

Mei sat back against the millet sack. She lifted her right hand, thumb and forefinger pinched together over the old blade callus as if she were holding a gavel.

“Car Two is disqualified for leaving the course and drowning in the refreshments,” Mei said with immense solemnity. “And the judge is fired. She doesn't know anything about aviation. Car Four wins by half a nose and gets the ribbon and two ice creams.”

“I didn't even want the vanilla one,” Amy muttered, though her shoulders dropped back against Mei's chest and her hair stayed solid pink all the way to the tips.

On the screen, Mornye tapped the paused monitor screen right under the belly of eight-year-old Amy's white-and-pink drone with the eraser end of her pencil.

“Your battery pack was taped twelve millimetres behind the centre of lift,” Mornye said conversationally. “That's why the tail pitched when you hit his wash.”

Amy sat bolt upright between Mei's knees. “It was a salvaged nickel cell from Dr. Luuk's blood-warmer! If I mounted it under the front bracket it blocked the downward optical sensor and the whole frame oscillated!”

“Then you counter-weight the nose skid with two steel washers,” Mornye said, leaning one elbow on her chair arm. “You had forty grams of spare thrust on those eight-inch blades.”

“If I put washers on the nose skid, the landing gear snapped every time I set it down on the lino! Remember the Tuesday I cracked the floor tile outside Room 302?”

“I remember,” Mornye said dryly. “Facilities billed my department.”

For the next eight minutes Mei didn't say a word. She rested her cheek against the rough burlap of the millet sack, her arms wrapped loosely around Amy's waist to keep the draft off her stomach, and watched the two of them argue rotor pitch and battery brackets with her eyes warm and quiet.

When Amy finally paused to take a breath, the room in Lahai-Roi was still for two seconds.

From the side bench by Mornye's radiator, just out of frame to the left, came a steady, mechanical *tick-tick, pause, tick* of a two-pen chart recorder drawing ink across a paper drum.

Amy tilted her head. “What have you got running on the drum, Professor?”

“A weir,” Mornye said evenly. “Did the Skyworks engineers have the low-temperature grease for Nyx's knee actuators, or do I need to send a tub down on the freight line?”

“Liangyu got me two tins from the hoist shed,” Amy said at once, and launched into an account of the frost seals.

When they finally said goodbye and the call clicked off, Mei reached up and tugged the tin fox-whistle once before letting Amy haul her to her feet.

In Room 304, the Terminal screen went black on the low tea table.

Outside the tall window, the Solistrees stood pale in the late afternoon light. On the side bench by the radiator, the two-pen recorder gave its steady *tick-tick, pause, tick* over the moving paper.

Buddy clambered down from the hovering Starstack, stumped across the gap onto the wheelchair armrest on his two short legs, and settled his warm, chubby weight along Mornye's left forearm. Mornye rested her right hand lightly over his back and watched his amber glow rise and fall through its slow eleven-count.

Chisa found Lupa sitting on the low stone kerb behind the salt-house, scraping a flake of dried mud off the butt-plate of her spear with her thumbnail.

“I am calling my mother,” Chisa said, stopping beside her boots. “Come and sit on the bench.”

Lupa squinted up at her, one ear twitching back toward the chimney flue. “She wants to talk to you. She doesn't need me sitting there like a guard dog.”

Chisa reached down, took Lupa by the wrist with her left hand—the loose red string brushing Lupa's cuff—and pulled gently until Lupa grunted, shoved her spear against the brickwork, and came over to the bench by the warm flue wall.

Chisa sat down with her thigh pressed against Lupa's and dialled.

In Lahai-Roi, the kitchen light was warm yellow over the small table.

“Kuchiba Chiyo,” her mother's voice said, clear and unhurried, before the camera had finished settling on the kettle. “I have just taken the iron off the board, and Lupa is sitting half out of the picture.”

Lupa's ears went flat against her skull and turned dull red from the base to the tips. She shifted two inches to the right so both her shoulders were in the lens. “The sun's in my eyes on this side,” she muttered at the flagstones. “Evening, ma'am.”

“Good afternoon, Lupa,” Chiyo said. She picked up her Terminal and carried it three steps across the linoleum to the narrow pantry door, pulling it open with her free hand.

Inside the door, hanging from a wooden peg on a broad hanger, was Chisa's dark school uniform. The white collar was wrapped in tissue at the neck; the small ridge of old mending along the right cuff had been pressed flat under a damp cloth, and every pleat of the skirt hung straight as a plumb-line.

“Rebecca helped me with the pleats,” Chiyo said, angling the lens down toward the hem. “When you come through the door, you can put it on before lunch and we will see whether the skirt wants letting down half an inch.”

“I have not grown since I left,” Chisa said.

“You don't stoop at the shoulders now,” Chiyo said simply, and carried the Terminal back to her chair at the kitchen table. She looked past Chisa's cheek at Lupa. “Does she sleep through the night there, or does she still wake before the fourth bell?”

“Mother,” Chisa said.

Lupa blinked, caught between Chisa's elbow and a direct question from an older woman in a kitchen, and went with the truth. “She wakes up whenever the wind shifts round the gorge,” Lupa said roughly. “Then she lies there listening to it like it owes her money, and goes back to sleep before I do. And if Wen leaves the rice pot by the window, she scrapes the burnt crust off the bottom with a wooden spoon before breakfast.”

“The crust has the oil in it,” Chisa said in her own defence.

Chiyo's mouth curved. “Her father used to fight her for the bottom of the pot with a teacup. What are they giving you to cook with up there, Lupa? Rebecca told me you had a stove on the wall.”

Within ninety seconds Lupa had forgotten to look at the floor. She leaned both elbows on her knees, gesturing with one broad hand as she explained the cast-iron braziers at the Hold, how Wen kept her lard in a crock buried in the snow by the kitchen door, and how the mountain peppercorns made your tongue go numb if you didn't toast them dry in the wok before the ginger went in.

Chiyo listened with her chin on her hand, nodding at the dry-toasting, and told her about the cheaper fishwife Rebecca had found two stops down the tram line who left the collar meat on the bone.

“On Thursdays we eat at twelve,” Chiyo said when the kettle began to hum on the ring behind her. “The table in this room takes two extra leaves when the girls come down from the dormitory. I have put the wooden stool at the end away from the radiator pipe, because Chisa says you get too warm indoors.”

Lupa stopped with her hand halfway through demonstrating the size of a grouper.

Her tail gave a single slow thump against the leg of the bench and went still. The red had come back into her ears, darker this time.

“You'll need a bigger pot,” Lupa said, her voice dropping into a mutter. “I eat three bowls when there's fish.”

“I bought the deep enamelled one on Tuesday,” Chiyo replied, as if that had been settled a week ago. “Give me your Terminal number before Chisa hangs up. You do not need to wait for my daughter when you want to tell me how the broth turned out.”

When the screen went dark two minutes later, Chisa kept her hand on the bench between them.

Lupa pulled her own battered Terminal out from under her belt, hunched over it with her shoulders up around her ears, and began tapping the keys with the flat of one claw, the tip of her tongue caught at the corner of her teeth.

Chisa watched her type **MRS KUCHIBA**, frown at it, back-space the whole line, type **CHIYYO**, notice the extra Y, delete two letters, and save **CHIYO** at the very top of her list.

Cartethyia started her call on the top step of the third terrace so Uncle Cosimo could see the sky-rings turning over the roofs, and then started walking down the hill before he had finished wiping the flour off his spectacles.

“Hold the glass still, child, you're making the ovens seasick,” Cosimo grumbled from the left half of the screen, though he came right up to the counter of the Egla Town bakery with dough up to both elbows. Behind him, the brick oven doors stood open and bright orange, and Rosa leaned past his shoulder with a wooden peel in one hand and a dishcloth in the other, waving the cloth at the lens.

“Rosa!” Cartethyia said, holding the Terminal out in front of her with both hands as she skipped down the cooper's stair. “The camellia cutting from Porto-Veno has a new leaf at the top! It uncurled yesterday morning and I put it in the storehouse window where the draft can't reach!”

“Don't over-water it when the frost is on,” came a second voice as the right half of the screen clicked open.

Cantarella sat on the slatted wooden bench inside the glasshouse at Porto-Veno, her dark sleeves turned back from her wrists and a pair of small silver secateurs resting across her palm. Behind her, among the rows of staked winter camellias, Carlotta walked past carrying a wooden drawing-board with two watercolour pears pinned to it; there was a smear of olive-green pigment along the knuckle of her right hand, and she paused between two terracotta pots to give Cartethyia a brisk, fond nod before returning to her easel.

“It only gets two spoonfuls when the soil goes pale, just like you said,” Cartethyia promised, turning the corner onto the second terrace lane. “Wait, I'm taking you to the Golden Reed! I call this street Via della Fonte because there's a copper cistern at the corner that sounds just like the one behind the shoemaker's—mind the cobbles, Uncle, somebody spilled cabbage water and it froze.”

“Where is your minstrel?” Cosimo asked, squinting as the painted signboards of the second terrace bobbed past the camera. “Rosa packed six of the lemon cakes with the coarse sugar in a tin for the mail boat, and if she's already eaten yours in spirit I want to hear her confession.”

“She is earning our supper,” Cartethyia whispered loudly, ducking under the heavy quilted door-curtain of the Golden Reed tea house.

Inside, the room was dim and warm with charcoal braziers and the smell of toasted buckwheat and jasmine leaves. Fifteen or twenty townsfolk and off-duty Wardens sat around low lacquered tables with their coats unbuttoned. At the far end of the room, on a raised cedar dais beside the lattice window, Ciaccona sat on a high stool with one booted ankle hooked over the rung, her lute braced against her ribs, playing a fast, sunny Ragunnese harbour jig with three fingers while her splinted left forefinger stuck out over the neck like a tiny flagpole.

Cartethyia tiptoed between two tables of old men playing dominoes, slid onto an empty stool in the very front row, and held the Terminal up at chest height facing the stage.

Mid-measure, Ciaccona looked down.

She saw Cosimo's flour-white beard, Rosa's checked apron, and the Duchess of Fisalia sitting side by side in her front row on a six-inch screen.

Ciaccona didn't drop a beat. Her mouth twitched at the left corner; she raised both eyebrows at Cantarella, threw an outrageously florid carnival trill into the turnaround with her third and fourth fingers just to show she could do it without the first, and brought the jig down on the final chord to a loud scatter of applause and knocking teacups from the room.

She set the lute across her lap, picked up her cup of weak jasmine tea from the floorboards, and stepped down off the dais to drop onto the bench beside Cartethyia.

“My public,” Ciaccona said, taking the Terminal in her good hand and leaning her elbow on the table. “Duchess. Tell me the city is dull without me.”

“Intolerably,” Cantarella said. Her gaze dropped briefly to the wooden splint bound with white tape along Ciaccona's left forefinger, lingered for a breath, and rose back to Ciaccona's eyes. “It rained on the quay from dawn until noon. The anchovy boats came in early under the south bridge with their sails wet, and old Tonio had his frying pans going under the tarpaulin by nine o'clock.”

Ciaccona stopped swirling her teacup. She rested her chin on her fist, looking at the damp glasshouse panes behind Cantarella's head.

“Did he burn the garlic again?” Ciaccona asked softly.

“The entire lane smelled of it,” Carlotta called from behind her easel. “I had to shut the south casement twice before lunch.”

“He uses the cheap oil when the tourist boats aren't running,” Ciaccona murmured, her thumb rubbing a small nick in the edge of the Terminal case. “Tell him if he poisons the harbour before I get back, I'll write a ballad about his nephew.”

In the left window, Cosimo dusted his hands together over the flour trough, raising a small white cloud.

“When do we air the mattresses upstairs, child?” he asked Cartethyia.

Cartethyia looked at Ciaccona's shoulder, then back at the two halves of the screen.

“We don't know yet, Uncle,” she said plainly. “Mei hasn't looked at the boats yet.”

Cosimo grunted—the exact, settled grunt he gave when the miller's wagon was held up by high water at the ford. “The flour keeps in the bin till you're through the door. And the tin's soldered shut, so the bard's teeth won't get into it on the ferry.”

Behind them on the dais, the tea-house proprietor set a fresh kettle on the charcoal tripod and tapped his knuckles twice on the rail for the second set.

Ciaccona tapped Cartethyia's knee and stood up, slinging the lute strap back over her shoulder.

Before Cartethyia pressed the red key, she held the Terminal a little closer to her face. Her right thumb came up and rested for a second against the right side of the glass, just beside Cantarella's cheek, where the greenhouse light fell across the Duchess's sleeve.

On the screen, Cantarella did not look down at her shears. She lifted her free hand an inch from her lap, two gloved fingers touching the air near the lens in answer, and kept them there until the picture went dark.

Up on the cedar dais, Ciaccona struck an open fifth—slower this time, a tune from the wet quays below the bridge—and Cartethyia folded her hands on the warm table and stayed to hear the rest of it.

By the second watch the flue under the salt-house kang had settled to a deep, steady heat that came up through the bricks and into the blankets.

Along the top of the upturned packing crate by the door, nine Terminals lay side by side on a split nine-way charging harness Amy had spliced together out of spare hoist wire, their small indicator lights glowing red and green against the whitewashed wall.

Mei lay between Iuno and the warm inner wall, her knee hitched over Iuno's hip to take the pull off her stomach, dutifully drinking the last three swallows from a tin mug because Lupa had stood over the mattress with her arms crossed until the water was gone. Cartethyia sat propped upright against the central roof-post with her eyes shut and her fingers tangled loosely in the end of Ciaccona's braid so she knew which way the door was. Near the foot of the brick platform, Suisui had handed Yangyang her horn comb, leaned her head back against the plaster, and fallen asleep sitting bolt upright before Yangyang had finished the first braid.

“Suisui,” Yangyang whispered, holding the comb out.

“Twenty-four chairs will never hold that courtyard if the guild-clerks bring their wives. Somebody will end up sitting on the rosemary tubs.”

“Shut up and go to sleep, princess,” Lupa said from the outer edge of the bricks.

Lupa opened one eye.

> **Chiyo**
> *Do you salt the fish twenty minutes before the pan goes on, or right before it hits the oil? Rebecca says right before. I told her I would ask the specialist.*

*Tell her if she does it right before the water comes out in the pan and steams the belly instead of crisping it, you have to do it twenty minutes ahead on a plate and wipe it dry with a cloth or the skin rips off on the iron.*

> *Twenty minutes before. Wipe it dry first. Tell her don't poke it with the spatula.*

She pressed send.

Underneath the box, the small grey word *Delivered* appeared. Lupa clicked the side button until the glass went black, shoved the Terminal flat under the corner of her pillow, and closed her eyes.

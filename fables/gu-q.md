Guī Qī

Mei finished wiping the last bamboo spoon on the linen towel across her thigh and tried to stand it upright on its handle on the bench beside her hip. It wobbled once and clattered flat onto the pine.

Across the room by the wash-tub, Amy hooked the wooden water-bucket onto its peg with her foot and tossed the wrung-out dishcloth over the basin rim. “That’s the lot,” she announced, wiping her wet palms down her skirt. “If anybody dirties another saucer before supper, they can lick it clean themselves.”

Mei stacked the dry spoons into a neat bundle, dropped them into the stoneware cylinder, and looked toward the shuttered window. For the last twenty minutes the steady drum of rain overhead had been thinning out; now the roof tiles had gone quiet altogether, leaving only a slow, heavy *plop-plop* from the eaves into the stone trough outside. Her right knee started bouncing of its own accord against the leg of the stool.

Before she could lean over to unhook the window latch, a pair of wet shoes scraped on the porch flagstones and the front door swung inward.

Ruoyun stepped over the sill first, her pleated hem damp at the ankle and her white ruffled cuffs somehow as crisp and spotless as if she had spent the afternoon in a drawing-room instead of a drainage cut. She reached back into the gloom of the porch, took Qingxiao firmly by the wrist, and marched her straight across the floorboards to the low stool beside the brick stove. Buling trailed in last, peeling her soaked round winter hat off her silver hair with both hands and slapping the wet paper talisman up off her forehead so it stuck to the brim like a salted leaf.

“The junior mason on the west sluice packed clay round the iron flange with the heel of his boot,” Ruoyun said to the room at large, her voice brisk and biting as she shook out a spare wool quilt from the end of the kang and dropped it squarely over Qingxiao’s shoulders. “I told him if the joint weeps before spring I’ll come back and pack him into the culvert headfirst. Sit down, A-Qing. Your stockings are wet through.”

Qingxiao sat where she was planted, drawing the quilt up to her chin with both hands. Her nose was pink at the tip from the gorge wind. She looked calmly at the orange coals behind the grate, gave a single small sneeze into her wrist, and let out a slow breath as the heat reached her knees.

“Wah, the water in the lower ditch was up to my shins,” Buling complained, hopping onto the corner of the grain-chest and tucking her damp leggings under her. Before her hat had finished dripping onto the boards, she had her Terminal out of her sleeve and was scrolling down the screen with one dry thumb. “Whole afternoon wading through dead leaves, and now the forum says the south ferry is backed up to the toll-house.”

By the hearth, Lupa didn’t ask who was hungry. She lifted the lid off the clay crock sitting on the rear ash-flue, ladled out three deep, steaming bowls of pork and winter radish, and carried the first two over in her calloused hands. Qingxiao took hers with a quiet nod and wrapped both palms round the glaze. When Lupa held the second bowl out to Ruoyun, Ruoyun stiffened half an inch and opened her mouth.

“I don’t—”

“Eat it,” Lupa said, shoving the earthenware rim against Ruoyun’s knuckles.

Ruoyun shut her mouth, took the chopsticks tucked across the top, and stood beside the chimney eating hot radish in stiff, silence-keeping bites while keeping one eye on Qingxiao’s cup. Buling caught her bowl one-handed without looking up from her screen, sniffing the star-anise steam happily before wedging her Terminal against her knee.

Outside, the light between the shutter slats faded from pewter to dark blue. Mei had just shifted her weight onto her heels to try for the window again when the iron latch lifted from outside, a boot-toe nudged the sticking bottom rail clear of the floorboards, and Suoming pushed the door open.

She stepped inside, gave Nine Shadows a single shake over the threshold pan, and propped the furled bamboo shaft against the wall before climbing onto her upturned wooden crate by the door. Hsin came in behind her in her tall shape, ducking her ears smoothly under the lintel and leaving her damp cuffs loose at the wrists.

Nobody asked where they had been since morning, and neither of them offered a word about it. Lupa simply scraped the bottom of the back crock, filled the last two bowls to the brim with dark broth and soft pork belly, and set one on the corner of the kang near Hsin and the other on the crate beside Suoming’s knee.

Suoming picked up her spoon with her left hand and began to eat, her heavy iron-plated boots hanging two inches clear of the boards. Hsin sat on the wooden edge of the kang with her nine tails curled neatly out of the draught behind her ankles. She lifted a spoonful of broth, angled her ears back out of the rising vapour, and blew on it twice before taking a careful sip.

When the bowl was clean, Hsin set it aside on the brick ledge and looked up the length of the quilt toward Yangyang.

“Miss Xuanling,” Hsin said, her voice easy and level in the lamp-lit room. “Are you using your ink-stone tonight? I have a letter to write before the morning cart goes down.”

Yangyang uncrossed her ankles under the quilt and reached behind the bolster for her canvas pack. “It’s dry,” she said, drawing out the small slate ink-stone, a stick of pine-soot ink, and a capped bamboo brush. She set them on the low pine table at the end of the kang and poured a spoonful of warm water from the teapot into the well of the stone.

Amy leaned over from where she was braided into the corner of the quilt, resting her chin on her forearm as Hsin pulled a folded sheet of thick paper from her right cuff. “Ooh, official correspondence? Who’re we writing to?”

Hsin laid the paper flat on the pine and picked up the ink-stick. “The Magistrates at City Hall,” she said plainly. “They want to know which day I’m coming down the mountain to look at the grain-rolls.”

“Sounds thrilling,” Amy said, and rolled back onto her spine to toss her tin fox-whistle at the rafters.

That was enough sitting still for one day. Mei pushed up off her stool, stepped past Cartethyia’s boots, and shoved the front shutter open until it banged lightly against the outside wall.

Cold, clean air washed into the room, smelling of wet limestone, pine needles, and gorge mist instead of boiled pork. Above the south cut, the heavy grey cloud-bank had torn wide open, and a lopsided white moon hung clear in the gap, turning the wet cobbles of the lane into a ribbon of hammered silver. Right beside the overflowing well-trough, Cartethyia’s white goose stood on one webbed leg in a puddle with its neck looped back and its beak buried deep in its wing-feathers, dead asleep.

Mei braced both hands on the damp sill, breathing in until her ribs lifted, and turned her head back toward the kang with her mouth open.

Iuno had already set her empty teacup down on the tray and was reaching one-handed for the dark blue wool cloak folded behind her shoulder.

“Yes,” Iuno said before Mei got a syllable out. “On one condition. We take the wide steps by the dyers’ arch, not the steep cut behind the cooper’s, and you keep your boots out of the gutter-runnels.”

Mei grinned from ear to ear, reaching for her own jacket on the wall-peg. “Deal. Wide steps, dry toes.”

The open shutter acted on the rest of the room like a dropped gate. At the door-bench, Cartethyia was already dragging her second boot on with both hands, while Ciaccona let out a long, theatrical sigh, set her cup down, and picked up her woollen scarf from the bench. Across the kang, Suisui tapped the sealed Mingting letter in her lap against her knuckles and looked at her sister.

“Walk with me down to the south gate post-house?” Suisui asked. “If we put this in the night-box now, it will go out on the first mule-cart at sunrise.”

Yangyang nodded, slipping her feet into her shoes and taking her sword off the peg by habit, though she left the hilt resting easily in the crook of her arm. Beside the hearth, Chisa stood up from her low stool and reached for her short black coat; Lupa rolled her shoulders with a crack and headed straight for the threshold in her sleeveless tunic.

“Hey, Wupa, it’s freezing out there,” Mei called, tugging her scarf round her throat as Amy hopped into her boots beside her. “You don’t have a coat.”

“Salt-house is like a bakehouse,” Lupa grunted, her hand already on the iron latch. “Fresh air’ll do me good.”

At the low corner of the table, Hsin didn’t look up from the slate well where she was grinding the pine-soot ink in slow, even circles. By the stove, Qingxiao had let her eyelids drop under the wool quilt, her head resting back against the warm mortar, while Buling stayed cross-legged on the grain-chest with the blue glow of her screen reflected in her red eyes. Suoming sat motionless on her crate beside the jamb as nine pairs of boots trooped past her knees into the moonlit lane.

“Last one out, shut the door properly,” Ruoyun called sharply from her stool by the chimney, glaring at the gap as Mei stepped over the sill behind Iuno and Amy. “I’m not heating the whole street.”

***

Chisa walked on the left side of the stair so her right hand could find Lupa’s on the turn of the steps.

The rain had washed the coal-soot and festival paper out of the crevices between the flagstones, and the air coming up from the cut was sharp enough to sting the inside of the nose. Every downpipe along the middle terrace was still gurgling with run-off into the stone gutters.

“Told them it wasn’t cold,” Lupa said, swinging her bare arms as they came out onto the second-terrace lane. “Bunch of hothouse flowers. Little breeze after a whole day breathing radish steam is nothing.”

When Chisa’s fingers slid into Lupa’s palm at the head of the cobbler’s steps, the skin of Lupa’s forearm was raised in hard goose-flesh all the way to the wrist, and a fine, tight shiver was running under the muscle. Chisa didn’t mention it; she only kept Lupa’s hand tucked against the cuff of her own black sleeve, where the red string was wound loose round her left wrist, and angled them toward the lit archway at the bottom of the lane.

Under the stone arch beside the dry-goods storehouse, an old woman in a quilted oilskin vest had hooked her rain-curtain back on two bamboo poles and hung a flaring brass colza-lamp from the keystone. Three long wooden racks of second-hand winter clothes stood out on trestles over the damp cobbles—wool carters’ vests, patched warden cloaks, sheepskin waistcoats, and felt hoods bought off the baggage-trains when the south road opened.

“Dry wool, good linings, no moth-holes!” the old woman called around a mouthful of roasted melon seeds, rapping a split bamboo yardstick against the nearest trestle. “Cheaper than firewood, young misses!”

“We’re just walking,” Lupa muttered, though her boots slowed three paces from the warm yellow pool of the lamp.

Chisa let go of Lupa’s hand and stepped up to the middle rack. She didn’t look up at the shoulders or the dye; she started at the left end of the pole and worked her way along with her fingers, pinching the cloth between thumb and forefinger to test the tightness of the weave and lifting each hem an inch to feel the weight of the lining. The first two were thin shoddy that would soak through in ten minutes of sleet. The third had a greasy tallow smell in the seams.

At the far end of the rack, Lupa made a sound in her throat and hauled a hanger off the pole with both hands.

“Look at this,” Lupa said, holding it out at arm’s length so the brass buttons clinked together.

Even in the smoky orange light of the colza-lamp, the coat was loud enough to stop a mule-cart. It was cut from heavy, unapologetically bright rust-red mountain wool, wide in the shoulders, with thick curls of cream sheepskin turned back along the lapels and cuffs and two rows of domed brass buttons the size of walnuts down the front.

“Belonged to a timber-factor from the upper reaches,” the stall-woman said promptly, spitting a seed-husk into her palm. “Sold it to me Tuesday when the sun came out. Real wether fleece inside, double-stitched at the armpits. You could sleep in a snowdrift in that and wake up sweating.”

Chisa reached out and took the left sleeve between her hands. The wool was coarse, dense, and dry all the way to the backing, and when she ran her thumb along the inside shoulder-seam the waxed linen thread didn’t give at all. She lifted the bottom edge; it was heavy as a horse-blanket.

“The seams are straight,” Chisa said. “Put it on.”

Lupa slung the coat round her shoulders and shoved her arms through the sleeves. The cuffs came down cleanly to her wrist-bones, and when she rolled her shoulders the wide back didn’t bind across her blades. Her ears pricked up straight above the thick sheepskin collar, and her tail gave a single, satisfied sweep behind her calves before she remembered to look unimpressed for the bargaining.

“Buttons are tarnished,” Lupa told the old woman, shoving both hands deep into the fleece-lined side pockets and letting out a barely audible sigh as the warmth hit her fingers. “And the red’s loud.”

“You picked it out from twenty paces off because the red’s loud,” the stall-woman replied without blinking. “Eighteen coppers, and I won’t charge you for the brass polish.”

Lupa haggled her down to fifteen mostly for the sport of it, counted the heavy coins out of her belt-pouch onto the upturned crate, and flatly refused the stall-woman’s offer of a twine string to carry it, keeping the coat buttoned to the second brass walnut as they walked back out into the moonlit lane.

For half a block Lupa just walked with her thumbs hooked into the deep wool pockets, rolling her chin against the curly sheepskin lapel. Then she snorted under her breath.

“Still had my good leather gloves in the pockets of the other one,” she grumbled, kicking a loose pebble down the wet gutter. “Hsin says the dragon’s bringing it back in person. If she means in person, she’s going to have to fly all the way to Lahai-Roi to drop it off. Hope your mother’s roof can take a dragon landing on the tiles.”

She said it in the exact tone she used for who was stirring the pot or which side of the bedroll went next to the wall. Septimont hadn’t come into her head at all.

Chisa reached up with her right hand and flattened a corner of the thick sheepskin collar where it had turned under behind Lupa’s neck, letting her fingers rest for a second against the warm wool before dropping her hand back to Lupa’s sleeve.

“The roof is concrete,” Chisa said. “It’ll hold.”

“Good. Because I’m not climbing up a ladder to chase her off the chimney.” Lupa lengthened her stride as the lane levelled out along the lower terrace wall, the heavy rust-red hem swinging against her shins. “When we get back there, Thursday lunch is still noon, right? Does your mother have a proper heavy iron pot, or just those little flat pans?”

“She has a cast-iron one under the sink.”

“Right. If she lets me near the stove without thinking I’m going to burn the flat down, I’ll do braised short-ribs. Not the lantern-chili kind—Rebecca’ll whine for a week if her tongue goes numb, and Lynae’ll probably turn up twenty minutes early with her helmet under her arm expecting three bowls anyway.” Lupa paused at the corner where the stone steps turned back toward the print-shop lane, squinting sideways at Chisa. “Your mother’s not going to get sick of having me at the table every Thursday, is she? I eat a lot of rice.”

“Ma likes people who finish the pot,” Chisa said plainly. “She always cooks too much and makes Rebecca take it home in plastic tubs.”

“Tell her she can save the tubs.” Lupa’s shoulders settled deeper into the fleece lining. “Hey—Rebecca wanted a postcard on the relay yesterday. Think that print shop up the alley sells cards with pictures of the Hold on ’em, or just grain notices? If we don’t buy one before we pack, she’ll send fifty crying faces.”

“The stall by the south gate had painted ones,” Chisa said. “We can look tomorrow.”

They stopped by the low stone coping above the dyers’ culvert, where the cleared rainwater was rushing fast and white through the cut below their boots. Across the gorge, the cliff face stood sharp and black against the sky, wet rock gleaming in patches under the lopsided moon.

Chisa rested her hands on the cold top of the stone wall. The rushing water down in the dark sounded wider than a mountain ditch when you didn’t look at it.

“I miss Honami a little,” Chisa said.

Lupa stopped mid-reach for her top button and turned her head, her boots going completely still on the wet flagstones. She didn’t interrupt or talk over her; she just waited.

“Not the rain,” Chisa said, keeping her eyes on the white water below the arch. “When we opened it last year, we left right after the boats came in. I haven’t seen it since the people moved back. By the east gate, where the tram turns round toward the bay, an old man used to have a cart with a striped awning. He sold warm custard puddings in little tin cups and tapped them out onto chipped plates with a wooden spoon. In the morning you could smell the caramel and the salt water from the harbour at the same time.”

She lifted one hand from the stone and tucked a stray black strand behind her ear. “After we go to Lahai-Roi, and see Ma on Thursday. I want to take the boat over and look at it.”

Lupa looked at her for two breaths. Then she reached out with one thick-sleeved arm, hooked her hand round the back of Chisa’s neck, and pulled her sideways until Chisa’s shoulder knocked squarely against the rust-red wool of the new coat.

“How many of those little tins does the old man let you buy at once?” Lupa asked. “Because if they’re the size of teacups, I’m ordering six before we even walk down to the beach.”

“You can buy a whole tray if you bring the tins back,” Chisa said.

“Done.” Lupa let go of her neck only to catch Chisa’s left hand in her own and shove both their hands together down into the wide, sheepskin-lined right pocket of her coat, wrapping her fingers warm and solid over Chisa’s knuckles as they turned up the steps toward the lamps.

***

Iuno chose the wide flight of flagstones under the dyers’ arch because the treads were ten inches deep and the moonlight hit them squarely without shadow.

Mei had her left hand tucked through the crook of Iuno’s right arm, leaning just enough of her weight into the step to keep the pace unhurried, while her wool scarf stayed wrapped high round her chin the way she had been told. Six paces ahead, Amy hopped down off a dry stone horse-block, turned around in the middle of the wet lane, and began walking backward down the slope with both hands jammed into her pockets, her pink hair catching the silver light from the roofs.

“All I’m saying,” Amy called back up the grade, her heels clicking cleanly over the wet cobbles, “is that if we are setting up an actual household in Septimont instead of camping in somebody’s guest wing, I get veto power on the street. No marble colonnades where people whisper when you drop a wrench. And the front door has to be blue.”

“Temple dye is ruinously expensive, Amymy,” Mei said around her scarf.

“Not Temple blue, Ma, nobody wants to live in a vestry. Proper bright cabin blue, like the enamel paint on the Hangar Four tool-lockers, so you can spot it three blocks off in a fog.” Amy skipped neatly over a shallow runnel without looking behind her. “And we’re getting a cat. A fat orange one that doesn’t care about military ranks and sleeps on my spare battery packs.”

“If you bring an orange tom-cat into my house,” Iuno said dryly, guiding Mei two inches to the right where a roof-spout was still dripping onto the kerb, “you are the one buying the sprats on Fridays. The last one chewed the tassels off three state cushions and bit the senior augur on the ankle when he tried to move him off the wool-chest.”

*He was an excellent judge of character, darling,* the High Priestess murmured behind her breastbone. *Old Metellus had ankles like salted turnips.*

“See? Distinguished service record,” Amy said promptly. “Right after we paint the door, Auntie, you’re taking me up the back stair behind the basilica to put flowers on Problem’s grave. Real ones, not those dried laurel twigs they stick on statues.”

“He is buried under the third fig tree behind the bakehouse drain,” Iuno said, the corner of her mouth softening despite the chill. “And he would much rather have a salted herring head than lilies, though the novices might complain about the smell.”

Mei squeezed Iuno’s forearm lightly as they stepped down onto the broad landing above the second terrace. “Okay, herring for Problem, blue paint for the door, and then I get my tour. You promised, Iuiu. I want to see the exact rope where the girls dumped the crows on the augurs, I want to walk out in the middle of the red sand where Augusta dropped her gauntlet, and then we’re climbing up onto that Temple roof at sunset with a bag of apples to see if the pigeons remember you.”

“The pigeons of the Tetragon are forty generations removed from my apples, sweetheart,” Iuno said. “And the roof-hatch has a three-inch lead lip that Augusta could barely get her bad hip over at twenty-nine. You are not climbing a vestry ladder until your side stops pulling on stairs.”

“It’s barely pulling at all tonight! And after Septimont, once the spring ferries start running, we’re going north to see the giant turtle Sentinel. Hsin said it’s bigger than the salt-house.”

“We are not going to see the turtle,” Iuno said.

*We are,* said the High Priestess, entirely resigned. *She will pack dried buns in a handkerchief and buy the turtle a ribbon.*

Amy slowed her backward walk until they caught up with her under the iron bracket of a street-lamp, falling into step on Iuno’s left side near the bare crystal of her shoulder. “Speaking of ferries,” Amy said, tipping her head sideways to peer straight at the breast of Iuno’s cloak, “has Augusta sent a fourth message yet, or is she still arguing with the ship’s captain in four-word telegrams? Because I noticed that little notebook of yours had a whole month gridded out in the back before you snapped it shut at lunch.”

Iuno kept her eyes on the wet flagstones ahead. “Augusta is currently somewhere west of the Headland informing the navy that sea-fogs are a failure of discipline. When she knows which day the tide will let her into the harbour, she will undoubtedly announce it to the customs pier with a brass trumpet. As for my notebook, some of us prefer to know which day of the week it is without consulting a broken Terminal.”

Inside her right coat pocket, beside the leather cover of the pad, the small velvet box holding the ring cut from her own shoulder crystal tapped once against her hip-bone as she stepped down the kerb.

“Uh-huh,” Amy said, wholly unconvinced, before darting three steps ahead to peer through the steamed-up lattice of a lantern-maker’s workshop. “Hey, Ma, look—this guy’s making paper carp with little bamboo windmills in their mouths!”

Mei let go of Iuno’s elbow for a moment to lean her palms against the workshop sill beside Amy’s shoulder, the two of them putting their heads together over the yellow window-square.

Iuno stopped a pace behind them under the dripping eaves.

Looking at the back of Mei’s dark head and the tin fox-whistle swinging from Amy’s neck, Iuno felt the afternoon’s story stir again under her ribs—not the clean, trimmed version with the four bribed novices and the red gravel that had made the kitchen laugh over their stew, but the rest of the hill that sat underneath every stone Mei wanted to visit.

Under the Colosseum awning where the dead crows had tumbled down, the Senate’s chief notary had dropped his reed pen over the marble coping into that same red grit, where it stuck upright by the feather while forty thousand people held their breath across the benches and House Silva walked out into the sea-wind on two sentences. Up at the top of the eastern stair, ten paces from the Temple roof where she had spat apple pips into the dusk, the greased sheepskin chart of the harbour sluice-gates had hung on its brass tack over a square of yellowed pasteboard while two boatmen carried a tarred sailcloth out through the ankle-deep salt water below.

She did not have to tell the Hold, or the Senate, or the street. That was Septimont’s business, and Septimont had struck her name from the marble with its own chisel.

Still, she wanted Mei and Amy to hear all of it from her own mouth before she opened the velvet box in her pocket and called whatever day it was Tuesday.

As Mei laughed at something through the glass and pointed a finger at the upper shelf, Iuno tried the places out in her head, one after another, the way she used to set out tea-stalls on a map.

At the ship’s rail on the crossing west? No: the wind off the strait always smelled of bilge and wet hemp, and Amy got restless whenever there was nowhere to walk beyond thirty paces of deck-planking.

Down in the dry salt-cistern under the eastern tower, where the brass rings still hung from the vaulting? Worse. It was thirty-eight broken steps into the dark, smelling of old vinegar, and Mei would sit on the damp limestone shelf trying to look cheerful while her scar pulled in the cellar draught.

The first evening behind Amy’s new blue door? Augusta’s orderlies would have sent round a jar of wine and a loaf of bread, and you did not christen a child’s threshold by laying thirty years of cold iron out on the supper table.

By Problem’s fig tree behind the vestry drain? Problem had only ever cared about Friday sprat-heads and warm wool-chests; a one-eared cat did not need statecraft dug up over his bones.

*Tell her on a stone bench in the sun with a twist of fried smelts between you, darling,* the High Priestess said quietly in the dark behind her eyes. *She isn’t the Curia. She won’t ask you to read the indictment.*

And then there was the part under the tarred canvas on the limestone shelf—the brown ankle crusted with dry white salt that belonged to the dead Lupa still carried in her fire. Iuno’s mind touched the edge of the sailcloth and flinched away from it as sharply as if the stone were hot. Not tonight. Not while Lupa was walking the lower lanes in a borrowed town.

“Iuiu?”

Mei had turned back from the workshop window. Her golden eyes were quiet and steady in the light from the lattice, looking past the crooked curtain of silver-blue bangs that still hung loose over Iuno’s left eyebrow from the afternoon.

She didn’t ask what Iuno was thinking about, or why she had stopped two paces back in the shadow of the eaves. Instead, Mei stepped close, reached two fingers lightly into the breast pocket of Iuno’s tunic where the loose silver hairpin was glinting, and drew it out.

“Your hair’s getting in your eyes,” Mei said softly, holding the silver pin out flat on her open palm instead of trying to stick it in herself. “And I’m only certified for mirror duty, remember?”

Iuno let out a slow breath that steamed white between them. She took the pin from Mei’s palm with her right hand, slid it one-handed through the loose coil behind her ear until the bangs cleared her brow, and then slipped her hand straight down into Mei’s warm fingers, lacing them tight before they turned down the wide steps together.

***

Yangyang walked on the outside of the wet lane so the water dripping off the lower shop-awnings hit her shoulder instead of Suisui’s silk sleeve.

Up on Suisui’s hair, tucked securely behind the lacquered spine of her good horn comb, Dumpling rode with its brass claws hooked into a braid, giving a faint *tick-whir* of its clockwork neck whenever a moth fluttered past a street-lamp. The south road cut was quieter now than it had been during the festival, though the cobbles were scored deep with fresh iron cart-ruts from the grain convoy that had come through the pass.

At the turn above the gatehouse stood the Hold’s post-station, a narrow stone building with its double doors propped open to the night air. Outside under a hooded lantern hung a blackened slate board chalked in big square characters: *FIRST BELL — MENGZHOU SOUTH STAGE. SECOND BELL — HUATING CROSSING & CANAL WHARF. MULE-FREIGHT ONLY.*

Inside, the room smelled of damp hemp twine, hot pine-rosin wax, and wet wool coats. Five or six townspeople and two Mengzhou soldiers stood in line before the counter with oilcloth parcels tucked under their arms—a string of dried mountain mushrooms, a bundle of mended winter boots, three tin canisters of tea—while a harassed clerk with ink smudges up to his wrist-bones weighed each package on a brass steelyard and shouted the copper rate over his shoulder.

Suisui stepped out of the queue toward the high slanted standing-desk pegged to the left wall, where a chained stub-brush, a chipped water-dropper, and a brass dish of red sealing-wax sat beside an oil taper for people finishing their wrappers.

She drew their father’s letter and her own two-page reply out of her dry cuff, smoothing the folded Mingting paper flat against the scarred wood.

Yangyang rested her hand lightly on the scabbard of her sword to keep the chape from knocking the desk-leg and watched her sister’s profile in the yellow taper-light. Once they all went down the south road, it would divide at the plain. New Year was still two months off; the north wall at Jinzhou would have its early winter watch-rosters posted, Chixia would be back on her feet and complaining loudly about Baizhi’s tonic broth, and at the solstice, when the longest night came down over the gorge, Yangyang would have to find a high, quiet roof to let go of her human bones and fly across the dark to her shijie.

“Jiejie,” Yangyang said quietly over the clatter of the clerk’s brass weights. “How much leave did the Guild give you when you came up from Jinzhou?”

Suisui paused with her fingers on the corner of the paper. For a second she kept her eyes on the brushwork; then she turned her head and let out a low, completely unrepentant laugh.

“Nobody gave me leave,” Suisui said, lowering her voice so the Mengzhou soldiers by the counter wouldn’t hear the Director of the Zhaoming Commerce Guild confessing to truancy. “I signed my own travel chit for seven days when I left the Mingting counting-house, and that ran out the morning we reached the gorge. Deputy Director Lin has sent four messages since the relay came back, asking why my seal is sitting on a mountain where nobody grows mulberry trees.”

Yangyang blinked at her. “Won’t the board make trouble if you don’t go straight back to Mingting?”

“Let them,” Suisui said serenely, lifting her closed fan an inch from her sash to tap her own palm. “If old Lin could untangle the winter grain-freight without me, he wouldn’t have written four times. Besides, the Guild factor’s house by the Jinzhou canal wharf hasn’t had its books looked over properly since the spring rains, and the factor’s wife makes very decent steamed buns.”

She set the fan down on the wood and looked straight at Yangyang, the teasing polish dropping away from her dark eyes.

“I’m riding back to Jinzhou with you,” Suisui said. “I can work from the wharf office until the twelfth month, and then we’ll take the post-coach home to Mingting together before Ma starts the New Year kitchen. If you want me to come.”

Under Yangyang’s chin, the small cerulean feathers of her collar lifted once against the warm hollow of her throat and settled flat. She didn’t look down at her boots or say that Suisui was too busy.

“Yes,” Yangyang said. “Come with me.”

Suisui’s shoulders eased half an inch under her cloak. She reached out, tucked a damp blue-tipped lock of hair behind Yangyang’s ear, and picked up the chained post-house brush from its groove.

“Good. Then we’d better tell Ba before he sends a clerk to look for both of us.” Suisui dipped the bristle in the iron ink-well and wrote a neat, swift column down the left margin of her second sheet: *We are going by way of Jinzhou until the twelfth month. Tell Ma to save the big steamer for the twenty-sixth.*

She held the brush out handle-first to Yangyang. “Your turn. He asked you a question.”

Yangyang took the stubby bamboo shaft and leaned over the slanted board. Beneath Suisui’s flowing Mingting hand, in her own smaller, plain Outrider characters, she wrote: *The feathers stay blue in the cold. Tailor Xu can leave the collar as it is.*

While Suisui blew softly on the wet ink and folded the sheets into their heavy paper sleeve, Yangyang turned her head toward the open doorway of the post-house.

Out beyond the stone arch of the south gate, the wet road dropped away in wide moonlight curves toward the valley floor. In a few weeks’ time, when the carts rolled out, the salt-house kang would be empty bricks again, and Mei, Iuno, and Amy would be on a ship heading west across the salt water toward Septimont while the canal in Jinzhou froze along the reeds. The walk by the bird-bridge would have to wait for the spring thaw.

“When we get to Jinzhou,” Yangyang said, watching Suisui hold the red wax stick to the taper flame until a bright drop fell onto the paper seam, “the Guild office is two streets down from the bird-bridge on the east canal. We can walk back that way in the evenings.”

“The bridge with the willow trees and the pear-cake stall?” Suisui pressed her bronze thumb-ring firmly into the soft red wax, held it for three breaths, and lifted it clean. “Every evening you aren’t on the wall. Though if you burn your tongue on the syrup again, I am not buying you a second bowl.”

She handed the sealed packet to Yangyang. Together they stepped past the counter to the heavy oak post-box bolted beside the door-jamb, and Yangyang slid the white letter through the brass slot, listening until it settled with a dry rustle among the packets going south at dawn.

Ciaccona had hoped the goose would stay asleep in its puddle, which only proved she still didn’t understand poultry.

The moment Cartethyia’s boot scraped the wet flagstone outside the salt-house shutter, the broad white bird had unlooped its neck from its wing, shaken a pint of rainwater over Ciaccona’s stockings, and fallen in three paces behind them like a fat municipal bailiff following a debtor. All the way up the west parapet stair it came—*slap, slap, honk* on every granite riser—until they stepped out onto the high battlement above the river cut.

Ciaccona leaned her hip against the wet crenel, tucking her splinted left forefinger inside the warm wool of her scarf, and looked down over the sheer drop. Four hundred feet below the wall, the gorge river ran black and fast between the cliffs, catching broken ribs of moonlight where the current hammered the rocks on its way out to the western plain and the distant sea.

Cartethyia planted both forearms on the stone coping beside her, her crystal thorn-laurel tipped slightly crooked over her right ear, while the goose parked itself squarely across her heels to preen its breast-feathers.

“When we get back to Ragunna,” Cartethyia said at her usual clear, level volume over the rush of the water below, “we have to go to Fisalia Manor before we take the cart road out to Egla Town. The camellia hedge along the south terrace burned down to the gravel, and Cantarella’s gardener is eighty-two. If you don’t dig the new trenches before the late winter rains set in, the cuttings won’t take for four years, so I told her I would come and help with the spade.”

“Magnificent,” Ciaccona said dryly, pulling her cloak tighter against the river draught. “We survive a mountain full of clockwork dolls and a falling Sanctum so you can work as an unpaid ditch-digger for a duchess. Will she at least let you take the crown off before you shovel dirt?”

“She said I could use the heavy iron spade from the orangery,” Cartethyia replied seriously. “And after we finish the south walk, we’re staying for dinner. Both of us. At the table in the small dining room, so you can meet her properly.”

Ciaccona’s head turned so fast her braid slapped her shoulder. “Hold on, cara. I have met Cantarella Fisalia. We stood on a wet dock together in the middle of a crisis, which is the safest way for a Toccata and a Fisalia to share air. Nobody said anything about sitting down with napkins.”

“Standing on a dock in the rain isn’t meeting someone,” Cartethyia said. She reached into her tunic pocket, drew out her Terminal, and held the lit screen up between them. “I sent her a message the night the relay came back. Look, she already answered.”

Ciaccona squinted down at the neat, square text glowing against the dark stone of the parapet.

*The south trench is marked out. Come on the first dry evening and bring your bard; I have told the kitchen she does not take pepper.*

For three seconds Ciaccona stared at the two sentences, while a cold, thoroughly unheroic weight dropped straight through the bottom of her stomach.

She knew Fisalia Manor from the outside—high grey sea-stone, iron-spiked water-gates, four centuries of poisoners and state councillors who kept their family receipts in locked cellars—and she knew what she had in her pack: one road-stained green doublet with the left cuff darned in mismatched thread, and a pair of boots that currently smelled of Xuanfang mud. She could see the whole evening laid out like a bad five-act opera: forty feet of waxed walnut table, six silver forks of diminishing size by her knuckles, a silent butler pouring white wine that might be vintage hill-grape and might be hemlock if she made a joke about the Order, and the Duchess sitting at the head of the board with those cool, exact eyes measuring just how unworthy a banished street-singer was of the Blessed Maiden’s company.

Down inside her wet boots, Ciaccona’s toes actually twitched toward the stair—the old, faithful reflex jumping up right on cue: *Tell Carte you have to check the lute-maker’s shop down by the ferry quay before dusk, buy a hot pie at the wharf, and come back when the soup plates are cleared.*

She caught herself half an inch into the turn, looked at her own boot-toe, and planted her heel flat back down on the flagstone.

“No pepper,” Ciaccona repeated aloud, pitching her voice light and easy as she leaned her elbow back on the merlon. “How touching. Does that mean the cook is seasoning the fish with nightshade instead, or do I get to bring my own silver spoon to test the gravy?”

“Cantarella doesn’t poison people at dinner,” Cartethyia said, frowning slightly at the slander. “That would be terrible manners. And she only uses the little silver forks when the harbour syndics come to argue about sulfur tariffs.”

“Even better. We’re getting the informal poison.” Ciaccona blew out a breath through her nose and knocked her boot lightly against Cartethyia’s ankle, forcing a grin. “Fine. I’ll come, and I’ll even borrow Suisui’s flat-iron for my collar so I don’t disgrace the knighthood. Though if she asks me what my father’s estate is worth, I’m playing a tavern jig on my soup-bowl.”

She looked back out over the dark gorge where the river vanished into the mist. Somewhere at the other end of that water, past the customs piers and the grand palazzos with their burned camellia hedges, was the low stone quay behind the Ragunna fish-market when the evening rain came down—a greasy paper cone of fried anchovies hot from the cauldron, six coppers’ worth of sour red wine in a tin cup, and Cartethyia standing close under one shared cloak with salt spray in her hair and nobody asking either of them for their names.

Beside her, Cartethyia lowered the Terminal into her pocket and rested her chin on her folded wrists over the parapet.

“She asked me two weeks ago what you liked to eat when we weren’t camping,” Cartethyia said in the exact same plain, unhurried voice. “Before the road closed. She doesn’t know how to talk to people she hasn’t rescued or threatened, and the house has been very quiet since the gates opened. She already had the gardener move the lemon-verbena pots out of the east bedroom because I told her damp air makes your lute-pegs slip.”

Ciaccona stopped with a second joke halfway to her teeth. She looked sideways at Cartethyia’s earnest, wind-flushed profile under the crooked crystal thorns, then down at her own splinted finger tucked in her scarf.

“The east bedroom,” Ciaccona muttered. “Right. Well. Tell her if the gardener leaves one spider in the shutters, I’m sleeping in the bakery at Egla.”

Down by their shins, the goose let out a sharp, imperious *honk*, stretched its long white neck forward, and bit the brass buckle of Ciaccona’s left boot hard enough to rattle the leather.

“Ow! Get off, you feathered turnip!” Ciaccona jerked her foot back, glaring down as the bird ruffled its tail and hissed triumphantly at her shin.

Cartethyia straightened up and reached down to pat the top of the goose’s flat white head, which it allowed with insufferable dignity. “He’s telling us the stone is cold on his feet,” Cartethyia said. “When we go down the south road to book the river-barge, we’ll need to buy a wide wicker crate with clean oat-straw so he has somewhere to sit on the deck.”

Ciaccona stared at her. “A wicker crate. For the barge.”

“He can’t walk all the way to the coast, Cia. We already talked about the gravel.”

“We are not taking a twenty-pound Huanglong goose on an ocean liner to Ragunna!” Ciaccona protested, throwing her good hand out toward the parapet. “He’ll bite the captain, eat the ship’s compass, and get us thrown overboard before we clear the straits!”

“A knight does not leave her household behind at a foreign well-trough,” Cartethyia replied serenely, already turning toward the parapet steps while the goose waddled closely at her heel. “Come on, your nose is red. We can ask Suisui how much a poultry ticket costs.”

“There is no such thing as a poultry ticket, cara!” Ciaccona called after her, and hurried down the wet stone steps to catch her sleeve before the goose took her spot on the inside rail.

Mei had both boots unlaced and her toes tucked under the edge of the warm quilt when the front door of the salt-house bumped open a third time.

Lupa came in first with Chisa right behind her, bringing a gust of cold mountain air and a massive, glaringly bright rust-red wool coat that looked as if it had been sheared off a very angry bear and buttoned with brass walnuts.

“Whoa,” Amy said from the middle of the kang, pausing mid-braid on her own damp hair. “Who’d you rob on the second terrace, Lupa? A circus general?”

“Timber-factor’s coat,” Lupa said, swaggering straight to the hearth without unbuttoning a single brass walnut and holding her wide sheepskin cuffs out toward the firelight. “Real wether fleece, double-stitched seams, fifteen coppers off the rack. Chisa checked the wool.”

“The seams are good,” Chisa confirmed, hanging her own black jacket neatly on the wall-peg before sitting down on her low stool to untie her damp laces.

By the chimney, Ruoyun looked up from her stool, took one look at the wet rust-red hem swinging six inches from Qingxiao’s sleeping knees, and pointed a sharp finger toward the mat. “Keep that dyed horse-blanket off the quilt, wolf, or you’re wringing it out in the yard.”

Qingxiao didn’t stir; she stayed propped upright against the warm brickwork under her two quilts, her head tipped slightly toward the mortar and her breath coming in slow, even puffs through her parted lips. On the grain-chest across from her, Buling had her round hat turned upside down in her lap like a basket and was tapping quietly at her screen with one thumb, her lips moving silently as she read a thread.

Down at the low end of the kang table, the sheet of Mengzhou paper lay flat beside Yangyang’s wiped ink-stone with a smooth grey pebble weighting the corner so the stove-draught wouldn’t curl it. The black characters down the page were dry now, dated at the top without a red seal anywhere on the sheet, and curled right beside the table-leg on the warm brick lay Hsin in her skinny fox shape, her one dark-tipped tail wrapped tight across her nose while her flank rose and fell.

Five minutes later the latch clicked again. Suisui and Yangyang stepped in out of the moonlight, followed immediately by Cartethyia, Ciaccona, and one loud, farewell *honk* from the well-trough outside before Ciaccona firmly hooked the wooden shutter shut from the inside.

“He has a dry corner under the eaves,” Ciaccona told Cartethyia, unwinding her damp scarf and tossing it over the bench-back. “And no, he cannot have a blanket.”

By the door, Suoming didn’t look up as they filed past her knees; she kept Nine Shadows upright between her boots on the wooden crate, her chin resting lightly on her stacked hands over the bamboo handle and her eyelids half-lowered against the lamp.

Suisui took the iron kettle off the side trivet of the brick stove, tested the weight with a flick of her wrist, and poured hot water into the big clay teapot, sending a fresh drift of roasted barley and white tea-leaf up to the rafters. Nobody pulled out a map or mentioned the morning carts. People simply shook out their damp hems, traded cold teacups for warm ones, and climbed onto the heated brick to claim their corners of the quilt.

Yangyang stepped over to the low pine table, picked up her dried ink-stone and capped brush—stepping carefully around the tip of the sleeping fox’s ear—and tucked them back into her canvas pack by the wall. Before she sat down between Suisui and Mei, she took two steaming cups from her sister’s tray and held one out to Mei.

“Thank you,” Mei said, wrapping both hands around the warm clay. She looked up at Yangyang’s quiet face in the amber lamp-light. “Hey. That walk by the bird-bridge on the canal—do the pear-cake stalls stay open when the weather turns warm?”

Yangyang’s mouth curved just a fraction at the corner as she sat down on the edge of the quilt, her sleeve brushing Mei’s elbow once before she folded her hands around her own cup. “All year,” she said. “In spring the willow branches hang all the way down to the water by the steps.”

“Good. Save me a cake with extra syrup.”

At the end of the kang, Iuno finished hanging her damp blue cloak over the drying-pole and came back to sit at Mei’s left side. Her silver-blue hair was still half-down over her collar where the single pin held the coil back from her brow. Before settling against the bolster, she drew the silver pin back out of the coil, let her hair fall loose over her collar again, and laid the pin neatly on the wooden ledge beside the small tin mirror, where it gave a tiny metallic *tink* against the frame.

“Six o’clock tomorrow morning, sweetheart,” Iuno murmured, leaning her good shoulder solidly against Mei’s arm and reaching for her tea. “Both hands on the mirror, and not a word about turnips.”

Mei grinned into her cup, leaned her head sideways until her cheek rested briefly against the silver-blue hair at Iuno’s temple, and took a slow swallow of hot tea while the stove-coals settled behind the grate.

## I. The Question in Each Room
### *Jinzhou, City Hall, morning after the moon turned full*

Jinhsi had written the opening sentence five times.

The first version began with *Citizens of Jinzhou.* The second began with *In this hour of common peril.* The third addressed *all who shelter beneath the youngest gate.* Each one arranged the city into a single listener and gave that listener a clean place to stand.

The actual city had four thousand one hundred and twelve people coming down from eleven villages, three hundred and twelve of them still waiting for assigned floors. It had fourteen wounded from the north line since midnight, one recovery yard below a school, a fish market whose west awning blocked the turning radius of Mengzhou’s largest cradle, and a tax-office goose asleep against a crate of resonance couplings.

The goose had acquired a blanket.

Jinhsi laid down her brush.

Changli sat across the table with her bandaged arm on the cloth Jinhsi had folded for her the previous night. She had read each opening. Her face had given every version the same warm attention.

“When I served as Secretary-General,” she said, “my office prepared forty-six addresses for flood seasons, harvest failures, border closures, and one locust year that became three separate political crises. The sentences traveled well. People heard them in kitchens, workshops, guardrooms, and fields. Each room selected a different meaning.”

“A speech gives fear a grammar.” Jinhsi turned the fifth sheet over. “It also gives the speaker a stage.”

“Then use tables. Ward meetings already have them.” Changli moved the stores return closer. “Read the figures. Bring officials who can answer questions about a particular street. Let the city remain plural.”

Jinhsi looked toward the terrace. The suspended sea had thinned with daylight. Its moon remained overhead, pale and complete, visible through the water like a coin at the bottom of a basin.

“The wards will ask what I intend to do.”

“Tell them what has been decided, what remains open, and when you will return.”

“You make uncertainty sound like an appointment.”

Changli’s mouth curved. “That skill fed me for twenty years.”

At the end of the table lay a sixth sheet. Jinhsi drew it toward her and wrote four headings:

**FOOD. SHELTER. WORK. DEPARTURE.**

Under each, she entered a place and an hour.

The public address went back to the speech desk with twelve clean sheets and a note instructing the copyists to attend their own wards. By breakfast, City Hall had scheduled nine meetings. By the second hour, there were eleven, because the floating-market ward and the people living beneath the south stair each objected to being combined with a wealthier street.

Jinhsi approved both objections.

The first meeting took place in the harbor guildhall, where the roof leaked into three blue bowls and the chairs had been borrowed from a noodle house that wanted them back by noon.

Mei carried the last six chairs in two stacks. Lupa carried the table alone, declined commentary on the matter, and set it down with one leg directly over a cracked tile. Amy and Iuno sat against the wall with Mrs Ren and three ledgers. Changli took the clerk’s place. Jinhsi stood at the table until Panhua came from the kitchen, put a bowl of congee beside her papers, and pointed at the chair.

The Magistrate sat.

Fifty-three people watched her take the first spoonful. The room’s shoulders descended by an amount Baizhi would have described as measurable.

Jinhsi set the spoon down.

“The northern drum reached four minutes and fifty-two seconds before dawn. General Jiyan holds the south shelf. Mengzhou has four heavy guardians in the line and one under repair below the terrace school. Jinzhou’s grain stores hold fifteen days at the present issue. The garrison kitchens begin millet substitution tonight, which extends the city stores by three days. The eastern road remains open for civilian traffic until the next assessment at sunset.”

She read the casualty figure. She read the number of carts. She read the floors.

“Three hundred and twelve people have transport assigned and lack indoor shelter at the receiving end. City Hall has inspected forty-one offered rooms. Twenty-six passed their first inspection. Eleven need locks, two need stove repairs, and two have floor damage. Those offers represent forty-seven possible places. The official remainder moves when the keys, household terms, and final inspections are filed.”

The guildhall listened.

A fish porter raised his hand. “My sister has two rooms above her salting shed. The stairs get slick, and the place smells powerful. Does that fail inspection?”

“The smell affects comfort,” Jinhsi said. “The stairs affect safety. The Bureau of Works can fit a rail this afternoon. Your sister sets the terms of the offer.”

“She’ll want the city to scrub the floor afterward. Those hill families bring half a mountain in on their boots.”

Clerk Wen wrote the condition into the offer column.

A woman in a brown apron lifted the ration notice. “You’ve counted grain. The well on Reed Lane went cloudy in last year’s rain. Three more households in that court will double the drawing.”

The ward engineer answered from the third row. “I can have a tank there by evening. The cart comes from the tannery at the fifth hour.”

“The tannery uses it,” the proprietor called from near the door. “Bring it back every morning before work, and I’ll release it overnight.”

Wen entered the hours.

The cooper whose shop supplied half the harbor stood with both hands on his knees. His eldest apprentice had joined the Rangers in spring. His youngest had a fever and was asleep upstairs while he spoke.

“The requisition man marked thirty barrel staves for stretcher splints,” he said. “Those staves are seasoned ash. I paid for them in winter. The paper says compensation follows the emergency schedule, and the schedule pays in the ninth month.”

Jinhsi looked at the Bureau of Works officer.

The officer opened his folder. “That is the current schedule.”

“He has six apprentices receiving weekly wages,” Jinhsi said. “Issue half from the emergency cash box today. The remainder becomes a named debt of the prefecture, due in the ninth month with the timber premium attached.”

The cooper rubbed one thumb along the other. “I’ll give you twelve staves at that price. The other eighteen finish the flour barrels I already promised.”

“Twelve,” Jinhsi said. Wen wrote twelve.

Near the wall, Iuno put a small mark beside the entry. The mark meant *consent with a stated limit.* She had invented it before sunrise, and Mrs Ren had improved its shape so a tired clerk could draw it in one continuous stroke.

A boatman offered his mule for three days. His brother withdrew the offer from the back of the room because the family planned to leave on that mule. The two men argued across four rows until Lupa leaned over and asked which one held the registration paper. The brother did. He kept the mule. His name entered the departure list beside five members of his household and one place requested on the south shuttle.

The first hour produced eleven indoor places, a water tank after dusk, twelve ash staves, six kitchen shifts, three handcarts, one room offered for two nights only, and a list of repairs longer than the list of rooms.

It also produced a retired schoolteacher who wanted every village child placed within walking distance of a lesson, a widow who asked City Hall to keep soldiers out of the chamber where her husband’s ancestral tablets stood, and a spice merchant who would release his loft after his pepper stock moved somewhere dry.

Amy found him a warehouse shelf through the floating-market channel. He wanted a receipt naming every sack.

“You’ll have one before the porters touch them,” Amy said. “Send me the count and the marks on the ties. I’m on the receiver until the sixth hour, and another operator takes the seat after that.”

The merchant gave her a longer look than the request required. Rumor had already made the girl into three incompatible things: a foreign singer, the Rover’s daughter, and a person who had once held a god inside the dark. Amy kept her pen over the form.

“Eighty-four sacks,” he said. “Blue ties on the hot pepper. Red on the mild.”

“That system seems designed to entertain somebody.”

“My wife thinks it’s funny.”

Amy wrote the colors exactly as given.

At noon the noodle house reclaimed its chairs. The meeting continued standing for fourteen minutes while Mei carried in benches from the tax office. The goose came with the second bench, riding under it in a position Chixia later described as a tactical insertion.

By the time the guildhall emptied, eleven places had cleared their final terms and filing. The remainder in Changli’s ledger fell from three hundred and twelve to three hundred and one.

The number had taken three hours to move eleven places.

Jinhsi stayed until the last person left. The last person was the cooper, who came back for the congee bowl he had accidentally carried halfway to the door.

Mei stood beside the stacked forms, drying rain from her marked hand on her trouser leg.

“I thought people would ask whether we’re going to win,” she said.

Jinhsi looked at the twelve-stave entry, the overnight tank, the pepper sacks, the mule heading south.

“Every question came back to where somebody sleeps, drinks, works, or leaves,” she said. “Ward Three begins at the bathhouse in forty minutes.”

“Those questions reach morning sooner than victory does.” Mei gathered half the pages. “I’ll bring the extra chairs.”

“The bathhouse has eight folding chairs and a bench beside the cold pool,” Jinhsi said. “Twelve more should fit along the tiled wall.”

The goose followed them to the door and stopped at the rain.

## II. Three Seals from Mingting
### *City Hall, the hour when the capital wakes*

The packets arrived together because the capital preferred disagreement in matching courier bags.

The first bore the Board of War seal. It directed every Mengzhou heavy combat platform beyond its registered defense district to return to Xuanfang by the thirtieth. The order cited two hundred and eleven kilometers of exposed western wall, declining replacement cores, and intelligence suggesting coordinated pressure across multiple frontiers. A country with seven gates had seven directions in which disaster could become urgent.

The second bore the Qiongqi of the Internal Security Agency. It suspended Senior Operative Qiuyuan, revoked his field discretion, ordered him to surrender his iron token, and instructed every civil and military authority to place him under secure escort for return to Mingting. His Rinascita report and every subsequent submission had entered evidentiary quarantine.

The third came from the Office of the Grand Marshal. Li Zhaoying’s column would reach the southern gorge road by the following evening. Jinzhou’s command was to preserve its current line, maintain the civilian route, and avoid entry into Norfall Valley until central command completed a theater assessment.

An enclosure from the Ministry of Sentinel Affairs observed that Hsin, registered resident of Mengzhou, occupation judicial, housing status ambulatory, had been sighted in Jinzhou during a meteorological event. The Ministry requested warm accommodation and accurate minutes.

Jinhsi read that enclosure twice.

“They have described a Sentinel’s breach of a military boundary as weather.”

Changli held out her hand. Jinhsi passed her the sheet.

“Minister Shen has retained his sense of proportion.” Changli read the final line, where a clerk had classified the nine-tailed sovereign as *partly cloudy, locally decisive.* “His office offers a jurisdictional shelter. The Board will contest it.”

“Can the shelter hold?”

“Through one round of memoranda. Two, if the Censorate assigns an auditor with an appetite for structure.”

Jinhsi opened Qiuyuan’s order again. “The ISA expects Jinzhou to arrest an armed senior operative during a Threnodian advance.”

“The Chief Commander expects the order to enter the record. His private expectation may occupy a smaller room.”

“You speak as though the Chief Commander expects a second interpretation.”

“I hired two of his predecessors and survived all three.” Changli placed the military orders side by side. “The Board of War sees Xuanfang. The Grand Marshal sees the road. Sentinel Affairs sees its charter. Each office has named a real danger.”

Jinhsi studied the seals. “Give me the available readings.”

Changli touched the first packet. “Old Seven remains in municipal repair custody. Its removal would kill the core; medical necessity keeps it here. Hsin’s four active guardians can be entered provisionally under the sovereign undertaking recorded in *A Record of Wind.* The frontier court accepted the Wind as a woman and Hsin’s promise as an act under her own authority. The Board will argue that the platforms remain military inventory. That argument takes time. Qiuyuan can answer a prefectural witness summons concerning the attempted murder of a Jinzhou officer. A local judicial obligation competes with his recall until the agencies confer. The Grand Marshal’s restriction concerns entry into Norfall. General Jiyan holds ground south of the boundary.”

“These readings buy several hours and attach a future dispute to every one of them.”

“Several hours can carry the upper villages across the road while it remains open.”

Jinhsi turned toward the terrace. Below, a recovery crew had begun moving Old Seven’s cracked ankle housing under canvas. Every lift required twelve people on two guide ropes while the automaton balanced its weight through three functioning legs. The boy with the model bridge stood outside the rope line, holding up a painted truss whenever the service lens turned his way.

“What does Jinzhou owe if I adopt those readings?”

Changli took out a clean page. “Repair stock to Xuanfang at replacement value. Transport for every surviving platform. An indemnity for exposure during their absence. Jurisdiction over Qiuyuan’s person and the duty to produce him when the summons ends. Your signature under an emergency compact that Mingting may call abuse of prefectural authority.”

“Would Mingting try to remove me from office?”

“A censure has strong odds. Suspension becomes plausible if the northern line fails. Victory would produce a committee.”

The completed moon moved behind a thicker current overhead. Light traveled across the room in pale bands.

Jinhsi reached for the compact. A smaller sheet slipped from beneath the Board packet and landed face up.

It carried an eleven-year chronology of Sentinel irregularities. The first date had been reproduced from a sealed Secretariat copy. Beside the date sat a reference number from Changli’s former office and the initials of the Secretary-General who certified it.

Jinhsi read the line. Her fingers settled on the table.

Changli’s face became very still.

“The lake’s backward answers began eleven years ago,” Jinhsi said. Her voice had the quiet exactness of a ruling. “The record in my archive gives four.”

“The first backward return came eleven years ago.”

“Your certification appears on both.”

Changli placed her scarred arm beside the packets. The folded cloth stayed beneath it, white against old feather-shaped burns.

“The Council opened a fitness inquiry when Jué chose you. Several members planned to argue that a fifteen-year-old receiving corrupted prophecy posed a danger to the gate. I moved the start of the anomaly into your fourth year in office. That chronology gave your administration an established record before the silence could be used against your appointment.”

Jinhsi’s gaze remained on the initials. “You altered Sentinel intelligence, maintained the altered date for seven years, and allowed me to govern from it.”

“I copied every original and kept them under my own seal. I also reviewed each runner’s map before it reached you. The decision belonged to me. Its consequences arrive here.”

“Article Nine of the Magistrates’ Code requires immediate disclosure of any material defect in prophetic intelligence. Article Twelve places the duty on the ranking counselor when the Magistrate’s capacity is implicated.”

“Both articles apply.”

The room carried the hammers from Old Seven’s repair. Twelve measured blows, a pause, then twelve again.

Jinhsi folded the eleven-year chronology along its existing crease. Her hands moved with great care.

“I asked you to stay last night, and you accepted while this packet was already traveling.”

“I accepted because you asked for the person in the room. I also knew the capital’s packet would arrive inside this window. The work and my fear used the same hours, and I gave the work every one of them.”

Jinhsi looked up then. Anger brought her face into repose. The gentleness stayed in her voice, and each word acquired an edge fine enough to mark paper.

“Your fear has governed my access to the truth since I was fifteen. I will hear the complete account at the sixth hour, with the original records on the table and Clerk Wen present. You will answer every question. Until then, these orders require the knowledge of a former Secretary-General, and I require the service of my Counselor. Trust will have its own meeting.”

Changli bowed her head. “I will prepare both rooms.”

Jinhsi signed the emergency compact.

She signed a prefectural summons for Qiuyuan as a material witness in an attempted unlawful execution, present location the northern field command, term forty-eight hours subject to renewal in person. She signed a repair bond for five Mengzhou platforms, four active and one under medical care. She entered the western exposure debt in Jinzhou’s name and ordered a duplicate sent to Magistrate Dou before the ink dried.

Then she wrote to the Grand Marshal:

> *Jinzhou holds south of Norfall and continues civilian movement. The valley remains outside the current order of march. General Jiyan will submit direct observations concerning the phantom formation. I have assumed the attached obligations under my seal. I request confirmation, amendment, or personal relief from office by a hand willing to assume them.*

Changli read it.

“The final clause will reach Li Zhaoying as a personal challenge.”

“It is addressed to the person carrying Falling Rain.”

“She has carried that sword for eleven years. The phrasing will be familiar to her hand.”

Jinhsi sanded the page. “Then she knows the weight of a named object.”

Outside, the boy lowered his model bridge. Old Seven lifted one repaired foot three inches from the stone and held it there while twenty-four workers listened to the bearing.

The service lens blinked once.

The boy raised the bridge again.

## III. The Man Whose Arrest Arrived First
### *the south shelf, where the phantom army kept its appointments*

Qiuyuan heard the detention order waiting for him at the camp gate.

Paper has a frequency once enough people have handled it. This sheet carried the dry rasp of the ISA corridor, the bronze mirror at its midpoint, the minute pressure change made by attendants replacing lamps in soft shoes, and beneath those the compact iron taste of a Chief Commander placing a necessary act into the record while reserving his private judgment.

The young Ranger holding it carried a different set of frequencies: two nights of broken sleep, a healing fracture in the left wrist, lamp oil on the cuff, and fear controlled through careful breathing.

Qiuyuan stopped outside sword reach and held out both hands.

“The order gives you authority to secure my weapon and token. My sword stays wrapped until General Jiyan decides where I stand. The token is in my inner coat. You may take it yourself, or I can place it on the gate table.”

The Ranger swallowed. “The prefectural summons says you’re a witness under Magistrate Jinhsi’s protection.”

“Both documents are valid within their own chains. Your general can determine which chain bears weight here.”

She looked over his shoulder at the road, perhaps hoping the law had sent somebody with sight and fewer swords.

“Put the token on the table, please. Keep the coat closed.”

Qiuyuan did. The Qiongqi landed with a low iron note. He unfastened his sword and laid it beside the token in its plain wrapping.

“Doctor Zhang’s flask is at my belt,” he said. “The contents are medicinal, very bitter, and unlikely to improve anyone’s afternoon.”

The Ranger’s breath changed around a laugh. She suppressed most of it and waved him through under escort.

The camp had moved twice since sunset. Jiyan’s green banner stood below the crest, shielded from the phantom line by a shelf of broken stone. Mengzhou’s four guardians occupied the rise above it: immense old combat platforms planted in a staggered arc, plates scarred by a century of weather, each broad shield facing north. Their cores sang in four related keys. A fifth voice answered faintly from Jinzhou, kilometers behind them, where Old Seven learned the pressure of a mended ankle.

Rain climbed from the ground around their feet.

Hsin sat on the shoulder of the eastern platform eating candied hawthorn.

Nine tails hung over the armor’s edge with sovereign carelessness. Her human form wore traveling silk the color of plum bark, dry beneath a small circle of clear air. A speaking relay, three engineer’s tools, and the Board of War’s recall order lay beside her. The recall had been folded into a square and placed under the hawthorn sticks so the wind would leave them alone.

A current came up from the south beneath the platform shields. It carried machine oil from Old Seven’s repair yard, wedding incense from Ward Six, wet ink from City Hall, and the cool mineral trace of a woman who had once crossed a dark between stories with one shell and one sentence.

Hsin set her hawthorn down and inclined her head.

The gesture belonged to sovereigns receiving sovereigns. Qiuyuan heard every engineer on the rise find sudden interest in a tool.

“You brought the summons personally,” Hsin said to the air. “Good. The capital brought thicker paper and less standing.”

The Wind moved once through her tails, accepted the greeting, and passed toward the black banner beyond the shelf. All four guardian cores adjusted their pitch to follow.

“Mingting sends me a wanted swordsman before breakfast,” she called down. Her voice carried easily through artillery. “Your capital manners have improved.”

Qiuyuan tilted his head toward her. “Sentinel Hsin. I hear your own order serving as a paperweight.”

“It has excellent stock. Authority should contribute where it can.”

General Jiyan stood at the map table beneath the banner. He had removed one gauntlet to annotate a casualty route and still wore the other. Mud reached his knees. His spear leaned within reach; a medical satchel lay open beside it, stocked and recently used.

“Senior Operative,” he said. “Your detention reached us twenty-six minutes ago. Your summons arrived seven minutes later. I have placed your token and sword under guard. Give me the reason Magistrate Jinhsi should spend a dispute with Mingting on your presence.”

Qiuyuan listened to the general’s stance: weight balanced, right shoulder tired, breath clear, one boot holding down the corner of a map that the upward rain kept trying to take.

“Three years ago, Geshu Lin entered Norfall under a command structure already touched by the same conspiracy that killed my master. Years before that battle, an order bearing Grandmaster Liang Dongyuan’s seal directed me to execute him for protecting civilians. I spared him because the act described in the warrant was righteous. The false order showed me that somebody could use the ISA’s hand.”

Jiyan’s bare fingers stopped over the map.

“You have evidence tying that order to the present field?”

“I have the residual frequency of the seal, a record from Rinascita, and an open case my service has chosen to close around me. The phantom beyond your shelf carries a trace from that first warrant. I came to hear how it entered him.”

Hsin bit through the sugar shell. “His Forte is wounded, General. He crossed half a country to listen with an instrument that charges him blood for every useful reading. This is either devotion or the most elaborate sick leave application in Huanglong.”

“Magistrate Dou transmitted the Board’s recall with her own inventory attached,” Jiyan said to her.

The fox’s ears turned toward Xuanfang. “Dou asked for a complete accounting and received one. Seven units were assigned to the Jinzhou approach. The Retroact filled their road with dead crews and old commands. Then the little gust carried Jinhsi’s recovery plan into Old Seven’s shield. It opened a service shutter for her and asked her to stay warm. Five units chose the march with their present crews. One returned to Jinzhou on a cradle. These four remain.”

She reached into her sleeve and produced a thin court copy whose title carried three variant settings and the same admissible blanks: *A Record of Wind*.

“This woman came to my terrace with fewer characters than a sentence and enough purpose to refuse a paradise printed for her. I promised her my line would walk when the war god came. She crossed the dark, found the hands I asked for, and brought my promise due. Xuanfang retains its working shield line. Mingting has asked me to value newer paper above an older word.”

“The order still governs the army surrounding your choices.”

Hsin folded another strip of hawthorn from the paper twist. “I have read every seal. I will keep the word I gave her, and Mingting may enter my reasons beside its penalties. Consequences improve the flavor of disobedience, General. I recommend small bites.”

Jiyan looked from the fox on the guardian to the blind swordsman under escort. “Both of you have arrived outside your instructions. The north shelf has limited room for private wars with Mingting. While you stand in my command, you answer field orders. If I tell you to withdraw, you use the road.”

Qiuyuan inclined his head. Hsin examined the red glaze on her candy and gave a gracious wave.

The phantom drum sounded.

Every drop of rising rain stopped at the height of a man’s heart.

Across the shelf, beneath the suspended sea, the dead army assembled from the ground upward. Boots formed first in the wet grass. Greaves, robes, hands, spear shafts, faces. The black command banner climbed last, torn along its lower edge. Its fabric carried an old wind that belonged to the third night of the Battle of Norfall and had spent three years crossing the same fold.

Geshu Lin came through the ranks.

The rain remembered him in ceremonial armor. Every plate shone. His hair lay in the arrangement used on memorial prints. Blood darkened one side beneath the ribs, placed with an artist’s understanding of where an audience would see it. He held his weapon at shoulder height, the point turned by exactly the angle carved into the statue in Victory Square.

Qiuyuan’s bamboo forest filled with iron.

Under the phantom’s frequency lay the living man he had spared: younger, impatient, breathing hard through a split lip while he stood between an ISA blade and three farmers. Around that true remnant, other hands had built layers. School recitations. Memorial incense. Official histories read aloud on the anniversary. An actor’s voice declaring a line to the back row. Applause, year after year, compressed until the applause sounded like distant artillery.

The phantom general looked at Jiyan.

“You carried bandages when I gave my last command,” he said. His voice arrived with a hall around it, every syllable shaped to reach the gallery. “Now you carry my spear and spend the army by inches. The valley remembers a wider hand.”

Jiyan stepped away from the map table. “Your last field order sent the wounded south and assigned me to triage. I completed it. Speak from the hour when you gave it.”

Geshu Lin’s face held its heroic calm. The expression had been painted on ten thousand festival sheets.

“One man stands where an army breaks. One name holds while ten thousand pass behind him.”

Sergeant Du made a small sound at the western gun.

Jiyan turned his head. “You know the line.”

“Second act of *The General at Norfall,* sir. They stage it in the ward schools every winter.” Du’s hands closed around the two halves of Deng Rui’s old bowl, which he had tied together with cord and carried at his belt. “My daughter played the southern messenger last year.”

Hsin slid from the guardian’s shoulder. She landed lightly beside the map, all nine tails lifted from the mud.

“Someone has fed him a role until the role learned to march,” she said. “Listen to the breath after each sentence. He carries an audience.”

Geshu Lin turned toward Qiuyuan.

The ceremonial face changed. A younger anger pressed through the painted calm, quick and human enough to move the old scar in Qiuyuan’s hearing.

“Mingting’s blind hound arrives after the gate has eaten its general.”

Qiuyuan stood with empty hands. His sword lay behind him at the camp gate. The black bamboo flask at his waist warmed as his pulse rose.

“The order against you carried a stolen authority. I declined it. The theft has continued through other hands, and I have come to follow its sound.”

“Your mercy bought the years that brought me here.”

“A life offered you its own account. Every day after that bore your signature.”

For one beat, the frequency beneath the armor cleared. Qiuyuan heard a man at twenty-one staring at an unexpected future with rain on his face.

Then the audience breathed.

Geshu Lin raised his weapon to the memorial angle.

“Norfall holds the final act. Bring your verdict there.”

The army turned as one body. Its first rank marched north, away from Jinzhou, boots entering the valley road in the exact diagonal used for the play’s closing tableau. Thirty phantom spears lowered together. Behind those thirty moved hundreds of lesser impressions, crowded into theatrical spacing built for thirty, shoulders passing through shoulders as the actual dead filled it.

The upward rain followed them.

Jiyan waited until the last black edge of the banner passed beyond the ridge. Then he knelt at the map and drew the phantom route from memory.

Sergeant Du found a charcoal nub and sketched the stage blocking beside it.

The lines matched.

At the third turn, where the actual battle road ran west around a sinkhole, both drawings went east toward a memorial stair built the year after the battle.

Jiyan looked at Qiuyuan. “Can you separate the man’s frequency from the performance?”

“For several breaths. The cost will increase inside the valley.”

Hsin crouched over the two drawings, tails moving slowly behind her. “The play gives the dead a route, the city gives them an audience, and Ovathrax gives the route weight. Three crafts have been joined with great skill.”

“We need the full script,” Jiyan said. “Every revision, every song, and the original casualty return.”

Du reached for the relay.

Jiyan caught his wrist before he struck the call tone.

“Use the speaking line. Leave the drum quiet.”

Across the valley, something vast shifted beneath the hanging sea, listening for its cue.

## IV. The Price, Itemized
### *eleven wards, one morning, and the road remaining open*

The next interval was four minutes and thirty-one seconds.

It arrived during the meeting at Ward Three bathhouse. The water in the cold pool rose in one flat sheet, held the reflection of the completed moon for half a breath, and struck the tiles. Twenty-seven people looked down together. The bathhouse proprietor climbed onto a stool, inspected a crack along the blue glaze, and asked the Bureau of Works to enter one tile against the emergency.

Then she offered the upper drying gallery for nineteen villagers.

“The ward has issued a gathering advisory.”

Her upstairs room entered the shelter inspection list for a term beginning after she surrendered the key.

The answer waited in the stairwell with them.

“It’s coming closer,” Mei said. “The Rangers are holding the road, and the city is moving people while they can. I wish I had a cleaner answer.”

The woman adjusted the blanket over her youngest child. “Clean answers usually arrive after the floor’s been swept.” She took the handcart handles from Mei at the bottom. “Go carry somebody else’s. Mine rolls straight.”

Mei watched her join the queue. Then she went back up for the room key and the inspection slip.

At the freight yard, Lupa found nine volunteers trying to lift a resonance brace with a rope rated for fish barrels.

“Four people on each guide line, two on the rear, and everyone eats before the next lift,” she said. “The brace can wait six minutes for breakfast. Your crushed foot will wait months.”

The yardmaster sent for chain. Lupa took a bowl from the garrison kitchen, ate standing beside the pallet, and watched all nine volunteers finish theirs.

“Steady thy hands,” Fleurdelys told the artificers in her deep ceremonial voice. “The elder shall stand.”

The chief artificer had spent twenty-eight hours awake and accepted divine encouragement at face value. “Two degrees inward, Blessed Maiden. Hold there.”

Fleurdelys held.

The boy approached the service lens when the guide ropes slackened. His bridge model had one tower painted green and the other blue.

“I think it needs a name,” he told Cartethyia’s vast armored knee.

“Old Seven has carried that one for a long while,” Fleurdelys said. “A name may grow while it serves. What would you add?”

The boy considered the cracked housing, the fresh bearing, and the platform leveled for him through the night.

“I would add Bridge-Looker, because he watches the middle part rise.”

Cartethyia came back in a rush of cloth and light. She wrote **OLD SEVEN BRIDGE-LOOKER** on the repair slate in letters large enough for the service lens to read.

The core’s fifth note strengthened by half a tone.

Baizhi looked up from the diagnostic table. “Repeat the name.”

The boy did. The core repeated the change.

The lamp was lit.

Sergeant Tan placed a paper label beside it: **WU’S. LEAVE ROOM.**

Old Seven Bridge-Looker turned its service lens toward the steam.

The tea unit counted from one in the cook’s voice.

At cup thirty-four, the ground sounded again.

Four minutes and thirteen seconds.

The kettle paused. Steam leaned north. Then the unit resumed counting.

By the fifth hour, the city had offered two hundred and six bed-nights, eighty-seven worker-shifts, twenty-two carts, one mule for three days, twelve ash staves, four rooms with family shrines excluded, six rooms after their owners departed, a tannery tank after dusk, a theatre loft after the matinee, and a courtyard whose owner required the chickens to retain priority.

It moved.

The pages rose from her case in order.

Now she moved through a city full of hands holding guide ropes, fitting bolts, tying red ribbon around a hammer, copying names, and raising one repaired bridge for an old machine to study.

At Old Seven’s lower shutter she rested in the warm gap the automaton had opened for her at Xuanfang. The service lens turned from the model bridge to the air. Its fifth core note carried north through four related shields.

Hsin heard it on the front and smiled around the last sour edge of her hawthorn.

## V. The Hero at the Back of the Stage
### *Bookbinders’ Row Theatre, where the curtain had learned a battlefield*

The theatre stood three streets from City Hall and one story above a printing shop. Every heroic death shook dust into the pressroom. The printers complained annually, the actors sent tickets, and the arrangement had entered its thirty-second year.

*The General at Norfall* had been written three months after the battle.

Its first edition ran two acts and eleven scenes. The seventh revision ran ninety minutes, used thirty soldiers in the chorus, and ended with Geshu Lin alone beneath a black banner while the ensemble marched into darkness around him. Ward schools performed a shortened second act each winter. The city theatre played the full text on the battle’s anniversary. Touring companies had carried it as far as Xuanfang and Mingting.

When Ciaccona arrived, the afternoon cast was rehearsing the final march for an audience of empty seats and sixty-four villagers settling into the loft above them.

The actor playing Geshu Lin had his weapon at the memorial angle. Thirty chorus members crossed behind him on the east diagonal. A drum marked three beats, held through a space, and struck once more.

Ciaccona’s blank card darkened in her hand.

Three grey marks. The closing interval.

“Keep playing,” she called from the aisle.

The conductor looked at the lute on her back, recognized a professional emergency, and brought the ensemble around again.

Amy set a field recorder on the center seat. Chisa walked the perimeter of the stage, listening to feet. Yangyang spread Jiyan’s two drawings across the prompter’s table. The wind arrived over her shoulder and made every program in the front row turn one page.

Cartethyia sat cross-legged beside the prop spears with the original casualty return in her lap.

The march began.

Thirty actors moved east. At the third drumbeat, the Geshu actor planted his rear foot. At the held space, the chorus turned their heads toward him. The final beat sent them forward while he remained under the banner.

Ciaccona played the drum figure on her lute.

The suspended sea above the tiled roof drew into one long ripple. Somewhere north, too far for ordinary sound, an answering impact reached the strings.

“Again,” she said.

The conductor lifted his hands.

Chisa stood near the wings with her eyes on the blur of colored faces and her attention on everything she trusted more: breath, footfall, leather strain, the advance warning in a shoulder before a turn.

“The chorus begins leaning toward the exit during the held space,” she said. “The drum releases a movement their bodies have already started.”

The actor playing Geshu lowered his weapon between repetitions. “That’s how the blocking works. We build the charge under him, hold for the line, then give the audience the last beat.”

“What does the audience do?” Amy asked.

“They usually stand. On the anniversary, veterans strike the floor with their canes.”

Ciaccona plucked the three notes again and listened past them. “The phantom carries that impact under its voice. Applause, feet, canes, school desks. Three years of people completing the bar.”

The conductor’s hands fell to his sides. “Are you saying the play caused the army?”

“A song can become a road after enough feet use it. Ovathrax found this one paved.” Ciaccona looked toward the loft, where the first village children had discovered the costume baskets. “The play carries the city’s love, grief, and appetite for a death that fits inside two acts. Those are excellent materials.”

The Geshu actor sat on the edge of the stage. Sweat had darkened the collar under his armor. Up close, he was forty-five and tired, with one knee bound beneath the costume.

“My brother died there,” he said. “I took the role because the first actor made him one of the chorus. Third spear from the left. He gets all the way across.”

Cartethyia ran one finger down the casualty return. “What was his name?”

“His name was Shen Ke. He hated pears and made our mother cook them separately.”

She found him on the fourth page. Signalman, north battery. Recovered on the second day from the west sinkhole road.

Yangyang put Jiyan’s field map beside the stage plan. The west sinkhole road lay nine lines from the chorus route.

“The script sends him east,” she said. “His unit went west.”

The actor took off Geshu Lin’s helmet. “The playwright had a communiqué and three survivor interviews. My mother says everyone was still speaking in funeral voices.”

Cartethyia drew a paper figure from the spoiled edge of a program and wrote **SHEN KE** across its chest. She placed it on the west line of the field map.

Then she cut another.

The theatre’s archive held seven scripts, six prompt books, twelve bundles of reviews, and one letter from a retired Ranger objecting to the position of the medical tents. Amy and the stage manager carried all of it down to the front seats. The villagers in the loft sent three older children to help sort because the costume baskets had lost their novelty after someone found a mouse.

The first edition ended with a full company tableau. Geshu Lin and every named officer stood together under the banner. The second separated the wounded onto a raised platform. The third gave Geshu the final line. The fifth removed the names of the chorus to meet a school runtime. The seventh added the three-beat march and printed a request for the audience to complete the final beat by standing.

Ciaccona stacked the prompt books by revision and played each ending.

The card’s grey marks deepened as the music approached the current version.

At the fifth, the Tollhide Echo in her pack began to chime.

At the seventh, the prop weapons trembled against the rack.

The wind left Yangyang’s shoulder.

It crossed the stage low, struck the black banner, and scattered three years of dust through the work lights. The actor’s weapon tipped in its rack. One spear slid free, struck the boards, and came to rest with its point facing the dressing rooms.

Yangyang looked at the programs in the front row. Every copy had opened to the cast list.

The names rose and fell under the passing air.

“Senior,” she said softly. “You’re reading the people.”

The wind pressed the cast pages flat.

Chisa had reached the oldest prompt book. She touched the indentation beside the final line. “Someone erased a direction here.”

The stage manager brought charcoal dust and laid it lightly across the groove. Amy held the page at an angle. Letters appeared in the paper’s pressure, written by the first director and removed before publication.

**THE GENERAL LOOKS BACK. THE COMPANY REMAINS VISIBLE.**

The printed direction beneath it read:

**THE GENERAL FACES NORTH. BLACKOUT.**

The Geshu actor read both lines from the stage.

“I’ve faced north for three years,” he said. “The company clears during my speech. By blackout, the stage belongs to me.”

Cartethyia had cut twenty-six figures. She placed them along the true roads in Jiyan’s map, one name at a time. Several remained in her lap, waiting for the casualty return to tell her where they had stood.

“He keeps receiving an empty stage,” she said. Her voice had gone quiet and country plain. “That is a very long time to hold a pose.”

The ground beat again.

Three minutes and fifty-eight seconds.

Dust fell from the rafters into the printing shop. Below them, a compositor swore, covered his forme, and kept setting the sixth-hour stores notice.

Amy stopped the recorder. “I’ll send Jiyan the revisions, the erased direction, and both maps. Ciaccona, give me the bars as separate files. Chisa, dictate the movement notes while I label them.”

The Geshu actor rose. “Send my brother’s actual road.”

Cartethyia handed him the paper figure. “You should keep him until we know where the map goes.”

He held Shen Ke in his gauntlet.

Onstage, the black banner moved in the wind from another life. The cast list stayed open across the front row, sixty-three names breathing under the work lights.

The theatre loft filled with the sound of bedframes being assembled.

## VI. The Counselor’s Two Rooms
### *City Hall, sixth hour: one for the record and one for the work*

Changli prepared both rooms as promised.

The record room contained the original lake returns, her altered chronology, the Council’s draft fitness inquiry from seven years earlier, and Clerk Wen at a side table with fresh ink. Changli placed a resignation beneath the inkstone. Its date was today’s. The seal space remained empty.

The work room next door contained the northern map, the capital’s four replies, the shelter register, the theatre packet, three speaking relays, and lunch.

The wall between them was paper.

Jinhsi entered the record room first.

She read the Council draft from beginning to end. At fifteen, she had been called *the juvenile appointee* seven times in twelve pages. Her first flood order appeared as evidence of “impulsive sympathy.” Her request for expanded kitchen stores appeared under “poor fiscal restraint.” A paragraph concerning the Sentinel’s backward dreams had been prepared with a blank date and a reference to the removal provisions of the Magistrates’ Code.

Changli had moved the date, sealed the copy, and made the paragraph useless to them.

Jinhsi set it beside the true lake return.

“Your alteration preserved my appointment through that inquiry.”

“That was my purpose, and I believed the urgency gave me sufficient authority.”

“You also preserved a Council that kept governing me through the information you withheld.”

Changli’s fingers rested along the feathered scar at her wrist. “Yes. I placed myself between you and their judgment, then allowed that position to become a private office with one member. The first act had a deadline. The following seven years became habit, skill, and fear.”

Wen’s brush moved. The sentence entered the record in Changli’s own hearing.

Jinhsi opened the earliest original. The lake had shown a lower city under water, a red cap on the wall, and the library burning from its center. The copied version preserved every image. Only the date had changed.

“How many decisions did this alter?”

“I can identify eleven with direct effects and twenty-six where influence remains possible. The full index is attached. I favored additional flood stores, earlier archive duplication, and the fourth-level survey. I steered your attention while telling myself the direction already belonged to you.”

“The Grand Library survey was still wrong.”

“My correction reached three floors. The hidden chamber sat behind the fourth.”

Jinhsi’s hand went flat on the old page. “You were fallible and arranged my government around your infallibility.”

Changli received the sentence with her eyes open. “I did.”

From the work room came Amy’s voice on the relay, fast and clear, requesting the current shelter remainder from Ward Six. A second voice gave two hundred and twenty-four. Somebody cheered in the background and was immediately told that seven stove inspections remained.

Jinhsi looked toward the paper wall.

“I am angry enough to decide your future badly.”

“The resignation gives you a clean instrument.”

“You made a choice on my behalf once already.” Jinhsi drew the unsigned page from beneath the inkstone and read it. “Put this in the day-book envelope. I will answer it after I can distinguish the needs of Jinzhou from the injury you have done me. Until then, you serve under the terms already recorded, and Wen attends every discussion concerning the Sentinel.”

“I understand the restriction and will follow it.”

“Your access to sealed prophetic material is suspended. Copies move through my hand and Wen’s. Your authority over the registry channel ends at sunset. Give me the name before the seventh hour.”

Changli’s breath changed once. “His name is Luo Zemin. Registry examiner, second class. His daughter studies civil resonance architecture at Huaxu Academy. The debt used to recruit him was manufactured through a grain partnership in the east district. He believes each disclosure diverts attention from her.”

Wen wrote the name.

Jinhsi closed the Council draft. “He receives a hearing, protection for his daughter, and counsel. Internal Security receives the evidence after his household is secure.”

“He has passed useful intelligence for two years.”

Anger quieted Jinhsi’s face further. “Then you may explain to him how usefulness changes the due process owed to a frightened father.”

Changli bowed.

The ground struck beneath the building.

Three minutes and forty-three seconds.

The ink in Wen’s dish climbed the inner rim and settled. From the work room, all three relays began speaking.

Jinhsi stood. “We continue this account after the next ward round.”

They crossed the paper threshold together.

The capital had used the last half hour well.

The Board of War rejected Hsin’s undertaking, classifying *A Record of Wind* as a literary court entry and citing the platforms’ serial marks and maintenance appropriations. Sentinel Affairs returned the frontier court’s ruling that the blanks were admissible, Hsin’s finding that the Wind resembled a woman, and the witnessed promise concerning the war god. It attached Hsin’s census card and requested that the Board identify the statute by which a ministry could release a Sentinel from her own word. The Censorate opened an inquiry into both positions and requested the repair yard’s tea receipts.

The ISA challenged Qiuyuan’s summons as interference with national security. Its Chief Commander added one handwritten line beneath the challenge:

> *Secure custody means alive, present, and capable of testimony. General Jiyan is competent to count all three.*

The Grand Marshal’s field direction reached them last. Changli read it standing over the map.

“Li Zhaoying has taken the dispute into her moving headquarters. Every office must reach her on the gorge road to contest the provisional entries. That buys us until she arrives or the line changes.”

“She keeps Norfall closed.” Mei stood at the opposite side of the table, rain on her shoulders and a red wedding ribbon tied around one wrist by a bride who needed both hands free for a hammer. “Jiyan’s packet says the phantom keeps going there.”

“The restriction preserves command while the evidence arrives,” Jinhsi said. “It also keeps people south of the valley for one more interval.”

Iuno had been reading the emergency authority schedule. She tapped three adjacent clauses with one finger. “The Board can order platform withdrawal. Sentinel Affairs can recognize a retinue. The Grand Marshal can retain allied forces in theater. Three authorities have produced one hour of practical ambiguity.”

Changli poured tea into six cups. Her right hand remained steady. “Several offices can prevent one office’s error from becoming the whole country. The same arrangement once took a season to buy a hinge.”

Jinhsi looked through the shelter ledger. “The drum gives us minutes, and compulsion can move faster. I can requisition every room on this list.”

“You can,” Changli said. “The order would place two hundred and four offered spaces into immediate use. It would also erase household conditions, departure plans, wage terms, and several locked doors. Enforcement would consume Patrollers already moving people.”

“The verified list since dawn has reached ninety-two.”

“Ninety-seven,” Amy said from the relay desk. “Ward Six just cleared the weavers’ hall kitchen. The groom’s mother inspected it herself and made the fire officer repeat the chimney test.”

Jinhsi turned the legal schedule over. “Continue the offers. Use directed requisition for military yards, public buildings, and goods already contracted to the prefecture. Private rooms remain under household terms. Emergency cash pays labor at the posted rate through midnight.”

Wen entered the order.

This decision spent money, hours, and the faster certainty of force. It left the doors with the people who owned them.

Jinhsi picked up the theatre packet.

“Now show me what the dead have been rehearsing.”

## VII. A Map of the Performance
### *five cities on three relays, speaking before the next beat*

They put the seventh script over Jiyan’s map.

The stage’s east diagonal crossed the old sinkhole road. The memorial stair sat where the playwright had placed a painted ridge. Geshu Lin’s final mark covered the valley’s central stone, a black circle drawn around a point every field record identified as the place Ovathrax first surfaced three years earlier.

Cartethyia’s named paper figures stood along the actual roads. Forty-three had arrived from the theatre before the runner ran out of hands. More were coming in a program box.

The north relay opened with artillery in the background.

Jiyan spoke first. “We received the revisions. The phantom formation has returned to the valley mouth and taken the seventh edition’s final positions. The actual dead continue to surface through the chorus spacing. Bodies overlap where the stage held thirty.”

Qiuyuan’s quieter voice followed. “The performance layer has increased since the theatre rehearsal. Repetition reaches the field immediately through the Retroact rain. The true signatures remain beneath it. I can hear individual variation when a name is spoken with a verifiable detail.”

Cartethyia leaned toward the relay. “Try Shen Ke, signalman, north battery. He was recovered from the west sinkhole road on the second day. His brother says he hated pears.”

The line carried Jiyan issuing the name to Sergeant Du. Then wind, distant movement, one gun crew breathing around the silence.

Qiuyuan returned. “A figure in the third rank turned west. The others continued north. The separation lasted two seconds.”

Cartethyia took a pencil and put a small mark beside Shen Ke’s figure.

Ciaccona laid her blank card on the map. “The march resolves on a beat supplied by an audience. We can change the music here, and the Retroact carries the rehearsal north. The phantom absorbs each repetition. A change may feed it another route with equal speed.”

Hsin laughed softly through the relay. “A bard discovering responsibility in distribution. Your father would be unbearable about this.”

“He earned the right.” Ciaccona plucked one muted string. “I need to know what happens during the held space at the valley.”

Jiyan answered. “Geshu Lin waits. The rank looks toward him. Ovathrax’s ground frequency rises until the final beat arrives from the phantom drum.”

“And when your own drum stays quiet?”

“The formation waits longer. The Threnodian supplies the beat after approximately four breaths.”

Iuno looked at the interval card. “It can complete its own instruction.”

“For now,” Hsin said. “The creature is accustomed to a country helping. Habits grow expensive when the room changes.”

Jinhsi sat at the head of the table with her hands on either side of the map. “Tell me what the evidence gives us today.”

Jiyan answered from the north relay. “The dead march the seventh edition’s road. A verified name can separate one person from the chorus for approximately two seconds. Our battle drums strengthen the final cue, so my units are using voice and whistle. The Grand Marshal’s Norfall restriction remains in force.”

Qiuyuan’s quieter voice followed. “The stolen seal continues beneath Geshu Lin’s true frequency and enters the valley with him. My reading from the shelf ends at the boundary. The bamboo decoction may extend it, at a cost Baizhi will describe more forcefully than I can.”

Baizhi joined from the school-apron line. “His documented Forte damage makes amplified use medically hazardous. I have requested the formulation and a baseline waveform. Doctor Zhang may defend the proprietary tenth root directly to me.”

Hsin let the physicians’ future quarrel pass with a pleased hum. “The Wind produces another observation. The phantom banners turn toward her, and Old Seven’s present core follows her through the same rain. She carries a record of Jinzhou from a printing Ovathrax has already consumed. She also carries fewer characters than a sentence. Treating her as an expendable instrument would be a remarkably efficient way to join the war god’s play.”

Yangyang’s hand closed around the edge of the table. The Wind pressed warm air against her knuckles.

“She sets her own route,” Yangyang said. “I can offer relay stations and tell her where the weather is strongest. She decides what she carries.”

“Good. Then her passage carries her consent, and every weather report she requests will reach her,” Hsin said, with the respect of one ruler recognizing the terms of another.

“Every ending I draft begins acting like a destination,” Ciaccona said. “The Retroact carries our rehearsals north as readily as theirs travel south. Three possible conclusions have already offered themselves, and each one wants to become another cage. I need the missing field voices before I write a bar.”

Cartethyia cut another figure from a program margin. “Forty-three names have roads. The casualty return has many pages left.”

Chisa listened to the scissors close. “The erased direction keeps the company visible. Someone removed it before the city learned the play. We should find the person who saw that first staging.”

Jinhsi entered each statement on a clean sheet. Evidence. Limits. Missing witnesses. Medical risk. The Wind’s terms. The blank beneath **COURSE OF ACTION** remained the widest part of the page.

Mei’s hand hovered above the valley’s black circle. Gold moved faintly along the branch of her mark.

“The rain chamber showed me beside Jué, standing in the middle of everything with a sword. Nivora wanted that picture to become a door. Geshu Lin has been inside his picture for three years.”

Amy pulled her chair closer. Iuno’s living hand stayed open on the table between them.

Jinhsi looked at Mei. “Can you see a way to bring him out?”

“I can see the shape that wants to take me in.” Mei took time before the rest. “The way back for everyone is still missing.”

The room accepted the missing answer.

Jiyan’s line carried a quick order away from the relay, then returned. “The valley remains outside today’s movement orders. We continue observation, extraction, and names.”

Jinhsi’s gaze moved across the people in the room: Mei with the mark lit under her skin; Amy at the recorder; Iuno beside the legal schedule; Ciaccona holding an unfinished interval; Cartethyia cutting paper people; Chisa listening to the building; Yangyang with the Wind reading over her shoulder; Changli at the capital packets; Wen writing all of them into one set of minutes.

“I still don’t know how to ask people to bear this,” she said.

Changli’s scars rested openly on the table. “As Secretary-General, I addressed *the people* because the phrase fit one line and one seal. The cooper heard ash staves. The bathhouse owner heard coal. The mother on the south stair heard three shuttle seats. They have answered the figures in their own units. The other sentence belongs to a later room.”

Jinhsi looked at the empty course-of-action field.

“We have yet to earn that room.”

She signed the seventh-hour stores notice, the next eleven shelter inspections, the medical reserve release, and a direction for the Grand Library to send original casualty returns under guard. She requested the first production files for *The General at Norfall*, including correspondence with survivors and every erased stage direction the archive could recover.

The ground beat.

Three minutes and thirty-one seconds.

The completed moon brightened through the suspended sea. Every relay hissed with the same northern rain.

Norfall remained a black circle on the map.

## VIII. The Hours Before the Answer
### *the work that continues while a sentence is missing*

At the north shelf, Qiuyuan signed receipt of his recall.

His Qiongqi token remained in Jiyan’s field coffer. His wrapped sword lay beside it. Beneath his signature he entered his current status in a hand trained by the same corridor that wanted him home:

**Material witness. Jinzhou prefectural summons. Field custody. Testimony in progress.**

The Ranger at the coffer read the line, checked the seals, and gave him a copy. Qiuyuan folded it into his coat beside Doctor Zhang’s bamboo flask.

Hsin used more paper.

Her answer to the Board of War included the Xuanfang inventory, the names of the five present crews who had chosen the eastern march, Old Seven’s recovery status, the four shield positions under Jiyan’s command, and the frontier court’s certified excerpt from *A Record of Wind.* At the end she wrote:

> *I gave my word when this woman carried fewer characters than a sentence. She has since crossed four seas, returned with many hands, and placed Jinzhou’s living road in mine. Five guardians answered with their crews. One receives repair in the city. Four hold the shelf.*
>
> *Xuanfang retains its working line, its magistrate, its court, and every opinion it has ever held about me. The Board may add its opinion to the proper column.*
>
> *My line walks until the promise is answered.*

She pressed one paw-print beneath it.

The Welcoming Wind arrived while the ink was wet. Hsin lifted the page with both hands, a courtesy the engineers had seen her offer to exactly three people in living memory.

“Read it before you carry it,” she said. “A promise concerning you belongs in your weather too.”

The page rose at its lower edge. The Wind moved across every line, paused at Old Seven’s name, and folded the copy once along the court clerk’s original crease.

Hsin opened her hand.

The Wind took the paper south.

Jiyan kept Jinzhou’s battle drum quiet. The phantom drum continued beyond the ridge. Between its calls, Sergeant Du and the theatre archive began assembling people from the chorus: a signalman who hated pears, a cook with a daughter born after the battle, a cartographer who drew maps because she liked maps, two brothers filed under one date, and every soldier whose verifying detail could reach the shelf through rain.

The names produced seconds. Jiyan recorded the seconds. Qiuyuan listened beneath them. Geshu Lin waited at the valley mouth in ceremonial armor, holding the weapon angle the city had taught him to hold.

The waiting remained evidence.

In Jinzhou, the bride in red shoes finished the last privacy screen and went downstairs to marry a man with sawdust in his hair. The bathhouse passed nineteen bedrolls. The freight-yard chain lifted six hundred and nineteen jin while ten fed workers called the count. Bookbinders’ Row began setting names from the casualty return with sorts borrowed across eleven shops.

The shelter remainder fell from two hundred and twenty-four to two hundred and fifteen.

Nine people acquired floors through one repaired stove, two shrine exclusions, a pepper loft cleared under receipt, and a room whose owner had reached the south shuttle and sent the key back with the driver.

The number stayed above zero. The road stayed open. The work continued.

Changli’s resignation remained in the day-book envelope. Her authority over prophetic records had ended. Her knowledge of Mingting still moved through the work room, where she answered three ministries, protected Luo Zemin’s daughter before the registry examiner received his hearing, and prepared the complete account Jinhsi had required. She and her Magistrate spoke through Clerk Wen on every matter concerning Jué. Each sentence carried its new distance.

At the seventh hour, Jinhsi returned to the terrace with the course-of-action sheet.

Mei stood there under the completed moon. Her red wedding ribbon had collected two spots of machine grease. She had spent the last hour carrying bedframes, answering three questions with figures, and feeling her mark turn north whenever the phantom drum reached the city.

Jinhsi placed the sheet on the rail between them. Evidence filled the upper half. The field beneath **COURSE OF ACTION** remained empty.

“My office keeps offering me a proclamation,” she said. “One voice, one seal, one city arranged to receive it.”

Mei rubbed the grease into the ribbon with her thumb. “The rain chamber favors its own picture too. It puts me at the center with a sword and sends everyone else out of frame.”

The Wind came through the terrace carrying Hsin’s folded answer. It set the page beside Jinhsi’s empty field and opened it. The paw-print lay dark beneath the promise. Along the lower margin, the court copy of *A Record of Wind* showed three missing characters as clean spaces in the line.

Jinhsi read it. Mei read it beside her.

Below them, Ward Eight began its meeting in the courtyard of a closed dye house. The first speaker requested payment for indigo spoiled by the rain. The second offered a boiler crew. The third wanted the latest report on her son at the south shelf. Their voices reached the terrace separately.

“She knew what she was asking the Wind to do,” Mei said.

“She also gave her a choice before the kite left.” Jinhsi rested her fingers beside Hsin’s paw-print. “I have the rule. I still lack the sentence.”

Mei looked toward Norfall. Gold warmed the branch of her mark and faded.

“I’m still looking for mine.”

They left the course-of-action field open.

There would be another account from Changli, another packet from the Grand Marshal, more casualty names from the library, more waveform results from Baizhi, more ward meetings, more arguments about doors and roads and whose hand owned an answer. Jiyan still had a living shelf to hold. Qiuyuan still had a stolen seal to hear. Hsin still had a promise walking on four shielded bodies. The Wind still carried fewer words than a sentence and more history than any file could set.

Norfall remained north of all of them.

Geshu Lin waited there with his soldiers inside a final act whose first direction had been erased: **THE GENERAL LOOKS BACK. THE COMPANY REMAINS VISIBLE.**

Ovathrax waited for the final beat.

Jinzhou spent the hours before the answer in many rooms.

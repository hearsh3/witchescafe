# Mannheldur

*]unless the student presents in person[*

The tall winter boots from the borehole trip were still standing by the radiator with Thursday’s *Exchange Gazette* stuffed tight into the ankles to draw the damp out of the felt liners, and whenever the steam-pipe gave a knock the dry newsprint rustled inside the leather like a mouse in a wainscot.

Sigrika sat propped against the iron bedstead with the patchwork quilt pulled up to her collarbone and a cold buckwheat pancake folded into quarters in her right hand. She had spread it too thick with her mother’s bilberry jam before coming upstairs, so the purple syrup was leaking out the bottom seam onto her wrist; she ate the whole thing in four rapid bites to beat the drip, licked the heel of her palm clean, and immediately wished she had brought a second one up from the bread-crock.

On the blotter under the amber desk lamp, the rest of her pockets had been turned out in a neat row: her brass candidate’s seal in its leather pouch, the small flat oval of greywacke from the Borehole Four scree sitting beside the jam jar, and her three pens—red, blue, green—laid flush with the fore-edge of her field journal.

She wiped her sticky fingers on the corner of her handkerchief, leaned over the side of the mattress, and fished into the deep outer flap-pocket of her heavy field coat where it hung on the bedpost.

Her fingers closed around the smooth, palm-sized plastic shell of the Dream Maker.

She had not run it since Wednesday night before they went down the Riseway shaft. If you left the reservoir sitting idle for more than four or five days in dry radiator heat, the thin rubber diaphragm inside the valve housing stiffened up around the seating ring and gave a tinny little click at the top of the stroke until it worked itself loose again.

Sigrika set the round white-and-blue casing on the pillow six inches from her ear, reached across to switch off the desk lamp, and pressed the blue side of the shell with her thumb.

In the dark, the tiny solenoid gave its soft initial *tock* and the membrane began to play the single recording it held: the low, double-stroked thump of a resting heartbeat.

She let her head sink back into the bolster, tucked her cold nose into the edge of the woollen quilt, and dropped her breathing into the pace she had used to go to sleep since May—four beats to draw the air in, four beats to let it out, sliding her ribs underneath the steady *lub-dub, rest, lub-dub* on the linen beside her cheek.

On the third breath she came in late.

The next first-stroke arrived while her lungs were still a quarter full, bumping against the turn of her diaphragm before she had finished letting the air go.

Sigrika swallowed, shifted her left shoulder out from under her stubby braid-ends, and waited for the next bar to catch it cleanly on the one.

Four beats in—and the fifth stroke clipped the tail of her inhalation again, half a step ahead of where her chest expected the floor to be.

She frowned into the dark. Either the two days of minus-twelve air on the basin rim were still sitting stiff in her bronchioles, or the third mug of roasted barley tea she had drunk at nine o’clock while accusing Lynae’s card deck of having five queens in it had set her own chest racing.

She reached out from under the quilt, took her steel-cased watch and her small brass pocket-torch off the nightstand, and laid the watch on the sheet in the narrow yellow cone of the bulb.

When the thin red sweep-hand crossed the twelve, she began to count the double-strokes under her breath.

*One, two, three, four—*

Around thirty-five her ear wandered from the numbers to the faint, dry rub of the rubber membrane inside the casing—it sounded drier than it had on Wednesday, as if the frost had got at the talc—and by the time she looked back at the dial she could not remember whether she was on forty-two or forty-four.

"Stupid," she muttered to the pillow, and waited twenty seconds for the red hand to climb back to the top of the glass.

This time she tapped the tip of her scarred right index finger against the watch-crystal on every primary stroke, counting out loud in a flat, clipped whisper so she wouldn’t drift:

"Fifty-seven. Fifty-eight. Fifty-nine. Sixty. Sixty-one."

The red hand crossed the twelve before the sixty-second stroke fell.

Sigrika stayed on her elbow in the dark for three seconds. Then she swung both bare feet out of bed, switched the desk lamp back on, and pulled her field journal across the blotter.

She flipped back through the summer bird-lists and the morphology notes to the middle of May, where a single line of red ink stood at the foot of a Tuesday page, entered the afternoon the third-year horn player from the conservatory annex had sat on the library steps with a clockwork metronome on his knee and timed the toy for her by ear:

*14 May. Reservoir loop, 60s manual check: 58.*

She looked from the red *58* on the paper to the round plastic shell still going *thump-thump, thump-thump* on the indented bolster.

A fixed loop sealed inside a toy cannot speed up. Whatever was on the tiny magnetic drum inside the reservoir had been laid down once before the record switch on the base was soldered shut; the drum had a fixed circumference, driven by a small brass worm-gear and a centrifugal friction governor that kept the rotation constant as the battery ran down.

If the loop was coming round sixty-one times a minute instead of fifty-eight, the little felt friction-pads on the governor must have shrunk or glazed over in the cold so they were no longer braking the spindle.

She had carried it in the unlined outer flap-pocket of her coat for thirty-six hours on the Borehole Four rim. When she knelt in the scree to rub the far stone, the coat skirt had lain right on the hoarfrost; when she slept under the windbreak slab at minus twelve, the coat had been folded at the foot of her bag near the open air. She had tucked her boot-liners into the foot-sack to keep the felt from freezing, and she had left the Dream Maker out in the frost for two whole nights.

Sigrika picked the toy up from the pillow in both hands. The white styrene shell was room-temperature from the bedroom air, but to her fingers it felt hard and brittle as an eggshell left in an unheated larder.

She reached up to the bedpost, took down the thick grey-wool knitted hat she had worn on the rim, and turned the cuff back twice to make a deep woollen pocket in the crown—the exact way her father had taught her to fold a cap when she was eight and brought a stunned goldcrest in off the woodshed snow.

She slipped the running toy deep into the middle of the knitted wool, unbuttoned the second and third buttons of her flannel nightshirt, and tucked the bundled hat directly inside against the warm hollow below her breastbone.

Then she blew out the pocket-torch, switched off the desk lamp, and lay flat on her back under the quilt with both palms pressed over the lump of wool at her ribs, feeling the small, rapid sixty-one-count tap through the knitting against her skin while the radiator pipe ticked into the dark.

At half past seven on Monday morning the attic kitchen smelled of scorched butter and wet wool.

Rebecca and Chiyo had gone out at seven for an early ensemble call at the conservatory annex; their two blue porcelain rice bowls and two pairs of wiped chopsticks stood upright in the wooden drainer by the sink, and Lynae was in charge of the stove.

She was standing on one foot in an oversized faded Academy athletics sweatshirt and striped knee-socks, her bleached hair twisted up with a carpenter’s pencil, holding a wooden spatula in her right hand and a rubber-banded stack of pink revision cards in her left while four eggs separated into tough yellow islands and watery whey in the cast-iron skillet.

"*Section Fourteen, Sub-clause B*," Lynae recited to the ceiling fan, waving the spatula for emphasis while a corner of egg browned against the iron. "*In the event of a municipal conduit traversing freehold slate below the thirty-metre datum, maintenance liability reverts to—* damn it, is it the Water Board or the surface lessee? Siggy, don't look at me like that, the pan was cold thirty seconds ago."

"You have the burner on full, Lyn," Sigrika said, reaching past her elbow to turn the brass gas-cock down to a blue bead before the butter smoked. "And it's the Water Board unless the shaft was sunk prior to the Charter of Seventy-Two."

"Show-off," Lynae said affectionately, flipping the pink card to the back of the pack and scraping the eggs toward the middle of the skillet. "Post came up while you were in the washroom. Left it by your plate."

There were two things sitting on the blue-checked oilcloth between the salt-cellar and the jam crock.

The top one was a brick-shaped parcel sewn up in coarse brown linen and tied round with greased hemp twine in her mother’s double butcher’s hitch, the cloth smelling through three hundred miles of mail-van of dried juniper and the pine-tar soap Halla used on the dairy tables.

Beside it lay a long buff envelope with the blue octagonal frank of the Academy Registry stamped over the window.

Sigrika sat down, slit the hemp twine with her pocket-knife, and folded the brown linen back first.

Packed inside in layers of dry oat-straw were two pairs of thick, unbleached sheep’s-wool socks with double-knitted grey heels, four dense dark wheels of caraway-and-rye flatbread wrapped in greaseproof paper, a bladder of smoked mutton dripping, and two sheets of ruled cream post-paper covered from edge to edge in her mother’s large, cheerful, forward-slanting hand.

*My dear girl,* Halla had written, *the black marsh below the hay-meadow froze across on Wednesday night hard enough to bear the calves, so your father took the iron runners down from the barn rafters after dinner and has been tallowing the bolts all afternoon. He says to ask whether you are coming down on the eighteenth coach or the nineteenth, as the road-trust is only clearing the upper drift on Tuesdays and Fridays if the wind stays north, and he wants to have the mare rough-shod before he brings the trap down to the post-house.*

At the bottom of the second sheet, cramped into the half-inch below Halla’s name because Halla never wasted a third stamp when two pages would hold the ink, stood three lines in the same hand:

*Your grandmother came in from the hen-house while I was sealing this and says I am to tell you the birch-soot is mixed with the mutton fat and sitting in the brown crock by the back door, and if it is left past the solstice it skins over and has to be strained twice.*

Sigrika folded the two cream sheets back into their crease, set the grey socks on top of the flatbread, and picked up the buff Registry envelope.

She slid her thumb under the gummed flap and drew out the contents.

It was her own yellow student disposition form from the first week of term—the middle box filled in with her neat, upright block capitals: *STATUS: absent, field. DURATION: until the weather turns.*—sent back unentered from the ground floor for the fourth time since September.

Only this morning the clerk had not stamped `INCOMPLETE` in the margin. Stapled across the top left corner of the yellow sheet was a half-slip of stiff pink card printed in condensed black pica:

*OFFICE OF THE REGISTRAR — WINTER CLOSING NOTICE (29 NOV).*
*Pursuant to Statute IV.11, provisional field-absence extensions carried over from the spring roll terminate at the close of the Michaelmas sitting. The registration under Ref. 09-441-D will lapse permanently at 17:00 on Friday 17 December unless the student presents in person at Window Two of the Registry counter, or a responsible officer of the Academy or Spacetrek Collective attests the student's current whereabouts in writing.*

Sigrika read the pink card through once from the statute number to the hour. Her face went quite flat.

Lynae came over from the stove with the iron skillet in a folded dish-cloth, set two plates of over-scrambled eggs on the oilcloth, and read the pink slip over Sigrika’s left shoulder while she ladled out the curds.

"Well, three cheers for Window Two," Lynae said dryly, rapping the edge of the wooden spatula against the rim of Sigrika’s plate. "Take 'em five months to find a lost library voucher, and the week it snows they suddenly discover the calendar. Eat your eggs while they're hot, Siggy."

Sigrika did not say anything about Window Two. She slipped the yellow form and the pink notice back into the buff envelope, laid the envelope flat beside her plate, and stood up from the bench.

She walked across the linoleum to the wall above the wireless, reached up with both hands, and lifted the round brass-rimmed kitchen clock off its iron nail.

"Can you call a minute for me?" Sigrika asked, setting the clock down upright against the water pitcher in front of Lynae’s plate. "From when the red hand crosses the six."

Lynae paused with a forkful of egg halfway to her mouth and looked from the clock to Sigrika’s face. "Sure. For what?"

Sigrika went to the coat-hooks by the landing door, took her chrome thumb-clicker and the Dream Maker out of her pockets, and came back to the table.

Before she pressed the reset knob on the side of the clicker, she glanced at the four little white digits in the window—`0042`—pulled her field journal across the oilcloth, uncapped her red pen, and wrote at the top of a clean page: *28 Nov, West Arcade rowans: 42 Bombycilla garrulus (winter flock, feeding).* Then she twisted the black knurled wheel until all four drums snapped back to `0000`.

She folded a clean linen tea-towel into four thicknesses beside the bread-board so the wood of the table wouldn't hum, set the Dream Maker in the middle of the cloth, and pressed the blue side.

In the quiet kitchen, over the hiss of the radiator valve, the small double-stroke began on the linen.

Lynae’s fork came down slowly onto the rim of her plate.

"Red hand's coming up on the six," Lynae said, her voice dropping half a tone into her flat, steady range-spotter’s cadence. "Three, two, one—mark."

Sigrika looked straight at the salt-cellar so the dial wouldn't distract her thumb and clicked the chrome lever on every primary beat.

*Snick. Snick. Snick. Snick.*

Across the oilcloth, Lynae watched the brass dial for the first thirty seconds; then her eyes slid sideways from the glass to the small round blue-and-white shell sitting on the folded tea-towel.

"And stop," Lynae said as the red hand cut the six.

Sigrika looked down at the counter window: `0061`.

She wrote *Run 1: 61* in red ink at the head of a column, twisted the reset wheel back to zero, and rested her thumb on the lever again. "Once more. On the twelve."

"Mark," Lynae said.

*Snick. Snick. Snick.*

Halfway through the minute, at the thirty-fifth click, Lynae’s gaze left the clock face altogether and fixed on the plastic casing on the towel. Her mouth was slightly open; the stack of pink water-law cards lay forgotten under her left wrist.

The thin red hand swept past the twelve, past the one, and on toward the two.

"Lyn," Sigrika said, her thumb still going. "Did it pass the top?"

Lynae blinked hard and jerked her chin back to the brass rim. "Yeah—sorry, my fault, I lost the tick. Stop there. It's three seconds past, call it sixty."

Sigrika frowned slightly, entered *Run 2: 60 (late call)*, zeroed the barrel, and ran a third minute while Lynae stared at the glass without moving an eyelash.

"Stop," Lynae said on the dot.

The window read `0061`.

Sigrika wrote *Run 3: 61* beneath the second line, switched the toy off, and capped her red pen.

Lynae picked up her fork and pushed a piece of cold egg across her plate. "Very steady little gadget," she said, her voice pitched a shade too light and conversational. "What's it supposed to be doing?"

"Fifty-eight," Sigrika said, turning the plastic shell over in her palm and looking at the tiny seam around the baseplate. "It's been fifty-eight since the spring. I left it in the outside flap of my coat for two nights when we were up on the rim, and I think the minus-twelve frost either shrunk the felt pads on the little governor wheel inside or dried the spindle grease out so it's running three beats fast."

"Oh, well, you know battery toys," Lynae said quickly, standing up to carry her half-eaten plate to the sink and turning the cold tap on full blast so it splashed into the enamel basin. "Leave a cassette walkman in a pannier overnight and the whole tape sounds like chipmunks till Tuesday. Probably just caught the dome schedule—everything in this building runs fast once they turn the winter boilers on."

To Sigrika, watching her friend scrub at a perfectly clean fork with the dish-brush while her pink exam cards lay scattered by the butter, it was completely clear that Lynae had six chapters of municipal statics on her head and was bored to tears by five minutes of counting clicks before eight o'clock on a Monday.

"I have the nine o'clock morphology seminar with Dr. Halvorsen," Sigrika said, shovelling her own cold eggs down in four rapid mouthfuls before Lynae could tell her to chew. She broke off a thick corner of Halla’s dark caraway flatbread, clamped it between her teeth so she had both hands free to buckle her satchel, and tucked the Dream Maker and the buff Registry envelope into her coat.

At the top of the scaffold ramp by the landing door, she paused with her mitten on the iron latch to check that she hadn't left her red pen on the oilcloth.

In the middle of the kitchen, with the sink tap still running behind her, Lynae was standing motionless beside the table with the round brass clock held in both hands against the front of her sweatshirt, looking up at the empty iron nail in the plaster above the wireless and making no move to hang it back up.

On Thursday afternoon a flock of sixty waxwings was in the bare upper boughs of the Solistree outside Room 304.

Every twenty seconds, while Sigrika stood by the corner of the desk waiting for Mornye to finish ruling a column in her ledger, a dozen of the plump cinnamon-crested birds would drop down together onto a lower fork, sending a puff of dry Thursday snow drifting past the tall window-panes, their yellow tail-bands and the tiny sealing-wax tips on their wing-feathers glinting bright as enamel against the grey bark.

Sigrika’s eyes kept sliding sideways from the bookcase to the glass, counting the first-winter birds on the third bough by the narrower yellow band on the tail—*fourteen, fifteen, sixteen*—until Mornye laid her steel rule down with a small, distinct *clack*.

"You have counted that branch three times since you knocked, Candidate," Mornye said without looking up from her blotter. "They are *Bombycilla garrulus*. They come down from the northern birch-scrub whenever the rowan crop fails above the snow-line, and they will sit in that tree until they have eaten every fermented berry on the avenue and fallen into the shrubbery."

Sigrika brought her chin back round so fast her braid-ends tapped her collar. "Sorry, Professor. The ones in the West Arcade on Sunday only had four wax-droplets on the secondaries—that big male by the gutter has seven."

She reached into her satchel, drew out a half-wheel of Halla’s caraway flatbread wrapped in clean greaseproof paper and tied with red baker’s string, and set it on the corner of the desk beside the Out tray.

"My mother sent a parcel from Bjartr on Monday," Sigrika said quickly. "It's rye and caraway—she doesn't put salt in the dough, so it won't make your fingers swell."

Mornye looked at the brown-crusted parcel for two full seconds as if someone had placed an unlabelled gear-assembly on her clean blotter. Above her high collar, the tips of her ears went faintly pink beneath the two plain steel clips holding her bangs back.

"I do not require tributary loaves during consultation hours," Mornye said in her flattest lecture-hall voice, and then reached out with two fingers and slid the parcel three inches inward so the corner of the Out tray wouldn't crush the crust. "Thank you. Tell your mother the caraway in the canteen bread is invariably stale. What have you brought me."

Sigrika took her field journal and the Dream Maker out of her inside pocket and laid both on the edge of the desk.

She opened the journal to the page where four neat vertical columns of red numbers stood side by side—Monday morning, Tuesday morning, Wednesday morning, Thursday morning, three one-minute runs in each column, thirty-six sixties and sixty-ones in a solid red block—and explained the problem strictly as a piece of frost-damaged mechanics.

"It's a sealed acoustic reservoir with a small electric drive," Sigrika said, keeping her voice brisk and practical. "Before we went down to the Borehole Four rim last week, the loop ran at fifty-eight cycles a minute. I left it in the outside flap-pocket of my coat for both nights at minus twelve, and since Sunday night it's been running at sixty-one. I wanted to ask whether there's a way to tell if the internal governor pads have shrunk or the drive spindle is slipping from the frost—without prying the casing apart."

Mornye glanced once at the four columns of red ink in the journal and did not touch the book.

"A kitchen clock and a manual thumb-tally have a combined operator latency of nearly half a second at the start and stop gates," Mornye said bluntly. "That gives you a household count, Candidate, not an instrument reading. If a mechanical governor is glazing or a drive-belt is slipping, the fault appears as phase jitter between cycles, not as a clean step-increase in angular velocity."

She released her left wheel brake, backed her chair half a turn toward the low, sawn-down tea table beside the radiator, and reached out with her left hand.

In a single, smooth motion—her fingers sliding flat beneath both the base of the brass kettle and a buff half-sheet of printed paper that was tucked underneath it—Mornye lifted the kettle and the paper together, set both down out of sight on the recessed lower shelf of the credenza behind her chair, and rolled over to the grey steel instrument rack by the wall.

From the middle shelf she lifted down the spare brass three-axis geophone head—the twin of the cylinder they had planted in the frozen clay at Kilometre 14.2—set it on the felt pad in the middle of the low tea table, and plugged its coiled black patch-cord into the Channel Two jack of the wide two-pen strip-chart recorder sitting on the side bench.

On the slanted brass platen of the recorder, the perforated paper roll was already moving at its five-millimetre-per-second inspection speed.

Along the left-hand grid track, Pen One was awake and drawing in red ink: a continuous, unhurried waveform that rose to a clean primary crest, dipped into a small secondary notch on the trailing flank, settled onto a flat trough, and rose again. Two centimetres to the right, on the second track, Pen Two sat motionless in its blue ink-well on the zero line.

"Set the casing flat on the top flange of the head," Mornye said, locking her wheel beside the bench. "Do not lean on the table."

Sigrika placed the Dream Maker squarely over the circular spirit-bubble at the crown of the brass cylinder, pressed the blue side of the shell, and stepped back half a pace.

The instant the small internal membrane gave its first stroke, Pen Two jerked out of its rest.

The blue steel nib swung across the right-hand grid, cutting a sharp primary peak and a small trailing notch on the moving paper.

For thirty seconds neither of them spoke. Only the synchronous drive motor hummed inside the grey enamel housing, pulling paper over the brass lip at five millimetres a second, while the two jewel-tipped pens went *scritch-scritch, rest, scritch-scritch, rest* across the rulings.

Mornye picked up her twelve-inch steel engine-divided scale, laid the bevelled edge along the wet blue track without touching the ink, and read the peak-to-peak spacing against the printed five-millimetre time ticks along the sprocket holes.

"Sixty-one point two cycles per minute," Mornye said. "Cycle-to-cycle variance is less than zero point three percent across thirty consecutive periods. There is no governor slip, no bearing chatter, and no voltage sag."

She did not look at the left-hand track, and she said nothing at all about Pen One.

Sigrika stood by the corner of the bench with her hands at her sides, looking down at the eighteen inches of paper that had fed out over the apron.

She had spent three years in the epigraphy room collating damaged runic witnesses across parallel columns—where you lay two fragments from different valleys side by side under a raking lamp and know they were cut from the same exemplar not because the words are the same, but because both cutters made the exact same slip of the chisel on the third stave of the fourth line.

Three centimetres apart on the white paper, the red line of Channel One and the blue line of Channel Two were marching down the grid as a single figure.

Where the red nib rose to its crest, the blue nib rose on the exact same horizontal millimetre ruling. Where the red nib caught the tiny secondary notch halfway down the falling slope, the blue nib cut the identical notch at the identical angle. Fourteen seconds down the strip, the red trace lengthened its trough by half a millimetre—the slight, unmechanical easing of a tempo at the turn of a phrase—and on the exact same line of the grid, the blue trace lengthened its trough by the exact same half-millimetre before both pens stepped into the next beat together.

Mornye kept her eyes on the brass platen. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped into the dry, impersonal demonstration register she used when walking a second-year lab section through an oscilloscope fault.

"When two high-gain channels on a shared chassis produce an identical trace in phase," Mornye said aloud to the recorder, "the standard source of error is electrical cross-talk across a common ground bus, or acoustic coupling through the floor joists."

She reached out with her left hand and lifted the running toy straight up off the brass geophone head, holding it suspended in the air six inches above the table.

On the right-hand track, Pen Two dropped instantly to a dead, ruler-straight blue line on the zero mark. On the left-hand track, Pen One carried straight on without a tremor—crest, notch, trough, crest, notch, trough.

"Not bus cross-talk," Mornye said.

She lowered the plastic shell back onto the brass flange. The instant the styrene base touched the metal cap, Pen Two leaped off the zero line—landing halfway down the falling flank of Pen One’s secondary notch, in dead step, without a tenth of a second of drift.

"Switch the device off," Mornye said.

Sigrika reached down and pressed the blue side. The toy went silent; Pen Two went flat. Pen One drew five solitary red peaks down the left-hand column.

"Switch it on."

Sigrika pressed the blue switch in the middle of the flat trough between two of Pen One’s peaks.

A recording stored on a magnetic loop or an optical drum has a physical beginning where the head sits when the drive stops; when you put power back to the motor, it starts at bar one, beat one, regardless of what any other instrument in the room is doing.

Pen Two did not begin at the rising edge of a beat. It stayed flat on the baseline for the remaining three hundred milliseconds of Pen One’s trough, and then rose on the exact same millimetre ruling as the red nib to draw the sixth peak together.

Mornye watched the platen for five seconds.

Then she reached out herself, pressed the blue switch off with her own thumb, waited until Pen One was at the very apex of its primary crest, and pressed the switch back on.

Pen Two did not start from the bottom of the stroke. It jumped straight to the apex of the crest, dropped into the secondary notch alongside the red nib, and carried the line down to the baseline in unison.

She did it a third time—switching it off for four seconds, then clicking it back on halfway through the secondary notch. Pen Two came in on the lower half of the notch and finished the stroke in step.

Three starts. Not once had the blue line begun from the head of a recording.

Mornye drew her hand back from the brass cylinder and laid both palms flat on the wool of her trousers. On the corner of the desk behind her, Buddy, who had been sitting quietly in the Out tray turning a brass paper-clip over in his two short arms, set the clip down, stood up on his two stubby legs, and gave a single, slow amber glow before sitting back down on the blotter.

"A closed-loop recording indexes to its start mark when the circuit is broken," Mornye said. Her sentences had shortened to flat, square blocks of five or six words, every syllable the same height. "This does not index."

Sigrika stood very still beside the bench, her eyes on the two wet lines moving down the paper. "Is it damaged?"

"It is not damaged," Mornye said.

Nothing followed it.

Sigrika reached down, lifted the Dream Maker off the brass cap, switched it off, and turned it over in her palms so the flat white baseplate faced the light from the tall window.

Set into a small rectangular recess on the underside was the tiny slide-switch stamped `REC`. Across the entire recess—burying the plastic toggle, filling the slide-slot, and spilling out onto the surrounding styrene in a thick, dull-grey lump that had scorched the edges of the plastic brown—sat a heavy bead of lead-tin solder.

Mornye looked down at the grey lump in Sigrika’s palm.

"That is a cold joint," Mornye said, with the immediate, narrow-eyed displeasure of a precision engineer looking at a botched weld. "Whoever sealed that switch used a heavy forty-watt iron with no rosin flux and three times as much metal as the lug could wet. They were not trying to make a clean circuit; they only wanted the slide not to move."

Eighteen inches from Sigrika’s right elbow, bolted to the front corner of the repair bench, Mornye’s own temperature-regulated pencil iron sat in its spiral brass cage, the grey rubber cord plugged into the wall socket and a spool of fine copper desoldering braid resting in the tray beside the sponge.

Sigrika’s eyes moved from the lumpy grey bead on the base of the toy to the fine tinned point of the pencil iron in its cage, and stayed there for three seconds.

Beside her, Mornye’s left hand left her knee and closed firmly around the black rubber push-rim of her wheel.

"I do not open sealed assemblies for which I have no manufacturer's drawing," Mornye said.

Sigrika turned the toy right-side up in her hands.

Mornye reached to the side of the chart recorder, flipped the drive toggle to `STANDBY` so the paper roll stopped feeding, and pulled the serrated brass tear-bar down across the perforations with a crisp *riiiip*.

She laid the eighteen-inch strip of grid paper on the corner of her blotter. By twenty years of bench habit, she uncapped her black draughting pen to fill in the printed header block at the top of the sheet—wrote *2 Dec, 15:40. Ch.1: S. Weir / Ch.2: Ext. head (bench)*—and stopped with the steel point hovering half an inch above the small boxed line stamped `PERMIT REF.`

She left the box empty, pushed the cap back onto the pen, and held the strip out across the desk.

"This was not taken under the permit," Mornye said.

Sigrika took the paper by both margins so her thumb wouldn't smear the blue track. "Thank you, Professor."

Mornye released her wheel brake and turned her chair a quarter-turn toward her inclined draughting board, her profile set toward the window where the last of the waxwings were fluttering up into the high grey branches of the Solistree.

"Carry it in an inside pocket when you are out of doors, Candidate," Mornye said briskly to the window glass. "Dry cells lose a third of their capacity below freezing, and there is no sense chilling the housing. Keep it warm."

Out in the third-floor corridor, with the heavy oak door of Room 304 shut behind her heel and the floorboards humming faintly over the basement steam-main, Sigrika stopped beneath the frosted ceiling lamp.

She folded the eighteen-inch strip carefully along its five-centimetre fan-pleats so the red track and the blue track lay face to face without rubbing, tucked the folded paper between the middle leaves of her journal, unbuttoned the top three horn buttons of her heavy winter coat, and slipped the Dream Maker deep into the inside pocket of her woollen cardigan against her flannel shirt before buttoning her coat back up to her throat.

At midnight the square dormer window across the lane over the noodle shop was still yellow behind its half-drawn cotton blind.

It had been lit when Sigrika walked down the ridge path from Hiyuki’s shrine at half past eight with this week’s sealed witness sheet buttoned inside her coat, and every half-hour since then, whenever she lifted her chin from her desk blotter to rest her eyes, she had seen the dark blur of Lynae’s shoulder shift an inch to the left or right against the lamp as she turned a page of her statics binder.

In Sigrika’s room the desk lamp was tilted low on its gooseneck so the amber cone fell squarely across her open field journal and left the bed in shadow.

Before she could stoop to pick up the spanner, the iron gate-latch at the foot of the ridge path clicked twice, and a pair of heavy, hobnailed boots came crunching slowly up the swept stone steps, pausing on the third riser for a long, wheezing breath.

Hiyuki left the spanner on the canvas and walked round to the front of the lattice.

Standing just outside the greywacke threshold stone was Master Vance, the senior boilerman from the Academy’s East Range heating plant—a thick-shouldered man of sixty with thirty years of coal-dust worked permanently into the seams of his hands, wearing his black Sunday coat over his flannel shirt and holding his tweed cap crushed in both fists against his chest.

His wife had died in the third week of October of the winter pleurisy. Hiyuki had written the witness sheet for the parish clerk herself.

"Begging your pardon for coming up the hill before the eight o’clock bell, Madam," he said, bowing once with the stiff, careful awkwardness of an engine-man who did not come up to the ridge twice a year. "Only the fitters finally got the new cast-iron header manifold bolted onto Number Three boiler in the East Range at midnight, and we ran her at forty pounds right through the four o’clock watch without a single knock in the risers."

He looked down at the tweed brim in his fingers.

"Martha spent three winters listening to that main riser hammer behind the bedroom lath in the tied cottage," he said. "Told me the week before she went that the Works Committee wouldn't sign the foundry order for a new casting before she was under the turf. I just wanted her told that the manifold's on, Madam, and the pipe behind the wall is quiet at four."

"Stand by the brazier out of the wind," Hiyuki said, and stepped into the centre of the open lattice.

She knelt on the woven reed mat beneath the ten thousand suspended crystals—each one a frozen bead of regret she had taken out of the dark over a hundred years, hanging clear and silent in the mountain draft—and folded her bare hands in her lap.

When she knelt on this mat and asked, the dead answered her. Every time, without exception.

For an October death within the city wall, she had no need to touch the bronze Suzu at her hip. She lowered her lashes, dropped her hearing down the quiet road beneath the frost, and put the office’s question in her level, unhurried voice:

*The new header manifold is bolted onto Number Three boiler in the East Range, and the riser behind the bedroom wall does not knock at four in the morning. Is that the whole of the errand, or did you leave something else on the table?*

At first what came back up out of the October cold was only the top layer—the thin, habitual fretting that repeats itself in the dark like a loose shutter banging in a draft: *Tell him not to walk across the front linoleum in his clinker-boots, tell him the third-floor committee are skinflints.*

Hiyuki did not hurry it, and she did not take the first answer for the true one. She stayed on her knees under the ten thousand crystals and asked a second time, in the exact same cadence, waiting for the surface water to run clear so whatever lay at the bottom of the well could show itself:

*The pipe behind the wall is quiet. What did you leave undone in the house?*

Three slow breaths went by in the cold. Then the pitch of the answer dropped an octave, losing the sick-room sharpness altogether and settling into an ordinary, brisk Tuesday afternoon in a kitchen:

*The iron hook on the scullery casement over the coal-bunker. If Arthur drops the hook all the way down into the staple, that big yellow tom-cat with the chewed ear from the bakehouse yard can't push the sash open with his nose when the sleet comes on, and because he isn't our cat Arthur never thinks to leave the pin on the sill.*

Hiyuki opened her red eyes, rose smoothly from the reed mat, and walked out to the threshold stone where the boilerman stood holding his scarred knuckles over the charcoal brazier.

"She knows the manifold is bolted down and the pipe is quiet," Hiyuki told him. "She says you are not to drop the iron hook all the way into the eye on the scullery window over the coal-bunker. You are to leave the hook resting on the lip of the latch so the yellow cat with the torn ear from the bakery yard can get in out of the snow."

Master Vance stared at her across the brazier coals for two full seconds.

Then a short, rough bark of a laugh came out of his throat—and broke off in the middle as his jaw twisted and he brought the back of his coal-grimed wrist up hard across both eyes.

"That thieving ginger brute isn't even ours," he said thickly into his coat sleeve. "Took half a salt haddock off the back plate-rack in April."

"It is an errand," Hiyuki said. "I am walking down past the East Range at four this afternoon to buy lamp oil. I will come through the back yard and see that the hook is left on the latch myself."

As he turned to go down the swept steps, pulling his tweed cap back onto his head with both hands, Hiyuki added one sentence more—deliberately, her voice slightly formal, like a person speaking a phrase in a language she had only recently decided she had the right to use out loud:

"You kept the cottage very warm for her in October, Master Vance. She was not cold."

The boilerman nodded twice with his chin tucked into his muffler and went down the ridge path into the pines.

Hiyuki turned back into the open pavilion to pick up the brass charcoal-tongs she had left beside the mat—and stopped dead.

In the middle of the frost-whitened reed mat, where her knees had rested for three minutes in minus-nine air while she asked her question, two dark, wet ovals of completely melted water had soaked clean through the heavy wool of her hakama to her skin.

At half past seven that evening a single shuttered oil lamp burned on the low cedar table inside the screen, while outside in the dark the ten thousand crystals of the lattice caught the faint amber glow rising from the dome ventilators below the hill.

Sigrika sat cross-legged on the opposite mat with her woollen scarf loosened around her neck and her canvas field satchel open beside her knee. Every two or three minutes, without looking down or breaking off what she was doing, her right hand came up and touched the front of her heavy winter coat over her left breast pocket, checking the shape of whatever was tucked inside her cardigan.

She had brought the pink Registry closing notice up the hill inside its buff folder and laid it flat beside the inkstone so Hiyuki could read the four lines of condensed pica for herself:

*...unless the student presents in person at Window Two of the Registry counter, or a responsible officer of the Academy or Spacetrek Collective attests the student's current whereabouts in writing.*

Hiyuki read the pink slip once and set it squarely back on the buff envelope.

A Miko of the Left Behind held no faculty chair at Startorch Academy and no warrant in the Spacetrek Collective. Her vermilion seal was accepted without question by every parish clerk from the Roya valley to the southern gulf for those who had crossed the threshold, and after five o’clock on Friday the seventeenth of December it would carry no weight whatever at Window Two.

Hiyuki opened the shallow drawer beneath the table, drew out a fresh sheet of tough grey shrine paper for the first week of December, and rubbed the stick of black pine-soot ink across the wet slate well. With her short-haired brush held vertically between her fingers, she wrote along the lower margin in her upright architectural characters:

*3 December. Attestation of continued field absence, Ref. 09-441-D. Witnessed: Hiyuki.*

She laid the brush on its porcelain rest, picked up her carved boxwood seal, pressed the face into the cinnabar pad, and set it down beneath her name.

Because the bare skin of her thumb and index finger had been damp with melt since midday, the smooth boxwood slipped half a millimetre sideways on the tough grey fibre as she lifted it, dragging the bottom bar of the vermilion character into a blurred red tail.

Hiyuki’s brows drew together.

Without a word, she moved the spoiled sheet to the corner of the table, drew a second blank sheet from the drawer, wrote the entire attestation line out a second time from the date to her signature, wiped the tips of her fingers dry on a folded linen cloth, and pressed the boxwood down again.

Just as the wood met the paper, a tiny bead of warm moisture gathered at the quick of her forefinger and ran down the side of the seal; when she lifted the block, the right-hand border of the vermilion square had bled into a wet scarlet blot.

Across the low table, Sigrika watched her set the second spoiled sheet neatly on top of the first.

"Window Two takes smudged ones," Sigrika said, her face completely solemn and her hands folded in her lap. "The clerk on the Tuesday morning turn dropped a piece of sugared cruller on my October sheet and stamped right over the grease spot. I've tested the margin of error."

Hiyuki raised her red eyes and gave her a single, level look across the lamp.

"A shrine does not issue blotted script," Hiyuki said.

She took a third sheet of grey paper from the drawer, wrote the line out a third time in faultless pine-soot characters, drew the cuff of her white woollen sleeve down over her bare fingers so her skin did not touch the boxwood at all, and pressed the seal straight down through the cloth.

The vermilion square came up crisp, dry, and razor-edged on all four sides.

While the black ink dried beside the lamp base, Sigrika reached into her field journal, took out the fan-folded eighteen-inch strip of recorder paper from Engineering South, and unfolded it flat across the cedar board between their knees.

"Channel One is the live South Weir feed from the basin floor," Sigrika said, her voice flat and exact, reading out the two headers in the top margin and adding nothing else. "Channel Two is the spare geophone head on Professor Mornye's bench with the Dream Maker sitting on the cap."

Hiyuki leaned forward slightly over the low board and looked down at the paper.

She followed the red track and the blue track from left to right across the eighteen inches of five-millimetre grid—past the fourteenth-second mark where both traces widened their troughs by half a millimetre on the exact same horizontal line, past the three flat gaps where the toy had been switched off and jumped straight back into step on the rising flank, on the crest, and in the middle of the secondary notch.

She looked at the strip for a long ten seconds without moving a muscle.

"Which trace is the toy?" Hiyuki asked quietly.

"The lower," Sigrika said. "The blue."

"Mm," Hiyuki said.

She rose from the mat and stepped behind the cedar screen to fetch the iron kettle and the two earthenware teacups from the small three-board shelf against the wall.

As she lifted the pewter tea-caddy from the middle board, the hem of her wide white sleeve brushed a small, flat green triangle that had been resting beside the canister since Friday—the folded fig leaf from the Borehole Four rim.

It slid off the edge of the board and dropped onto the woven mat beside Sigrika’s ankle.

Sigrika reached down to pick it up—and paused with the folded leaf held between her thumb and her scarred forefinger, tilting it into the yellow circle of the oil lamp.

Eight days after being picked and carried forty kilometres through minus-twelve frost in a canvas pack, the leaf had neither browned nor curled at the serrations; the veins were as supple as oiled kidskin, and the blade was the deep, living orchard green of late summer.

"This should have gone brown and papery by Sunday," Sigrika said, turning the triangle over in her fingers. "Even if you'd had it under a dictionary."

"Yes," Hiyuki said, setting the two earthenware cups on the board.

"May I log it?" Sigrika asked, her left hand already moving toward the three aligned pens on the edge of her closed journal. "Just the provenance date and the state of the tissue."

Hiyuki looked down at the green leaf in the girl’s hand, inclined her head once, and poured the boiling water over the roasted tea-stalks.

Sigrika opened her journal to the current date, uncapped her red pen—not the green—and wrote in her brisk, tidy apparatus hand at the foot of the page:

*2 Dec, 19:50 (Shrine). Ficus carica fol., 1 specimen retained from 25 Nov (km 14.2 rim); 7 days unpressed at ambient indoor temp; lamina pliable, no margin necrosis or desiccation, colour full summer green.*

She capped the red pen and laid the folded green leaf carefully back on the corner of the table beside Hiyuki’s saucer.

Hiyuki wrapped both palms around her warm earthenware cup. The steam rose straight up between them in the windless shelter; overhead, a draft off the northern pass stirred the outer rank of the lattice with a faint, dry *chink* of ice against ice.

Looking across the lamp glass at the two stubby, uneven ends of bright orange braid resting against the collar of Sigrika’s woollen cardigan, Hiyuki opened her mouth to begin a sentence about the night of the twenty-fifth on the basin rim—about two o’clock in the morning, when the blue-white resin coal was low—

"On the night we were at the rim—" Hiyuki began, and stopped on the sixth word.

Sigrika looked up from aligning her red pen with the edge of her journal cover, her eyebrows lifting politely.

"—the freeze came down the vents very early," Hiyuki finished in the exact same level cadence, without a seam in her voice. "Have you booked your place on the southbound coach for Bjartr?"

"Not yet," Sigrika said, folding the eighteen-inch chart strip back along its pleats and slipping it into the journal beside the clean third witness sheet. "My mother wrote on Monday to ask whether I want the eighteenth or the nineteenth. I told her I'd write before the fifteenth so my father knows when to bring the mare down."

When she had drunk her tea and stood up at the threshold stone to pull her woollen mittens on, Sigrika glanced past the corner of the screen toward the open door of the lean-to shed, where the four squared oak blocks and the folded grey tarpaulin were still sitting untouched on the oiled canvas beside the front wheel of the motorcycle.

"You're late putting the bike up on blocks this winter," Sigrika remarked, slinging her satchel strap over her shoulder. "Last year you had the float-bowls drained before the November snow schedule started."

"Yes," Hiyuki said.

She stood at the top of the swept stone steps and watched the small yellow circle of Sigrika’s pocket-torch go down the steep zigzags of the ridge path between the pines until the light reached the fourth-terrace arch and went out.

Then Hiyuki walked back to the low cedar table, picked up the two spoiled grey witness sheets with their smeared vermilion seals, folded both of them into neat four-fold triangles the exact size of the fig leaf, and laid them side by side on the middle board of the tea-shelf where the leaf had been.

At four o’clock on Friday morning the oil lamp inside the screen had been blown out, and the alcohol thermometer on the north gate-post stood at fourteen degrees below zero.

Hiyuki knelt alone in the middle of the woven reed mat beneath the open lattice, her white cloak unclasped at her collar and the small bronze Suzu hanging on its silk cord at her left hip within two inches of her bare fingers.

Above her head hung the ten thousand crystals.

Every single one of them was a name she had spoken into the dark and been answered by. In a hundred years of keeping the office of the Left Behind, she had never once knelt on this mat, put the question to the frost, and heard nothing come back—unless there was nobody on the far side of the threshold to answer.

She folded her bare hands in her lap, lowered her eyes toward the dark throat of the northern pass, and formed the office’s question in her mind—the exact, patient phrasing she had used at eight o’clock yesterday morning for the boilerman’s wife, with one name changed at the head of the line.

She did not speak it aloud, and she did not drop her hearing down the road.

Directly overhead, in fourteen degrees of mountain frost, the lowest thirty crystals of the lattice—the ones hanging immediately above her crown and shoulders—had gone completely wet and glossy in the starlight. Clear drops of water gathered at their lower points, hung heavy for a beat, and fell *pat, pat, pat* onto the white wool of her shoulders and the backs of her bare hands.

Hiyuki stayed on her knees for five slow breaths while the meltwater ran down her wrists.

Then she rose from the mat without touching the bell at her hip, walked out from under the dripping eaves, and went round the swept gravel path to the lean-to shed.

In the dark under the tarred rafters, she knelt beside the front tyre of the motorcycle, picked up the ten-millimetre box spanner from the oiled canvas, and slid it back into its slot in the leather tool-roll, pulling the strap tight through the buckle.

She swung her leg over the cold leather saddle, turned the brass fuel tap under the tank from `OFF` to `MAIN`, pulled the choke knob out a quarter-inch, and drove the heavy kick-starter iron down once with the heel of her boot.

The twin cylinders caught on the first stroke—a deep, oil-warm, four-stroke *thump-thump-thump* that shook a dusting of dry rime off the shed beams and sent a grey plume of exhaust curling out into the pines.

She let the engine idle for the space of one long breath, her bare palms resting on the handlebar grips until she felt the first faint warmth of the iron cylinder fins come up through the steering head, and then turned the ignition key off.

She stepped down from the saddle, lifted the folded grey winter tarpaulin off the canvas sheet, and hung it back up on its iron spike against the rear studs of the shed. One by one, she picked up the four squared oak axle-blocks, carried them across the gravel yard in her arms, and stacked them neatly on top of the split birch logs on the woodpile.

Twenty minutes before first light—a quarter of an hour earlier than her daily rule—she walked to the front eaves of the shrine, took the hardwood striker in both hands, and struck the great bronze bell once.

The single low note went out from the ridge across the sleeping tiers of the Academy dome and straight north down the mountain pass toward the frozen shelves of the Dimmr Plains, while directly behind her back, from the thirty lowest crystals of the lattice, clear meltwater dripped steadily in the minus-fourteen dark onto the swept greywacke of the threshold stone.

At half past four on Friday morning every brass wall-lamp and tallow-dip in the kitchen under the lake was lit, and the foot of the scrubbed oak dinner table was covered with old newspaper and frozen rubbish.

Viviane had turned out the tarred canvas salvage sack she carried clipped to her belt whenever she took a turn under the shore-ice margin—spreading her week’s haul across the Tuesday shipping news to dry: two bent iron survey pins from the eighty-four dyke, the threaded brass ring off a broken pocket-lens, three feet of black friction tape stiff with lake silt, and a square green apothecary bottle with no cork.

Up on the middle shelf of the dresser beside the blue earthenware soup bowls, standing upright in an empty stoneware marmalade jar where it had stood since last Friday morning like a single winter stem in a vase, was a twelve-inch pin of galvanized wire with a small rectangular flag of yellow oilcloth stitched to the top.

For seven consecutive mornings Herta had stared at that marmalade jar over her porridge spoon as if it had been put there for the express purpose of calling her scholarship into question.

"It is a perennial southern rock-rose," Herta snapped from the head of the board, perched on her two thick quartos of maritime law with her steel nib poised above her commonplace folio and her enormous black hat pinned three inches back from her brows. "Or a forced glasshouse variety of *Helianthemum* with a dark basal spot. To walk out onto a sheet of shore ice, stick a piece of fence-wire beside seven intact five-merous corollas, and write *Unidentified* on the tag in green ink is the act of a turnip."

Overhead, slung lazily on one hip in the web of crimson silk ropes biting into the empty air two feet below the rafters, Black Swan reached down with one long, bare arm to trail her fingertips through the warm draft rising off the toast-rack.

"Don't be a pedant before cinco, Herta," Black Swan murmured from the ceiling, her dark eyes half-shut and entirely amused in the tallow smoke. "A Latin binomial pins the poor things to a board. *Unidentified* leaves the window on the latch."

By the hearth, Viviane had her sea-booted ankle propped on the iron fender and an oil-rag in her lap, wiping down the bevelled teeth of her six-foot buzzsaw staff while wearing Phrolova’s grease-darkened Ragunnan kidskin gloves. On Tuesday evening she had slit the black leather of the right thumb open along the seam and stitched it back around a jointed brass-and-steel mechanical thumb-splint she had filed out of an old steam-governor linkage to give her extra pinch on rusted nuts.

"Doesn't signify what you call 'em," Viviane said, clicking the brass thumb-splint twice against the saw-guard. "Found property on a survey line goes back where it was pulled from before the seventh day's out, or the boundary drifts. I'm setting it back in the glaze at first grey."

Phrolova sat in her accustomed chair at the far end of the oak board from the stove, her dark woollen dress buttoned to the throat, paring the crust off half a slice of barley-toast. Without looking down from the dresser shelf, her bare right hand reached two inches to the left of the butter-dish, closed around the pewter salt-cellar on the first touch, and set it by her plate.

She lifted her eyes to the stoneware marmalade jar on the dresser, where the stiff yellow oilcloth flag hung angled downward toward the table, and read the small waterproof-inked inscription upside down.

Across the middle of the waxed cloth, in a brisk, upright apparatus hand that she recognized at once from the flyleaf of a waterlogged navy-blue journal at the bottom of the borehole shaft, stood:

*26 Nov. — 7 — Unidentified.*

And stamped squarely in the lower right corner of the tag, pressed down hard enough with both thumbs to indent the weave of the oilcloth, was a round brass candidate’s seal from Startorch Academy. Around the outer ring of the impression ran the name in tiny, reversed block capitals:

*SIGRIKA — CANDIDATE.*

*Sigrika,* Phrolova read once in her head, fitting the three sharp northern syllables to the round, earnest face with the two stubby ends of sheared orange braid she had watched crouching on the ice through the Architect’s brass field glasses at six o’clock last Friday morning. She did not say the name aloud, and her mouth did not move by a hair.

Out of the buttery passage came Elaina, her flat wicker basket hooked over her left forearm, setting a fresh stoneware crock of salt butter and two loaves of dark rye bread onto the side counter—right past the wide cut-glass preserve dish by the door-latch, where the two kilos of yellow-green lemon-acid drops still sat piled to the brim in their white sugar dust, untouched since the morning she tipped the brown sack out.

Elaina glanced over her shoulder at the marmalade jar on the dresser and beamed.

"Oh, are you taking the little wire flag back up to the ice this morning, Viviane?" Elaina asked cheerfully, folding her white linen market napkin into quarters by the hob. "That belongs to the obliging girl from the fifth-tier sweet stall—the one I gave the two figs to by the black cable last Thursday. She has the most honest face in the world; when she tested the lemon drop for me at the counter, her left eye squeezed shut three whole seconds before the right one, and she didn't pretend for a single instant that she liked it."

At the foot of the table, Phrolova’s butter-knife paused for the space of one sixteenth-rest against her crust.

For ten weeks she had read this household like an enemy’s full score, counting every entrance, watching every hand that passed the salt-cellar, listening for the concealed downbeat that would tell her what the Banat al-Rih had brought her in out of the snow to do.

And all week long, while she kept her teeth shut about the drowned room at the bottom of the shaft and the girl with the sheared orange braids on the last page of the cut album, the youngest witch in the house had been buying boiled sweets with that very girl in a municipal market, handing her fresh purple figs on the shingle thirty feet above their chimney, and keeping her wire specimen-flag in a marmalade jar on the kitchen dresser without once thinking it worth mentioning over the herring.

"If you leave surveyor's pins sticking out of the glaze," Phrolova said in her flat, bored mezzo, lifting her teacup, "you will have half the Academy's third year trampling over the roof before noon."

At the head of the board, Herta gave a short, furious sniff. She climbed down off her two volumes of maritime law, marched across the flagstones to the dresser with her steel pen in her fist, plucked the wire stake out of the marmalade jar, and held the yellow oilcloth flag flat against the shelf board.

"I am not having an unindexed botanical entry sitting over our chimney until April," Herta muttered, and with three tiny, cramped strokes of her violet nib she wrote a single small note in the corner of the oilcloth below the green *Unidentified*.

Viviane reached over from the hearth, took the wire stake out of Herta’s hand before Herta could append a bibliography, and wiped a speck of dresser-damp off the yellow oilcloth with one stroke of the greasy brass thumb-splint on her kidskin glove.

At ten minutes to five Phrolova rose from the board, lifted her dark woollen cloak from the peg beside the settle, and stepped into the narrow larder passage where the cut-stone spiral went down toward the lower pool.

Deep below the oak joists of the kitchen—farther down than the borehole shaft, down in the unmapped limestone hollow that moored itself beneath the house wherever their bark door was hung—Lamia slept on under ten thousand years of tide, her slow, bottomless overtone turning without a ripple, and on the stone bench at the water’s edge Alice’s iron-gall nib went *scritch, scritch, rest* across the folio at sixty-four to the minute.

On the flat flagstone at the first turn of the stair, weighted against the draft with a lead plumb-bob as it was every morning before five, lay Alice’s fair-copy slip from the fourth watch.

Phrolova did not go down to the pool. She stopped on the top step, held her small brass tallow-dip six inches above the stone, and read the slip where it lay.

There was only a single short line on the rag-paper this morning, the black sand from the pounce-box still glittering along the wet downstrokes:

*3 Dec., fourth watch (04:12). Mid-water, no displacement; unvoiced velar indistinct:*

*[Harder / colder] than this. Not until it [bears / will bear].*

*[Note: The comparative adjective lacks a case-vowel; the final verb is third-person singular present used with future force, as in the river-crossing proverbs. No turn in the pool. — A.]*

Phrolova read the two bracketed pairs through once, the way an orchestra leader reads a second-horn slip found loose on a music stand when the conductor’s score is locked in the sacristy chest: a single bar of inner harmony with the clef and the time-signature torn off the page.

By eight o’clock Herta would have covered all four margins of the rag-paper with red query-marks arguing whether an impersonal river-proverb could take a present-future predicate without a modal particle, and Black Swan would hang upside down from the ceiling beams and say nothing useful whatever.

Down in the hollow, the steady sixty-four-count of Alice’s quill went on without a break, and beneath the quill the deep tide slept without stirring.

Phrolova set the brass tallow-dip back in its wall niche, turned her back on the cellar stair, and walked to the bark door at the front of the kitchen.

She lifted the wooden latch, stepped out across the threshold onto the invisible cellar-tread in the thirty feet of black water, and walked straight up through the dark toward the southeastern shore.

When her head and shoulders broke the surface at the frosted shingle of the spur, there was no wind at all, and the air above the basin was four degrees harder than last Friday—sixteen below zero, so dry and fierce that the first breath she drew took the inside of her nostrils like a pinch of powdered alum and froze the damp hem of her cloak stiff as sheet-iron in three seconds.

Five o’clock in the morning, her second hour on the household slate.

The basin lay flat and white in the pewter dark before the Solvein Heartwood relit its winter pearl, the three-inch sheet of grey shore ice stretching out from her boots to the ten-metre circle of open black water in the middle, where the seven red flowers sat locked under their clear collar of glass.

Phrolova walked ten paces up the frosted greywacke shingle toward the flat boulder below the old haulage roadbed where she sat to keep her hour—and stopped two yards short of the stone.

Up on the lip of the roadbed, three paces east of the kerb-stone where the bronze conservatory token had lain in the rime last week, there was a patch of hoarfrost about two feet square where the long, feathered ice-fronds had been crushed flat to the gravel.

Someone had stood there during the night, facing out across the basin toward the open water and the northwestern rim—not walking past, not shifting from boot to boot to keep the blood moving, simply standing still in one place for an hour or more until the steady pressure of two dry leather soles left a clean, double-arched print in the frost.

And hanging in the dead, windless minus-sixteen air directly above the crushed rime, faint as a cambric handkerchief left on the velvet rail of an empty opera box after the house has cleared, was the thin, unmistakable scent of spring blossom and bitter orange peel.

Nobody was on the roadbed. From the southeastern spur to the bleached bulk of the Heartwood four kilometres north, the haulage track was bare slate and mist.

Phrolova looked at the two neat boot-prints in the hoarfrost for one slow bar, and a flat, heavy boredom settled behind her eyes—the exact, leaden boredom that always came over her when her blood went cold.

She stepped past the crushed patch without setting her heel in it, sat down on the flat boulder at the head of the shingle with her dark cloak drawn over her knees and her bare hands folded in her woollen lap, and dropped her hearing straight down through the seventy metres of ice and absence to the bedrock shelf under the lake.

The girl was there.

Eleven metres beneath the concrete floor of the drowned ward, in the solid rock where no corridor went, Denia’s frequency beat on—steady, unhurried, sixty-one and a fraction to the minute, leaning forward into each next beat the way only a living pitch leans into the bar ahead of it.

Phrolova listened to her for sixteen bars, checking the intonation against the slow eleven-count of the distant tree the way she had checked it every morning at five since Friday.

Then she tilted her chin a fraction of an inch to the east—away from the water, toward the great dark shoulder of the mountain four kilometres off where the tiered vaults of the Academy dome sat tucked inside the rock.

Threaded through the minus-sixteen air above the plain, so faint that anyone without four hundred years of the stick would have taken it for the shiver of an iron guy-wire in the frost, came the exact same figure.

Sixty-one and a fraction. Double-stroked, in the same key, from somewhere up under the eastern dome.

Phrolova’s spine straightened against the cold stone.

A musician does not trust a two-bar coincidence; a roomful of pocket-watches will tick together for ten seconds by chance before parting company. She held her breath and listened to both ends at once—the deep, fundamental sixty-one coming up through the shale between her boots, and the thin, hair-fine treble thread coming down the mountain pass from the east—waiting for the fourteenth-second turn where the phrase below always widened at the end of a breath.

Down under the bedrock, Denia’s count eased by half a beat at the turn.

Four kilometres away under the Academy dome, on the exact same fraction of a second, without a hair’s breadth of lag, the thin eastern thread eased by the exact same half-beat and stepped into the next bar in dead unison.

It was not an echo off the mountain wall; an echo across four kilometres of minus-sixteen air trails the downbeat by twelve seconds. And it was not a gramophone loop or a stamped metal cylinder; Phrolova had spent four centuries cutting loops out of the dead, and she could hear the splice on a loop from three streets off. There was no splice on the eastern pitch.

Someone up there under the dome had an instrument in a room that was doubling the part under the lake in real time, beat for beat, breath for breath.

She had no idea what the instrument was made of, or whose hands were holding it. She had one name stamped around the rim of a brass seal on an oilcloth flower-tag downstairs, and no proof whatever that the girl with the sheared orange braids was the one sitting by that eastern pitch at five in the morning—though she would very much have preferred them to be the same person, because one door was simpler to walk up to than two.

And as she kept her ear trained eastward up the Riseway throat to hold that thin thread against the frost, she heard two other frequencies sitting in the same quarter of the dark, neither of them touching the sixty-one-count and neither of them aware of the others.

Lower down in the middle tiers of the dome, steady as a violoncello bow held on a single open C, hummed a small, flat sixty-hertz electrical drone—some wheeled or bench-mounted machine sitting at rest in a room where nobody had gone to bed.

And while she was listening to the machine, from the high ridge directly above the dome’s eastern eaves, a single bronze bell-stroke rang out across the snow—clear, unhurried, pitched at D below middle C, struck twenty minutes before dawn by someone standing awake under the frost.

Three separate places on the eastern hill keeping the fifth watch of the morning at once, and not one of them knew there was a household word for the hour they were keeping.

Phrolova let the bell-note die away into the mist and turned her ear back to the two locked sixty-one-counts—the one under the lake and the one in the dome.

A single, ice-cold thought crossed the back of her mind, once, and left no theory behind it: *Every one of the hundred and eighty-nine was played from one end only.*

She did not dress the thought up as curiosity, and she did not pretend to care who was sitting awake in an attic dormitory four kilometres away. She wanted that far instrument in her own two hands, to take the back off it and see how a living frequency was held across two rooms without splitting at the fourth bar, for the bakehouse and the square and the houses south of the seam.

And she knew one thing more, with the certainty of six decades at a desk down the hall from the Architect’s: whatever an ear could pick out across four kilometres of frozen slate, needles and strip-charts and third-tier clerks would triangulate sooner or later.

Without noticing when she had moved, Phrolova had shifted her weight on the flat boulder at the head of the spur—her boots planted on the landward side of the rock, her shoulders squared toward the eastern mountain pass, and the frozen lake completely behind her back.

At twenty to six the first thin smear of pewter grey came into the mist along the eastern ridge, and the bark door under the water clicked open.

Viviane came up out of the black mirror at the edge of the shingle in her heavy tarred sea-boots and tricorn hat, carrying the twelve-inch galvanized wire stake with its yellow oilcloth flag in her gloved right hand.

She did not summon her jellyfish, and she did not test the glaze with her saw-staff. She walked straight out across the sixty metres of nineteen-centimetre grey shore ice at her long, rolling deck-hand’s stride—the ice beneath her sea-boots giving not a single creak or crack under her weight—until she reached the foot-wide collar of clear glass around the ten-metre circle of open black water in the middle.

She crouched by the fourth of the seven frozen crimson flowers, found the tiny pinhole in the clear glaze where the wire had been pulled on the twenty-sixth, and pushed the galvanized stake two inches back into the exact same socket so the yellow oilcloth flag stood upright facing the northwestern bank.

On her way back across the ice toward the kitchen threshold, she cut across the toe of the southeastern shingle ten feet below Phrolova’s boulder, both hands tucked into the pockets of her peacoat with the greasy kidskin thumbs hooking the lapels.

"Pin's plumb," Viviane remarked dryly to the mist as she passed. "Herta's violet ink looks like a beetle died on the corner, though."

Phrolova did not turn her head from the eastern pass.

"How far is the dome by the road," Phrolova asked, in the flat, bored voice she used when she might as well have been asking the price of salt herring.

Viviane paused for half a beat on the frosted gravel, glanced once at the dark wall of the mountain above the Heartwood, and did not ask why a woman who tore A-flat seams through the air wanted the walking time along a municipal cart-track.

"Hour and a quarter to the lower shaft if you don't slip on the culvert glaze," Viviane said. "Forty minutes up the steps if the hoist-man's having his tea. Shut the outer latch when you come down, Elaina's making drop-scones."

Two strides down the shingle, the black water took her tricorn brim without a ripple, and the unseen door below clicked shut.

Phrolova stayed seated on the boulder, facing east.

For twenty minutes more the two sixty-one-counts ran together through the pewter dark—the deep one beneath the rock behind her heels, and the thin thread up under the dome ahead of her eyes.

Then, on the stroke of six o’clock, right in the middle of a rising two-beat figure, the thin voice in the east stopped dead—switched off cleanly between one beat and the next—while forty metres under the ice behind her back, the one below the floor beat on alone into the morning.

Her hour was over.

Phrolova did not get up from the stone. She sat on the landward side of the boulder with her cloak over her knees and her back to the water, looking straight east across the frozen shelves toward the mountain wall, where high up along the rim of the Academy dome the yellow night-lamps were going out one by one.

# The Sleeve

Chixia made it as far as the cupboard before the Wind caught up with her.

The crutch had a new rubber foot. Baizhi had fitted it after breakfast, sitting on the floor with the offending part between her knees while Chixia explained what a considerable improvement she would be upon standing up. Now the rubber squeaked on the boards. At each squeak, a small pressure gathered beneath Chixia's elbow.

“I'm getting the sewing basket,” she said. “You can see the entire expedition from your chair.”

The pressure followed her to the cupboard.

Yangyang sat beside the stove with her right sleeve spread over the arm of a chair. She had worked her arm out of it a few minutes earlier, turning the sleeve inside out as she went. The seam stood up along its length. Her clean shirt lay across her knees.

She had hoped to leave some warmth in the old one. Already the cloth was cold enough to chill her fingers when she straightened it.

The empty sleeve lifted toward Chixia and settled.

“Bring the basket here,” she said. “The light is better.”

“You've put me where everybody can watch. Very clever of you.”

Chixia hooked the basket over the crutch handle and made the return journey. She lowered herself onto the bench, manoeuvred her injured leg onto its cushion, and fished her red cap out from beneath her coat.

The lining had come loose along the back. A strip of dark thread hung from it. Chixia put a finger through the gap and examined the extent of her afternoon.

“Baizhi, how much faith have you got in my sewing?”

“I have put the blunt scissors in the basket.”

“Thanks for the vote of confidence. I handle loaded pistols for a living.”

“You used the good scissors on that rubber foot before I took it off.”

Chixia looked down at the cap. She smoothed the lining over her knee, then reached for the thread.

Outside Mrs Ren's guesthouse, a porter called for room on the stair. Somebody above him asked which household wanted the onions. Three afternoons had passed since they came home, and deliveries were finding their usual doors again. A basket of clean clothes stood in the passage with a sock draped over its handle. Yangyang had stepped around it four times.

She put on her clean shirt. The Wind came back while she was fastening the cuff, and she left the last button open.

Iuno appeared in the doorway carrying the teapot.

Her coat hung from its accustomed hook. The new dressing crossed the crystal at her shoulder, with one narrow amber stain showing through near the lower edge. Below it, her shirt held a fan of creases at the waist. She smoothed them once as she came through the door; the creases stayed.

Yangyang's eyes found the stain before they reached Iuno's face.

“There is enough for everyone,” Iuno said. “Mrs Ren has acquired a larger kettle. She gave me a very thorough account of why she needed one.”

“I heard her telling Mei,” Chixia said. “Mei offered to carry it home for her. That seemed to improve the account considerably.”

Iuno set the pot on the table. She turned its handle toward her living hand, checked the cups, and moved a spoon clear of the spout.

Yangyang waited.

“I would like to speak with you both about the shore,” Iuno said at last. She looked toward the open cuff as she said it. “I have been thinking about what I said at Norfall.”

The air against Yangyang's wrist cooled.

“We can stay here,” Yangyang said. “Chixia and Baizhi know what happened.”

Baizhi put the lid on the small jar she had been using. Chixia drew the loose lining through her fingers and laid her needle across it.

Iuno stood beside the table a little longer.

*Take the chair, darling. They have left you a perfectly serviceable chair.*

She sat.

Yangyang poured the Wind's tea into the wide bowl they had begun keeping beside the stove. She placed it on the table within reach of her sleeve. The steam rose, wandered toward the window, and gathered there.

Iuno followed it with her eyes.

“On the balcony in Lahai-Roi, you asked why I woke her. I told you about the plan. Then I told you about Amy, and the three weeks we'd had together, and how Mei had finally begun sleeping through the night.”

Her hand rested beside her cup.

“You stood there while I explained how much I wanted her to keep sleeping.”

A bead of water formed on the outside of the Wind's bowl. It travelled down the glaze and soaked into the wood.

“I remember the frost on your tea,” Iuno continued. “I remember being frightened of what you might ask of her. I expected you to hear that fear and be careful with me.”

She reached to move the wide bowl away from the table's edge.

Cold closed over her knuckles. It held there, concentrated along the joints, until she drew her hand back. The bowl stayed where Yangyang had put it.

Iuno rubbed her fingers beneath the table. A pale line lay across each one.

Yangyang looked at Chixia's hands. One held the cap open. The other had closed around the little spool, hiding it completely.

“Did you know about the library then?” Yangyang asked.

“You had given me the warning,” Iuno said to the steam. “You said you had watched Ovathrax win. You told me what the city called it afterward.”

The window rattled once in its frame.

“I understood that you had lost people,” Iuno said. “By morning I was discussing the journey with six people I loved. We had a kitchen, and somebody had bought eggs, and Amy was complaining about the timetable.”

She could remember where everyone had stood. Mei against the counter, her daughter close enough to steal from her plate. Lupa eating while she argued. A little spill of tea drying beside Iuno's thumb.

“I could carry what you had told me into that room and be looked after.”

Yangyang drew her loose cuff across her palm.

For a while the loudest sound came from Chixia working the spool free of her fingers.

Then Yangyang said, “What would have brought you here the first time?”

Iuno looked up.

“In her Jinzhou,” Yangyang said. “When she was still going out on patrol with them.”

She glanced at the bench, and the effort of bringing her eyes back to Iuno felt strangely physical.

“She would have had her letters in a drawer, as I did. She would have been waiting to learn where to send them.”

The tea had begun to steam against the windowpane. A narrow patch of glass cleared beneath it.

Iuno brought her hand into her lap.

“Mei stayed west,” she said. “The kite brought us here. Before that, we were making plans for the life we had.”

Yangyang watched her.

“I can follow my own decisions,” Iuno said. “I know how hard I argued when the woman who had seen the flood was standing in front of me. In her first life, that woman was still on your wall, holding a sword. By the time she could reach my balcony, her city had burned.”

The loose cuff tightened around Yangyang's wrist.

She had been thinking of the shore as a place someone reached too late. She could picture wet stones, the dark water, Iuno's hair against Mei's cheek. She had seen those things hanging over the valley.

Now she pictured her room before the Wind began coming to it. The letters in the drawer. Her boots set beneath the bed for an early departure. Chixia shouting up from the lane that breakfast was getting cold.

She would have gone down to eat.

And later, when the beacons changed, she would have taken her sword north.

Yangyang pressed her free hand over the cuff. The pressure inside it trembled against her pulse.

“You would have gone on having breakfast,” she said.

Iuno's mouth parted. She drew a breath.

“News would have reached us afterward,” she said. “I would have read it in a room with her.”

Chixia put the cap down.

Her needle slipped between the folds of the lining. She found it by touch and laid it on the table, where she could see the whole length of it.

***

The first cap had been heavier.

Water had filled its lining. She had lifted it out by the brim and the weight had pulled against her fingers, a stubborn little weight, while black water streamed from the seam down her wrist. She remembered turning it over because she had wanted to see inside.

The street had been deep enough for a boat. Beneath the surface lay the stone where Chixia used to stand to reach Yangyang's window, announcing herself to the whole lane. Once a neighbour had thrown a peel at her. Chixia had caught it and spent the walk to breakfast giving her assessment of the woman's aim.

For a year, the cap had dried wherever she slept. She would hang it on a branch, a nail, the projecting end of a roof beam. Each morning she would reach up and take it down before fastening her pack.

She remembered the last morning she had done that. Her hand had risen to the nail as she stood. The shrine keeper was waiting by the door, carrying the bowl in which he accepted things people brought him.

She had held the cap against her stomach while she found a question to ask about the roof. Whether the rain came through. Whether he brought the bowl farther inside during storms.

Here, the red cloth lay open on Chixia's knee. Her leg smelled of clean dressing and the sharp ointment Baizhi used. The thread hung from the lining, caught on a knuckle that moved when she breathed.

The Wind went toward that movement.

Chixia lifted her face a little to the left of where she should have.

“I've still got the needle,” she said. “You can come closer. It's on the table.”

Air touched the back of her hand.

She swallowed. The small sound carried through the room.

“Yangyang told us about the cap,” she said. “Before we went north. I've been trying to think of something useful to say about it ever since.”

Her fingers spread over the red cloth.

“I keep getting as far as how long you carried it. Then I start thinking about where you put it when you needed both hands.”

Baizhi lowered her eyes to the jar beneath her palm.

The Wind settled along Chixia's fingers. The cloth lifted between them, shaping itself around the space her hand had left.

Chixia held it there.

The skin across her knuckles was warm. Yangyang knew that warmth. In winter Chixia would complain about their hands and tuck them under her arms anyway, making a considerable public event of being useful. Yangyang watched the red cloth rise a little higher, as though someone beneath it were trying to remember the fit.

Iuno shifted in her chair. The dressing caught against its back.

Her breath stopped halfway in.

The cap fell flat.

Baizhi was already standing. She came round the table, moved Iuno's chair into the light, and touched the edge of the dressing.

“Hold your shoulder here. The lower layer has adhered.”

Iuno obeyed. Her living hand gripped the chair beside her thigh.

The Wind retreated across the table. The steam broke apart around her. Yangyang felt the cold reach her cuff and then pass it, heading toward the window.

She laid her forearm along the sill.

“Shijie, I have something to say about the sword.”

The curtain pressed against the back of her hand.

Baizhi dampened a corner of the dressing. The cloth darkened slowly beneath her fingers.

“There was amber beside the guard when I cleaned it,” Yangyang said. “Some had dried underneath the binding. I was picking it out yesterday, and I began thinking about Amy.”

Iuno looked at her across Baizhi's bent head.

“I kept trying to work out how I would have told her.”

Her forearm stayed on the sill. She could feel the grain of the wood through her clean shirt, a rough place where the paint had lifted in the damp.

“I knew the stroke as soon as it began. My hands knew where to go. Afterward there were people to bring home, and I could keep doing the next thing. Yesterday I sat with the sword across my knees for almost an hour.”

The pressure behind her hand grew very still.

“I miss practising with you,” she said. “When I picked it up this morning, I could feel where you had meant it to land.”

The curtain drew away from her skin.

Yangyang kept her arm where it was.

Across the room Chixia had one hand on her crutch. Baizhi lifted another inch of cloth free and laid it back over itself. The crystal underneath held a narrow, wet line.

“And you saw it coming,” Yangyang said to Iuno.

“I had time to move. The angle was clear.”

“You were watching Mei. I was trying to turn my sword, and you were watching her.”

Iuno's hand tightened on the chair.

“I trusted her to stay where she was,” she said. “For a moment, when the blade came, I could understand exactly what was being asked of me. I could stand still and take it.”

Baizhi paused with the cloth between her fingers.

“Then you had to do the rest,” Iuno said to Yangyang.

Yangyang looked down at her open cuff. Her thumb had found its last button and was pressing hard enough to leave its shape in her skin.

“I wanted you both home,” she said. “I had enough to do with that.”

Iuno bowed her head. A strand of hair fell across her mouth; she moved it with the back of her hand, awkwardly, because Baizhi had asked her to keep the shoulder still.

“I am sorry,” she said. “You were already holding her together. I left you with my life as well.”

Yangyang breathed through her nose. The button slipped out from under her thumb.

“Please move if it happens again.”

Iuno looked toward the window, where the curtain held a fold slightly clear of the wall.

“I want to live,” she said to the Wind. “If you raise a blade against me again, I will act on that.”

Water clicked into Baizhi's basin as she wrung out the cloth.

“That would improve the afternoon for several people,” she said.

Her voice was level. She was looking straight at Iuno.

Chixia let go of the crutch. Her hand left a damp print on its handle.

***

Baizhi asked for the clean packet on the cupboard. Chixia reached for her crutch again, considered the distance, and pointed it out to Yangyang.

Yangyang fetched the packet. When she came back, the Wind was beside the bowl, drawing its remaining warmth into a small, uneven spiral.

She sat and laid her forearm along the table.

Iuno watched the steam while Baizhi worked.

“When I intercepted the waking, I was arranging a death,” she said. “I intended Mei to reach Septimont at an hour I had chosen, and to die there for a result I had calculated. The current was moving east. I entered it as weather, then worked out how to hold her west.”

Yangyang's hand closed slowly.

“By the time I loved her, I had spent years deciding what other people should know. I brought that habit into our room. I thought about the shore in terms of what it had done to us. What I had meant to do to her. What she eventually learned about me.”

Baizhi placed a clean pad against the crystal. Iuno's eyes flicked toward it, then returned to the steam.

“You crossed all that distance to bring her east. You had given up your body to get there, and I held her west while you pushed against me. Then you spent years finding a way to reach her again.”

A thread of steam escaped the spiral and flattened across the table.

“And you brought me too,” Iuno said.

She looked toward Chixia. At the crutch resting against the bench, the cap in her lap, a pair of blunt scissors lying beside a cup.

“I have been eating in the places you lost. I can walk down to Panhua's and find you all arguing about lunch. You brought me here, and I have enjoyed it.”

The pad shifted beneath Baizhi's hand. Iuno made herself sit forward while the bandage passed behind her shoulder.

“This morning Mei was asleep with her hand tangled in my shirt. Every time I eased a finger loose, she caught another fold. I spent several minutes working out how to get up for the kettle while letting her keep it there.”

Her thumb found the creases at her waist. She pressed them lightly against her body, and for a moment her mouth softened around the memory.

“I want that morning again. I want a great many of them.”

The cloth pulled tight beneath her hand.

Iuno came forward against the table. Baizhi caught the dressing before it slipped. The gathered shirt dragged sideways, hard, bunching above Iuno's hip in the place her thumb had been stroking.

She gripped it and braced her foot, moving her chair back until she could lift her weight off the table's edge.

A stitch snapped beside the lowest buttonhole.

The cold reached her through the cloth, sharp enough to make her stomach contract. Her fingers closed harder. Across the table Yangyang put her palm into the current, and the force of it turned her cuff back against her wrist.

“Shijie, that hurts,” she said. “I need my hand back.”

The pressure held for another breath.

Then it went through the window gap with a sound that made Chixia's crutch fall against the bench. Outside, the laundry lifted straight from its line. A peg struck the glass and dropped into the lane.

Iuno sat with a fistful of her own shirt pressed to her stomach. The place beneath her fingers warmed as she held it. A short length of broken thread lay across her knuckle.

She opened her mouth. The curtain struck the wall.

Her mouth closed again.

The Wind remembered that thumb moving over the creases. The small, private pleasure that had entered Iuno's face while she spoke. She had seen it often, from a window, over a roof rail, at a stove. On the shore in the rain, the same face had bent over the woman she had spent herself reaching.

She had pushed until she came apart, gathered herself out of the salt air, and pushed again.

The body on the stones had moved west.

Now a woman sat across the table holding her shirt closed over warm skin, frightened and alive, with somewhere to go when she left this chair.

The Wind came back through the gap. She passed Iuno at the height of her hand, cold enough that the loose thread lifted. Iuno held the cloth more firmly.

At Yangyang's chair the discarded sleeve rose by its cuff.

She remembered the nuisance of waking with a hand asleep. Chixia's head had pinned it to a table one winter evening; Baizhi had been trying to finish a paragraph before they left. She had sat with her fingers prickling and watched the steam disappear from her dinner, hungry, irritated, waiting for one of them to move.

The sleeve turned. Its inside seam caught on the chair's rough edge. She pulled, and the cloth came free all at once and fell across the seat.

Yangyang held her reddened wrist in her other hand.

“I heard her laughing this morning,” she said to Iuno. “While I was trying to get my arm out of the shirt.”

Iuno looked at her.

“I thought the stove might help. I moved the chair close enough that the wood was hot under my knee. Then I put the sleeve against my face to see how she was.”

Her eyes filled. She looked down at the cloth on the chair.

“I could feel the cold through it.”

Baizhi secured the dressing. The little clip took two attempts. She set the used cloth in the basin and covered it, then kept her hand on the basin's rim while Iuno eased upright.

Chixia drew the loose lining through her fingers. Her needle lay on the table, already threaded. She looked from Iuno's hand to the empty sleeve, then toward the kitchen door.

“I like having you here,” she began.

The words brought Iuno's eyes to her. Yangyang looked up too. At the chair, the cuff lifted a little, with the familiar attention Chixia had learned to expect when somebody in the room sounded troubled.

Chixia looked at it for a long moment.

She put the needle into the pincloth and folded it over the point. Then she set the cap on the cushion beside her.

“Baizhi, I'd like to walk as far as the kitchen,” she said. “Come and help me with the step. I could use your arm.”

Baizhi glanced at the unfinished seam. She put the lid on her jar and stood.

Chixia took the crutch. Usually she began talking as soon as she was upright; this time she waited until Baizhi had come round the bench, and leaned into the offered arm.

The Wind moved toward them.

At the doorway Chixia turned her head toward the coolness. Her mouth trembled. She pressed her lips together for a moment before speaking.

“I'll be sitting with Mrs Ren. You'll hear us from here. I'm coming back for my cap.”

She looked at Iuno once as Baizhi helped her over the step. Then she gave her attention to the passage, making a careful business of placing the crutch ahead of her feet.

Baizhi moved the laundry basket onto a stool and picked up the sock. Her voice came back through the doorway, explaining the height of the next step, and Chixia answered her at an ordinary volume.

The Wind stayed by the door until they reached the kitchen.

Mrs Ren asked what had happened to the cap. Chixia began describing her sewing. By the second sentence she was speaking loudly enough to be heard through the wall.

***

The tea had gone cold.

Iuno lifted her cup. The rim caught against her teeth, and she put it down. She folded her hand over the torn buttonhole instead.

From the kitchen came a scrape of wood as Baizhi drew out a chair. Chixia thanked her. Water ran into a basin, and Mrs Ren began asking about the stitches.

Yangyang reached for the discarded shirt. The Wind drew the sleeve out from under her fingers.

She put her hand on her knee.

“When you were resting earlier,” she said to the sleeve, “I spent twenty minutes trying to get out of my shirt.”

A little pressure touched the inside of her wrist.

“I had the clean one ready. I thought I could leave you in the sleeve until you woke.”

The inside-out cuff rested at the chair's edge, its button turned toward the wood. She could see where she had begun mending it last winter. Three stitches in dark thread, then three in a slightly different shade because the spool had run out.

“Then Chixia got up, and you went after her. Yesterday it was the porter on the stair. The night before, somebody dropped a tray downstairs.”

In the kitchen, Chixia explained how the lining had caught on her hair. Mrs Ren asked to see it, and Chixia said she would fetch it in a little while.

“I keep listening for the thing that will make you leave,” Yangyang said. “I think you have begun listening for it in me.”

Iuno's gaze lowered to the open cuff. Yangyang rubbed her wrist, following the red mark with her thumb.

“When I learned your name, I spent half the journey home remembering my own life. The courtyard. The registry office. How frightened I was in Yuezhou. I kept reaching a morning I remembered and trying to work out how far beyond it you had walked.”

The pressure lay against her pulse.

“I've started doing it when I get dressed. I fasten a strap and wonder how many more times you did that. I pick up my sword and think about the last time you had hands.”

The cold stirred beside her hand. She watched the hairs on her forearm lift.

Yangyang looked toward the narrow bed visible through her open door. Her patrol log rested on the coverlet with a marker halfway through the page she had been reading. She had meant to finish it before lunch.

“And sometimes I want to get dressed and go downstairs because I'm hungry.”

Her voice had become so ordinary that Iuno looked up at her face.

“I get angry with you for being in everything,” Yangyang said. “Then I remember how long you kept coming to my window, and I sit down again.”

The sleeve slipped farther down the chair. Yangyang leaned forward, caught the shoulder before it reached the floor, and laid it across her knees. Her fingers stayed on the body of the shirt, clear of the cuff.

“That is something I needed to say. It has been a difficult morning.”

The sleeve lifted at the elbow, held its shape for a few seconds, and folded back on itself. Yangyang watched it fall. Her hand had begun moving toward the hollow; she rested it beside the seam.

Iuno leaned forward.

“At Qichi, you saw what the sound was doing to me,” Yangyang said. “You gave me room to get my breath back. I have remembered that several times today. How quickly you understood what I needed.”

Iuno's hand stayed over her shirt.

“I can picture how you looked after Mei,” Yangyang said. “You could have a pot ready before she asked. You would know when she was tired. She would fall asleep in a chair, and you would get her to bed.”

Her face had grown wet while she spoke. She wiped it with the heel of her hand and looked across the table again.

“I keep trying to put my boots on, and I find myself thinking about you.”

The kettle lid clattered into place beyond the wall.

Iuno sat back. Her thumb moved once beneath the broken thread and stopped there.

“I said I would do the talking until Mengzhou,” Yangyang told Iuno. “I meant it. There is so much I want to ask her, and I keep finding questions that can be answered by touching one of my hands.”

She spread them on the table.

“Eventually I run out of hands.”

Iuno looked at the two palms, and at her own, resting on her knee.

“I keep waiting for you to answer,” she said to the Wind. “I finish a sentence and leave you a space. I have been doing it all afternoon.”

Air gathered above the bowl. Yangyang felt the familiar narrowing, the effort that had preceded a word at Norfall. The tea drew into a small depression at its centre. The curtain lifted from the wall.

Iuno leaned toward it.

The bowl scraped across the table and struck her cup. Tea spilled over her fingers.

She held the cup until it steadied. The water in the bowl lay flat again.

“She used the last one at the meal,” Yangyang said. “I asked her to keep it for today. Then Mei asked about tomorrow, and she wanted to answer.”

Iuno took the wet cloth from beside the pot. She dried each finger, slowly.

On Yangyang's lap, the sleeve gathered into tight folds. She could feel the cold coming through to her knees.

Yangyang drew the bundle from the inner pocket of her clean shirt. She had moved it across before she started with the sleeve. Its weight had been against her chest all morning.

She laid it on the table and unfolded the corners.

Both shells lay with their openings upward. In this light the worn patches showed clearly, pale ovals where a thumb had passed over the ridges for years. Along one inner curve, the inscription ran into a space. On the other, small, closely set characters filled it.

Yangyang touched the edge her mother had smoothed.

“I've been wanting to ask Ma about the wrapping,” she said. “I keep remembering that I could have asked when Suisui brought it. I spent that whole visit arguing about leave.”

The Wind passed over the shell. One corner of the cloth folded inward, then lifted and lay flat again.

Yangyang watched the movement.

“You remember something else,” she said quietly.

The cold lay along her fingers. Whatever the Wind remembered stayed there with it.

Iuno leaned forward a little, stopping as the new bandage pulled.

Yangyang looked at her.

“At Mengzhou, I want to hear what they make of these. And I want to ask about the morning you saw in the basin. What you knew when you reached into it.”

“I will tell you what I remember,” Iuno said. “Including the parts I find embarrassing to say aloud. Some of them may look very small beside what followed.”

“Then start with the moment you entered her as weather. I've been trying to picture how long it took.”

Yangyang turned one shell so the light reached farther inside. The fine characters held their shadows. She left them facing upward.

The Wind moved across the open cloth. She turned her shell until the broken inscription faced Iuno.

Iuno looked at the spaces between the remaining characters. The shell rocked once, settled against the other, and stayed there.

She rested her hand on the table.

“I'm sorry I held her west,” she said. “I put the direction I wanted into that water and kept it there while you tried to turn her.”

The cold reached the tips of her fingers.

She had spent much of the morning imagining how she might say it. In one version Yangyang asked a question. In another the Wind came close enough for Iuno to feel her against her cheek. She had been able to picture the end of each conversation, the moment she rose, the shape of the room behind her.

Now the cold crept beneath her nails. She flexed her fingers and left them on the table. The broken thread brushed her waist as she breathed.

Yangyang began wrapping the shells. Her right hand shook when she drew the first corner across them. She put it flat on her knee until it steadied, then tried again with her left.

Iuno watched the work take longer because of the hurt at her wrist.

The Wind watched too.

When Yangyang reached for the final corner, a pressure arrived beneath her elbow. Her hand stopped. The pressure withdrew, and the cloth fell back against the shells.

“Give me a moment,” she said. “I can finish this when my hand settles.”

She sat with the wrapping open and the tie between her knees. Iuno's hand had started toward it. She drew her cup closer and held that instead.

In the kitchen, Chixia laughed at something Mrs Ren said. The sound came through the wall loudly enough for every word after it to be clear: an argument about how many onions four people could reasonably carry home.

The Wind crossed toward it. A sleeve rose from Yangyang's lap, its cuff pointing at the door.

She had heard that laugh in a room on Bookbinders' Row. The house had set the evening carefully. Baizhi's correction, Chixia's delight, the table by the window. She remembered paying to stay until the lamps were trimmed, and the sound of the sorts being lifted into a tray afterward. The founder had read each one aloud. At the time, she had still been able to ask him to break the table.

From the kitchen, Chixia began another story. Mrs Ren asked her to speak up.

The sleeve fell across Yangyang's knees.

***

Baizhi appeared at the doorway with fresh tea.

“Mrs Ren has put the water on again,” she said. “I brought some while it was hot.”

Iuno moved her cup to make room for the pot. Baizhi set it down, glanced at Yangyang's wrist, and placed a clean, dry cloth beside her hand.

Chixia came through behind her. She stopped at the bench, picked up the cap, and rested it on her head with the loose lining tucked out of the way.

“I've claimed the onions,” she said. “She'll keep them in the kitchen until somebody stronger than me feels useful.”

Baizhi helped her sit. Chixia began adjusting the cushion beneath her leg, paused with her mouth drawn tight, and let Baizhi take it from her.

Iuno poured into the wide bowl. Steam rose over the two shades of mending thread on Yangyang's cuff. A little moisture gathered along the inside seam.

She poured herself a cup as well.

For several minutes they sat while the bowls cooled. Baizhi dried Yangyang's wrist, folded the cloth around it, and set her hand carefully back on her knee. Chixia kept the cap on. Her fingers rested over the place where she had meant to sew.

At last Iuno lifted her cup.

“Mei asked me to find her afterward,” she said. “She knows I came to speak with you. I would like to sit with her for a while.”

The sleeve moved on Yangyang's lap.

Iuno watched it. Her hand tightened around the warm porcelain.

“I keep wanting her to make this easier for me,” she said. “I am going to have to tell her that.”

Chixia looked down at the cushion. Yangyang pressed her lips together, then bent over the shells and finished the knot.

Iuno rose. The creases at her waist opened with her body and gathered again as she reached for her coat. She worked her arm into its sleeve, using the edge of the doorframe to settle it over the dressed shoulder. The cup waited on the table until she came back for it.

At the door her eyes went to Yangyang's lap.

Cold moved along the boards between them. Iuno shifted her feet. Then she went into the passage, carrying the tea close to her chest.

The fourth stair creaked beneath her.

Above it, a door opened. Mei's voice reached the landing, low and familiar, and Iuno answered. There was a pause, and a small sound from Iuno that Yangyang had heard once before, when Mei took her hand at the field kitchen and she had been very tired.

Yangyang lowered her eyes to the shirt.

The Wind had gone into its sleeve. The cloth lifted at the shoulder and drew toward the cuff, dragging the inside seam across Yangyang's knees. She put her good hand beneath the opening.

The cuff trembled against her palm. For a moment the sleeve rose along its whole length, and Yangyang raised her hand with it, following.

Then the cloth folded at the elbow and settled flat.

Its button lay against her skin. She could feel the rim of it, the four holes, the small knot of thread her mother had taught her to keep on the inside.

She held the cuff open.

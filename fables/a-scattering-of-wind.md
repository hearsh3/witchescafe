# A Scattering of Wind
### six passings, set down one character to a piece
*A "Lyre, Speak to Me" side story.*

風行水上。
*Wind moves over water: the pattern makes itself.*
— the Book of Changes

---

## 鵲 — the magpie

The bird the eastern counties call the xuanling — azure down the wing, gossip down to the bone — travels in loud family parties and holds committee on every roofline, and this one had lost its party in the storm and lost half a wing's worth of feathers to the same argument, and was making its way home anyway, in hops. Fly, fall, walk, scold. Fly, fall, walk, scold. Ten li of it, over harvest stubble, under a sky scrubbed raw.

The wind came down the road behind it and slowed to the bird's pace.

She slid under the ruined side only. The good wing she left alone — the good wing was working, and the working of it belonged to the bird — and she carried just the lack, just the missing feathers' worth, so that the flying stayed the bird's own flying, a little longer between falls, then longer, then no falls at all.

The magpie scolded her for thirty li. Roundly. Personally. Every grievance in its inventory: the storm, the flock, the injustice of stubble fields, her. She took it the way you take family.

The roost, when it came, was a racket of blue in three pines, and the whole committee turned out shrieking, and the young one landed in the middle of its relatives and was absorbed.

She had turned south when it caught her — flew straight up into her, hard, the whole body of it, and let go of what it carried in its beak. A thread of blue silk, robbed off some clothesline in a better neighborhood. Toll, or payment, or magpie opinion. The bird dropped back to the pines and bobbed once, the way they do, and screamed at her to be about her business.

She owned, after that, one blue thread.

## 簫 — the flute

The storeroom in Mingting keeps the household's patient things: the loom weights, the New Year molds, the case on the third shelf, twelve years shut.

At the third watch the lattice breathed and the case was open, and the flute lay on its cloth, and a sound came off it with almost no body to it at all — a scale first, walked slowly, the way you walk a horse that has been stabled too long. Then, lower, hardly a sound, more a shape the silence made: the lullaby. The one for summer fevers. She had it from the same mother, in a different house, and her fingers had forgotten it years before her breath did, and breath was all she was now, so the lullaby came out whole.

Lamplight arrived under the door. The latch lifted.

The mother stood in the doorway in her night robe with the lamp held level, and looked at the flute lying on its cloth in the dark, sounding.

The sound stopped.

The stillness that followed had a center.

The mother crossed the room, set the lamp on the shelf, and took up the flute. She wiped it down. She oiled the joints, worked the wrap, sighted along it — the full service, unhurried, at the third watch, in silence — and laid it back on the cloth out of its case, facing the lattice, where the air comes in.

At the door she paused, and bowed to the room. The small bow. The one you give a guest.

The flute went east the next week, wrapped in oiled cloth, with a note about damp.

## 窗 — the window

Westbound, on the one night of wind in a month of stillness, with a country to cross and a debt to raise at the far end of it, she gave the detour four breaths.

The room held its usual order. The hat on the bedpost, the bird stitched at the brim. The sleeper deep under a thin blanket, one arm flung out in the paid-up sleep of Outriders. On the desk, under the inkstone: a season of salutations, and one page, finished at last.

She read it without lifting it. The reading came up off the paper like steam off tea, and she took it into her ribs whole, every stroke, and it weighed what letters weigh, which is everything and nothing, depending on the reader.

The blanket had slipped to the sleeper's waist. She measured the night and found it warm, and let the blanket be. One strand of hair had strayed across the sleeper's mouth; she moved it — off the brow, back, the licensed gesture, the one handhold a wind has — and the sleeper sighed and turned and slept on.

She left the room precise to its dust.

At the sill she turned back once, the way people do in doorways.

Then she flew the first li too fast, the way you leave when the leaving is the errand.

## 茶 — the tea

At Master Ping's the attendant had taken up a new habit with the hinge cup: she rinsed it with hot water first, the way you warm the cup for an honored guest, and only then poured, and set it steaming by the door on its saucer, and marked the chalk stroke on the frame.

The wind settled over it at dusk and drank the way she did everything now: the part that rises.

She took the steam in slow courtesies — a guest with manners older than the house — and when the cup had cooled to stillness she paid the kitchen its compliment the one way open to her. She gathered the scent of the pot, the good leaf and the char of the crispy thing and the sugar of the back room, and carried it out the door and laid it down the whole street, shopfront by shopfront, all the way to the bridge.

The house filled by the second pot. Ping looked at the door hinge, and looked at the crowd, and said, to the kitchen, "More cups."

## 鳶 — the kite

Over open water there was only the work.

She kept the red kite at the one altitude where the air stays kind, and fed the string out and took it in by the weather's mood, and the string — spun out of what a wind has for thread, which is staying — hummed as strings hum, and she let the hum carry the lullaby's shape, hour after hour, for the company.

The storm on the fourth day she answered by becoming the weather around the weather: a lee the size of a kite, held against a sea's worth of argument, until the argument moved on.

At the midpoint of the crossing, farthest from every shore, with the ocean below and the nearest auditor a sea away, she let the kite climb. All the string there is. Straight up into the high sun until it was a red syllable on the whole blue page of the sky, and she held it there for the length of one lullaby, for the joy, for the free and unaudited joy of it, and then she brought it back down to the working altitude and went on west, the long way round the weather, which was the way she had.

She carried it down a country's chimney into a city with a handmade sun.

And on a rooftop under the false sky a woman came up off her cup, one foot on the parapet, hand out — and the string crossed the stranger's palm, and the fingers closed, and for one moment they held it together, one at each end of a spun-out staying.

The wind held her end one heartbeat past the handoff.

Letting go was the delivery.

## 梅 — the plum

The kite lives on the roof rail now. Amy filed it as crew.

Mei comes up at night with two cups — her own coffee, cardamom, criminal strength, and a tea for the rail. "It's not Earl Grey," she tells the dark, setting it down. "You're welcome."

Then she talks. She is her Outrider's mirror in this, hemispheres apart, the same posture at the same kind of rail: the day's absurdities, delivered to the weather. Lupa versus the vending machine. Cartethyia's homework. Iuno pretending the second teapot is for guests in general. "She told me everything, by the way. Took two pots. I held her hand through the third-act part." A sip. "We're all very proud."

The air at the eaves stays at the eaves.

"The shell gives me a word a night," Mei says, conversationally, to the sky that is a ceiling. "I'm up to eleven. I do the punctuation myself." She turns the cup on the tile, a quarter-turn, settling it. "*Every wind is met once.* That's five of them. You can imagine my week."

The eaves hold their distance. Mei looks over at the shadow under the gutter-line and reads it the way she reads everything, feral and kind and correct.

"…Saving it," she says. "The once."

The vents tick. Somewhere below, the magnolia lets go of a petal, and the petal crosses the courtyard the wrong way.

"Okay." Mei pulls her knees up, wraps the coffee in both hands, and makes the deal of it, plainly, the way she makes all her deals. "Then we do it properly. Jinzhou. The shore you meant. I'm good at washing up on beaches — ask anyone, it's my whole origin story. You find me there, like you meant to." Her voice does something and she lets it. "And that's the once. And it'll be worth your fare."

The kite lifts its long tail off the rail, holds it a moment, and lays it down.

Mei falls asleep before the coffee does, mid-sentence, head on her arms, the way she sleeps now that the house is full — anywhere, trusting the building.

A strand of hair slides across her mouth.

The wind takes up the slack of the kite's tail, and with paper, at the full length of her staying, moves the strand. Off the brow. Back.

Then she climbs to the eaves, where the distance is, and ties herself off for the night on one blue thread, and stays — the whole night, at anchor, over the sleeper — a being made entirely of leaving, moored, by choice, till the handmade dawn.

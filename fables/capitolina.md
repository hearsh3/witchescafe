## I. The Lake That Boiled

Scorching. That was the first thing Lupa kept.

The summers in Caldara ran so long the lake forgot its own cold. By midseason it lay flat and glassy under the plateau sun, breathing steam at dawn, and the village children swore it boiled, and the village elders let them swear it, because a boiling lake was a better story than a dying one. Caldara sat three days above the Sanguis Plateaus in the dry hills, and it sent the Hill its wool, its salt, its obsidian, and — every fourth year, when the recruiters made their circuit — its children.

The year the Great Agon returned, the children of Caldara held their own. They cut spears from fence posts and shields from feed-trough lids, drew a ring in the dust behind the tannery, and fought in teams of two the way the Ephor's new rules demanded, because even in Caldara the rules of Capitoline Hill arrived before the news did. Lupa was seven, and she won everything. She won so much the older boys invented a handicap and she won under the handicap. Her ears heard a feint before it left the shoulder. Her nose told her which opponent had skipped breakfast. Her tail — the tail the midwife had crossed herself over and her grandmother had kissed — kept the score behind her, sweeping the dust like a broom that only worked when she did.

Halfway through a match she would win, someone called her home.

The house was one room and a curtain. Her father sat at the table with his hands folded, the way he sat when the House was going to be discussed, and her mother stood at the window with her back to it, the way she stood when the future was. They had served House Silva once, on the Hill itself — polished its floors, kept its ledgers, worn its wolf on their sleeves — and the year of the second Tide, when the whole city locked its gates and counted its own, the House had counted them out. What her parents carried back to Caldara was a dismissal letter, a daughter on the way, and a wound they took turns dressing in front of her every night of her childhood: the House was cruel and they wanted back in; the House was cruel and they should begin again somewhere the wolf crest meant a wild animal and no more.

The questions came in the usual order.

"If the House would sponsor you," her father said, "would you go to the Hill?"

"Yes."

"If the Mark takes — the healer says it will take — would you fight? A Gladiator's life. Would you choose it?"

"Yes."

Her mother turned from the window. Both of them exhaled, both at once, and their faces did the thing that puzzled Lupa for years afterward: relief. Fresh every time, as though the yes were a coin that might someday land tails, as though the answer were something they were borrowing from her at interest. She had said yes to those questions since before she understood them. The relief renewed itself anyway.

Then the argument started, the old one, mend the tie or cut it, crawl back or start clean, and their voices took on the tone she knew best in the world — hungry and flinching at once, a hand reaching for a door it expects to be slammed on. They spoke of the glittering city that way. They spoke of the House that way.

They spoke to her about her future that way, and it took her twenty years to hear it.

She sat and listened until the argument closed over her head like water, until they had forgotten there was a third person at the table. Then she got up, quietly, the way you leave a room where a match is being fought that has nothing to do with you, and walked back to the ring behind the tannery.

The heat went with her. It always did — a wet, clinging heat that lived under her skin in the summers, heavier than the air, an anger with humidity in it. It made her run faster. It made her hit harder. She called her opponent back into the ring, some patient boy with a feed-lid shield, and she drove at him with everything the kitchen table had put in her, and somewhere in the fourth exchange the crowd of children went silent all at once, the way a field of insects goes silent.

Lupa looked down. Fire was climbing her arms. It came off her elbows in ragged pennants, orange going white, and the dust around her feet was blackening in a slow ring, and the fire made a sound she would spend the rest of her life learning to read: low, moving, many-throated. A howl wearing flame.

The boy dropped his shield and ran. The children scattered. And the adults came — from the tannery, from the well, from the drying racks — and stood at the edge of the scorched ring and looked at the burning girl the way they looked at the autumn wool when the weighing came out heavy.

That was the day a price formed around her. She felt it settle over her shoulders like a garment being fitted, and she stood still for the fitting, seven years old, on fire, because standing still was what everyone's faces were asking her to do.

One other thing happened that season, small enough that she filed it under mysteries and kept it there for thirty years. Twice that summer, before dawn, someone left supplies at the village edge — waybread, clean bandages, dried fish, a hand-drawn warning about Discord movement in the eastern gullies, everything wrapped in oilcloth and weighed down with a stone. The watchman swore he had seen the courier once: a tall woman, scarred, already leaving. The village argued about it for a week and then ate the fish. Lupa remembered the wrapping. Someone out there tied very careful knots for people whose names she never asked.

## II. The Hill

The city announced itself by sound first: windbells. Thousands of them, strung from eaves and archways, so that the whole Hill rang faintly whenever the sea wind climbed it, a shimmer of bronze over the forge-hammers and the crowd-roar rolling down from Colosseum Olymdos like weather. Then by color: white stone everywhere, volcanic black beneath it, digital billboards burning gladiators' faces forty feet tall beside braziers that had held the same fire for a century. Heroes stood in marble on every corner, sword up, chin up, dying beautifully.

The Silva estate crowned the fourth terrace, and above its gate hung the crest her parents had worn on their sleeves all her life: a she-wolf in bronze, teats heavy, two small children beneath her, reaching up. The House claimed descent from the foundlings. Every great House on the Hill claimed descent from the foundlings; the wolf belonged to the Silvas the way everything eventually belonged to the Silvas, by filing first.

A porter read her papers at the gate, looked from the designation to the crest and back, and laughed through his nose.

"They named her for the door," he told the guard.

Inside the walls waited forty other children with prices on their shoulders. The trials ran nine days. Lupa fought in the mornings and mended in the afternoons and slept in a hall that smelled of forty kinds of fear, and one by one the forty became twenty, and the twenty became nine, and on the ninth day she stepped over the last of them — a plateau boy with a broken guard, weeping into the sand, whose smell she would remember longer than his face — and walked, teeth gritted, spear hand cramping, into the grand hall of House Silva.

Domina Julia came down the hall alone. She was younger than Lupa had imagined a Domina, and she moved the way expensive things move, and her eyes were the green of deep wells with the buckets cut loose. She stopped in front of Lupa and looked at her for a long time, and Lupa understood, at seven, with total clarity, that she was being read the way the village elders read the wool: staple, weight, yield.

Instinct turned her head. The hall behind her held forty pillars and two hundred shadows.

Her parents had chosen from among the shadows.

She learned the arithmetic of it later, in pieces, the way children learn everything. The fee had been generous. Generous enough to re-open certain doors on the Hill for a certain dismissed household, or to open new ones far from it — the House was indifferent to which, and so, in the end, was the record. Guilt is cheap in Septimont. The House had overpaid on principle.

Lupa stood in the grand hall under the painted ceiling and waited to feel the thing the poets said you feel. What arrived instead was quieter. Something in her chest pulled out a chair, sat down, and stayed seated. She was seven years old and dying had already turned out its pockets in front of her; there was nothing left in them she wanted. One item stayed hidden — the shape of the thing, the how and the when — and the hiding frightened her, and would go on frightening her for the rest of her life, at a low professional hum. The rest of fear she retired that night, folded neatly, like a uniform belonging to a smaller girl.

Her debut came fast. Julia believed in striking while the story was warm — *the wolf-child of House Silva, bought from a burning ring in the hills* — and the Colosseum's junior card put a nine-year-old with a training spear in front of thirty thousand people. Lupa remembered the tunnel, the smell of oiled sand and cheap wine, the wall of light at the tunnel's end. She remembered opening her mouth as she walked into it — to say something, to say anything, her own name maybe, just to hear it once in her own voice in that place.

The roar took it. The roar took everything. Whatever she said in the light belonged to the crowd now, dissolved in it, and she learned the first law of the sand before her first exchange: the arena speaks in one voice, and the voice is loud precisely so that nobody has to hear anyone in particular.

High above, in the Ephor's box, sat the two figures every gladiator learned by silhouette. The Ephor herself, scar-knuckled, watching the sand the way masons watch a wall. And at her right shoulder, blue silk and stillness, the High Priestess, who watched something else — the crowd, or the sky, or a place fifteen years away. Lupa was nine. She catalogued them both under *far away* and went to work.

She won.

## III. The Asset

By fourteen she was meeting herself on every corner.

The Lupa on the billboards stood nine feet tall and drank a soda the flesh Lupa had never tasted. The wall-Lupa endorsed supplements, blade oil, a chain of bathhouses, a brand of instant noodles whose mascot was her own tail. The wall-Lupa smiled with all her teeth. Domina Julia owned the teeth — clause four, likeness and name, all public and commercial uses. Some mornings, running the terrace stairs before dawn, Lupa passed under her own forty-foot face and the two of them ignored each other, colleagues on different shifts.

The wins stacked. The teammates cycled.

The first partner lasted a season; he read her speed as an insult and his own body as a betrayal, and he asked for reassignment with his eyes long before his mouth caught up. The second was dismissed by Julia over dinner-table politics Lupa never saw the shape of. The third came in already sold — a mole, seeded by a rival house, feeding out training schedules and injury reports and, at the end, the rumors: *the wolf is Julia's creature, the wolf's matches are bought, the wolf sleeps in a kennel.* The House found him eventually. Julia's mercy had a standard form, and it read: *barred from every arena on the Hill, duration at the pleasure of the Domina.* Lupa came back from the hearing to a training room already emptied of his gear, sat down in the dark — her eyes worked fine in the dark; the dark was one of the Forte's gifts, along with the fire and the nose — and waited for the next one to be issued to her, like boots.

And then there were the other kind. The ones who fought well and were liked fine and simply could not survive the mathematics of standing next to her — who heard, night after night, fifty thousand voices chanting one name, and did the subtraction, and came up as the remainder. She tried words on a few of them. The words came back sharpened and were used on her: *you'll never know what it feels like. You've never lost. You've never even been in the room where losing happens.* And when the dust settled she was, every time, the one who came out ahead, which proved their point better than they could, so she retired the words too. Silence packed light. Silence traveled well.

What she kept, instead of teammates, was a recording.

She found it at sixteen, deep in the House's match archive, misfiled under an old sponsorship code: the junior circuit and early Agon rounds of a gladiator from twenty years back. Contender Mya. Ragunnan-born, which the commentators mentioned every third sentence. The weakest Resonator in her bracket, which they mentioned every second. And she fought like — Lupa ran the tape four times before she found the word — like someone *arriving*. Every exchange met head-on. Every gap in her own guard paid for in full and paid again. A fighter with a real heart, one commentator said, in the tone people use for a horse they like and would never bet on.

The tape had a flaw, a few seconds of tunnel audio caught before a bout, the boom left live by accident. A woman's voice, low, fast, talking to itself: *you love this, you love this, they came to see you, don't — please — let me not disappoint them, please, just this once more —* and then the roar took her too.

Lupa watched the tape until she could fight both sides of it from memory. Somewhere in the fortieth viewing she started talking back.

"You drop your shoulder before the lunge," she told the screen. The screen dropped its shoulder. "There. Again. Every time. Someone should have beaten that out of you in a season."

The screen lunged, shoulder first, and won anyway, on heart and arithmetic, and the tiny crowd of twenty years ago made its tiny roar, and Lupa sat in the dark with her tail curled around the chair leg and let the flicker carve shadows into her face. That was the good thing about the woman on the tape. She could be told things. She stayed. She was the first colleague Lupa had ever had who stayed.

The records on Mya thinned out toward the end. A championship, won. Then a gap. Then vanished mid-tournament — the archive's own word, *withdrawn*, stamped over a final bracket with her name scratched from it — amid a sediment of old forum rumors: a manager named John who booked her breakdowns like venues; nobles running a side-book on which round the crying would start; a girl heard talking to empty air in the training tunnels. Then the second Dark Tide had come up the coast, and the city had bigger dead to count, and the record simply ran out, the way records do when nobody is paid to continue them.

Old Vopiscus, the cutman who taped Lupa's hands for a decade, had a theory, delivered while winding gauze, in the voice of a man reciting weather. "The Tide has ears, pup." Tug, wrap. "It listens for the ones who eat alone. Every fighter who ever went strange in this city went quiet first. Quiet, then strange, then gone." Tug. "You eat with the tape again last night?" Wrap. "Thought so. Eat with something that eats back."

She was seventeen when the Ephor's reforms reached the senior circuit — teams of two mandatory, Echoes registered, the old assassination politics dragged into daylight and killed there, merit boards published where the touts used to stand. Half the Hill called it the end of the culture. Lupa watched Augusta announce it from the arena floor, scarred hands flat on the podium, jaw set like a lintel, and filed the Ephor under a category with one other entry in it: *survived something.* She could smell it from the fiftieth row. Some fires bank themselves and burn for thirty years.

The frightening thing about the woman on the tape was the stamp. *Withdrawn.* Twenty years of heart, converted by one word into filing. Lupa was Septimont's biggest star by twenty; she could read a crowd, a bracket, and a betting line like scripture; and every line of scripture said the same thing about the ones who stumbled before the end: the city remembers its champions in marble and its contenders as clerical noise. She had stopped fearing death at seven. What the tape taught her to fear had better aim.

*If you fail to reach the end, they remember you as nothing.*

She trained accordingly. She won accordingly. And every night the fire under her skin — the wet, howling heat from the ring behind the tannery, older now, better fed — paced its enclosure and asked her the question fires ask.

## IV. The Line

The ledger found her by accident, the way true things on the Hill always traveled — sideways, after hours, in the wrong drawer.

She was twenty-three and partnered with Emilio, a second-generation House fighter with beautiful footwork and a courtier's face, and they had just taken a title bout in four rounds against the Ashen Pair, and something about round three had been sitting wrong in her ever since the sand. The Ashen striker had a counter off the left shoulder that Lupa had studied for a month — a real counter, a killer — and in round three the opening had come, textbook, gift-wrapped, and the striker had looked at it and let it close. Fighters miss openings. Fighters do that. But the striker's smell had been wrong for a missed opening. Missed openings smell like fury. The striker had smelled like a man completing a delivery.

She went looking. It took her a week of wrong drawers.

The book kept itself in the language all such books keep themselves in — schedules of "appearance considerations," "outcome insurance," "narrative management fees" — but the spine of it was plain enough for a girl raised on wool weights: matches priced, rounds scripted, opponents compensated for the quality of their losing. Years of it. Her matches were in it. Her *clean* matches were in it, hedged both ways, because a fixed win and an honest win paid the House the same as long as the House had written both endings in advance.

She took it to Emilio first, because a partner is a partner, and laid the book on the table between them, and watched his courtier's face do a slow professional inventory of exits.

"You knew."

"Everyone above a certain floor knows." He said it gently, which was the worst available way to say it. "Lupa. Look around you. The Hill runs on this. The purses, the odds, the House shares — the sand is the *advertisement.* You think the Domina keeps forty children a year in boots on ticket sales?"

"The Ashen striker had me. Round three. He was paid to put it away."

"He was paid to keep the story on schedule. You're the story." Emilio spread his hands, reasonable, weary, twenty-six going on Julia. "Not everyone has the strength to survive Septimont with a clean conscience. Most of us make our peace by the second season. You've had, what — sixteen? That's late, for peace. But it comes."

She looked at him for a long time. The fire under her skin had gone very quiet, the way it went quiet before it committed.

"If there's no punishment for crossing the line," she said, "what's the point of a line at all?"

"Ah," said Emilio, softly, like a man watching a fine investment burn. "There she is. The village is still in you after all."

She went over his head that night, to the only head there was.

Domina Julia received her in the small study, the one with the bronze wolf in miniature on the desk, and heard her out in a silence with excellent posture, and when Lupa had finished — the ledger, the striker, the round, the years — the Domina folded her hands and delivered the House's position in the tone of a woman correcting a child's grammar.

"You were purchased," Julia said, "at seven, from a family the House had already discarded once, at a price that restored their standing. Every meal you have eaten since, every hour of training, every stitch of armor, every face of yours on every wall of this city — assets, all of it, and assets are managed. The sand is managed. The story is managed. You," and the green eyes came up, fathomless, buckets long gone, "are the finest instrument this House has held in three generations, and instruments do not audit the orchestra." A pause, calibrated. "Your parents understood the terms. Curious, that the child hasn't inherited the sense."

Lupa stood very still while the fitting settled over her shoulders one last time — the price, the garment, seven years old in a scorched ring — and then, for the first time in her life, she moved inside it, and it split along every seam at once.

"Keep the walls," she said. "Keep the teeth. I'm taking the one that bleeds."

The exit was paperwork, and the paperwork was a massacre. The House retained her registered name in all commercial uses; the House retained her likeness, her endorsements, her trust winnings pending "review"; the House circulated, within the day, its regret at the departure of a beloved athlete whose recent conduct suggested strain. She walked out of the fourth-terrace gate with a spear, two changes of clothes, half a season's purse in loose shells, and a designation the law had just informed her she was wearing on loan. Above the gate the bronze she-wolf went on feeding her bronze foundlings, teats heavy, eyes elsewhere, and Lupa stopped under it, and looked up at it properly for the first time since she was seven.

"You'd bite," she told the crest. "They forgot that part."

Her plan fit on a betting slip, and she had written it that way on purpose. The Great Agon opened in a month. Free agents could file. The House had money on her — the House always had money, both ways, on everything — and the House's models priced a wolf without handlers as a wolf without a chance. She was going to enter the Agon in her own unowned skin, and win it, and stand on the champion's dais while every quiet position House Silva held against her came due at once, and the Domina learned in public what the ledger had always priced at sixty to one: withdrawal, reason unpublished, of Lupa, from the Silvas.

She checked on her parents exactly once, that same week, through a records clerk who owed her a favor. The registry held a transfer of funds, seventeen years old, and the phrase *no forwarding address.* She read it twice, paid the clerk, and walked home through the fighters' quarter under her own forty-foot face, and the two of them ignored each other, colleagues on different shifts, and the last thing her parents had ever asked her was still, and would always be, a question she'd answered yes.

## V. The Road

Dusk, a month later, on the north road below the second terrace, six of her former colleagues stepped out of the rocks with practice steel and House instructions, and Lupa leapt off the wagon track to meet them, glad, if she was honest, of the appointment.

She recognized the choreography before the first exchange — Emilio's footwork school, staggered pairs, herd the target off the road, nothing on the record, a free agent regrettably injured in a brigand incident three weeks before filing. She broke the first pair's rhythm with the spear haft, put one of them in the ditch, and had turned into the second pair when the evening produced two strangers.

The first came in from the road at a dead sprint: black-haired, golden-eyed, sword already drawn, form like a duelist who had been taught by someone very patient and very good. She hit the flank pair the way weather hits, and beside her, at a stroll, arrived a silver-haired bard with a lute across her back and her hands in her pockets, who took up a position of maximum spectating and began, audibly, to compose.

"Yours?" the swordswoman asked, falling in at Lupa's shoulder like they'd drilled it, nodding at the six.

"Former colleagues."

"They seem fond."

"It's the severance package."

The bard, from her rock: "*Six wolves set upon the wolf, at dusk, upon the northern road* — no. Too many wolves. Give me a minute."

It lasted four more exchanges. Emilio's school had beautiful footwork and had been trained, to a man, against opponents paid to be slow, and the black-haired stranger had visibly never met a paid opponent in her life; she fought every one of the six like they were the last problem in the world and she was delighted by problems. When it was done — six in the ditch, breathing, filing already composing itself in their heads — the stranger flicked her blade clean and stuck out a hand.

"Mei."

"Lupa." The grip was good. Working calluses, sword and rope both. The bard came off her rock and offered her own hand, and the moment their palms met the woman went strange — pupils flaring, breath caught, fingers tightening a half-second past politeness, staring at Lupa's palm like it had spoken.

"Ciaccona," the bard said, recovering, in the tone of someone deciding to be alarmed later. "You've — hm. Have you ever met a knight? Small, blonde, laurel crown, catastrophic table manners?"

"Cartethyia," said Mei. "We're looking for her. She came here to bleed for something and we'd rather she didn't."

The name did a thing to Lupa that she had no drawer for. It landed the way a smell lands from a great distance — smoke from a fire already out, familiar in the tissue and absent from the record — and her tail, that traitor, went still and attentive, pointing at nothing.

"Never heard of her," Lupa said, which was true, and felt like a lie all the way down.

The proposal came out of the swordswoman over the ditch-side fire that evening, laid flat, the way Lupa preferred her terms: the Agon finals were where the Lioness of Glory appeared; a champion's fame shook loose any information in the city; Mei needed a partner and a way into the bracket; Lupa needed exactly one thing from the world and it happened to be the same bracket. Lupa's answer was a spear leveled at Mei's chest and an invitation to earn it, and the sparring went four minutes, and included one spectacular faceplant into volcanic dust that Ciaccona set to music on the spot, and ended with Lupa disarmed by a coil-and-cut she had never seen from anything on two legs.

She looked at her spear in the stranger's hand. She looked at the stranger, who was already holding it back out to her, grip first, like the whole exchange had been a loan.

"There's a registration office on the second terrace," Lupa said. "It opens at six."

At the counter, under the yellow light, the clerk asked for a team name, and Mei took the form and wrote two words in a fast, tilted hand and slid it across for inspection.

*Wuthering Waves.*

"That's a weather report," Lupa said.

"It's a heading," said Mei.

Somewhere on the walk down, in the manner of a woman declaring cargo, Ciaccona mentioned that she and Mei were, technically, wanted heretics in Ragunna, fifty thousand shells between them, dead or alive. Lupa shrugged and kept walking. Septimont answered to strength, and the Order's paper had never once stopped a spear. She told them the local rate for lodging, and the name of a tavern where nobody watered the wheat juice, and which billboards to walk under fast, and it took her three streets to notice she was doing it — provisioning them, mapping their exits, walking on the traffic side — the full logistics of keeping, performed unasked, for two strangers she had known for six hours.

Her tail had started its count the moment the sword hit the flank pair.

She told herself it was strategy, and put out the fire, and took the watch.

# What the Fire Keeps

*Lupa has no diary. She has a habit twenty years old: she talks to a woman on a tape. Later, she talks to the fire, because the fire is where the woman lives now. These are the parts the fire keeps.*

## i. Mei

*[ and the foundling reached up*
*with both hands,*
*which is the whole of the instruction ]*

You'd have hated her first, Mya. Then you'd have kept her.

She fights like the openings are gifts and she's embarrassed to take them. First week of training I told her so, straight to her face: *you fight like you're apologizing.* You know what she did? Pictured somebody on the practice dummy and took its head clean off. Then apologized to the dummy. She smells like moonflower some mornings, which means the blue silk visits her at midnight and leaves her worse, and she carries it the way you carried the crowd, right behind the sternum, smiling on schedule.

I told them about you. Fulvia's tavern, after the bard sang the Maiden ballad, and the story of you climbed out of me sideways, the way it does, and my voice did something in the middle of it that I'm choosing to call the acoustics. Mei promised she'd do her best in the final. Do her best. Twenty years I've watched fighters do their best, Mya; the stamp at the end reads the same. I cut her off. *Don't. Just win.* The old script, the only one I had.

Here's the part I'm reporting, because you're the one who taught me what it costs.

The night before the final, when we knew what was riding in my spear — the whisper-thing, your whisper-thing, the one that ate you in front of a betting book — I gave her my weak points. Said them out loud, in order. The left ribs, the old break, badly set, third season. The tunnel vision when the kill is close. *If it takes me, strike there. Put me down.* Sixteen years on the Hill and I never told a partner where I break. In this city, Mya, that sentence is the whole dowry. That's the ring, the vows, the will.

And she refused the terms. Sat there with the cracked cup of Heartpour and rewrote my last testament to my face — *"I'm not killing you. And I'm not letting you die."* — and when I called it soft she said the thing I've been chewing since. *Living takes more courage than dying.* From anyone else on this Hill that's embroidery. From her it landed like a diagnosis, because I've been ready to die since I was seven, Mya, ready the way luggage is ready, and she looked at the luggage and asked where I was planning to *live.*

We won. You know we won; the whole city knows; my half of the medal is getting framed. The Ephor split it down the middle in front of fifty thousand people and said true strength lay in protecting the ones beside you, and the crowd cheered the sentence the way crowds cheer sentences, and I stood there holding half a medal and thinking: the roar took my voice at nine, took yours at whatever they'd worn you down to. It's still loud. But there's a woman standing in it next to me who heard me say *ribs, left side, third season* and answered *then I'll stand on your left.*

I ate with something that eats back, Vopiscus. Took me long enough.

## ii. Cartethyia

*[ twice-met:*
*once in the burned draft,*
*once in the fair copy ]*

I have to report something crooked, Mya, so hold still.

I've met the knight twice, and the first time got erased. It came back to me on the cold stone outside the orrery with everything else — every drowned cycle, every draft of this city the playwright burned behind him — and in one of them there was a girl with my body and none of my name, rain-wrecked, coral at the wrists, done, kneeling in a dead Septimont and asking the dark to hurry up. And a knight who had crossed a sea for a city she'd never seen crouched down in front of that nothing-girl and refused the surrender. *This is your city. Some things only you can do.* Gave her a sliver of her own frequency and an instruction — live, jump to the next story, carry my message — and the girl faded out promising to try.

I was the girl, Mya. The message got through. Postal service in this country is a miracle.

So when she came gliding down out of the sky outside the Hub — whole, laurel bright, eyes her own — I crushed all three of them into one armful and wept like a recruit, and I've decided to let the record stand, because she was the one who taught the nothing-girl that a life mocked by fate can break its curse, and a wolf pays her debts in the open. She knows what erasure tastes like. Twenty years sealed in a tower, sewing dolls and naming them so the dark couldn't take the names. When she says *Lupa* it comes back to me with the weight on, certified, all cycles included. There's exactly one other person on this continent who can do that, and I'm talking to her.

The bell told me the rest of you. I want you to know that I know, and that I said it out loud where the water could hear. Julia's boat, twenty years ago. Your body, traded up the chain like wool. The Priestess standing in her blue silk letting the playwright praise her cleverness while they carried you into that lab, and twenty years of them growing their tide-thing fat on your bones. I named her tower for what it was, built on bones, and I put my spear through the machine they made of you, and steam is all that's left of it, and then — Mya, then you came, the last of you, and asked me for the crown of glory, and I gave it to you the way you asked, clean, through the heart, joy and fury, race to the finish. You thanked me *for remembering.* Twenty years of this city and the one payment you asked for was witness.

Your sword's in the ground on the cliff now, in the viburnum, the little white ones your people used to throw. The knight touched the grip and said you fought well, and coming from her that's a battle citation with a god's countersignature. I told the cliff what our city is — drunk, laughing, carving statues, burning like fire, drenched in victory and defeat alike, holding all our disappointments and all our hopes — because both things are true and you earned a eulogist who'd say both.

And here's the change, since I'm filing changes: the fire's different now. It used to be anger with humidity in it. Since the orrery it's *inhabited.* You're in it. I felt you the first time it came off my arms afterward — that many-throated sound it makes, one of the throats is yours. I did that on purpose, Mya. Ghosts get carried or they get dropped, and I've seen what this country does with the dropped ones.

We hunt together. Even the dead. Especially the dead.

## iii. Ciaccona

*[ she hid the truth in her fist for years —*
*her father said: open the hand,*
*raise it, burning, so the road shows ]*

The bard writes us into a mock-epic, Mya. Cantos. There's a running gag where my tail has opinions, delivered in iambs. She gets the meter of the tail deliberately wrong so I'll argue prosody with her, and I argue, every time, because arguing prosody with a bard at midnight over wheat juice is apparently a thing I do now. File that under changes.

Here's the real filing.

Her family got erased too — the Toccatas, banished one by one for singing what was real, her father last, handing her a key and a creed with the guards already at the door: raise the truth like a torch, so it lights the way for others. She's carried it alone since she was small. Banished herself for it. And in the collapsing orrery, when they pushed me out alone into the next page of the story, what was lying in the dust beside me — placed, Mya, aimed — was her copper bell. The instrument that rings the past up out of the air. A bard, going down with a sealed fiction, spent her last free motion leaving her torch where the wolf would land.

I rang it against the dark and it gave me you. Your death, your refusal, your viburnum. The bell is why anyone alive knows what actually happened to Mya of Ragunna, and the bell was a gift, and I have been sitting with what that means for the thing I've feared since I was sixteen in a dark archive room.

I feared the stamp. *Withdrawn.* I feared ending the way the record ended you — twenty years of heart converted to clerical noise. And marble, it turns out, is a decoy. This city is *made* of marble, Mya; marble is how Septimont forgets people expensively. The real countermeasure is a woman with cracked fingertips who stands in a square with a warrant on her head and sings the erased back into the ledger, and dares the ledger to do something about it. She played individual griefs back to a god once, one at a time, until being witnessed beat being ordered. I watched it work. On a god.

So I've taken up the trade, in my dialect. I tell your story now. Tavern by tavern, recruit by recruit, whenever some kid with a price on her shoulders asks who the best I ever saw was, I say Contender Mya, weakest Resonator in her bracket, never once ran, and I watch the name go into them like a coal into dry grass. The bard says I'm becoming an oral tradition. I told her oral traditions bench-press. She's putting that in the epic too.

The torch business, Mya. It turns out you can do it with a spear.

## iv. Chisa

*[ the wolf came down to the reeds*
*because someone had left something there —*
*that is the entire myth,*
*everything else is city ]*

New one, Mya. Count her in.

Girl named Chisa. Scissors the size of a door, cuts space itself, can't read faces — every human face a smear of color to her, so she learned people by gait and voice and habit, which means she reads truer than anyone in the pack, because faces are where people keep their lies. Her hometown feared what she was, and she decided her existence cost her parents too much, so she took the one tool anyone had ever handed her and cut herself out of their lives. Then a dead city swallowed her for twenty years with one friend, and the friend walked up a frozen tower alone and spent herself, entry by entry, laying a road out of her own bones so the girl could be found. *That's enough for me,* the friend wrote, half glass. *I feel content.*

I read that entry standing in the cold, Mya, and I went so rigid the pack gave me a radius. A girl alone in the dark, methodically making her own death easy for others to find, and calling it enough. I have seen that arithmetic before. It's the Hill's arithmetic. It's the Priestess's, it's yours, it's this whole country's — everyone pricing themselves for someone else's ledger, everyone eating alone, and old Vopiscus in my ear across thirty years: *the Tide has ears, pup.* Of course it has ears. This place *farms* the lonely. The deep just harvests.

So when the exit was open and the city was coming down and the girl froze on the threshold — grief pulling her back into the collapse, the friend's road ending three steps short of done — I did the only load-bearing thing I know. I hauled her over the line bodily and I roared it into her face: *YOU'RE GONNA CARRY THAT WEIGHT.* Carry it. Walk anyway. And afterward, quieter, the second half, the half I never got at seven: you have a door. Your parents kept a door. Go back and see if they missed you more than they feared the whispers. Don't be an idiot and pretend it isn't there.

She went. The mother had kept her room untouched for twenty years, Mya. Twenty years. The girl came back to us with a red string on her wrist and a quieter spine, and my own registry entry — *no forwarding address*, seventeen years old — sat exactly where it always sits, and weighed exactly what it always weighs, and I found I could carry it better for having watched someone else's door open. That's the trade I run now. Other people's doors. I keep a whole ring of keys that fit none of my own locks, and it turns out that's a living.

Months on, a forest bench, after the worst of it. She sat down next to me on purpose — she does everything on purpose, it's the scissors in her — and asked her question with the ledger open: *When you told me to carry the weight. Did it get lighter?*

I gave her the truth, because she reads gaits. "No. You just get used to walking."

She nodded, and held the scissors without opening them, and we sat there getting used to it together, and my tail did the slow sweep it does when the census comes out right.

The census, Mya. That's my post now, did I tell you? Somebody has to say out loud who belongs. Nobody ever said it for me — I got a designation, a crest, a clause four — so I say it, plainly, in rooms, where it's binding: *she's ours. We hunt together. That's the rule.* I said it over a priestess who'd earned a spear and took the yes-yes-yes out of her myself first. I said it over an impostor kid with a stolen name and a forged letter, and stood in front of the arresting officers as a wall with a tail. Saying it is the belonging. I checked. There's no other mechanism. The whole apparatus is a wolf, saying it.

Hand on the new kid's head at the muster. Soup outside the grief-room, two portions, second one taped shut so a certain someone remembers meals exist. Thermal flask of the priestess's earl grey slid across a floor at noon, no note, no waiting for thanks — thanks is an ambush, I change the subject to logistics. This is the arsenal, Mya. This is what the howling flame turned out to be for.

## Coda: The Fire Is Too Big

The Guild charter passed eleven days early. There's a concourse board at Olymdos now, public, permanent, every fighter's contract terms in daylight where the touts used to stand — and a column I fought for line by line, because every person who came from somewhere deserves to have that somewhere acknowledged. Hometown.

I filed my own form last week. Free agent, own name — the Temple's verdict stripped the House of theirs; the Silvas are banished, the accusation was mine, the wolf came off their gate with a crane and two chains and I watched. The clerk reached the hometown field and looked up.

"Caldara," I said.

"Spell it?"

I spelled it. The board learned the word by evening, six letters of light on Capitoline Hill, and somewhere three days above the Sanguis Plateaus there's a shallow lake that thinks it can boil, and it's on the record now. First time this city ever pronounced it. It won't be the last; there's a kid from the Plateaus in the junior bracket with a nose like mine, and when she wins something she's going to point at her town's name in lights, and the roar — the same roar, Mya, the one that ate our voices — is going to carry a village up the Hill whether it likes it or not. I rebuilt the machine. Same crowd, new freight.

Tonight the pack's on the terrace and I built the fire too big again. Cartethyia's stringing crescents. The bard's tuning. Mei's asleep on the priestess, who is awake, on watch, learning the trade. Chisa's scissors are catching the light and Lynae is pretending she isn't warming her hands. Everyone has to sit close — heat distribution, I'll explain, if anyone asks, and the tail will sell me out, and I'll allow it.

You told me to go win things.

Here's the tally, Contender. One medal, half of it mine, framed. One House, down. One village, in lights. One story — yours — in circulation, tavern-rated, god-tested. Seven, eight, nine people, counted out loud, binding. One fire, oversized, on purpose, with room in it for everybody I've ever lost and everybody I haven't yet, because I checked the founding hymn and the marble got it wrong: the wolf came down to the reeds *because someone was there.* That's the entire myth. Everything else is city.

Rest now. I've got the watch.

Chapter 1: Pillow Talk

When Iuno lifted the wooden latch of the back bedroom a little past the tenth bell, the cold from the stone step came in with her, tucked into the folds of her wool coat along with the smell of woodsmoke and strong bergamot.

She clicked the latch shut behind her with her heel so it wouldn’t rattle.

The pinch-wick lamp on the upturned crate had been turned down to a bead of yellow the size of a lentil. Beside it, between Constable Whiskers and Deputy Lump, sat Barnaby the pine-knot frog, wearing Amy’s pale blue school tie draped in a loose loop around his cross-eyed neck like a ceremonial stole.

Amy was out cold in the middle of the pallet. She had finally taken off Lupa’s sheepskin vest, using it to wrap her folded summer blouse into a neat bundle that she was hugging to her chest with both arms, her chin tucked down and her pink hair spilled across half of Mei’s pillow.

Mei was on the far side by the wall, and she was not even pretending to be asleep.

She lay propped on her good side with her palm flat under her cheek, her golden eyes wide open and catching the tiny reflection of the lamp-wick across the top of Amy’s head. The instant Iuno’s boot touched the floorboards inside the threshold, Mei’s eyebrows went up.

Iuno didn’t speak. She walked to the crate, shrugged her coat off her shoulders so it dropped onto the foot of the mattress, and stood before the small propped hand-mirror, reaching up with her right hand to pull the first silver pin from her hair. The crystal at her left shoulder gave a faint, cold chime as her weight shifted.

“You were out there a long time,” Mei whispered across Amy’s hair.

Her whisper was built for an amphitheatre; it bounced off the timber wall behind her and rattled the paper label inside Barnaby’s stomach.

Iuno set the first pin down on the crate lid beside the frog’s left foot. “Lower your voice, or you’ll wake the child and she’ll make you explain the salted cocoa at eleven at night.”

“She sleeps like a boulder when she’s got her laundry.” Mei dropped her volume by half an inch, wiggling her shoulders a little closer to the middle of the bed while keeping her abdomen flat against the mattress. “What were you and Suisui talking about on the step? I heard the door click after the camera flash cleared.”

Iuno pulled the second pin free, letting a heavy coil of silver-blue hair unroll down the back of her neck, and shook it out with her fingers.

“Barley,” Iuno murmured.

Mei squinted at her in the dim orange light. “Barley.”

“The Huai syndicate is holding six barges at the Mengzhou river locks until the hard freeze,” Iuno said, setting the third pin down in a neat parallel line with the other two. “And the Wednesday post-coach schedule.”

Every word of it was true. Not a single syllable mentioned a velvet box in her coat pocket or a letter promised to a Mingting safe.

*Masterful, darling,* the High Priestess murmured inside her head, already yawning behind a phantom fan. *Thirty years of Senate subcommittees, finally put to domestic use.*

*Hush,* Iuno thought. *Go to sleep.*

Mei stared at the back of Iuno’s neck for three beats, clearly weighing whether to poke at *barley*, and then lost interest entirely because the thing she had actually been saving up for an hour bubbled back to the top.

She let her head sink off her palm onto the pillow, looking at the back of Amy’s pink crown two inches from her nose. Up near Amy’s left temple, the little acid-green copper star was still clipped into the hair so she wouldn’t lose it in the blankets.

“Iuiu,” Mei whispered as Iuno came around the foot of the pallet and sat down on the near edge to slip off her boots.

“Mm.”

“Fish-shears,” Mei said to the pillow.

Iuno paused with her hand on her boot-heel and glanced back over her shoulder. Mei wasn’t grinning the big, loud grin she used in the kitchen; her mouth was just curved, soft and crooked, her thumb absently tracing the grey ink-smudge at the base of her own hand where Amy had drawn the star at dinner.

“She had bangs when she was eight,” Mei whispered, as if Iuno hadn’t been standing two feet away on the scullery steps when it happened. “Because of the birch sap. And she hid behind the stove for two days in her wool hat.”

“I was there, sweetheart,” Iuno said quietly, setting her second boot neatly beside the crate and sliding under the quilt on Amy’s other side. The mattress dipped, rolling Amy an inch toward Mei’s ribs; Amy let out a small, snuffling grunt in her sleep and burrowed her nose deeper into the folded sailor blouse.

“I know you were there,” Mei whispered, reaching her arm across the hump of Amy’s shoulder so her fingers could find Iuno’s wrist over the wool. “I’m just saying. It worked. You said to just ask her something stupid at breakfast, and I did, and she didn’t even get weird about it. She just yelled at me.”

“She is very talented at yelling at you,” Iuno said, turning onto her right side so her good arm could rest across the blanket between them, her fingers hooking loosely into Mei’s. “It’s where she gets the lung capacity.”

“So tomorrow,” Mei went on, her eyes brightening in the dark as the plan took shape, “at lunch—or maybe on the walk back up from the coach, when everybody’s awake—I’m gonna ask her if she ever lost a tooth in an apple. Or which tooth came out first. And then on Tuesday I can ask what her favourite soup was when she was ten, and whether we had a sled or if she just used a coal-shovel on the hill—”

Iuno’s thumb pressed down once on the back of Mei’s knuckles, stopping the list mid-stride.

“Mei.”

“What? Those are good questions! Normal questions! Zero tragedy!”

“They are normal questions if you ask one a week when you happen to see an apple,” Iuno said dryly, keeping her voice low enough to slide right over the sleeping girl’s ear. “If you sit down with a mental clipboard and ask her one every morning after the pancakes like a census clerk auditing a parish, she is going to figure out what you’re doing by Wednesday, and then she will either start making up lies about pet walruses to see if you write them down, or she’ll go lock herself in Nyx’s cockpit until Friday.”

Mei blinked across the dark. Her mouth opened, then closed again as she thought about it.

“...She’d totally make up a walrus,” Mei admitted.

“She would name it, give it a rank in the seal parliament, and make Cartethyia salute it,” Iuno said. “You have the rest of your life, darling. Stop trying to cram twelve years into a weekend before the stitches come out. Let her drop the pieces when her hands are full.”

Mei let out a long, quiet breath that stirred the stray pink hairs on the pillow between them. Her fingers relaxed inside Iuno’s grip.

“Okay,” Mei whispered. “No clipboard. Only when there’s an apple.”

For five seconds the little room was completely still except for the muffled *whuff-creak* of the wooden turbine turning on the storehouse sill outside.

Then, from directly between them, muffled by four layers of unbleached cotton sailor blouse and half a feather pillow, a third voice spoke without moving a muscle:

“It took till May.”

Mei jumped half an inch, winced as her middle reminded her not to do that, and stared down at the top of her daughter’s head.

Amy hadn’t opened her eyes. She hadn’t lifted her chin out of the laundry. She was lying in the exact same curled ball she’d been in for forty minutes.

“...Amymy?” Mei whispered. “I thought you were asleep!”

“Was asleep,” Amy mumbled into the cotton, her voice thick and flat as cold porridge. “Then somebody started breathing loud about walruses right in my ear.”

“I wasn’t breathing loud!”

“You think loud, Ma. Your eyeballs click when you’re making a list.” Amy shifted one knee an inch toward Mei’s thigh to steal the warm spot, her eyes still screwed tightly shut. “And the bangs took till May to grow out. Not April. I only said April on the steps because Lupa was laughing.”

Mei’s whole face lit up in the dark, so bright Iuno could see the gold in her eyes without the lamp. Mei bit her lower lip hard to keep from cheering and ruined it immediately by poking Amy’s shoulder with one finger.

“Why’d you think it was April if it was May?” Mei whispered.

“Because you forgot to tear the March page off the kitchen calendar behind the flour tin,” Amy muttered to the blouse. “Because you used the back of April to draw a diagram of a winch gear with charcoal, and then you pinned it back up backwards so we had two Februarys. I went to the settlement school on the first of May wearing a winter coat and earmuffs and everybody else was in shirt-sleeves.”

Iuno closed her eyes on her side of the pillow, the corner of her mouth pulling despite herself. “A winch gear.”

“It was a broken winch!” Mei defended her past self in a frantic whisper. “Probably! If a winch is broken, you gotta draw it while you remember where the teeth go!”

“And,” Amy added, rolling a quarter-turn onto her back so her nose pointed at the ceiling beams while her eyes stayed shut, clearly emptying her pockets now that she’d been woken up, “when I was nine, you made me wear the blue wool hat with the two giant yarn pom-poms to the seal rock. And I told you the Prime Minister wouldn’t take my treaty seriously if I had pom-poms on my ears. And you said seals respected pom-poms because they looked like sea-urchins.”

“Do they look like sea-urchins?” Mei asked earnestly.

“Seals don’t even eat sea-urchins, Ma! That’s otters! The Prime Minister sneezed on my boot and went back in the water and I had to renegotiate the whole fishing boundary in November!”

Mei clapped her free hand over her mouth to keep the laugh inside her chest so her stitches wouldn’t tug, her shoulders shaking against the mattress until the wooden frame gave a tiny creak.

“Okay, okay, I’m writing it down—not on a clipboard, in my head!” Mei wheezed softly through her fingers. “Two Februarys. Otters eat urchins. Sorry about the treaty, my star.”

“Don’t call me that, I’m asleep,” Amy mumbled, already sinking back down into the mattress now that the back-dated grievance had been delivered to the proper desk. Her left hand slipped out from around the folded blouse and landed heavy and warm across Mei’s forearm, pinning it to the quilt, while her right heel slid sideways under the blanket until it wedged squarely against Iuno’s shin.

“Auntie,” Amy slurred, ninety percent gone again.

“Yes, Amy,” Iuno murmured.

“Your shoulder’s cold. Put the blanket over it.”

Iuno glanced at her bare crystal stump, then pulled the corner of the wool quilt an inch higher with her chin so it covered the edge of her collarbone. “Done.”

“And are you ever gonna tell her what day it is, or do I have to listen to her ask the ceiling every morning till July?”

Mei’s hand froze under Amy’s wrist.

Iuno didn’t flinch. She reached across the sleeping bundle between them and pinched the tiny yellow flame of the oil lamp on the crate between her thumb and forefinger, plunging the room into warm, lye-scented dark.

“Go to sleep, Amy,” Iuno said.

Amy let out a long, whistling breath through her nose and was genuinely out before the smoke from the wick reached the rafters.

In the dark, Mei’s fingers crept two inches across the wool until they found Iuno’s palm again, lacing through them over Amy’s middle.

“...Hey, Iuiu,” Mei whispered, so softly it was barely more than air moving.

“What is it.”

“What day is it tomorrow?”

“Monday,” Iuno said to the dark.

Mei’s thumb brushed once across Iuno’s knuckles. “Okay. One more day, then.”

Iuno didn’t answer that. She just closed her fingers around Mei’s starred hand and left them there until Mei’s breathing slowed down to match their daughter’s.

At the far end of the main room, behind a head-high L-shaped partition built out of stacked barley sacks and an old canvas wagon-tilt, the second lamp had been out for twenty minutes.

“I am not groaning,” Cartethyia whispered to the ceiling rafters.

“You just made a noise like a church organ losing its bellows,” Ciaccona whispered back from the left side of the double pallet.

“That was a knightly exhale. For circulation.”

Cartethyia was lying flat on her back with her crystal laurel sitting on her lute-case beside the pillow. Her legs were straight, her chin was up, and both of her arms were laid out stiffly along her sides over the top of the blanket like a marble effigy on a crusader’s tomb.

After three seconds of heroic silence, Cartethyia’s right hand twitched.

Slowly, with her teeth clenched in the dark, she tried to bend her elbow to pull the corner of the wool quilt up over her collarbone, where a draft was sneaking between two sacks of barley. Her forearm rose three inches off the mattress. Her deltoid gave a tiny, pathetic flutter—the exact muscle Lupa had made her use to haul five feet of consecrated steel up to her ear forty times in a row against the iron-elm stump before breakfast—and her hand dropped back onto her stomach with a soft *flop*.

“...Cia,” Cartethyia whispered.

“Yes, my stoic paladin.”

“The blanket is very heavy tonight. I think the mountain damp has gotten into the weave.”

Ciaccona sighed through her nose, rolled over in the dark, and pulled the quilt all the way up to Cartethyia’s chin with one hand before propping herself up on an elbow.

Without waiting to be asked, Ciaccona reached over to the floor beside the lute-case, where Wen the cook had left them a small stoneware jar of rendered goose-fat mixed with crushed wintergreen leaves after watching Cartethyia try to lift her soup spoon at dinner with both hands. Ciaccona scooped a dab onto two fingers, dragged Cartethyia’s limp right arm across her own lap under the blanket, and dug her thumbs squarely into the knotted muscle below Cartethyia’s elbow.

“Ow!” Cartethyia squeaked, her toes curling under the wool. “That’s—oh, that smells like peppermints. Ow.”

“Hold still. Your forearm feels like a bag of walnuts,” Ciaccona muttered, working her thumbs up the tendon with the practiced, ruthless precision of a string player who had spent twenty years un-cramping her own wrists after four-hour tavern sets. “Honestly, letting that wolf teach you footwork. She doesn’t have a pedagogical bone in her body. Her idea of a lesson is barking in four-four time until somebody’s arms fall off.”

“She was very helpful!” Cartethyia defended her coach loyally, though she let her head tip sideways on the pillow toward Ciaccona’s shoulder as the wintergreen heat started to sink in. “She said my left heel only went to the next county on the eighth strike instead of the coast! And tomorrow morning for the escort, I won’t even need to swing anything, I just have to hold the two gatehouse braziers while I’m big so Sir Honk can see the stairs.”

“About the poultry,” Ciaccona murmured, moving her thumbs up to the cap of Cartethyia’s shoulder and pressing into the tight seam behind the collarbone. “Did you actually clear this with Old Man Zhao, or are we kidnapping a goose at four in the morning?”

“Master Zhao is bringing the hemp-seed mash in his apron,” Cartethyia said with total confidence. “Every six steps he drops three seeds on the frost so Sir Honk stays in the middle of the lane. If you drop four, he stops to chew and the vanguard bunches up.”

“Gods preserve us. I’m going to be playing a Mingting love ballad behind a bird eating birdseed off the stairs.”

“Did you fix the fourth bar?”

“Of course I fixed the fourth bar,” Ciaccona scoffed softly, switching arms to drag Cartethyia’s left wrist across her stomach. “Though if that lutist by the dyer’s sticks his head out the window to yell at me about the river-turn before I’ve had coffee, I’m putting a Sylph in his nightcap.”

She hummed it under her breath as her thumbs worked the left forearm—just two bars in the dark, a low, warm G-mixolydian turn that went up on the fourth beat instead of down, soft enough that it didn’t carry past the barley sacks.

Cartethyia went quiet while she hummed it.

When the two bars ended, Cartethyia didn’t immediately jump back in about the braziers or Qingyan’s dress sash. She turned her face half into the pillow so her nose was pressed against the coarse linen ticking, her voice dropping out of its bright, declarative bounce into the plain, level register she used when she was talking to lonely things.

“Cia?”

“Mm?”

“This morning in the woodyard,” Cartethyia whispered into the pillow. “Before Lupa came out. Yangyang watched me cut the knot off the side of the stump. And she knew right away that the swing was made for somebody three feet taller than me.”

Ciaccona’s thumbs slowed down on Cartethyia’s wrist, then kept going at an easy, steady pace. “Yangyang has good eyes. Even when she’s trying to figure out how her own elbows work.”

“I told her I made it up when I was fourteen because I was short,” Cartethyia said. She pulled her right hand—the one Ciaccona had already rubbed—up under her chin and curled her fingers into the edge of the pillowcase. “I used to look up at the painted saints on the chapel ceiling in Egla Town while I was failing scripture, and they all had these long, slow arms that looked like they never had to hurry for anything. So when they made me the Maiden, I copied the ceiling.”

She paused for a beat. Outside the shutters, a gust of wind off the gorge rattled an icicle loose from the eaves; it fell into the snowbank below with a soft *chink*.

“If I made her up out of a ceiling because I wasn’t tall enough,” Cartethyia asked the pillow, very plainly, “does that mean the big one is just a costume? Like—is the one who dropped the bun off her head at breakfast the only real one, and the rest is just me standing on a box?”

Ciaccona didn’t stop rubbing her arm. She didn’t sigh or pat her head either.

“When I was eleven,” Ciaccona said in the dark, her voice dry and conversational, “my father took me to the old opera house in Ragunna to hear Signora Bellini sing the storm aria from *The Doge’s Daughter*. I was four feet high and my voice sounded like a tin whistle in a stairwell. For three years after that, every time I opened my mouth to practice scales, I did Bellini. I held my chin the way she held her chin, I rolled my R’s at the back of my teeth the way she did, I even took a breath on the third beat of the bridge where she had a bad lung from the canal fever.”

Cartethyia turned her head a fraction on the pillow so one eye was clear of the linen. “Why did you copy the bad lung?”

“Because I was eleven and I thought that was where the gravitas lived,” Ciaccona said. “You copy what’s big enough to fill the hall when your own chest is too small to reach the balcony. Everybody does. Then you turn twenty, and you’re standing on a table in a noisy tavern with your wrist half-healed, and you open your mouth to shut the drunks up, and Bellini isn’t in the room anymore. It’s just your voice.”

Ciaccona gave Cartethyia’s shoulder one last firm squeeze, wiped the wintergreen salve off her thumbs onto the hem of Cartethyia’s own blanket, and slid back down under the covers.

“Nobody calls a singer’s chest voice a forgery just because she learned it off the ceiling, cara,” Ciaccona murmured, pulling the quilt over her ear. “You needed nine feet to reach the rafters, so you built nine feet. Now both of them eat too many pickled radishes and snore in G-flat. It’s the same girl.”

Cartethyia lay very still for four seconds, digesting that in the dark.

“I don’t snore in G-flat,” she whispered, though all the tightness had gone out of her shoulders, her arms finally going slack and heavy against the mattress. “Chisa only said that because the floorboards were resonating. And tomorrow when I hold the braziers for Suisui, I’m going to hold them with my left heel down so Lupa sees I was listening, and if Sir Honk tries to bite the lead horse—”

She didn’t finish the sentence.

Between *lead* and *horse*, twenty years of practice at falling asleep in three seconds flat caught up with forty minutes of chopping iron-elm. Her jaw went slack; her breath dropped into a deep, even whistle; and both of her sore arms flung themselves wide open across the bed like a starfish falling off a cliff, her right hand landing squarely on top of Ciaccona’s face.

Ciaccona didn’t even blink. She picked Cartethyia’s hand up off her nose by the wrist, deposited it back on Cartethyia’s chest, and slid her own cold left foot across the bottom of the pallet until her heel was tucked securely into the warm hollow of Cartethyia’s ribs.

For another two minutes Ciaccona lay awake staring at the dark canvas above them, her fingers tapping a silent four-four on her own collarbone while she hummed the river-turn one last time—up on the fourth beat, clean and true—before she let her eyes shut.

Out in the main room of the storehouse, the big iron stove had been banked down to a single glowing red seam along the bottom grate.

Above the hearth, stretched between a meat-hook on the mantle and the corner of the drying rack, hung a length of kitchen twine. Clipped to the twine with four of Widow Lin’s split bamboo clothespins were the four wet darkroom prints Amy had developed after supper, curling slightly at the edges in the dry chimney heat and smelling faintly of vinegar and fixer salt. Over by the pantry door, Suisui’s cedar-and-lacquer trunk sat beside the padlocked two-gallon willow hamper, both of them casting long, squat shadows across the floorboards.

On the two pallets closest to the firebox, Chisa was sitting cross-legged with her back straight and her dark hair unpinned over her shoulders. Beside her knee lay her folded white summer blouse; on top of the blouse lay the pale blue silk tie and Cartethyia’s narrow sky-blue ribbon, smoothed out flat.

Behind her knelt Lupa, wearing her sleeveless sleeping tunic and a pair of faded arena breeches, with a small flat tin of comfrey-and-lanolin salve wedged against her thigh.

Neither of them said anything about it. It was simply what happened when the lamps went down.

Lupa didn’t need light to find the latch at the back of Chisa’s neck; she had been doing it by feel since the road out of Septimont. Her big, scarred hands—hands that had wrung four wool blankets dry like dish-rags yesterday morning and swung a six-pound splitting maul before breakfast—moved with slow, ridiculous care. She hooked one calloused forefinger under the lower rim of the dark suppression collar, easing the cold composite band a fraction of an inch away from the skin without triggering the lock, and slid her other thumb along the red chafe-line underneath to rub a warm smear of lanolin over the vertebrae.

Chisa tilted her chin down two degrees to give her room, her hands resting open on her knees.

Because Lupa was incapable of doing two quiet things at once, she was whispering logistics into the back of Chisa’s hair at a volume that could have reached the gatehouse.

“Fourth bell on the dot,” Lupa rasped, her thumb working a small circle behind Chisa’s right ear where the collar-hinge sat. “Not a quarter past. The Guild lady wakes up like a trapdoor spider—if she hears the iron lid come off the porridge pot, she’ll be out here in her slippers taking over the ladle before the water’s even hot. I told Wen to leave the millet soaking in the cold crock so it cooks in ten minutes.”

“The millet is on the second shelf behind the salt-jar,” Chisa said in her quiet, level voice, barely louder than the coals settling in the grate. “And you are whispering at sixty decibels.”

“I’m whispering fine,” Lupa grumbled, dipping her thumb back into the tin for another trace of salve. “And before I light the kindling, I’ve gotta unstrap the left side of her trunk by the pantry and wedge that smoked ham in between her petticoats. If I put it on top, she’ll spot it when she checks her inkstones at the relay, and then she’ll try to give it to the driver because she thinks she’s too fancy for bladder-wrapped pork.”

“That ham weighs four pounds,” Chisa said, turning her head half an inch to the left so Lupa could reach the other side of the collar. “I heard you drop it on the scullery scale after supper.”

“It’s two pounds. Compact.”

“The spring on Wen’s scale squeaks at three and a half. It squeaked.”

“You’re a snitch, you know that? Worse than the bard.” Lupa finished the left side of the neck, wiped the extra lanolin off her thumb onto her own knee, and clicked the tin lid shut with one hand. She didn’t move back to her own pallet right away; she stayed sitting back on her heels behind Chisa’s shoulder, looking toward the pale square of cotton blouse folded by Chisa’s knee.

“You two looked like a pair of plucked pigeons out on those steps this morning,” Lupa muttered, her voice dropping half a register so it actually resembled a whisper. “Short sleeves in the frost. Thought your teeth were gonna crack before you got through the door.”

Chisa reached down and touched the folded cuff of the summer blouse with two fingers, feeling the raised stitch of the two white stripes.

“It was warm enough once we were by the stove,” Chisa said. She paused for a beat, listening to the slow, steady rhythm of Lupa’s breathing behind her shoulder. “What were you doing when you were sixteen?”

Lupa snorted softly through her nose, shifting off her knees to sit cross-legged between the two pallets, her bare elbow bumping Chisa’s arm.

“Sixteen?” Lupa said to the red line of the stove. “At sixteen I was five-foot-ten, I lived in the third-tier stone barracks under the east gate of the Colosseum with twelve other girls who smelled like wet dog, and I was eating boiled horse-beans out of a tin bucket twice a day because the lanista said grease made rookies slow on the turn.”

She reached up absently and scratched behind her own scarred forearm.

“Second week of the autumn qualifiers,” Lupa went on, her mouth twisting into a dry grin in the dark, “some three-hundred-pound Myrmidon from the river pits grabbed a fistful of my hair on a clinch and nearly took my ear off against the fence. That night old One-Eye Marcus—he was the equipment smith, had three fingers on his left hand and smelled like vinegar—sat me down on an anvil-block in the forge and hacked the whole lot off with a pair of rusted sheep-shears so nobody could grab it again. Left me with spikes sticking out sideways like a chewed broom.”

“Like Amy’s,” Chisa said.

“Worse. Amy had a wool hat. I had to walk out under the trumpets the next Saturday in front of twenty thousand people looking like a mangy terrier that got stuck in a thresher.” Lupa leaned back on one hand, her eyes reflecting the dull orange coal-light. “Didn’t care, though. Won sixty coppers on the undercard, marched straight down to the armourer’s stall under the aqueduct, and bought a pair of secondhand bronze greaves that were two inches too short and pinched my calves raw every time I ran. Wore ’em to bed for a week so the other rookies couldn’t steal ’em. Nobody bought me anything stupid and blue, if that’s what you’re fishing for.”

Chisa didn’t say *that’s sad*, and she didn’t offer to pat her head.

Instead, Chisa picked up the pale blue silk tie from the top of her folded blouse.

She turned half-round on her pallet toward the sound of Lupa’s voice, reached out with both hands until her fingertips brushed the rough wool collar of Lupa’s sleeping tunic, and looped the narrow strip of blue silk around the back of Lupa’s neck.

Lupa went dead still. Her ears pinned flat back against her skull.

“...What’re you doing,” Lupa rasped.

“Hold your chin up,” Chisa said calmly. “You’re hunching.”

In the dark, without needing the standing mirror that was still propped in the turnip closet down the hall, Chisa’s fingers flipped the wide end of the blue silk over, under, and through the loop against Lupa’s collarbone—the exact four-in-hand knot she had tied for Amy before dawn—and slid the neat little triangle up until it rested squarely over the hollow of Lupa’s throat, right above the leather case where half a gold medal lived under her shirt.

Chisa rested two fingers lightly against the sides of the knot to check the symmetry, then let her hands drop back to her lap.

“Your neck is fifteen and a half inches,” Chisa said in her plain, even voice. “This one is cut for a fourteen-inch collar, so the tail is two inches too short. When we get to Mengzhou, we’ll buy the broader bolt of silk from the south wharf and I’ll hem one that fits you properly.”

In the orange gloom by the stove, Lupa stared down at the pale blue school tie hanging down the front of her sleeping tunic. Her neck had gone hot all the way from her jawbone to her shoulders, burning through the dark like a branding iron.

“I’m not wearing a schoolgirl tie in Mengzhou,” Lupa barked in a furious whisper. “I’m the Champion of the Seventh Games! People know my face! If I walk into a tavern wearing a blue ribbon round my windpipe like a prize heifer, Cia’s gonna put it in a song and I’ll have to throw her in the canal!”

“You don’t have to wear it in the tavern,” Chisa said, lying down flat on her pallet and pulling her wool blanket up to her shoulders. “You can wear it to bed, like the greaves.”

Lupa opened her mouth, found zero tactical counter-arguments available in any language she spoke, and flopped down onto her own pallet beside Chisa with a muffled *whump* that made the four hanging photographs sway on their string.

She reached up with one hand to yank the silk knot off her throat. Her fingers closed around the triangle, lingered there for three seconds where Chisa’s knuckles had smoothed the dimple, and then dropped back onto the blanket without untying it.

“Stupid,” Lupa muttered into her pillow. “Choking hazard. If I strangle myself before the fourth bell, you’re carrying that damn trunk down the stairs yourself.”

Beside her in the dark, Chisa didn’t answer. She simply slid her left hand out from under the edge of her blanket and laid her wrist flat across the two inches of floorboard between their mattresses, so the loose tail of Sumika’s red string brushed against the back of Lupa’s scarred knuckles.

Lupa didn’t move her hand away. She shifted her arm half an inch closer until her warm wrist was pressed solid against Chisa’s bone, and closed her eyes.

From the far corner behind the barley sacks, a faint, nasal *honk-shoo* drifted over the partition, followed by Ciaccona muttering something indistinct in Ragunnesi about elbows.

“Shut up back there,” Lupa growled softly at the room, with no heat in it at all. “Go to sleep.”

It was three minutes short of midnight when the wooden turbine on the storehouse sill outside sped up to a brisk *whir-click-whir* as the night wind came down off the high karsts, and the iron latch of the front door lifted without making a sound.

Yangyang stepped over the threshold so quietly the draft barely moved the hem of the curtain across the room.

She closed the timber door behind her, holding the iron ring with her thumb until the tongue settled into the catch, and stood on the mat for a second to let her eyes adjust to the faint red line of the firebox.

The northeast parapet was four hundred feet above the gorge floor, open on three sides to the wind coming off the snowfields. Frost had rimed the silver cloud-work at her cuffs and dusted the black-to-white layers of her skirt; her fingers around her scabbard were stiff to the second knuckle, and her left side had the dull, tight ache it always got after three hours of holding her breathing flat so she could listen under the roar of the water spires.

She hung her sword on the wooden peg beside the doorframe, took off her hat, and shook the ice crystals out of the brim onto the boot-scraper Chisa had bought for Lupa.

Then she crossed the floorboards in her stockinged feet to the hearth.

On the warm stone beside the ash-lip sat the heavy ceramic cover. Yangyang lifted it with both hands and set it on the flagstone.

Underneath, on the clean plate Iuno had put down five nights ago, sat two crooked steamed buns—the ones from the fifth-bell batch with her own thumbprints still dented into the pleats—and a deep earthenware cup of ginger-and-scallion broth wedged directly against the hot iron cheek of the stove so a curl of steam still rose off the grease on top.

From the pallet three feet away, a rough voice spoke out of a tangle of wool and a crooked blue silk tie:

“Eat both of ’em, bird.”

Yangyang didn’t startle. Lupa hadn’t opened her eyes, and her breathing hadn’t even changed rhythm.

“Thank you, Lupa,” Yangyang whispered, barely louder than the ash settling.

“Shut up and drink the soup before your teeth start clicking.”

Yangyang sat down on the warm hearth-brick with her back near the stove wall, tucked her skirt around her ankles, and drank the ginger broth first, holding the earthenware cup in both palms until the stiffness went out of her fingers and the tight band around her three healing ribs loosened by a notch. She ate the first bun slowly, and then—because Lupa’s left ear was still angled two degrees toward the stove in her sleep to count the chews—she ate the second one too, down to the last pleat.

She wiped her cup and plate with a damp cloth by the basin and set the ceramic cover back over the warm stone for the morning.

She didn’t go straight behind the curtain.

Instead, Yangyang stepped over to the corner of the long pine table, where a tiny clay night-light burned in a saucer of rapeseed oil beside Ciaccona’s forgotten teacup. She reached into her tunic pocket—careful not to brush the heavy, folded letters resting over her breastbone—and drew out her leather-bound patrol log and a short stub of carpenter’s pencil.

At the back of the log, past the pages of watch-rotations and the three items she had written down to say aloud to her shijie when the winter solstice came, she turned to a clean leaf.

Sitting on the edge of the bench with her head bent low over the wood so her blue-and-white hair fell forward across her cheeks, she wrote three short, level lines in the bottom corner of the page. She didn’t pause to think about the wording; she had worked it out on the parapet between the tenth bell and the eleventh while watching the frost form on the crenels.

When she finished, she laid the flat of her thumb along the margin, tore the corner off in a clean, straight square without a ragged fibre, and folded it four times—edge to edge, crease pressed flat with her fingernail—until it was a neat one-inch packet, the exact size and shape of the notes she used to leave under the blue-and-white porcelain flour jar in the Mingting kitchen when she was eight years old and going out to the courtyard post before dawn.

She put the log back in her pocket.

Crossing silently to the pantry door, where Lupa’s monstrous two-gallon willow hamper sat lashed and waiting, Yangyang didn’t undo the padlock; she simply slipped her narrow hand through the gap under the wicker lid on the hinge side, past the waxed cloth of smoked ribs and the flat-topped chili jar, until her fingertips found the cold brass rim of Suisui’s small osmanthus-cake tin at the bottom of the basket.

She pried the corner of the tin lid up half an inch with her thumb, slid the two spare walnut cakes she’d raided from Wen’s pantry before watch into the tin, tucked the folded one-inch square of paper neatly under the brass rim where Suisui would see it first thing, and pressed the lid shut without a click.

Then she stepped across the room and slipped behind the heavy wool curtain into the side alcove.

***

The alcove was six feet wide, smelled faintly of Suisui’s camellia hair-oil and cedarwood, and had no lamp at all—only a thin grey bar of starlight coming through the high crack in the outer shutter.

Suisui was lying on the narrow pallet against the wall.

For the first time since she had walked into Xuanfang Hold, her hair wasn’t pinned up in a single coil or loop; it was combed out loose across the pillow like a bolt of black silk. Her fur-lined travel cloak hung on the peg by the curtain, and her outer robe lay folded across a stool, except she had kept her folded inner silk sleeve tucked right beside the edge of her pillow. Inside the cloth, resting four inches from her right hand, sat the cool, dense square of Sentinel Hsin’s Prism.

Suisui’s eyes were open in the dark. As the curtain dropped back into place behind Yangyang, Suisui’s fingers brushed the silk-wrapped square once—just a light, automatic check—before she drew her hand back under the quilt and shifted her hips six inches toward the timber wall without a word, clearing the outer half of the mattress.

Yangyang unhooked her outer cloak, hung it over Suisui’s on the peg, and lifted the corner of the quilt.

When they were seven and ten in the south wing of the Mingting house, before the sword-rack and before the provincial abacus exams, there had been a strict winter protocol whenever the brazier in the nursery went out before midnight.

Yangyang slid under the covers on her side, kept her face solemn in the dark, and planted both of her freezing, parapet-chilled bare feet squarely against the warm backs of Suisui’s calves.

Suisui’s whole body jerked three inches into the air. She clapped a hand over her own mouth a fraction of a second too late to stop a sharp, high *“Yip!”* from escaping into the pillow.

“Yangyang!” Suisui hissed, trying to kick backward under the heavy wool and finding her ankles pinned by two blocks of solid mountain ice. “What is wrong with you?! Have you been standing barefoot in the water-butt?!”

“The northeast parapet has a stone floor,” Yangyang said to the back of Suisui’s shoulder, her voice completely flat and unrepentant as she kept her toes wedged firmly behind Suisui’s knees. “And my boots have thin soles for the roof-tiles.”

“Then put your feet on the stove brick before you come to bed! You have been doing that since you had your baby teeth!” Suisui twisted around under the quilt, glaring at the dark shape of her little sister’s head two inches away. “And I thought you were not speaking to me! At three o’clock you turned your back to me at the table, ate my walnut cake, and told the stove bricks to have a safe trip!”

Yangyang didn’t move her feet a millimeter. She lay facing the wall-curtain with her hands tucked neatly under her chin.

“That was during business hours,” Yangyang said. “It’s past midnight. The dark doesn’t count.”

Suisui stared at the back of Yangyang’s neck—at the small cerulean feathers of her collar resting soft and slightly rumpled against the white pillowcase—and let out a helpless, quiet laugh through her nose, giving up on escaping the ice-blocks and pulling the quilt down tightly behind Yangyang’s shoulders so the draft couldn’t get in.

“‘The dark doesn’t count,’” Suisui repeated softly, settling back onto her side so they were lying face-to-face on the narrow pillow with four inches of air between their noses, the matching blue Resonator marks on their foreheads faintly warm in the gloom. “Where did you learn to be such a lawyer? Certainly not from me.”

“From Iuno,” Yangyang said. “She puts terms on everything. It seems very efficient.”

“Do not take legal advice from a retired priestess who lets Mei keep a wooden frog in the bedroom.” Suisui reached out under the blanket, found Yangyang’s cold right hand, and tucked it between both of her own warm palms against her collarbone, rubbing the stiff knuckles until the blood came back into the fingertips. “Amy hung those four photographs by the chimney to dry. I saw them when I went for water at the ninth bell.”

Even in the dark, Suisui could feel the sudden wave of heat that went straight up Yangyang’s neck to the tips of her ears.

“Mei was shining a wash-basin in my eyes,” Yangyang muttered defensively. “And Ciaccona was flapping her cloak at me.”

“You look like an owl that has just been informed its taxes are overdue,” Suisui said, her voice full of wicked sisterly delight. “At least in the first three. Mother is going to drop her teacup when I take them out of the satchel.”

“I told you not to let her fret.”

“She will fret for exactly eight minutes,” Suisui said, her thumb moving over Yangyang’s fingers. “She will ask if you are molting on the furniture, she will ask whether feathers require camphor in the rainy season, and she will blame Father’s side of the family because Great-Uncle Heng once kept racing pigeons. And then at the second bell she will put Exhibit A in her embroidered sleeve, walk straight across the street to Madam Lin’s mahjong table, and lay it down next to the bamboo tiles without saying a word until somebody asks if her younger daughter has become an immortal.”

Yangyang’s mouth twitched against the pillow. “And Ba?”

“Father will put his spectacles on, hold it at arm’s length for three minutes, say *‘Hmph, her posture is adequate,’* and then steal it out of Mother’s room after supper to prop against the ink-stand on his study desk.”

Yangyang let out a small, quiet breath that warmed the four inches of air between them. Her toes, finally thawed out against Suisui’s shins, uncurled.

For a little while neither of them spoke. Through the timber partition behind the curtain, they could hear the faint, rhythmic creak of the storehouse settling in the frost, and the distant, unending hum of the great water spires turning at the edge of the cliff.

Suisui’s hands stayed wrapped around Yangyang’s fingers, her thumb drifting up an inch to touch the edge of Yangyang’s sheer, cloud-worked wrist cuff.

“This morning in the woodyard,” Suisui said quietly, keeping her voice on the same level, unhurried note she’d used for their father’s ink-stand. “When I was sitting on the firewood with Ciaccona. You knocked that piece of elm out of the air with the willow stick.”

Yangyang’s eyes blinked once in the dark. “It was going to land in Cartethyia’s watering-can.”

“Your right elbow was three inches out from your ribs when you turned,” Suisui said. “When you were nine, Shijie used to walk behind you in the courtyard with a split bamboo ruler and rap your arm every time you let the elbow float on a backswing. I used to hear it from the veranda while I was doing the cloth tallies. *Clack, clack, clack.*”

“Shijie was very fond of the ruler,” Yangyang murmured.

“Does it pull?” Suisui asked—not looking at the bound ribs under the sash, just looking at her sister’s eyes in the thin line of starlight. “When you let it go out like that? Or does it feel like someone else’s arm?”

Yangyang was quiet for three breaths, thinking about it the way she always thought about a question before she spent words on it.

“No,” Yangyang said plainly. “When I was small in the courtyard, the sword always felt like something heavy I had borrowed off the rack and had to carry around without dropping until supper. Even in Jinzhou, sometimes.” She flexed her fingers once inside Suisui’s warm hands. “This morning it felt like the bone in my shoulder went all the way to the point. I didn’t have to hold on so hard to keep it on the line.”

Suisui looked at her for a long moment. She didn’t ask what Startorch would say about it, and she didn’t ask what would happen when the winter solstice came and the sky turned dark again.

“Good,” Suisui whispered, borrowing Chisa’s word for once because nothing larger fit in the space. “Shijie always held her shoulders like a crab anyway. Don’t tell her I said so.”

Outside the shutter crack, a deeper gust came off the gorge, carrying the high, cold ring of the bronze calipers where Sentinel Hsin’s Astrolabe Core turned alone above the clouds.

Beside Suisui’s pillow, her right elbow brushed the folded silk sleeve where the Prism lay.

In the grey stripe of light across the pillow, Yangyang saw the small muscle in Suisui’s jaw tighten once. She saw Suisui’s mouth open by a sixteenth of an inch—saw the exact shape of the sentence *There are four seats in the coach tomorrow, come to Mengzhou with me now and let the Ministry send someone else* rise all the way up to the back of Suisui’s teeth—and she watched Suisui shut her mouth on it and swallow it back down into her chest without making a sound.

Yangyang didn’t wait for her to struggle with it.

She pulled her hand gently out of Suisui’s grip, reached across the four inches between them, and hooked her forefinger into the silk fold of Suisui’s collar, giving it a single, tiny tug.

“Liangyu fixed the watch roster at the east gate before I came down,” Yangyang said in her plain, Mingting kitchen voice. “We have two Wardens on every turn now instead of one. Lupa won’t let Mei or me walk down the road to the market without Chisa or Cartethyia in sight, and when the gorge road is signed off, we’ll come up together. Not before. And if the fog moves an inch before then, I promise I’ll take the stairs, not the roof.”

Suisui’s eyes were very bright in the dark. She didn’t nod right away, because if she nodded too fast her chin was going to wobble, and she was the elder sister and the Director of the Zhaoming Commerce Guild.

“And you won’t forget to eat lunch because you’re reading labels in the armoury,” Suisui whispered fiercely.

“Mei is on the second line of the ledger,” Yangyang reminded her softly. “If I’m ten minutes late to the stove, Lupa threatens to put me in the soup, and Iuno looks at me over her teacup until I sit down.”

“They are very pushy people,” Suisui said, her voice thick. “I approve of them entirely.”

“And on the twenty-eighth of the twelfth month,” Yangyang added, her voice going up just a fraction at the end, “I’ll be at the Mingting gate before the water boils for the dumplings. Even if I have to fly the last ten miles.”

“If you fly over the neighbour’s wall, Mother will charge admission,” Suisui murmured. Then she narrowed her eyes across the pillow, poking Yangyang’s nose lightly with one finger. “And you still took the middle osmanthus cake at lunch. The one with the walnuts on the bottom. Don’t think I’ve forgotten that just because your feet are warm now.”

“You told me you were leaving on the five-bell coach with the door already open,” Yangyang said placidly.

“I had just hung up on Little Zheng! I found out on the step!”

“Then we’re even. You can have the big one at New Year.”

Suisui let out a long, tired sigh that seemed to come all the way from her heels, her shoulders dropping against the mattress.

For five days she had been sleeping on a storehouse pallet after walking into a mountain fog in city shoes, playing weiqi for her life, fighting the Ministry of War by correspondence, auditing stolen roofing slate, and holding sixteen thousand people’s winter barley in her head while pretending she wasn’t terrified every time her little sister walked out the door with feathers on her neck. Because she had shifted all the way over to the timber wall to give Yangyang the warm middle of the bed, the draft coming through the shutter-frame was hitting the back of her neck, and she was unconsciously hunching her left shoulder up toward her ear to keep the chill off the tight knot beside her spine.

Yangyang looked at the hunched shoulder.

She didn’t ask permission. She pushed herself up on one elbow, dragged the heavy edge of the wool quilt across both of them, and tucked it firmly down behind Suisui’s back against the timber wall—the exact, methodical way Suisui had tucked her in every winter night of her childhood. Then Yangyang slid her warm right hand under the curtain of Suisui’s loose black hair and pressed the heel of her palm squarely into the knotted muscle between Suisui’s shoulder blade and her spine, working it in a slow, steady circle.

Suisui went completely rigid for two seconds.

Nobody rubbed Suisui’s back. Suisui was the one who brought the ginger tea, who noticed the stiff neck, who put the hot stone in the foot-sack.

“Yangyang,” Suisui whispered, bewildered and slightly scandalized in the dark. “What are you doing? Your ribs—”

“My ribs are on the left. This is my right hand. Stop talking, Jie, your shoulder feels like Lupa’s stump.”

“It does not feel like a stump, I have excellent posture—”

Yangyang pressed her thumb into the top of the knot, right below the nape of the neck, and smoothed it down toward the shoulder blade.

The protest died right out of Suisui’s throat, turning into a tiny, boneless sigh as her chin tipped forward onto the pillow.

“...Oh,” Suisui murmured into the linen, her eyes fluttering shut before she could stop them. “That’s—when did you learn to do that?”

“Ciaccona showed me how to un-cramp a bow-arm on Wednesday,” Yangyang said quietly, keeping the circles slow and even in the dark while the cerulean feathers at her collar brushed softly against her own cheek. “And you’ve been carrying your fan like a club since Little Zheng called. Let your elbow drop.”

“I do not carry my fan like a club,” Suisui mumbled, though her left arm went completely slack under the blanket, her breath lengthening out between one syllable and the next as the five days of mountain cold finally let go of her spine. “And tomorrow morning... do not let Lupa wake everyone up... just a cup of hot water at the gate...”

“Of course,” Yangyang lied gently, without a single tremor in her voice. “Just a cup of hot water. Go to sleep, Jie.”

Suisui didn’t answer.

Halfway through a breath to tell Yangyang to pull the blanket over her own ear, the Director of the Zhaoming Commerce Guild fell dead asleep with her nose squashed against her little sister’s collarbone.

Yangyang stayed awake for another minute in the quiet alcove, her hand resting still and warm between Suisui’s shoulder blades while Dumpling gave a tiny, sleepy click from the rafter overhead, before she tucked her chin against the top of Suisui’s loose hair and closed her eyes.

***

Twenty minutes later, in the pitch-black back bedroom, Mei rolled off her right hip onto her back to give her shoulder a rest, felt Iuno’s warm ankle hooked over her left shin and Amy’s pink head wedged securely into her armpit, and was struck by such a sudden, overflowing wave of domestic contentment that she completely forgot the storehouse walls were made of half-inch mountain pine.

“Goodnight, everybody!” Mei whispered happily at the ceiling rafters.

It carried through every crack in the mortar like a brass gong.

Right beside her in the dark, Iuno didn’t even open her eyes. “It is half past midnight, Mei.”

In the middle of the bed, Amy burrowed her face furiously under the folded summer blouse with a groan. “Ma, shut up, your ribcage is vibrating like a subwoofer.”

Across the main room, behind the stacked barley sacks in the far alcove, Ciaccona’s voice drifted up out of the wool, hoarse and deeply aggrieved: “Some of us have a four-bell call time for an unauthorized street opera, thank you very much.”

Beside her, Cartethyia—still flat on her back like a fallen starfish with one foot hanging off the mattress—spoke clearly and earnestly into her sleep: “The goose... has the right of way...”

Down by the red seam of the stove, Lupa’s rumble came out of the dark like gravel rolling down a chute: “Next person who makes a noise before the fourth bell gets strapped into the trunk with the ham. And that includes the bird.”

From the pallet six inches to Lupa’s left, level and entirely awake, came Chisa’s voice: “Goodnight, Mei.”

And from behind the heavy wool curtain of the side room, where Suisui was already snoring softly into her sister’s collar, Yangyang’s quiet voice answered last, barely louder than the draft:

“Night, Mei.”

In the back room, Mei grinned to her ears in the dark, clamped both hands over her mouth so she wouldn’t say *Night!* back and get packed with the pork, and settled down into the pillows.

After that, the storehouse actually went quiet.

By the first bell, nothing moved in the back bedroom except Amy’s foot occasionally twitching in a dream about geometry quizzes. Behind the barley sacks, Ciaccona’s heel stayed tucked warm in Cartethyia’s ribs. Behind the side curtain, the two sisters breathed in the exact same slow four-count they had kept in the south wing in Mingting when the rain was on the roof.

At half past the first bell, Lupa rolled off her pallet by the stove without making a board creak—the pale blue school tie still sitting in a slightly crooked four-in-hand knot against her collarbone.

She padded barefoot across the warm flags to the chimney string first, touching the bottom corner of the fourth photograph with one dry knuckle to make sure the paper was drying flat and not curling into the soot.

Then she stepped over to the pantry shadow, unbuckled the left leather cinch-strap of Suisui’s cedar trunk with her teeth so the brass buckle wouldn’t clink, lifted the lid three inches, and wedged the double-wrapped two-pound smoked ham and a greased iron axle-pin snugly down between three pairs of folded wool drawers and the silk petticoats so the ledgers wouldn’t slide an inch on the mountain ruts. She lowered the lid, left the final strap ready to haul tight at the quarter-bell, and walked back to the hearth.

From the covered steamer crock at the back of the hob, she took one fresh, crooked bun, lifted the ceramic cover on the warm hearth-stone, and set it on the clean plate over the spot where Yangyang’s cup had been, so nobody waking up in the dark would find the stone empty.

She banked the red coals with half a shovelful of grey ash and set the iron poker down without a sound. Then, standing over the pallets in the dark, she worked the crooked blue knot loose from her own throat, smoothed the silk flat between two fingers, and laid it back on top of Chisa’s folded blouse for the morning, before she lay back down on her pallet—sliding her left arm out across the narrow gap until the back of her hand rested against Chisa’s wrist and the tail of the red string.

Outside in the freezing dark, four hundred feet below the silent, turning jade orb of the Astrolabe Core, the four-foot butt of seasoned iron-elm sat waiting in the frost with its rusted wedge and its cathedral notch; the sixteen-foot cedar ladder leaned ready against the grey timber wall; the little wooden turbine on the sill turned with its slow, steady *whuff-creak* against the wind off the gorge; and inside behind the latched door, the whole house slept together for the three hours they had left before the lamps went back on.

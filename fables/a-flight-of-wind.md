### being the return of the courier, entered at the youngest gate on the winter solstice
*A "Lyre, Speak to Me" side story.*

摶扶搖而上者九萬里。
*It beats the whirlwind and rises ninety thousand li.*
— Zhuangzi, "Free and Easy Wandering"

*Solstice. Wind rises. Inside the deepest cold, the first warmth is born.*

## 冬至 — Solstice

The kitchen at Mingting was warm at the grey hour because Suisui had been up since the fourth watch, which was a thing she did on the solstice and had done for years with two bowls on the table and one of them cooling.

This year the second bowl had a woman in front of it. The dish on the floor had a bird.

The letters lay on the board in the space where the second batch went, fifteen of them, folded small, in a stack that leaned. Yangyang had spent the night before on the last one and her right hand had a callus coming along the second finger where the brush sat, which Suisui had looked at over breakfast and said nothing about, in the fluent way, and then rubbed with the side of her thumb.

"She can't carry paper," Yangyang said.

She had the report voice now. It had come back with the rest of her over two months, a word at a time, and it was the last thing to arrive and the first thing she reached for when a sentence had a hard part in it.

"The dark keeps what is made of sound." She put her hand flat on the stack. "Paper is made of paper."

Suisui put the pot down.

"Then read them to her."

Yangyang looked at the bird on the floor.

The bird had finished her dish and was standing beside it with her head on one side, in the posture of somebody at a counter who has settled for the price. Two months of Suisui's kitchen had done what it does; there was a gloss on her that she had lacked on the road. The gap in the left wing was the same gap. A place a blade has been stays the shape of the blade. She had learned to fly around it.

"All of them," Yangyang said.

"All of them. She has to carry the reading. So read." Suisui pulled out the bench opposite and sat on it with her arms folded, the way the woman at the relay station had stood to watch a bowl be eaten. "I'll be here. I'm told I make a good audience for things I've already heard the ending of."

So Yangyang read her letters aloud.

She read them at the kitchen table in Mingting at the grey hour of the solstice with the lamp still lit, in the order she had written them, to a bird on the floor, and the bird came up onto the bench beside her after the first one and stood on the table after the third, and by the sixth she had her feet on the edge of the board with the letters under them, and her head down, listening to a woman's voice do the thing a woman's voice does when it has a hard part in it and goes through anyway.

The cook's. The keeper's. The window that is usually shut. The terrace, and the hawthorn. The kite, and the paper people, and the lute, and the string that goes up, and the stairhead. Yangyang read the one to the harbour ward in the flattest voice she had and it took her three stops to get through the part about the scissors. She read the one to the ledger and Suisui, at the stove, said "warm, unquantified" under her breath and laughed once, wetly, and turned round to the pot again.

She read the one to the scarf. She read it to the bird and she read it to her sister's back, and her sister's back stayed where it was, and at *she is you* the ladle stopped in the pot and stayed stopped for the length of a paragraph.

She read the one to the hook by the door. She read the one to the bed with four feet. At the blank in each she stopped, with her mouth open on the width of the word, and then went on past it, and the bird, both times, put her head down a little further.

Then there was one left.

Yangyang picked it up. It was going soft already; she had read it to herself eleven times since the great snow.

"This one you can read yourself," she said. "When you have hands."

The bird looked at her.

She looked at the bird for a while, and then she unfolded it, and read it aloud anyway, from *Shimei* to *with hands*, at the kitchen table, in the report voice, and at the elbow the report voice ran out the way it does, and she let it, and read the rest in the voice underneath, the one for letters she had written at a window and had kept in a drawer against the day she was sure.

At *the border keeps you on your own shelf* the bird made a sound. It was a small one. A bird has a small chest.

At *come ■ when it's cold* Yangyang stopped on the blank, and the bird said the word for her, once, quietly, from the table, into the gap, and Yangyang closed her eyes and went on.

At the end she folded it again along the soft folds and put it on the top of the stack, and put her hand over the whole of it, and left the hand there.

Suisui came and stood behind her and put both her own hands on her shoulders.

"Well," Suisui said. "She's heard them."

⁂

The boot-lace was one of Suisui's. She had produced it from the drawer with the tea in it and declined to say what it had been doing there.

Yangyang tied the bundle to the bird's breast herself. It took four attempts. Her hands had learned the brush in two months and had a long way to go with knots, and the bird stood on the table with her wings held a finger's width out from her sides and held still while a woman's shaking fingers went round her three times and failed to close.

"Hold still," Yangyang said, on the fourth attempt.

"I am holding still."

"Hold stiller."

The knot closed. It was a bad knot. It was tied on a bird, by somebody who had been a wind, with a lace out of a drawer of tea, and it would hold across a dark because everything in it had been read aloud first.

Suisui put a last piece of steamed bread in front of the bird, the browned side, and watched it go.

"You'll want feeding when you get there," she said. "Tell whoever's warming your bowl that once is plenty. Twice makes it tough."

The bird looked up at her with the bread gone and her head on one side.

"I'll tell her."

"Mm." Suisui reached down with her clean hand and put one finger under the bird's chin, the way she had on the first evening, and lifted it, and looked into the eye. "Dumpling. Come back fat."

"That is up for discussion."

"It is not."

⁂

The gate was on the latch.

Yangyang opened it. She stood in it and looked down the lane the way her sister had looked down it every evening for years, east, toward the wall and the scars and the river without a bridge, and found that she had picked the habit up, and could say the week. Two months. Two women at one gate, both of them checking a distance.

The bird came out past her ankle onto the lane.

"Every winter," Yangyang said.

"I know the way now."

"You had a wind in your wing last time."

"I have a knot on my chest this time. It'll do."

The bird hopped once, and turned, and went up.

She went up past the eaves, and past the ridge tile where she had stood at dusk for two months checking the east, and she came round once over the courtyard the way she had come round once over a fortress in the rain, and on the way past she dipped, low, and took a strand of the woman's loose hair in her beak and tugged it. Once. With intent. The cadence of a sister's hand.

Yangyang stood in her own gateway with her hair pulled and her face turned up.

"Manners," she said, to the open air.

Then the bird was a mark on the sky over the north wall, and then a smaller mark, and then the sky was a sky again, with a solstice wind coming up over the courtyard from the north, the way it does on that day, the year's first warmth inside the year's deepest cold, and Suisui came out and put her arm around her sister at the gate, and they stood in it.

"She'll be back when it's cold," Suisui said.

"It's cold now."

"The next cold, then. Come inside. I've got the water on."

## 風起 — Wind Rises

What lies between stories is a dark that reads you.

It had read her twice. Once as a bird carrying a wind in a torn wing, singing both halves of a song with two breaths in one throat, and once, before that, in a gorge, as a woman on her knees with her outline going soft, though she had been too far open to know she was being read. It read her now as she was, which was a bird with a gap in her wing and a bad knot on her chest, alone, at the height where the air stops helping.

She sang.

The first call. *The Xuanling flies.* Her chest ran out three words in, the way it had before, and she went into the rest between the line and the next line, and the rest was empty.

Last time the Wind had poured.

She held the rest.

An Outrider counts. She counted the silence to its written value, one, two, three, four, with her wings out and her whole weight leaned on empty air, and at four she came in on the beat with the answer, alone, from a chest that had just run out, and the answer came out thin as a thread and it was a note, and a note stays exactly where it is sung.

*Over the wide water.*

Rest. Count. Answer.

*The mountains so grey.*

The toll came off her the way it had before, feathers, one at a time, off the right wing and then off the left, around the gap, and then, because those were gone, from the breast, from under the knot, the down, the soft underlayer a bird wears against cold, going off her in the dark in silence, each one felt, each one counted, because she was an Outrider and the count was all she had.

The dark leaned in and read the rests.

It filled the first one with the cliff. Her own outline going soft, her fingers in the rock, the crying of a mountain coming into her through every open door, and the ease of it, the terrible ease, the thought that came gently: *you could open now. The wing is empty. Hold still for whom? Open, and pour, and reach it all.*

She had heard that before too. She had heard it on a slope with a hole in her side and she had answered it with her mother's lullaby, badly, and then with a marching song, and then she had stood up and opened her arms and said *you carry me*, and that had been the answer: somebody had to be the part that holds still, and it had been the Wind, and she had watched her sister do it for a whole dark and had learned how it was done.

She held still.

She held the rest to its value and came in on the beat and sang the next call into the empty and answered it herself, and the down went off her breast in the silence between, and she let it, because the knot stayed, and the knot had fifteen readings in it in her sister's voice, and the dark could take feathers off a bird all night and the things read aloud at a kitchen table with the lamp still lit stayed exactly where they had been read.

*Tell Father, tell Mother, don't stand watching the road for me.*

She sang it and heard it, this time, from the outside. She had sung it her whole life as the soldier. She had sung it once in a gorge and understood that the bird does the answering. She sang it now as a bird, alone, in a dark that reads you, with the down coming off her, and understood the third thing, which is that somebody always stands watching the road anyway, and that the song is a lie the going tell the staying so that the staying will let them go.

Two women at one gate, checking a distance.

She kept singing.

⁂

The shelves went past.

The scorched one, with the pages that stopped mid-line, was behind her now and had a woman on it with a callus coming on her second finger, and it went past like a house going past a window, and she let it. Twelve others. Thirteen. Fourteen houses on fourteen lanes, each with a post.

On one a light had been left on.

She had gone past it once with the Wind in her wing, because it had pulled at her like a hand at a hat brim and she had had somewhere else to be. She came toward it now the way a needle comes round when you finally stop walking.

The light was several lights. She could hear them, which is how a bird finds a shelf.

A woman at a roof rail above an east market, at the hour the lamp-grid goes gold from the north side, setting down a second cup on the tile for the dark, local leaf, and saying to the dark, every night, at the same hour, since a moon over Xuanfang, *same time tomorrow?*, and turning the cup a quarter-turn, and going down the ladder.

A woman in a red cap with a new lining sewn in badly, saying hello to the half-inch gap of a window that would not latch, eight degrees to the left of where the gap was, every morning, on her way past, for two months, to the gap.

A one-armed woman with her boots by the door.

A woman in a cook's apron at a stove in a rented kitchen, with a bowl on the shelf above it, covered, that she took down every evening and looked at and put back, and had warmed, so far, zero times.

*Every wind is met once.* Every bird is called by more than one voice. She had known that in a gorge with a stone in a dead girl's hand. She had it now from the other side of the dark, with the down gone off her and the knot holding and four voices on one shelf calling a thing home in four different keys, and she leaned her whole weight on the loudest one and let it take her.

The page turned.

## 一陽生 — The First Warmth Is Born

The keeper of the ninth tower had left the rope off the cleat since the twenty-eighth of the seventh month.

*Reason: they may come again.* She had written it in the log and had been asked about it twice by the Bureau and once by the Magistrate's clerk and had given the same reason three times and it had stood. The rope hung free. On the solstice at the grey hour there was snow along the crest wall and her breath stood in front of her and she was trimming the north lamp with the glass off when the wind came up over the north face, out of the mountains where the tremors had gone to fourteen days and then to nothing, the solstice wind, the year's first warmth inside its deepest cold, exactly on time.

Something was in it.

It came over the parapet low and listing, holding one wing a little out from its side, blue-black in the places where it was anything at all and pale underneath in a way that made her think first of a plucked thing and then of snow. It had a bundle on its breast and a lace round the bundle. It came along the wall at the height of a woman's shoulder, at a magpie's pace, fly and fall and fly, toward the northeast shoulder where the beacon's light reaches last, and it went past her lamp close enough that she saw it had an eye, and the eye looked at her.

She put the glass down.

She took the rope.

She had rung eight beats once, for a column of nineteen hundred, on the morning the seal came off. She had been told by the Magistrate's own standing instruction that any column counted returning was to be counted whole, however constituted, and she had asked the clerk what *however constituted* meant and he had said that it meant however constituted.

She rang eight.

⁂

Eight beats went along the wall at the grey hour on the solstice and the city did what it had done twice before, which was decide, unmet.

At Panhua's the oil was already on. At the tax office the goose stood up. At the east gate Shen touched the lid of her tin. On Bookbinders' Row a compositor with a forme half-locked looked up at the ceiling and counted, and got to eight, and waited for a ninth out of habit, and went back to work. In ward four, Clerk Wen opened the register to the heading he had written in the seventh month and had left, since, with one thousand nine hundred and seven names under it and a rule across the foot, and took a pencil, and waited.

And at Mrs Ren's, in the front room with the window that would not latch, a woman with a crystal shoulder sat up in bed and said, in the voice she keeps for numbers, flat, no run-up:

"It stopped."

Mei was already up. Mei had been up since the first beat.

"Your arm."

"Four hours ago. I thought it was the solstice." Iuno had her living hand over the crystal. "It stops when the weather stops. The weather has stopped."

On the stairhead, Lupa's boots hit the floor. Through the wall a girl with a headset said *eight, eight, that was eight* to a channel that was still empty. Upstairs a bard's hand found a lute in the dark and a knight's found a coat and a woman who cannot see faces put two fingers on the sill of her window and said, to the room, clearly:

"One set of wingbeats. North face to the shoulder. She's alone."

Chixia was at the door with her cap on before anybody else had a coat.

⁂

The northeast shoulder of the crest wall is where the beacon's light reaches last, and there is a girl who stands there at first light, the fishermen will tell you, checking a distance for somebody particular.

The bird landed on the parapet.

She landed badly. Landing was still the part she skipped. She came down on the snow along the top of the wall and the wing with the gap in it folded under and the bundle on her breast hit the stone and she lay on her side on the parapet of her own city with her feet drawn up and her eye open and the solstice wind going over her, and she was very small. There was very little of her. The crossing had taken the down and most of the rest and left the flight feathers, the long ones, the ones with the sheen, and the sheen was electric in the grey light, blue going to white at the tips, and under them she was a bird the size of a fist with a lace around her.

The household came up the beacon stair at a run and stopped.

Mei went the last of it alone.

She went the way she goes to things, too fast, and then she stopped, four paces off, because she has learned the difference between catching somebody and standing where they can reach you, and it has cost her every time, and she stood in it. She went down on one knee in the snow on the wall. She put her right hand out, open, palm up, on the stone, at the height of a bird lying on its side, and she left it there.

"Slowly," Mei said. "The stones roll."

The bird's eye moved.

It took her a while. She got her feet under her on the parapet, and the wing with the gap dragged, and the knot on her chest dragged, and she walked, the walking part of a magpie's progress, along the snow on top of the wall, four paces, at the pace of a thing that has been singing both halves of a song alone across a dark and has run out, and she walked onto the open hand, and it took her weight, which was a fist's worth, and stayed open, and held.

"Hi," Mei said. Her voice did something and she let it. "You're right on time."

Then Amy, from the stair, in the broadcasting register, because somebody had to and it was her job:

"Number coming back."

The bird lifted her head off Mei's palm. A bird has a small chest, and she had spent the whole of it on a song, and she had one word left in the purse and she had been keeping it since a gate in another printing for exactly this.

"…One."

Mei's hand shook, under her, once.

"Come back different," Amy said.

The bird looked at the hand she was standing on, and at the woman it belonged to, and at the wall behind her with eight people on it and a keeper at the far end with her hand still on a rope.

"…Did."

⁂

She came apart on the wall.

She had done it once in a gorge, the other way, with everything pouring in and folding: a wingspan, a tail, a chest that could sing. Her body knew the crease. It knew that a crease can be run a third time, and it ran it, on the parapet, on an open hand, in the solstice wind, with a fist's worth left to fold, and what it had left it folded, and what came out of the fold was a woman.

She sat down hard on the snow on the wall with her legs out in front of her, because the fold left her sitting, and she was so tired that the sitting was the whole of what she could do.

The household stood on the wall and looked at her.

She was Yangyang. That was the first thing and it was the whole thing: the face, the line of the jaw, the mouth settling where it settles when the feeling has been filed for later. She had two hands and she looked at them the way you look at hands you have been without for four months and then she put them flat on the snow beside her to feel it, and it was cold, and she made a small sound about the cold that was entirely her own.

The rest was what the crossing had left of the bird, and she was wearing it.

Sleeves. Sheer, and wide, and falling from the shoulder in a gradient the colour of a lake at fish-belly white, with silver clouds worked into them so faintly that they showed only where the light moved. A collar of feathers, small ones, soft, cerulean, framing her throat where a scarf had once held a cold. A short skirt, layered, that fell like sharp plumage and went from ink-black at the waist to white at the tips, the exact gradient of a flight feather with the sheen on it, and behind her, trailing on the snow, ribbons of translucent cyan, two of them, long, that lay along the wall the way a tail lies when a bird has landed and folded.

Her hair had come loose in the fold and lay across the collar. It was black at the crown, her own black, and it went to blue as it fell, electric, the blue of one silk thread robbed off a clothesline in a better neighbourhood, and at the ends it went to white, and the white was feathers. Small ones. Soft. The last of the down.

And in the middle of her forehead, where a hat brim used to sit, small and clean and unmistakable to every Resonator on that wall, there was a mark, and the mark was blue.

The wall held its breath.

Then Cartethyia's two fingers went to her own forehead, and came away, and she looked at Ciaccona, and Ciaccona said, very quietly, "Thursday," and Cartethyia nodded.

Then Baizhi came forward with the thermometer already out of her coat.

Then Chixia, who had been standing eight degrees to the left of where she needed to be for four months, took her red cap off her own head and walked the four paces and put it on Yangyang's, over the blue hair and the white feathers and the mark, and pulled the brim down, hard, the way you pull a brim down on somebody who has been out in the weather too long, and said, at full volume, to the whole wall:

"There. Now I can see you."

Yangyang looked up at her from under the brim of a cap that had been floating in a Sphere four months ago and had a new lining in it, sewn in badly.

"…Your stitching," she said. It was the second thing she had said with a mouth. "It's crooked."

"I used the blunt scissors. On instruction."

Yangyang's face performed the family craft, and the craft failed, and she wiped her eyes with the back of a hand in the practical way, once each side, and the sleeve went with the hand and the silver clouds in it caught the first of the sun coming over the eastern rank.

⁂

She wanted to walk down.

Lupa had come to the parapet with her arms already out and the intent already in her shoulders, and Yangyang had looked at her, and said "Let me walk," and Lupa had stood there for four full seconds with her tail entirely still, and then taken her arms back, and come round to Yangyang's right side, and stayed there, at the same speed, the whole of the beacon stair, the way she had once walked a woman who cannot see faces through two lanes and a bridge on a day when all the furniture had moved.

Yangyang walked down the beacon stair on her own feet, in the wrong shoes, which were Suisui's, and had crossed a dark tied to a bird's foot, and were still the wrong size.

At the foot of the stair the fishermen made room.

At the arch, Iuno stood with her coat on and her living hand out, and Yangyang undid the bad knot with fingers that had learned knots on a bird and put the bundle into the hand, and Iuno looked down at the top letter, which was addressed to a corner table and a bowl, and at the one beneath it, and turned the bundle over and found her own, near the bottom, addressed to a hook by a door, and read the address twice, and put the bundle inside her coat, every seal intact, against the shirt pocket, where a ring lived, and a paper.

"Your aunt says hello," Yangyang told her. "In the form of a great many words."

"I gathered. I felt them arrive." Iuno's hand was flat over the coat. "She has been out of them for so long that I had forgotten what it is like to be corresponded with by her. I expect it will be worse."

"It is."

At Mrs Ren's, on the step, Suisui had the bowl.

She had brought it down off the shelf where it had lived for four months, covered, and she had warmed it, once, on Panhua's oil, at the eighth beat, on her own say-so, and she stood on the step of a guesthouse above an east market in a cook's apron with her fan in the other hand and the bowl held out in front of her, and her face did the thing it does before it decides, and decided, and both of the things it had been deciding between happened at once.

"You're late," Suisui said. "It's been warmed once. That is the only time it is going to be warmed."

Yangyang came up the step in the wrong shoes.

"She says once is plenty," she said. "Twice makes it tough. She says to tell you."

Suisui looked at her.

"Who says."

Yangyang took the bowl in both hands. It was warm. It was warm the way a thing is warm that has been kept for four months and heated for one minute, which is to say it was warm in the place where the hand held it and cool at the rim and the steam off it went straight up, because the air over it was empty, and she stood on the step and looked at the steam go straight up and leaned toward it herself.

"Yours," she said. "The other one. She has grey at both temples and a patch on her apron in three cloths and she said to come back fat."

Suisui's fan came down, all the way.

Then she put her arms around her sister on the step, over the bowl, with the bowl between them where it had to go, and she did what her hands did, one adjustment and then another, and Yangyang put her forehead down into the place under her chin, with the cap on, with the brim in the way, and Suisui pushed the brim up with one finger so that the forehead could arrive, and it arrived.

"She'll have said it very politely," Suisui said, into the blue hair.

"She did."

"Then we're related."

⁂

She ate the bowl. All of it. Suisui watched every mouthful with her arms folded and the fan under one of them.

Baizhi took her wrist with the flat courtesy of a physician in a public kitchen and counted, and wrote the number down, and then put the thermometer in the collar of feathers at her throat for ninety seconds and wrote a second number, and looked at the two numbers together, and closed the book, and said, in the voice she uses for a splint and for a compliment:

"Warm. Quantified."

Then Yangyang went up the fourth stair, which announced her, and into the back room, and sat down at the desk under the window, and took the last letter out of the inside of her own sleeve where she had moved it at the arch, and put it under the inkstone, at the address, because that was where it was addressed to.

She read it there. The window stood open a hand's width. The paper was going soft. She had heard every word of it at a kitchen table in her sister's voice and it was different on the page, in a hand that was her own hand set slightly wrong, the way a familiar tune is different when somebody else plays it on your instrument.

At *come ■ when it's cold* she stopped, and looked at the blank, which was the width of a word she had, and said it, aloud, to the desk, for a woman on the far side of a fold who could hear it now, if hearing is a thing that goes through.

Then she got up, because she was hungry, and went downstairs.

## 臘月 — The Twelfth Month

Leave was granted for the New Year, as promised, as threatened.

She rode the post road west with a red cap in her pack and her breath standing white ahead of her, in the sleeves the crossing had left her, which were warmer than they looked and which every relay-keeper on the road looked at and declined to ask about, and Mingting received her the way it receives anyone: as if she had stepped out an hour ago on an errand, and where had she put the good ladle.

Suisui was at the gate.

She was younger than the other one by exactly the years the other one had waited, and she had a bird on her shoulder called Dumpling that was a bird all the way through, and she had heard from Baizhi by post about the hair and had been preparing, she said, to pretend it was ugly, and had found on sight that the pretending was beyond her.

"You've grown feathers," she said.

"Some."

"Ma would have plaited them."

"Ma would have said nothing."

"Fluently. Yes. Come in. I told the kitchen."

Yangyang stood in the gate.

She had come through this gate at nineteen going out, alone, with a registry office in front of her and a shell she would owe her sister a thank-you for, years late. She had come through it once since, for a solstice, and cut a post with a wind in her arms and covered the cut with her eyes on the kitchen door. She stood in it now with her hand on the wood, on the latch side, and looked down the lane, west, and then east, checking a distance, and then she stopped checking, because both roads were empty of anyone she was waiting for. She was the one who went. That was the arrangement. It was the same one from both sides.

"Jie," she said.

Suisui waited.

"There's a word I'm supposed to say for somebody. At the gate. To you."

"Then say it, darling. I'm standing here."

Yangyang said it.

It was a small word. It was the word for where a person is from, and she had it, and she said it in her own gateway to her own sister at the turn of the year with a bird on her sister's shoulder and feathers in her own hair, and it cost nothing, and it went where it was sent.

Suisui's face did the thing before it decided.

"Well," she said. "You've never once said that in this gate in your life. You've said *back* and *leave* and *the ladle's where you left it.* I'll take it. Whoever it's for, tell them I took it."

"She'll know."

⁂

At dusk, with the kitchen lit and loud behind her, she went out alone to the courtyard.

The post stood where it had stood all her life. Black-grey, iron through and through, holding twelve years of her in tenths of inches, and across them all, higher, a guest's single stroke, three inches deep, the grain parted like water: the measure. The wooden sword stood leaned at its foot, edge to the east, where two winters ago a wind had left it and where last winter she had put it back after a cut that had had two arms in it.

She picked it up. It knew her hands.

She took her stance in the snow-lit dark. There was a wind coming over the courtyard wall, the solstice's, the north-born one, the year's first warmth, and it came down the roof tiles and along her arms the way it does, and it was weather, plain, with the air empty inside it. She waited for the pressure at her right elbow, out of a habit four years deep, and the pressure was a memory, and she brought the elbow in herself, half an inch, the way she had brought it in on a broken landing above a gorge with her sister's cuff pulling toward the drop.

The arm went out looking for a wall. It found air. She brought it in.

She breathed out.

The cut.

What the post said, the court has heard from the courier and declines to copy; some cuts belong to their holders. The record admits only this much. She stood in the follow-through, breathing, in sleeves with clouds in them, with a red cap in her pack and a blue mark on her forehead and feathers in her hair, in a courtyard in Mingting at the turn of the year, alone, and the snow came off the roof in its soft applause, and she uncovered the cut.

She looked.

Then she went inside, where the table was crowded and steaming, and her sister was winning an argument with the seating, and a fifth pair of chopsticks lay neat at the empty end, at the seat nearest the door, and she left the question of who it was for where it lay, and sat down in the fourth, and ate.

Later, at the window in the old room, with the lamp turned low and the shutter open a hand's width, she took a sheet of paper and the second ink and wrote the first letter of the year, to a woman on the far side of a fold, in a hand the founders would recognise.

She told her the sound it made.

## Colophon

*entered at the youngest gate at the winter solstice, and at the New Year, in two hands and a second ink; the court keeps the register open*

**I. From the Almanac Bureau, Eastern Copy.** The margin under **接風** has acquired a third hand. It reads: *The bird has shed its down and rides the wind toward the heights. From here on, too, she will stride forward, toward everything she believes in.* The keeper has asked who wrote it and has been told that it was found there. The Bureau has left it standing and has, at last, ruled the second column: **人**.

**II. From the ninth tower.** *Eight beats. Solstice. Grey hour. Column of one, constituted as a bird. Counted whole. Rope left off the cleat. Reason unchanged.*

**III. From Huaxu Academy.** The physician's log for the solstice carries two numbers side by side and a note beneath them in her smallest hand: *gradient closed. Subject warm at the collar and at the wrist and, by report, at the forehead, which I have declined to measure, on the grounds that there are already two people in this household with a mark in that place and neither of them has asked me to.*

**IV. From Bookbinders' Row.** The founders have set this edition around the blank in the fourth section, where the courier stopped describing the dark, and have left the blank the width she gave it, which is the width of a woman holding still. They observe that a page arrived at the New Year addressed to a room in a printing this house has never seen, sealed, to be carried at the next solstice; that the office has weighed it; and that it weighs what a letter weighs.

**V. The register.** The girl at the northeast shoulder at first light has been seen there twice since the solstice, in sleeves with clouds in them, and on both mornings she looked east for the length of one breath and then went down to the stair foot and bought the fishermen breakfast, and on neither morning did she check the horizon for anyone.

The fried dough was excellent.

The court keeps the register open.

# Crooked on Purpose

*a rondo, for a big sister*

> *Amy, 6. Amy, 6 and a half (she insisted). Amy, 7.*
> *Amy, 8. Amy, 9. Amy, 10. Amy, 11. Amy, 12. Amy, 13. Amy, 14. Amy, 15. Amy, 16. Amy, 17. Amy, 18.*
>
> — kitchen doorframe, cabin with the blue door, Roya Frostlands.
> The first three in an adult's hand. The rest in a child's copy of it, each line crooked, each crooked differently.

---

## i. Bird

The dormitory magnolia had stood eleven years without being climbed, and at 04:51 on a Tuesday S.I.G.M.A. logged a mass of sixty-one kilograms in its upper branches and filed the observation under BIRD.

Iuno came out at five with tea.

She stood on the path in her coat with the cup balanced in her one hand and looked up through white blossom at a woman lying along a limb the way a cat lies along a wall, one leg swinging, wine-dark hair full of petals.

"You're in the magnolia."

"Best branch in the courtyard."

"It's ornamental."

"It's structural, and I'd know. I've been up here since one." The woman turned her head. Her eyes had a garden's patience in them and no sleep at all. "Your gate system thinks I'm a bird."

"It thinks a great many things."

"It's not wrong, exactly. I migrate."

Iuno drank her tea. Somewhere behind her the Solistrees were coming up amber for the shift change, and the false dawn slid across the courtyard the way it did every morning, competent, on time, entirely fake.

"The flower stall," Iuno said.

"Mm."

"You winked at me."

"You were staring."

"I was working."

"Same thing." The woman sat up, folded her arms across the branch, and rested her chin on them. Petals came off her shoulders in a small white weather. "Slower."

Iuno turned her cup a quarter-turn against her palm, which she did when a conversation had become interesting and she had decided to conceal that.

"What are you."

"Rude question."

"It's five in the morning and you're in the tree outside a room where my family sleeps."

"Better question." The woman considered the tea, the coat, the crystalline stump held easy against the ribs, all of it, with the frank appraisal of someone inspecting new furniture. "You've had a week to run me. What did you get?"

"An eleven-year-old registry entry for a merchant vessel that doesn't exist. A physician's note in Ragunna, on Carnevale night, describing a patient with a heart rate of thirty and an apology for the door. And a flower on a windowsill upstairs that has been in the same glass of water for two months without dropping a petal, and I have changed the water four times, and it has never needed changing."

"You changed the water."

"Yes."

"For a flower that doesn't drink."

"I didn't know that at the time."

"Mm." Something crossed the woman's face and left before it could be catalogued. "That's the most interesting thing anybody's told me this year."

"What do you want."

"Ten minutes with the kid."

Iuno's cup stopped an inch from her mouth.

"She's nineteen."

"She's twenty-nine and she's got a nineteen-year-old's mouth on her, and I heard her show." The woman swung her legs off the branch and let them hang. "Off the map. Borrowed receiver. Room with a lot of fog in it. Somebody said *there's a girl on channel nine again* and I sat down on a crate and listened to three hours of a stranger being extremely funny about seals."

"She's good."

"She's very good."

"And?"

"And she does her mother's voice."

Iuno set the cup down on the low wall. She did it carefully, which meant something.

"She learned it from her mother."

"She learned the *bright* one. That's a different instrument and it's kept in a different case, and I have listened to somebody play it for longer than this city has had a name, and I never once got underneath it." Petals dropped. The woman watched them go. "I'm not doing that again. I've decided. It took me an embarrassingly long time to decide anything, so I'd like some credit."

"None available."

"Worth asking."

Two floors up, a light came on behind a curtain. Both of them looked at it.

"Four o'clock," said the woman.

"Four o'clock."

"How long?"

"Since the eleventh week."

"Does she know you know?"

"She knows I know. She's begun leaving the kettle warm so I'll assume she went back to bed." Iuno's jaw did something small. "It's a very good lie. It's a considerate lie. It's structurally identical to about nine hundred lies I have personally received from her mother, and I would like to be finished with the whole product line."

"You've tried."

"I've tried."

"How."

"Directly."

"Mm." The woman dropped out of the tree. She landed without a sound, which for sixty-one kilograms from four meters was a statement, and straightened up and shook the blossom out of her hair like a dog coming out of a river. "Well. There's your problem."

"You have a better method."

"I've got two thousand years of watching people put a hand on a door and stop." She started up the path toward the dormitory as if the conversation had concluded in her favour, which it had. "The trick is you don't knock."

"What do you do."

"You move in."

## ii. Rematch

Amy came down at six-forty with her hair doing something criminal and found a stranger at the kitchen table eating her cereal.

The stranger had gone through the cupboard with the efficiency of a professional and had assembled, on a plate, a construction that involved the good jam, four crackers, and a boiled egg she had put on herself in somebody's saucepan. Lupa was standing at the counter with her arms folded and her ears at an angle Amy had personally catalogued as *pending*.

"Morning," said Amy.

"Morning."

"That's my bowl."

"It was on the shelf with the other bowls."

"It's the one with the seal on it."

"There's a seal on it," the woman agreed, and ate another spoonful.

Amy looked at Lupa. Lupa looked back with the face of somebody who had already lost this and intended to make a full report about it later.

"Is she ours?"

"Iuno says yes."

"Did Iuno say it in the voice?"

"She said it in the voice."

"Then she's ours," said Amy, and got the second-best bowl, and sat down, and the whole thing took nine seconds and had the shape of a house that had absorbed a great many people at short notice and had developed a procedure.

Under the table her hands had gone cold.

She had known before she reached the bottom of the stairs. She had known from the landing, from a laugh coming up the stairwell that had no business existing in a building she had lived in as a ghost, and she had come down eleven steps on a body that had stopped consulting her, and she had walked into the kitchen and said *morning*, because that was what came out, because it was what always came out, because the machine that produced *morning* had been running for twenty-two years and did not require her supervision.

The woman across the table had white blossom in her hair and a mouth full of Amy's cereal and no idea who she was.

"I'm Camellya," she said.

"Amy."

"I know."

"Then why'd you say yours."

"Manners." A crack of a grin. "I've got some. They're seasonal."

---

The pack arrived by attrition over the next hour, which was how the pack arrived at everything.

Chisa came in first, stopped in the doorway, and stood there long enough that Cartethyia walked into the back of her.

"Chisa?"

"Mm," said Chisa.

"What."

"She's very loud."

Camellya raised a hand. "I haven't said anything for four minutes."

"You're loud underneath," said Chisa, and came in, and got a cup, and sat down at the end of the table facing her with the composure of a woman who intended to keep an eye on a weather front. "It's like standing next to a river with something big in it."

"I like her," said Camellya, to nobody.

And then Cartethyia came round the doorframe and stopped dead, and dropped her stick, and said, "*Cliffside*."

Camellya turned.

What happened next took about a second and a half and involved a chair going over backwards, and the boiled egg ending up on the floor, and Cartethyia — who had a hip, and a physiotherapy appointment on Thursdays, and a carved finch on a walking stick she had grown to love — crossing four meters of kitchen at a speed that made Lupa put a hand out on reflex.

"THREE DAYS," said Cartethyia, into Camellya's shoulder. "You gave me *three days* and then you were *gone*—"

"You were sleeping."

"I woke up and there was a *note*—"

"There was a note. That's more than most people get." Camellya was holding her by both arms and looking at her with an expression Amy filed instantly, permanently, and did not examine. "You're taller."

"I'm exactly the same."

"You're standing taller. Different thing. Who taught you that?"

"Everybody," said Cartethyia, and wiped her face with the back of her wrist, and went to pick up her stick, and then had to explain the stick, and by the time she had finished explaining the stick the entire household was in the kitchen and Ciaccona was making the second pot.

Ciaccona took one long look at the woman with the flower at her sternum and said, "Absolutely not."

"Cia—"

"I know what that is. That is a *character.* We've got enough characters. We are at capacity."

"She trained Carte."

"She *abandoned* Carte, on a *cliff*, after three days—"

"I left a note," said Camellya.

"WHAT DID IT SAY."

Cartethyia went pink. "It said *don't apologise for the wood.*"

Ciaccona put the pot down.

"...Fine," she said. "That's good. That's annoyingly good. I'm making a note that I said it was good, so that later, when I'm being unbearable about her, there's a record."

---

Mei came in last, barefoot, with her coat on over what she had slept in, holding a mug that was already empty, and Amy watched her mother's whole face change in the doorway.

"Oh," said Mei. "*Oh.* You."

"Me."

"The flower woman."

"That's an unimprovable name and I'm keeping it."

"Have we met?"

"Mm." Camellya considered the question honestly, which took her a while, and Amy watched her take the time, and something behind Amy's sternum went very carefully still. "Not yet."

"You said that last time."

"It's still true. It's an ongoing project."

Mei laughed — the leakage one, the one that escaped her, and Amy heard it and heard exactly which one it was, and had to put her spoon down and look at the table. Her mother came round and pulled out a chair and sat in it backwards and leaned her arms on the back and said, "The flower doesn't die."

"No."

"I've had it in a glass for two months."

"You could've thrown it in a drawer. It'd be the same."

"That seems rude."

"To the flower?"

"To you," said Mei.

The kitchen carried on around them, and Lupa was arguing with Rebecca about whether an egg on the floor was a health matter or a fun surprise, and Amy sat with her hands flat on her knees under the table and watched a woman who had taught her to fall out of a wisteria without breaking her arms look straight into Ma's face and find nothing there she owned.

Camellya's flower had gone a shade darker at the edges. It came back by the time Mei got up for coffee.

Nobody saw it.

One person saw it.

---

That afternoon Amy came down the east stair with an armful of cable for Rebecca and stopped at the landing window, because there were two people in the courtyard under the magnolia and one of them was her mother.

They were three meters apart, which was a distance somebody had chosen. Mei had her hands in her pockets and her head tipped, doing the thing she did, which was standing in a conversation the way other people sat down in one.

Camellya said four words. Amy counted the mouth.

And her mother's face went through something — fast, complete, gone by the time it finished — and she said something back, short, and Camellya nodded once and walked off toward the Solistrees, and Mei stayed under the tree for about a minute and a half looking at the place where the flower stall used to set up on market days.

At dinner she was fine. She was better than fine; she was hilarious about a capybara.

Amy did not ask her. She thought about asking her for the whole of the soup course and then put it in the drawer with everything else, and noted, with the detachment of a woman filing her own scan results, that the drawer had begun to open on its own.

---

They played that night, because Amy made them.

It was a bad idea and she knew it was a bad idea while she was proposing it, and she proposed it anyway, out loud, at dinner, in her show voice, with a grin on — *four-player, house rules, I'll take anyone* — because the alternative was a whole evening of sitting in a room with a person who did not know her, and Amy would rather run a table than sit at one.

They set up in the common room. Rebecca hauled the machine down. Lynae took one look and posted up on the arm of the sofa without a word, which was Lynae for *I am fully invested.* Cartethyia lasted four rounds and was eliminated with dignity. Lupa lasted six and was eliminated without any.

Camellya won eleven games in a row.

"That's *cheating*."

"It's a controller. I'm holding it the way you're holding it."

"You've never *seen* this—"

"I've never seen anything," said Camellya, thumbs moving. "That's the whole trick."

"That's not a trick, that's a *statement*—"

"You're playing with a memory of the last time you played." She took the corner without slowing. "You've got a whole library in there. Every match you ever lost, every thing that worked once. You consult it. It's very impressive and it costs you about a fifth of a second." On the screen, something of Amy's came apart in a way that the room made a noise about. "I've got a body. The body's read the room already. There's nothing in the way."

"That's *horrifying*."

"It's restful."

"Play left-handed."

Camellya swapped hands without looking up and won the twelfth.

Amy threw the controller into the cushions and lay flat on the floor and stared at the ceiling, and the room laughed, and Ciaccona said something withering, and for about four seconds Amy was nineteen and losing at a video game in a warm room, which is a thing that ought to be available to everybody and is not.

"Rematch," she said, to the ceiling.

"Any time."

"No, I mean — " and she caught it, and it went past her anyway, twenty-two years of momentum and no brakes fitted: " — I've been *saying* rematch for twenty years, I *asked for this*, I said it out loud to like four people, I said *I wish we could gather for a rematch someday*—"

The room did not change.

Lupa's ears came round. Chisa's fingers found her scissors and stopped. Iuno, in the corner with a book she had stopped reading eleven minutes ago, did not lift her head, and the not-lifting was the loudest thing in the building.

And Camellya, on the floor with her back against the sofa and a controller loose in her hands, said, easily, without turning:

"Twenty years is a long time to hold a rematch open."

"Yeah, well." Amy sat up. The grin came back online; it had a good technician. "You were *busy*."

"Was I?"

"I heard you were recuperating."

"Sounds like me."

"You came all the way north once, while you were supposed to be lying down, just to check on somebody." Amy got her feet under her. Her voice had not moved off its mark; the show voice was a very good voice. "And you saw she was okay and you left without saying anything, and I was seven, and I sat on the step for two hours because I thought you were coming back in for tea."

Nothing in the room moved.

"Anyway," said Amy, brightly, "you're up. Best of five. I'm going to lose."

She lost.

## iii. The Fifth Bar

Camellya hummed while she played. She hummed while she cooked and while she cheated at a card game she had learned that afternoon, and she hummed on the stairs, and it was always the same four bars, and the four bars always stopped in the same place, and the stopping had a shape to it — the shape of a doorway with nobody standing in it.

Ciaccona lasted two days.

On the third night she came into the common room with the lute already in her hands and the brace already off, which was a Thursday and a violation and Cartethyia was going to have views, and she sat down on the low table directly in front of Camellya and said, "Say it again."

"Say what."

"The thing you do."

"I don't do a thing."

"You've been doing a thing for sixty-two hours." Ciaccona set her fingers. "Four bars. It goes up at the third and it hangs on the fourth and then you swallow it, and you've done it in this room nine times, and I have been *very patient*, and I am a bard, and there is a limit."

Camellya looked at her for a moment with something almost like alarm, which on that face was an event.

"Don't," she said.

"Hum it."

"Girl—"

"Hum it," said Ciaccona, "or I'll play it wrong all night, and I'll do it in the corridor, and I'll do it outside your window, and I am *very* good at wrong."

Amy, on the stairs, had stopped with one hand on the rail.

Camellya hummed the four bars.

They were nothing much. That was the terrible part; Amy had thought about this for thirteen years and could never get past it. Four bars of a kitchen tune, the kind a person makes up with flour on her hands, going up at the third the way a question goes up, hanging on the fourth, waiting.

And Ciaccona — who played the fourth note now, who had spent a lifetime finding the next thing by feel, who had a hand that woke her at night and a brace she had left upstairs — leaned into the lute and played the fifth bar.

She got two notes of it out.

Camellya came off the sofa so fast that the sofa moved. She was across the room and through the door before the second note had finished ringing, and she took the doorframe with her shoulder hard enough that the frame complained, and the smell that came off her going past was green and crushed and wrong, like a lawn after a machine.

The lute rang on into a room where nobody was breathing.

"...What did I do," said Ciaccona.

Amy was already gone.

---

She found her on the landing between floors, on her knees, with both hands flat on the concrete.

The flower at Camellya's sternum had gone the colour of a bruise four days old, purple-black at every edge, and it was pulsing, and the pulsing had a rhythm that Amy's whole body recognised before her mind caught up: a frequency running past its tolerance and holding anyway. Under the skin of her forearms things were moving. Something had come out of the back of her hand, green and thin and blind, and gone into the seam of the stair tread, and cracked it.

"Okay," said Amy.

"Get out."

"Yeah, no." Amy sat down on the step. "Hand."

"*Get*—"

"Give me your hand, it's a rule in my house." She held hers out, palm up, and waited. "It's rule two. There's six of them, they're on the back of a lab printout, they're on the fridge. Rule two is somebody's got a hand on you the whole time. It's not for you. It's for the person holding."

Camellya turned her head. Her eyes were doing something Amy had no file for.

She put her hand in Amy's.

It was too hot, and there was something under the skin of the palm that moved when Amy closed her fingers, and Amy closed her fingers anyway.

"Rule four," she said. "There's a clock and it's running. Eleven minutes."

"Eleven's arbitrary."

"Eleven's the number I can tolerate," said Amy, and did not explain, and somewhere two floors up a woman with one arm turned a page she was not reading.

They sat on the landing.

At four minutes the vine in the stair tread went slack and drew back in, and Camellya made a sound like a person putting down something heavy at the top of a staircase.

At six the flower came off black and went a tired rose.

At nine she said, "That's the first time in about six hundred years that anybody's done that."

"Done what."

"Sat down."

"Somebody should've."

"Mm." Camellya looked at their hands. "Somebody did, once. I remember the sitting and I don't remember the somebody. That's most of my life, if you want the tour."

"I don't."

"Liar."

"I *really* don't."

"You've been lying to me for three days, weed," said Camellya, without heat, "and you're very good, and I've been enjoying it, and I'd like to point out that the reason I know is that you keep *checking my flower.*"

Amy's hand stayed exactly where it was. Her face stayed exactly where it was. Her whole body held a stillness with the discipline of a woman who had been a standing wave over a city for ten years and knew to the decimal what stillness cost.

"You called me weed," she said.

"Did I."

"Just now."

Camellya frowned. It was a real frown; it went in and did not come out for a while.

"I don't know that word," she said.

"You've said it four times since Tuesday."

"I don't *know* it." She pulled her hand back and pressed the heel of it against her sternum. "That's how it comes. It comes out of the mouth before it gets to the head, and by the time it gets to the head there's a wall there and the wall's on fire." She got up. She did it badly and did not accept the arm Amy offered. "Eleven minutes. That's a good number."

"It's Iuno's number."

"It's a good number," said Camellya, and went up the stairs.

## iv. The Riseway

Amy found her at four in the morning on the north pad, sitting on the nose of a decommissioned freight sled with her bare feet in the frost, looking at a false sky the colour of a switched-off screen.

"You're up."

"So are you," said Camellya.

"I'm always up."

"I know. I've been counting." She kicked her heels against the hull. "Three nights out of three. You get up at four, you put the kettle on, you take the cup upstairs and you pour it out the window at five so the level's right, and then you sit on the sill in the dark and hold a house key on a piece of string."

Amy stood very still on the cold concrete.

"That's *deeply* invasive."

"Mm."

"That's like — that's stalking."

"It's tracking. It's what I'm *for*." Camellya turned. In the pad lights her eyes were flat and patient, a hunter's eyes with nothing behind them but attention. "I've been made to do exactly one thing, and I got out of the job, and this is the only part of the training I kept. Do you want the good news or the bad news."

"Bad."

"You breathe differently at the window."

Amy sat down on the sled.

The false sky did its thing over them, which was nothing; Helios ran a low blue between the hours, and above it were sixty fathoms of stone and above that a sealed sea, and the whole arrangement had the honest decency to make no promises.

"There's a shuttle at five-fifteen," Amy said, to the dark. "Goes up the Riseway. Takes an hour and eleven minutes and it's the ugliest hour of your life and there's a woman who sells terrible coffee at the top."

"Where's it go."

"Up."

"To?"

"A cabin," said Amy. "There's a blue door."

The pause went on for a while. Camellya's flower moved once, small, under the collar of a jacket she had stolen from Lupa on the second day.

"Right," she said.

---

The frostlands did what they always did, which was continue.

They stood at the shuttle window with their foreheads near the cold glass and watched white go past to the circumference of the world, and the wind combed the drifts into long parallel sentences, and the sealed sky hung overhead where stars used to be.

"You know what I did for ten years," said Amy.

"Some of it."

"Who told you."

"A woman with a lot of fog and forty-seven robots." Camellya breathed on the glass and drew something in it and rubbed it out. "She's got you on file. She's got everybody on file. It's a personality flaw and she's aware of it and she's decided to keep it."

"Is she nice?"

"She's *worse* than nice. She's *loyal.*" Camellya said the word the way a doctor says a diagnosis. "I've watched her love somebody for a very long time from a distance she measured herself, and I have never once been able to work out whether to congratulate her or throw a rock at her."

"What'd you settle on."

"Both. Rotating."

The shuttle climbed. Somewhere below and behind them the sealed gate held, on schedule, the way it had held for a hundred years and thirteen, and Amy did not look back down the valley, and Camellya watched her not look.

"You smell like a door," said Camellya.

"Excuse me?"

"You smell like a door I walked past a long time ago." She said it the way a person says a house number. "I don't remember where. I remember the cold and I remember that I didn't like it and I remember walking faster. And you've got it on you, and I noticed it in the kitchen on the first morning, and I've been trying to lose it for three days."

Amy watched the snow.

It fell at a little under one meter per second, every flake finishing a decision it had made forty minutes ago, high up, in the dark, where nobody was watching.

"That's not a thing," she said.

"Mm."

"That's a smell thing, that's — you're an *animal*, that's not evidence—"

"I didn't say it was evidence," said Camellya, and let it go, and Amy sat with her hands in her lap and her jaw tight for nine kilometers.

---

She came back to it at the top, at the shuttle pad, with the terrible coffee in both hands and the wind coming down off the ridge.

"I stood in your snow," Camellya said.

Amy stopped with the cup at her mouth.

"Eleven days before the thing you did. I've counted it back four times since the kitchen and I get eleven every time." Camellya was looking down the valley at nothing. "I don't remember why I came. I never do. I get a pull and the legs go and the head follows about a week later asking questions nobody's going to answer. I came up that road and I stood in a hollow by a lake and there was a cabin with the snow up to the sills, and there was a blue door, and there was no smoke, and I stood there for about an hour."

"Camellya—"

"And I looked in the window," said Camellya, "and there was a fire laid in the hearth. Small sticks first. Done properly. And one match on the stone next to it."

The wind came down off the ridge.

"And I looked at it," she said, "and it meant nothing to me. It was a fire that hadn't been lit. That's all I had. I ate soup off a man two valleys over and slept in a pine and went south in the morning."

Amy's cup had gone still in her hands.

"A laid fire and one match is a *goodbye*," said Camellya. "Anybody who has ever kept a house in her life could have read that off the glass in four seconds. I had kept a house. I *had* one. I just didn't have it any more, and the reason I didn't have it is that I'd put it in a room and locked the room, because keeping it was killing me, and I chose to live." She turned around. "So that's my file. That's what's in it. I was eleven days early and I couldn't read the window."

Amy stood in the wind on a shuttle pad in the frostlands with a coffee going cold in her hands, and heard, coming out of a stranger's mouth, the exact accounting her mother used to do at a kitchen table with the lamp off — and ran it, and got the same total she had always got when she ran it for Ma.

"That wasn't your fault," she said.

"Mm."

"You didn't *know*."

"No."

"You couldn't have known, you were — the room was locked, that's not a *choice*, that's a *medical*—" Amy's hands had come up on their own. "You'd have gone in. If you'd known, you'd have gone in that door and you'd have sat down in my kitchen and you'd have made me look at you and you'd have said *what is this fire for*, and I'd have had to say it out loud, and if I'd had to say it out loud I don't know if I'd have—"

She stopped.

Camellya waited. She was extremely good at waiting; she had done nothing else for two thousand years.

"Say that again," she said. "Slower."

"No."

"Say it again and put your mother's name in it."

"*No.*"

"Then say it and put yours in."

Amy threw the coffee. It went about four meters and made a brown hole in a clean drift, and the wind took the steam off it, and the two of them stood there looking at the hole.

"That was a terrible coffee," said Camellya.

"It's famously terrible."

"Then it's a mercy killing."

"Shut up."

"Mm."

"I mean it, shut *up*—"

"I'm going to keep talking," said Camellya, kindly, "because I've been the quiet one for a very long time and it turns out it doesn't work. Show me the cabin."

## v. A Fire, Laid

The blue door was a blue door. Some colours keep their promises.

Amy took the key off the cord around her neck and stood in front of the lock for eleven seconds, and Camellya stood behind her and did not say anything, and then the key went in and the cold came out and they went inside.

It smelled of cold wood and old paper and, very faintly, thirteen years down under everything, of cocoa.

Camellya walked into the middle of the room and stopped.

Her flower went dark. She put a hand flat on the table and stood there breathing through her mouth like someone at altitude, and Amy — with a hand half out and no plan for it — watched a kitchen do to an indestructible woman what two thousand years of the world had failed to manage.

"Rule two?" she said.

"Give me a minute."

"That isn't one of the—"

"Give me a minute, weed."

Amy gave her a minute.

At the end of it Camellya straightened up, took her hand off the table, and looked at the four fingerprints she had left pressed into the wood, which was oak, which was eighty years old.

"Sorry about your table."

"It's not my table."

"Whose is it?"

"Ours," said Amy, and went to lay a fire, and found one already laid.

---

She had known it would be there. She had known for thirteen years, the way you know a thing you have refused to check, and she knelt down in front of the hearth and looked at her own eighteen-year-old handiwork — small sticks first, done properly, the way she had been taught by a woman in a coat — and at the single match lying on the stone beside it, and she did not move.

Camellya came and looked over her shoulder.

"That's a good fire."

"Yeah."

"You'd have been what, eighteen?"

"Nineteen. Just."

"It's a good fire," said Camellya again.

Amy sat back on her heels.

"It's for when she comes home," she said.

The wind did something in the chimney.

"She came home," said Camellya.

"I know."

"She's asleep in a bunk two hundred kilometers south with her mouth open. I looked in on the way out. She's got a foot out from under the blanket and the seer's got a hand on her ankle like she's holding down a tarp."

"I *know*—"

"Then what's the fire for?"

Amy sat on her heels in front of a hearth in a house she had left ready for a woman who had already arrived, and had no answer, and heard the shape of the question keep going after the question had stopped, the way the four bars kept going after they stopped.

She picked up the match.

Her hand shook, which annoyed her enormously, and she struck it on the stone and it went out, and she said a word she had learned from Rebecca, and Camellya laughed out loud — a real one, a bark, delighted — and got a second match from a tin on the mantel and handed it over without comment.

The fire took.

It went up the way a fire goes up when it has been laid properly by somebody who was paying attention, all at once and with no drama at all, and the room came out of the dark in pieces: a record player. A shelf of cartridges lined up by hand. A mug on the left side of the shelf, handle out, ready.

Amy sat down on the floor with her back against the couch and watched it burn and did not say anything for a long time, and Camellya sat down beside her and did not either, and outside the snow went past the window at a little under one meter per second.

"It's warm," Amy said, eventually.

"Mm."

"I want to be really clear that I'm not — this isn't a *breakthrough*, I'm not having a *moment*—"

"You're having a moment."

"I'm having a *small* moment."

"You're having a whole one. I've seen a lot of them. I'd know."

"You'd forget."

"Reliably," Camellya agreed. "Which is why I'm going to be extremely obnoxious about it now, while I've got it."

---

Later, with the fire down to its working heat and the kettle on, Amy showed her the doorframe.

It stood between the kitchen and the front room and it went up like a ladder, and Camellya put her thumb on the bottom marks and read her way up.

"Six. Six and a half — "

"She insisted."

" — she *insisted*, seven. That's an adult's hand."

"That's Ma."

"And then that's — " Camellya's thumb moved. " — that's a kid trying to write like an adult."

"That's me. Every year. Ruler flat on the head, pencil in the other hand." Amy leaned on the frame with her arms folded. "You can't measure yourself. That's the whole thing about it. Somebody else is supposed to hold the pencil."

"Is that why they're all crooked."

"They're crooked *on purpose*, thank you. After the third one I stopped correcting them." She said it flat, the way she said everything that mattered. "It's evidence."

"Of?"

"That nobody was holding it."

Camellya said nothing. Her thumb went up past *Amy, 12*, past 15, past 18, into the blank wood above, all that empty frame somebody had left room for.

Then she stopped.

"What's this one."

"That's the weird one," said Amy. "Don't ask me, I've never worked it out. There's no name on it and it's about nine centimeters above eighteen and it's *straight*, which means it isn't mine, and I think Ma did it as a joke about how tall I was going to get and then forgot to write on it." She shrugged. "It's the only line up there that's dead level. It used to really annoy me."

Camellya looked at the mark for a long moment.

"Mm," she said, and went and got the kettle.

---

## vi. Going In

They had the hot chocolate at the table, made to the correct recipe, which is a crime.

"There's salt in this."

"There's supposed to be salt in it."

"There's *salt* in it."

"It's a *secret ingredient*—"

"It's a *crime*, weed, it's a documented—" Camellya stopped with the mug halfway down.

Amy watched her stop.

"Yeah," she said. "You said that too. You said it every time. You said it was a crime and then you drank the whole thing and asked for more, and Ma used to make an extra cup and put it by your elbow without saying anything, and one time you fell asleep at this table with your face in your arms and I drew on your hand and you had it on you for two days before you noticed."

The mug went down.

"Tell me," said Camellya.

"No."

"Amy."

"*No.*" Amy put both hands flat on the table. "You went through a wall last night because a bard played you two notes. Two. I've got twenty-two years of you and you can't take four bars of a kitchen song."

"That's mine to spend."

"It's not, though, is it." Amy's voice had gone somewhere very level. "That's the thing nobody says out loud in this family and I'm going to say it, because I'm the newest and I've got nothing to lose. You spend it, and then there's somebody on a landing at four in the morning holding your hand and doing sums about whether she just killed you, and *that part's mine.* You don't get to hand me that and call it your business."

Camellya sat back.

"Who taught you that."

"Everybody in that dormitory, over about eleven weeks, at extremely high volume."

"Mm." Camellya turned her mug a quarter-turn on the table, which was Iuno's move, and Amy noticed her notice it, and neither of them said anything about it. "Now do the other half."

"What other half."

"The half where you apply it to yourself."

The fire moved in the grate.

"Don't," said Amy.

"You've got something in a drawer."

"Camellya—"

"You've had it in there for months. Everybody in that house knows the drawer's there and nobody can find the handle, and your seer has been walking past it every morning with a cup of tea in her hand pretending to read." Camellya leaned forward on her forearms. "I've watched somebody run this exact operation for two thousand years, and I want you to understand that I am not guessing. I know the *sound* of the floorboard."

"It's not the same."

"Then tell me why."

"Because it's *hers*," said Amy.

And there it was, out, in a kitchen, at a table with four fingerprints pressed into it.

"She sleeps eight hours," Amy said. "Do you understand what that took. Do you know what she was like in week three, do you know what she was like in the *first* week — she'd wake up and check the room and count us, she counted us, out loud, in the dark, and she thought nobody could hear her doing it, and now she sleeps eight hours and she *laughs* at things, she laughs at *nothing*, she laughed for eleven minutes last Thursday because Lupa wore a paper crown to breakfast, and I sat there and watched her do it and I thought: I have got a thing in my pocket that turns this off."

"So you keep it."

"So I keep it. Yes. That's — yes. That's the whole strategy, thank you for coming."

"And what's it costing you."

"Nothing," said Amy.

Camellya laughed. There was some warmth in it, and it went on slightly too long.

"You think you're carrying it," she said.

"I *am* carrying it—"

"It's carrying *you.*" She tapped the table twice. "Here's how you tell. It's a very old test and it's never once been wrong: check which one of you decides where you go. You get up at four — did you decide that? You've poured four cups of tea out of a window since I got here to keep a woman with one arm from asking a question. Did you decide that?"

"That's—"

"You've got a house up a mountain with a fire in it that you couldn't light for thirteen years, and the reason you couldn't light it is that you were still holding a door open for somebody who walked through it in *autumn.*"

Amy sat very straight in her chair and said nothing.

"You've built the whole shape of your day around a thing you won't look at," said Camellya. "That's furniture, weed. That's what furniture does. It holds a room open around an empty chair."

The fire moved.

"Then what do I do," said Amy.

"I've got no idea."

"That's — that's *useless*, you're *useless*—"

"I know. I'm sorry. I'm the worst possible person for this and I'm the only one who came." Camellya pushed her mug aside and put her hands flat where it had been. "But I'll tell you what I want, and you can do whatever you like with it. I want you to tell me one thing about the child in this cabin. One. Small. And I want it now, at this table, with the fire lit."

"It'll hurt you."

"Yes."

"It might do worse than hurt you."

"Also yes."

"Then why."

"Because I have never in my entire life wanted to remember anything," said Camellya.

The wind did something in the chimney.

"Two thousand years," she said. "Longer. I've thrown all of it away and I'd throw it away again tomorrow and I've never lain awake once. I've watched empires go under and thought about lunch. And I came up a mountain on a shuttle with a coffee I didn't want, and I stood in a room and it *hurt*, and I wanted to stay in the room." She looked up. "So it's new. And I've got very little that's new, and I'd like to spend some of it on you, because you're the reason it started, and because whatever I was to you back then, I was standing in your snow eleven days early and I read the window wrong, and I would like to file an appeal."

Amy sat in her mother's kitchen with her hands around a cup of hot chocolate she had made wrong on purpose.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay?"

"Okay. But we do it my way." She got up. She went to the shelf and came back with a wind-up clock that had lived on it since before she was born, and set it on the table between them, and turned it to face Camellya. "House rules. All six. You've done two and four already, you're going to hate the rest."

"Read them out."

"One. You say *going in* before, and when you come back you say a number." Amy sat down. "Different one every time. Then I know it's you and I know it's now."

"That's clever."

"That's *Chisa.*" She held out her hand, palm up, and waited. "Two. Somebody's got a hand on you. Whole time."

Camellya put her hand in Amy's.

"Three. Somebody listens. That's me; I've got about the best ear in this hemisphere and I'll know before you do." She set the clock. "Four. Eleven minutes and I'm coming to get you, and I don't know yet how I'd do that, so let's find out never."

"Five?"

Amy hesitated.

Then she reached into her coat and took out nine inches of felt with black yarn hair and a coat from a year she had missed and two small gold buttons, which she had carried up a mountain in a pocket without mentioning it to anybody, and she put it in Camellya's other hand.

"Five. Ma goes in the hands," she said. "Fold your fingers over her before you go. Then they're closed, and nobody in the room has to look at them open."

Camellya looked down at the doll for a while.

"Who made this."

"Cartethyia. During."

"Mm." She folded her fingers over it. "That's a good kid."

"She's four hundred."

"They're all kids," said Camellya. "What's six?"

"Six is you do it in front of people," said Amy. "No sneaking off. That one's Ma's."

"Is she right?"

"She's *insufferably* right, and she got there by being wrong about it for two lifetimes, which is the only method anybody in my family respects." Amy squared her shoulders. "Ready?"

"Give me the thing."

"You taught me to fall," said Amy.

Camellya's hand tightened.

"I was six," said Amy, "and I climbed the wisteria over the porch because the seals were coming up through the ice and I wanted the high ground, and I got stuck, and Ma was two hours away at the trading post. And you came round the corner of the house with a dead something over your shoulder, and you looked up at me for about a second and a half, and you put the dead thing down, and you said — "

She stopped. Her voice had gone somewhere and she waited for it to come back.

" — you said, *don't stiffen up, it's the stiffening that breaks you, go loose and let the ground have you.* And I said I'd die. And you said *you'll die eventually, that's not today's problem, let go.*"

The clock ticked.

"And I let go," said Amy. "And you caught me. And then you made me do it eleven more times off the porch rail, and you counted them out loud, and on the eleventh you *didn't catch me*, and I hit the snow and I rolled and I got up, and you said — " she was smiling; it was a terrible smile and she could not do anything about it " — you said *there. Now you can jump at anything.*"

Camellya's flower had gone black.

"Going in," she said.

"Number when you come back."

"Going *in*, weed."

"Say it properly—"

"Going in," said Camellya, and the lights in her went out.

---

It went wrong at ninety seconds.

There was a sound from her that Amy had heard exactly once before, out at the rim, from a stratum of drifting testimony, ten thousand voices preserved at the moment of subtraction, and Amy did not let go of her hand.

At two minutes the vines came.

They came out of both forearms at once and went into the floor and the floor gave, and they went along the boards and up the wall and around the doorframe, and the doorframe held, and the room filled with the smell of crushed green and something underneath it that was frost cracking, and the mug came off the table and broke, and the fire went sideways in the grate and stood back up.

At three the record player came on by itself for four seconds and stopped.

Amy stayed where she was.

"Four minutes," she said, to a woman who was not there. "You're at four. That's fine, that's *fine*, Iuno gave me eleven and she gave it to me because eleven's what she could stand, and I've been in a room where nine people picked a number for me, and you've got the whole eleven, you can have all of it, it's *paid for*—"

At five the vines went slack.

At six they drew back in, all of them, everywhere in the room at once, and the boards came back down more or less flat, and the wall let go of what it was holding, and the doorframe stood there covered in green and did not fall.

At six-forty Camellya's fingers moved on the felt.

"Ninety-six," she said.

Amy's eyes closed.

"That's a lie," she said. "You never gave me a first number, so ninety-six is a lie, and you did it on purpose to see if I was paying attention."

"Were you."

"*Yes*, you absolute — "

"Good," said Camellya, and opened her eyes, and Amy saw them come back on, one after the other, like a building.

They sat there.

Camellya's flower came up off black, through purple, and settled at rose. She looked at the wreckage of a room she had gone through without moving, and at the four fingerprints in the table, and at the crockery, and then she looked down at nine inches of felt in her fist and unfolded her fingers very carefully, one at a time, as if the doll might have been damaged by her.

"I held a pencil," she said.

---

## vii. The Pencil

"What?"

"I held a pencil in this house." Camellya was frowning at the middle distance, and her mouth was moving slightly ahead of her, the way it had in the kitchen with the word *crime.* "You'd had a bad day about something. You were small and you were *furious* about being small, and you'd been standing at that frame stamping, and I came in with snow on me and you turned round and you were absolutely — " she made a shape with one hand, a small vertical enraged shape " — *incandescent*."

Amy had stopped breathing.

"And I said, how tall do you want to be. And you said *taller than everybody.* And I said that's greedy and unrealistic and I respect it." Camellya got up. She went to the doorframe with her hand out in front of her like a woman walking in the dark. "And you asked me how tall you were going to *get*, and nobody can answer that, and you were seven and you needed a number."

She stopped in front of the frame, in front of the blank wood above *Amy, 18*, and put her thumb on the straight, level, unlabelled line nine centimeters above it.

"So I put the pencil where my own head was," she said, "and I drew a line, and I said *there.*"

Amy came off the chair.

"That's your—"

"Stand me against it."

"Camellya—"

"*Stand me against it,*" said Camellya, and turned round, and put her heels to the frame and her shoulders flat and her chin level, the way you do, the way everybody does, the way children have done at doorframes in every house in every world since somebody first thought of it.

Amy got the ruler out of the drawer.

Her hands were not steady. She put the ruler flat on the top of Camellya's head and slid it in against the wood, and looked, and had to look again, and then had to put a hand on the frame.

The line crossed the edge of the ruler dead centre. It went through it the way a line goes through the thing it was drawn against, at the same height, on the same wood, by the same body, thirteen years apart.

"You're the same," said Amy.

"Told you."

"You *didn't* tell me. You don't remember telling me. You've *never* told me—"

"I'm telling you now," said Camellya.

Amy put her forehead against the doorframe.

She stayed there a while. There was green all over the wood from the vines, drying, going brown at the edges, and under it thirteen years of pencil, and under her forehead a mark that a woman had made as a promise on an ordinary afternoon and then lost, and that a girl had looked at ten thousand times without knowing it was a record of somebody having been in the room.

"Everything moved," Amy said, into the wood. "Everything. Ma's face moved, her *name* moved, my body moved and then it stopped moving and then I didn't have one, the city moved, the *sky* moved, they built a new one — and this stupid line's been sitting here the whole time being the right height for you."

"Mm."

"Do you know how insulting that is?"

"It's very insulting," Camellya agreed. "I'm extremely reliable in the least useful possible way. Get a pencil."

"Why."

"Because it's got no name on it," said Camellya, "and I'd like one."

Amy wrote it.

She wrote it in her own hand, which had been trained at eight to copy an adult's and had never entirely stopped, and it came out crooked, and she left it, and the frame between the kitchen and the front room of a cabin in the Roya Frostlands acquired, thirteen years late, the word *CAMELLYA* beside a straight level line nine centimeters above *Amy, 18*.

"There's no number on it," said Camellya.

"You don't have one."

"Everybody's got a number."

"You're *immortal*—"

"Put one," said Camellya. "Any of them. It's a frame. It's supposed to say a year."

Amy thought about it, and then wrote a small *?* and, underneath, *(she wouldn't say)*, and Camellya read it and laughed so hard she had to sit down on the floor.

---

And then she said, "Right. You."

"Me what."

"You stopped at eighteen."

"I stopped *growing* at—"

"You stopped at eighteen on the frame," said Camellya, from the floor, "and it's been eighteen for thirteen years, and there's a nineteen missing, and I'm holding the pencil. Get over here."

Amy stood in the middle of the room.

"That's — " she said. "That's a lot."

"Mm."

"That's genuinely a lot, actually, I'd like it noted—"

"It's noted. Come here."

She came.

She put her heels to the frame and her shoulders flat and her chin level, and it was the most exposed she had felt since a technician opened a pod and found the word gone out of the clay, and Camellya stood up and put the ruler on her head and did not say anything about the fact that Amy's eyes were shut.

The pencil went against the wood.

"Hold still."

"I'm holding—"

"You're *vibrating*, weed."

The line went on.

Amy stepped away and turned round and looked at it, and it lay on top of eighteen. The same wood. The same height. A girl who had stopped growing at twelve and had gone on climbing a ladder that stayed where it was for six years out of pure spite.

"That's the same line."

"Mm."

"It's *straight*, as well."

"Mm."

"Put a wobble in it."

"No."

"Camellya—"

"No," said Camellya, and wrote *19* beside it.

And then, without asking, underneath, she wrote *29*, and put a bracket down the side joining the two.

Amy's mouth opened.

"You can't—"

"You did the ten," said Camellya. She did not turn round. She was writing small, with her nose close to the wood, the way people write when they intend a thing to last. "You did ten years on your own in a machine over a city, with the eye on you, keeping a calendar nobody was going to check and observing Tuesdays for an audience of nobody, and every single person in that dormitory would take a knife for you and not one of them counts it, because you don't let them, because you only ever bring the ten out when it wins you an argument."

"It's not — that's not real time, that's—"

"It's on the frame now," said Camellya. "That's what a frame's *for*." She stepped back and looked at her work: two numbers and a bracket and a crooked name and thirteen years of a child's handwriting going up a piece of eighty-year-old oak in a house nobody had lived in. "A frame's a list of afternoons when somebody was standing there with a pencil. The height's an excuse. It's always been an excuse."

---

## viii. Attendance

She left at dawn, which was a false one, and which Amy had known she would do since the kitchen on the first morning.

They walked down to the shuttle pad through snow that had come in overnight and taken the path Amy had salted, fairly, at the same speed it took everything.

"You're going to leave without saying goodbye," said Amy.

"I'm saying it now."

"You're saying it *forty minutes early*, which in your family counts as—"

"My *family*," said Camellya, and stopped walking, and turned round in the middle of a white field with the wind coming down off the ridge, and looked at a girl in a good scarf with a house key on a string under her collar, and something went across her face that had a great deal of weather in it and did not resolve into anything a person could use.

"What," said Amy.

"Nothing. It's a good word. I've heard it about nine times this week and it's always been about me and I keep waiting for somebody to correct it." She started walking again. "Spring."

"What?"

"You said you'd tell her. You didn't say it out loud, so I'll say it: you're going to tell her."

Amy walked eleven steps.

"Spring," she said.

"Why spring."

"Because the wisteria comes up in spring." She had her hands in her pockets and her eyes down the valley. "And she'll have something to look at while I talk. She's better at hard things when there's something to look at. She does this thing where she goes and stands at a window and then she can say it."

"Mm," said Camellya. "That's a good plan."

"It's a *stalling* plan."

"It's both. Most good plans are." They came round the last turn and the pad was below them with its one light on. "Will you have somebody in the room?"

"Iuno."

"Not the wolf?"

"Lupa'll break something."

"Then she'll fix it badly and pretend she meant the crack." Camellya nodded. "Take the seer. Seers are good in a room; they've already had the conversation nine times in their heads and they've got the boring parts pre-solved."

"You've met a lot of seers?"

"I've met two and I've been unbearable to both," said Camellya. "It's a small field and I'm well known in it."

The shuttle came up out of the white with its lamps swinging.

"Hey," said Amy.

"Mm."

"When are you coming back."

Camellya stopped with one boot on the step.

Two thousand years of a woman who followed a pull and never once made an appointment stood in the door of a shuttle in the Roya Frostlands with white blossom still in her hair from a tree two hundred kilometers south, and did the arithmetic, and it took her four seconds, which at her age constitutes a life's work.

"Spring," she said.

"You don't do that."

"I'm aware."

"You've never *once*—"

"I'm aware, weed, and I'd like a receipt," said Camellya. "Say it back so it's binding."

"Spring."

"Spring." She stepped up into the car. "And if I'm late, assume weather and start without me, and then tell me the whole thing afterwards at that table with the salt crime, and I'll go through the wall about it and you can hold my hand and read me the rules."

"That's a *terrible* arrangement—"

"It's the arrangement," said Camellya, and the door came across, and the shuttle went down the mountain into the dark with its lamps swinging.

---

She hummed all the way south.

Four bars, going up at the third, hanging on the fourth the way a question hangs.

And somewhere past the second tunnel, with the frostlands giving way to steppe and the steppe arguing with the coast, her mouth went one note further than it had gone in a very long time — one, a fifth note, small and low and unremarkable, the sort of note that costs nothing to sing and eleven days to earn — and Camellya sat up straight in an empty carriage with her hand flat on her sternum and laughed out loud, alone, at nothing, the way she had laughed at a table in a border-town bar the night a tired woman put an arm around her own shadow.

The flower stayed rose the whole way.

## ix. Day One

Amy got in at half nine at night.

The dormitory was warm and loud and smelled of the thing Rebecca had done to the stove, and Lupa was wearing the paper crown, and Cartethyia had a timer going and Ciaccona had eleven minutes left and was rationing them like a woman in a lifeboat, and Chisa looked up from the couch and said, "Where's the river gone?"

"South."

"Mm," said Chisa. "It's quieter."

"Is that good?"

"It's quieter," said Chisa, which was Chisa for a great deal.

Iuno was at the kitchen table with the book she had been copying out by hand for four months, one hour every morning, in two inks. She looked up as Amy came through, and took in the snow on the shoulders and the burn of a face that had been outdoors and crying at some point in the last twenty hours, and turned a page.

"Kettle's warm," she said.

"I'm not—"

"I know you're not," said Iuno. "It's warm anyway. That's how it works when somebody else does it."

Amy stood in the doorway.

"Spring," she said.

Iuno's pen stopped.

She kept her eyes on the page and her pen where it was, and left the word standing there on its own, which Amy understood, and was grateful for, and would think about at four in the morning for eleven days.

"I'll clear the afternoon," said Iuno.

---

Mei was on the floor of the room at the end of the corridor with her back against the bed and a controller in her hands and the game paused on a mission select screen it had been paused on for twelve years and change, and she had made no move toward the final quest, because there was a rule about that, and the rule was binding.

"Hey," said Amy.

"Hey. You're cold."

"I've been up a mountain."

"I know," said Mei. "You left a note. It said *gone up the mountain,* which is a very good note and I've put it on the fridge."

Amy sat down on the floor next to her mother, which is where this family conducted its business, and leaned into her shoulder, and Mei took it the way she took everything, both hands, open-faced, and put an arm round her, and asked nothing.

The window had a glass on the sill with a camellia in it that had been standing in the same water for two months and had never dropped a petal.

"She's gone," Amy said.

"Mm."

"She'll be back in spring."

"That's nice," said Mei, and there was a pause with a great deal inside it, and Amy — who had been reading that particular pause since she was six — sat in it and let it go by.

"Ma."

"Mm."

"Don't worry about me—"

She heard it come out. She heard the exact sentence, in the exact voice, in the exact shape it had been when it walked out of a blue door with a coat on, and she stopped it in the middle of the air with the whole weight of eleven weeks of a household yelling at her about it, and Mei had gone completely still beside her.

"— actually," said Amy. "Worry a bit."

Her mother put the controller down and let a silence go by that lasted about four seconds, which from her was an enormous amount of restraint and cost her visibly.

Then she shifted over four inches on the floor, and lifted her arm, and made a space, which was all the answer that had ever been available and had been the right one for two lifetimes.

"Not tonight," said Amy, into her shoulder.

"Okay."

"In spring. I'm going to need you to sit down for it, and I'm going to need you to not do the thing where you make it easier for me while I'm saying it."

"That's going to be hard."

"I *know* it's going to be hard—"

"I'll sit on my hands," said Mei.

"You'll *narrate* sitting on your hands—"

"I'll narrate it *once*," said Mei, "and then I'll shut up, and Iuno can hold the clock, and I'd like it noted that I'm agreeing to a thing I don't have the details of, which I'm told is called trust, and I'm told I'm bad at it."

Amy laughed. It came out wet and it came out real, and her mother's arm tightened, and downstairs somebody dropped a pan and four people shouted about it at once.

Later, when the house had gone down and the corridor light was off, Amy got up and stood at the sill in the dark for a while with a house key on a string in her fist.

Then she went and filled a jug and came back and poured a little water into a glass on the windowsill, carefully, up to the mark, the way you do.

Behind her, from the dark, entirely awake:

"It doesn't need that."

"I know," said Amy.

She did it anyway.

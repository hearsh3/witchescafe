Chán Juān

Hsin heard the old lime-burner’s wooden clogs scrape on the plaza cobbles long before he reached the bench.

He was the last in the morning queue. The winter sky hung low and grey over the gorge, smelling of wet slate, and only four people had come out to the well after sunrise. The old man stopped across the cabbage-seller’s trestle with his hands tucked into his sleeves and gave his younger brother’s name: Gao Shun, a ditch-digger on the north wall who had kept a peeled chestnut in his cheek on night watch so he wouldn’t fall asleep standing up.

Hsin dipped her brush and wrote the characters down in the red-ruled column. Her fingers were stiff with the damp, and the ink took a long time to sink into the sizing, though her hand stayed steady enough to keep the strokes inside the lines.

While the old man waited for the ink to dull, Hsin noticed where her own ears were pointed. Both of them were angled forward toward the lime-burner’s wheezing chest. All morning she had not turned an ear back once to check the upturned butter tub behind the well-curb, or to listen for where Suoming’s hands were. At City Hall, the last person to stand at her Ma’s shoulder had been an Intendant with a knife in the sleeve, and a smell of rotten pear she had never been able to put a name to.

The lime-burner bowed to the bench and shuffled off toward the lower arcade.

A dark spot the size of a copper coin appeared on the blank paper beside the wet column. Another struck the porcelain brush-rest and ran down the glaze.

Hsin’s left hand twitched upward toward a casement latch that wasn’t there—shut the window before the paper cockled, before the smell of wet street stone got into the rug and ruined Ma’s afternoon. She caught her fingers halfway off the wood and put them back down.

Behind her, the staves of the butter tub creaked. Hsin glanced over her shoulder. Suoming hadn’t reached for Nine Shadows yet; she sat with her boots dangling clear of the cobbles and her face tipped up toward the open sky, her light-sore eyes wide in the grey gloom as the first cold drops hit her cheekbones.

Hsin pulled the cover of the ledger shut before the red ruling could bleed. As she reached for the flat river stone to weight it, the stiff corner of the morning’s letter poked against the inside of her right wristbone where she had tucked it into her cuff. It had come up the south road with the first post-cart from Mengzhou, written in a clerk’s careful, square hand from City Hall, asking when the Sentinel would come down the mountain to receive the Magistrates before the year turned. For two hundred winters every packet from the plain had carried the Intendant’s seal on the wrapper. This one had her name on the front, and she hadn’t written an answer.

Across the square, the noodle-sellers were already pulling tarpaulins over their firewood. The morning queue was gone, the third-terrace latch sat tight in its mended housing down the ditch, and the noon bell was still an hour off. Hsin looked at her ink-stained thumbs on the closed book. She had no idea what people did with an hour when nothing was broken.

“What do people do when it rains?” Hsin asked.

Suoming lowered her chin. She picked up her cold clay cup from the well-curb and set it upside down on the stone.

“Stay in,” Suoming said. “Or walk.”

“The salt-house smells of wet wool.”

“Then walk.”

Suoming braced her hands on the bamboo shaft of Nine Shadows, pushed herself off the tub, and popped the canopy open overhead. The gunshot crack sounded flatter than usual in the damp air. Hsin rose from the bench, her ears brushing the level of the iron pulley-frame, and looked down at the round oil-paper roof. It came to her ribs. If she walked down the terraces under that, half her coat would be soaked through before they passed the bathhouse. She reached for the ledger to tuck it under her arm so she could fold down—and stopped. A fox couldn’t carry a folio book without leaving tooth-marks in the paper.

Suoming held out her left hand, palm up. “Give it here.”

Hsin laid the ledger in her palm. Suoming undid the top two frogs of her faded plum coat and slid the book inside against her ribs, right where the black lacquer of the Lock sat, then buttoned the wool back over it with her thumb. Hsin watched the coat settle flat over both of them. Then she let her shoulders drop, folded down into four skinny legs and a dark-tipped tail, and stepped in under the oil-paper beside the iron-plated boots.

***

Suoming kept to the wall side of the stair where the flagstones were less worn, holding Nine Shadows steady against the rain.

It came down straight, without enough wind off the gorge to slant it. Across the lower roofs and the cut, Xuanfang Hold had gone the colour of watered ink soaking into cheap paper, slate tiles and wet limestone smudging into one grey wash until the far cliff was only a darker stain in the mist. Nothing in the lanes had kept any colour except the dye-works gutter along the middle terrace, which ran bright madder red from yesterday’s vats, and the narrow streak of gold trotting beside her left boot.

For once, holding the umbrella at her own height covered both of them. Her left shoulder was dry under the oiled ribs. She shifted her hand an inch down the bamboo grip and kept her pace unhurried on the wet treads.

Inside her coat, the stiff cardboard corner of the ledger tapped once against the upper rim of the Providence Lock with every step down.

At the turn of the stair her breath caught low on the left side, a dry scrape against the bottom rib like an iron bar on a worn staple. She didn’t slow her boots, and by the bottom landing it let go.

Beside her ankle, the fox’s ears swivelled toward the gurgle of a downpipe in the wall. The creature couldn’t leave a mechanism alone for ten minutes. A faint hum went through the wet cobbles at Suoming’s feet, and two smaller foxes peeled smoothly off Hsin’s flank, shook their ears once, and trotted out from under the umbrella—one splashing down the madder-red ditch toward the third terrace, the other ducking under a water-cart along the second-terrace kerb.

Suoming walked on without looking after them. A Sentinel who had to listen to every pipe in her town didn’t know how to set a watch, and one who split her own hide to do it had too much time on her hands.

At the mouth of the cooper’s lane, the second small fox came pattering back through the puddles and folded into Hsin’s ribs with a faint snap like winter static in wool.

Hsin stopped in the rain outside an old widow’s stoop. The iron drain-grate in the gutter there was choked with sodden red paper and split bamboo from a discarded festival lantern, and grey water had backed up over the kerb, lapping half an inch deep against the bottom of the woman’s door-sill.

Suoming stepped off the flagstone into the puddle. She planted the steel ferrule of Nine Shadows in the mash of wet paper, levered the tangled bamboo ribs up out of the bars, and kicked the clot clear of the grate with the iron heel of her boot.

The backed-up water sucked down into the dark pipe with a hollow rattle. Within three breaths the puddle had dropped below the stone step, leaving a ring of red pulp on the iron. Suoming took her boot off the grate and walked on down the lane.

***

The rain turned heavy at the bottom of the cooper’s alley, drumming loud enough on the oil-paper overhead to flatten Hsin’s ears against her skull.

Cold water was bouncing up off the cobbles now, soaking her belly fur and the dark tip of her ninth tail despite the canopy. When they came level with the open timber doors of Master Shao’s print shop in the narrow lane behind the cooper’s shed, Hsin trotted three paces ahead and ducked inside over the dry sill.

The room smelled of pine soot, boiled linseed oil, and damp paper. At the back, under a smoky tallow dip, old Shao was leaning over his press-stone with an ink-ball in each hand, too deaf in the rain-noise to turn around. Near the doorway, a boy of nine or ten stood on a low wooden box in front of a slanted type-case, an open galley tray propped beside his elbow.

Hsin planted all four paws on the floorboards and gave herself a hard, thorough shake from nose to tail-tip.

A spray of dirty ditch-water flew across the counter and spattered a stack of fresh proof-sheets. The boy flinched back, snatching his sleeve out of the wet, and when he blinked down at the floor again Hsin was standing in her own shape, stooping slightly to keep her ears clear of the drying-lines strung between the rafters.

The boy’s mouth fell open. His ink-blackened thumb froze over the galley tray.

“Go on with your sorting,” Hsin said, smoothing her wet skirts down with both hands. “Your master charges by the candle-inch after noon.”

She leaned one elbow on the high lip of the case and looked down into the wooden tray. The furniture wedges had been knocked out, and the boy was picking up the damp lead sorts four or five at a time, dropping each character back into its little square compartment in the case. Hsin read the backward metal upside down without effort: *The third storehouse millet stays as it is. The south cut opens at the first bell.*

Her own orders from the well-head, coming apart in a child’s greasy fingers so the lead could say something about turnip prices or mule-hire tomorrow morning. Seen from above, the rows of dark, square compartments looked remarkably like the small red blocks she fought with.

“Everything one says at sunrise,” Hsin began, her voice taking on its courtly, melodious lift, “comes back by noon to a box of little square—”

“It’s type,” Suoming said from the doorway, where she was giving Nine Shadows a single shake into the street gutter before propping it against the jamb.

Hsin’s ears dropped half an inch. She shut her mouth and looked up at the damp sheets hanging on the twine overhead instead.

The boy’s elbow bumped the corner of the galley tray. A single lead sort hopped over the wooden rim, struck the edge of his stool, and rolled across the floorboards with a dull chink to stop against Suoming’s iron toe-plate.

Hsin waited for the boy to climb down for it. Instead, Suoming bent at the knee, keeping her bound right forearm level across her waist, and picked the small block of lead up with her left hand.

While she had stood watching by the doorpost, the rasp at the bottom of Suoming’s left ribs had been loud enough to reach Hsin’s ears under the drumming roof-tiles, tight and dry like an ungreased pawl catching on a ratchet. As she stooped for the sort and straightened up again, the catch eased out into a slow, even breath.

Hsin’s eyes narrowed a fraction. She watched Suoming squint at the reversed face of the metal in the grey doorway light, run her gaze along the pasted paper labels on the lower case, and drop the character—*road*—into its proper box.

Hsin filed that away where she kept the tolerances of the west-ring valves.

***

Suoming chose the noodle stall at the corner of the rope-makers’ lane because the bench was dry and nobody else was sitting on it.

A strip of patched canvas hung from the shop-front over the street, sagging in the middle with rainwater and dripping a steady grey thread into a wooden bucket at the kerb. The woman behind the cauldron was the mason’s sister-in-law who had stood in the plaza queue on the first afternoon to give a name for the red book. Suoming remembered the healed lime-burn across the back of her thumb.

When Hsin ducked under the awning and sat down on the inside half of the bench, the woman looked at her once, ladled two bowls of clear mutton broth over wheat noodles, and set them on the scrubbed plank without a word.

Hsin took two copper coins from her belt-pouch and laid them beside the wet chopsticks. The woman swept the coins into her apron pocket, went back to her earthenware jars by the stove, and returned with a small saucer of pickled radish in chili brine, which she put down directly in front of Suoming’s elbow.

Suoming slid the saucer two inches toward the middle of the table and pulled a pair of bamboo chopsticks from the cylinder. Somebody else had boiled the bones and skimmed the pot, and when she walked away she wouldn’t have to scour the grease off the iron.

Across the narrow table, Hsin’s fingers went to the hem of her right cuff to tuck it back out of the steam. Halfway to her wrist, her hand stopped. She left the ink-smudged cloth hanging loose around her knuckles and picked up her porcelain spoon instead.

On the other side of the lane, a travelling silversmith had pegged an oilcloth sheet between two shutter-hooks to keep the rain off his charcoal pan. An old woman in a patched wool hood stood under the edge of the eaves with a bundled infant asleep against her shoulder, watching the smith’s hands. Clamped in his wooden block sat a hollow silver lock the size of a walnut. His graver moved in short, bright pushes against the curve, cutting the strokes for *hundred years* into the soft metal.

Suoming watched a tiny curl of silver peel up ahead of the point. Her left hand rose to the back of her head, her fingers touching the cold spherical lock at the end of her hairpin. The metal was damp from the street air. She lowered her hand to the bench and took a mouthful of noodles.

Across the rising steam, Hsin was watching her.

“When you talk to them at night,” Hsin said quietly. “Before you take the pin out. What do you tell them?”

“What happened,” Suoming said. “Who ate what. Whether it rained.”

Hsin lifted a spoonful of mutton broth, blew on it twice with her ears angled back out of the vapour, and drank. “Do you tell them about me?”

“Sometimes.”

Hsin set the spoon down in her bowl. Her golden eyes fixed on Suoming’s face, bright and shamelessly expectant. “What do you say?”

Suoming picked up a slice of pickled radish with her chopsticks. “That you blow on your soup like a six-year-old.”

Hsin’s ears went flat against her hair. She looked down at her bowl, took up her chopsticks, and ate a large mouthful of noodles straight from the hot broth, wincing slightly when the scalding liquid touched her lip.

They ate in silence until both bowls were empty. Suoming drank the last of her warm pot-water and set the clay cup upside down on the wet plank.

***

Halfway up the steps toward the rope-walk, Hsin smelled wet straw and cold earth over the rain.

The downpour had thickened into solid grey sheets coming off the upper eaves, and Suoming wanted tea. The noodle-woman’s kettle had held roasted barley water, which Suoming had looked into once and left alone; the only tin of plain white tea in Xuanfang Hold sat in the corner of the two rooms over the rope-walk that she had lent to Mingxiao’s masons.

On the narrow stoop of a shuttered cobbler’s house beside the landing, a cracked earthenware pot stood just inside the drip-line. Three bare grey canes of a tree peony rose out of the black dirt, wrapped tight in dry straw and bound round and round with faded red string to keep the gorge frost out of the pith until spring.

Hsin slowed her boots on the wet step. Her fingers twitched against her wet hem. It would take less effort than striking a flint to draw a gold thread up through the frozen root-ball and open a heavy pink flower at the top of the cane, one that wouldn’t bruise in the rain or drop a petal before the third month.

Behind her, the steel ferrule clicked once on the granite tread. Hsin took her eyes off the red string and kept climbing. The cobbler could wait for his own spring.

At the top of the covered gallery stood Suoming’s grey wooden door. Hsin dropped into fox shape on the wet boards, flattened her ribs, and wriggled through the warped two-inch gap at the bottom where the sill had worn away.

Inside, she shook the rain out of her fur onto a canvas lime-sack and stood up into her own shape just as two flat knuckles rapped against the outside of the door.
“It’s open,” Hsin called out without thinking.

Out in the gallery, nothing moved for three breaths except the rain ticking into the lead gutter. Then the iron latch lifted, a boot-toe caught the bottom rail to lift the sticking hinge clear of the floor, and Suoming shoved the door open.

The two rooms were low-ceiled and smelled of wet mortar and cold tallow. Mingxiao’s crew were all out working on the Sanctum steps in the rain, but they had left ten grey bedrolls laid side by side across the boards, a bucket of brass plumb-bobs under the window, and chalked timber marks all over the west wall.

Hsin had to stoop under the main pine tie-beam to keep her ears out of the rafters. She looked at the cramped space, the draught coming under the sill, the grey light through the single oiled-paper pane. She could have pushed the back wall out ten paces into a fold and hung a dry cedar roof over their heads before the kettle warmed, and the small woman standing on the threshold would have told her to put it down before she unstrapped her umbrella.

Hsin left the walls where they were and sat down on the end of a rolled mattress with her knees drawn up.

In the far corner, tucked neatly behind a mason’s saw-horse, sat a small earthenware brazier, an iron kettle, a square pewter tin, and a single clay cup. Suoming knelt by the brazier, poked the banked ash until a red coal showed, and set the kettle over the grate. When the water boiled, she warmed the single cup, dropped a pinch of pale dry leaves into it, and handed it up to Hsin. For herself, she took a chipped celadon rice-bowl off the nearest Celestial bedroll, rinsed it with hot water into the ash-pan, and poured the rest in.

Hsin held the warm clay between both palms. Before she drank, she reached inside her right cuff, drew out the folded sheet of Mengzhou paper, and held it out across the brazier.

***

Suoming set her borrowed bowl down on the floorboards and took the letter from Hsin with her dry left hand.

The thick paper had gone soft along the fold from a morning in the fox’s cuff, and a smear of plaza ink had come off on the margin. She opened it toward the grey light of the window.

It was ordinary Secretariat work, written in the small, square clerk’s hand they taught in the lower annex: the district Magistrates had brought in their winter grain-rolls, and City Hall waited upon the Sentinel’s pleasure to name a day before the year-end seal-closing to receive them in person.

At the bottom, one column to the left of the date, ran the eight-character closing formula: *May the north wind spare the eaves.*

Suoming had written that line herself. The clerks had copied it ever since.

She folded the sheet back along its creases and held it out over the charcoal pan.

“It’s addressed to you,” Suoming said.

Hsin took the paper back, though she left it lying in her lap instead of tucking it into her sleeve. Her ears came forward, and she drew a quick breath as if she meant to argue a term.

“Inten—” Hsin began, and stopped dead. Her ears angled back half an inch. “Suoming. This tea tastes of hot water.”

“It’s tea,” Suoming said. “Not soup.”

She picked up the mason’s rice-bowl and drank while it was hot. When she was done, she set the bowl down on the floorboards beside the brazier, right way up.

Outside, the rain settled into a steady, heavy drumming on the low tiles directly overhead, loud enough to fill both rooms. Hsin finished her cup, set it beside the pewter tin, and folded down onto the boards near the ash-pan. She curled her skinny body into a tight gold ring against the warmth and pulled the dark-tipped ninth tail across her nose until only her ears showed above the fur. Within five minutes her flank was rising and falling in a slow, even rhythm.

Suoming stayed where she was on the low stool in the corner, her back against the plaster wall and Nine Shadows upright between her knees, her boots hanging an inch clear of the boards.

The rain on the rafters sounded the way it did on low grey tiles from the kiln across the ferry, the thick ones with a thumb-mark pressed into the lip so the runoff went straight into the water-butt instead of the cabbages.

Nobody was waiting outside in the gallery. The morning queue was done, the ledger was dry against her ribs, and the third-terrace latch was holding on its own pin. In the close heat of the charcoal, the iron scrape at the bottom of her left lung quieted down. Her breath went shallow and very slow, dropping away beneath her breastbone like a stone let go into deep well-water, where the light didn’t reach and nothing needed lifting.

Her eyelids drooped against the grey window.

On the floorboards beside the kettle, the Mengzhou letter was curling up at its damp edge. Suoming leaned forward from the stool, picked the paper up, and laid it flat on a spare brick near the brazier so the heat would take the crease out.

*May the north wind spare the eaves.*

Her breath gave a sharp, dry hitch against the bottom rib, pulling cold air back into her chest. She sat upright on the stool again and undid the top frog of her coat, sliding her left hand inside past the ledger to rest her knuckles against the lacquer of the Lock.

“Rained before noon,” she said under her breath, barely louder than the cooling tick of the kettle. “The grate by the cooper’s lane was—”

By the brazier, the fox’s left ear pivoted round in its sleep, the dark tip angling straight toward her knees while the rest of her stayed curled under the tail.

Suoming stopped. She left her hand against the Lock for a breath, then drew it out and did the frog back up. The rest would keep until dark.

***

When Hsin opened her eyes by the brazier, the charcoal had burned down to white ash, and for a breath she did not know whether she was on the corner of the desk or the floor.

She kept her nose under her dark-tipped tail and breathed in, waiting for the smell of iris and cold rainwater on casement lead. Instead she got damp mason’s wool, tallow, and the dry grass scent of white tea leaves.

She had gone to sleep on a floor with her back turned to somebody in the room, and nothing had touched her.

Against the plaster wall, the iron heel-plates clicked once as Suoming dropped her boots to the boards.

“You slept through two bells,” Suoming said.

Above the rafters, the tiles were quiet. The rain had stopped. Hsin stretched her forelegs out, stood up into her own shape in one quick motion, and cracked the crown of her head squarely against the low pine tie-beam between her ears.

“…Ow.”

Suoming was already at the grey door with Nine Shadows tucked under her good arm and the coat buttoned over the ledger. Hsin rubbed the top of her head with one hand, picked up the dried letter from the brick to slide it into her cuff, and followed her out onto the gallery.

Down across the wet terraces, the Hold was coming indoors for the evening meal. One after another, yellow squares of light appeared behind paper windows along the dyers’ lane and the coopers’ steps—flint struck on steel by eighty different hands, oil wicks trimmed and lit in rooms she had never entered and had no conduit tied to.

On the wet shutter of the salt-seller’s house beside the stair, someone had pasted a red paper-cut window flower up early for New Year: a scissor-cut butterfly with open wings. The damp had got behind the flour-paste on the left side, and one crimson wing hung loose, curling away from the plank. Hsin knew what people said about butterflies. She paused on the step and pressed the damp red wing flat against the wood with the ball of her thumb, holding it for three breaths. As soon as she lifted her thumb, the paper curled slowly back out again. She left it and walked on.

When they came out into Huizhen Plaza, the black cloud-bank over the south cut tore open along its bottom edge. A cold, lopsided moon, three nights past the full, rode out into the gap, throwing hard silver light across the puddles and the bare cobbles where the cabbage-seller’s trestle had stood.

Hsin stopped by the stone well-curb and looked up at the pared-down disc. In a few weeks that same crooked lantern would hang over whatever road Mei’s household took, and over the roof where Yangyang would be reading her father’s letters. It would not reach the window at City Hall two hundred years ago, or the walkway where a dented harmonica had gone out mid-breath.

She leaned over the stone lip of the well and looked straight down the shaft. Thirty feet below, the black water held the moon in the middle of its ring, small, white, and unbroken.

Hsin took the wet hemp rope in both hands and let the wooden bucket drop.

It hit the bottom with a flat slap that shattered the white disc into a hundred jumping rings. She stood with her hands on the cold hemp while the ripples knocked against the stone lining and died down, piece by piece, until the pale round pulled itself back together in the dark water. Then she hauled the full bucket up hand over hand, hooked the bail on the iron notch, and dipped the gourd ladle in. She held the dripping handle out to Suoming.

***

Suoming took the gourd ladle from Hsin in her left hand and looked out across the wet plaza.

The flagstones around the well were dark with standing water, reflecting the torn white clouds over the cut, and the flat river stone they used to weight the ledger sat by itself on the cobbles where the bench had been.

She drank the well-water down in three swallows. It was cold enough to ache behind her front teeth and tasted of nothing at all except wet limestone. She handed the empty gourd back and hooked her thumb into the leather strap of Nine Shadows, rolling the damp oil-paper tight around the bamboo ribs and fastening the toggle. There was no sun overhead to hurt her eyes, and the rain had gone down the gorge.

Hsin set the ladle back in the bucket and touched the outside of her right cuff where the Mengzhou paper sat.

“I’ll borrow the Watcher’s ink-stone at the salt-house tonight,” Hsin said, her voice plain in the cold air. “I’ll write the answer myself.”

“Put the day of the month at the top,” Suoming said. “Don’t use the court seal. If you stamp it in red, the Secretariat will spend a week arguing over which box to file it in before they read it.”

“All right.”

Suoming turned from the well-curb and started up the west lane toward the salt-house kang, her iron heel-plates clicking cleanly on the drying stone. Hsin fell in beside her without folding down into fox shape.

The lopsided moon hung directly behind their shoulders over the gorge gap, throwing their shadows long and black ahead of them up the wet incline: Suoming’s short, square silhouette with the furled umbrella slanted across her coat, and right beside it the tall shadow with nine long tails fanning out over the cobbles, moving in time with her boots.

Beside her, Hsin was walking with her chin tilted up and her ears angled back, watching the moon ride through the torn cloud. Suoming kept her eyes on the flagstones six paces ahead.

Halfway up the slope, her breath caught low on the left side, a dry rasp against the bottom rib. She didn’t stop walking to wait for it to pass.

“Mind the step,” she said. “The mortar’s loose on the left.”

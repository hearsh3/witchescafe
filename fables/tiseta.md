# Útiseta

*a oneshot of the Banat al-Rih*

---

*]sit on the stone where the lane gives out[*
*]count the roofs while the tallow cools[*
*]nobody knocks on a shuttered house[*

---

The flue in the third Solistree drew crooked whenever the wind backed into the north, which in January was four mornings out of five.

At twenty minutes past seven it would give a soft, asthmatic cough behind the chimney-breast and send a thin blue ribbon of birch-smoke curling out under the mantelpiece to hang among the pale ceiling-roots at the exact pitch of F-sharp above middle C. Phrolova knew the pitch because she had been listening to the house for three weeks the way a tuner listens to a damp harpsichord, waiting for the pin-block to slip.

She was not getting used to it. Getting used to a room meant letting your shoulders drop half an inch when the latch clicked behind you, and Phrolova had not dropped her shoulders since the crater cooled. What was happening—and she observed it with cold, clinical irritation—was that her knees had learned which floorboard outside the buttery creaked before her eyes were open in the morning, and her right hand had begun reaching for the salt-cellar two inches to the left of the butter-dish without her looking down from her plate. The body was a vulgar, opportunistic animal; give it three weeks of hot soup at regular hours and it would start treating a trap like an address.

Everything in the house was slightly wrong, and nobody in the house had the decency to hide the wires.

By the coal-scuttle sat Viviane, one booted ankle propped on the fender, re-pinning the brass hinge of a storm lantern with a greasy awl while wearing Phrolova's dark Ragunnan kidskin gloves. There was a shiny smear of black axle-tallow across the leather of the left index finger—leather Phrolova had kept spotless through the fall of Rinascita—and every time the mechanical joints inside flexed against the seam, the little steel ratchets went *whirr-tick* by the fire.

Ten feet overhead, slung in her crimson silk ropes where the great pale roots arched across the rafters, Black Swan hung upside down by one knee, her dark hair trailing an inch above the tallow-dips on the sideboard. She was dealing her tarnished-gilt cards onto the flat of a crossbeam, upside down, sliding each drawn card back into the middle of the pack with two long fingers without ever tilting her chin to read the face.

In the center of the oak table sat the wooden trencher of purple figs. It was deep winter outside—the snow drifted four feet up the bleached boles of the Solistrees, so dry and cold it squeaked like powdered glass under a boot—and every morning Elaina set out six fresh figs split with sugar, warm enough to fog the pewter salt-cellar beside them, as if she had just stepped in from a south wall in August. There was no fig tree within six hundred miles. Phrolova had checked the woodshed, the pantry, and the cellar bins on the fourth day, found nothing but salt herrings and winter turnips, and declined to ask. Asking was how you signed the receipt.

And at the far end of the hall, where the narrow stone steps wound down into the limestone dark beneath the roots, Alice's iron-gall nib went *scritch, scritch, rest* against her folio. Twice a day the girl came up the stair, warmed her ink-blue fingers around the belly of the iron kettle for three minutes without speaking, gave Phrolova the small, harried nod of a clerk passing another clerk in an archive, and went back down to the black water where the landlady lay asleep.

None of them ever asked Phrolova where she had been before the snow.

That was the part that kept her spine locked against the chair-back through supper. In four centuries of dealing with the living, Phrolova had learned that people who took you in always presented the bill by the third Friday. Even the Fractsidus, who prided themselves on professional indifference, had wanted her score-sheets on the table. Here, nobody mentioned the coast, or the rift, or the dead in the water. When they spoke to her at all, they asked about Stellavento, and they asked as if they were inquiring after a cousin's orchard in the next parish.

"Does the rain actually reach terminal velocity," Herta demanded one Tuesday over the pickled herring, her enormous black hat tilted back so she could squint at Phrolova down her nose, "or is it just wet atmosphere descending at an arbitrary bar-rate? And what's the nanometer reading on the noon sky? From the ridge it looked like four-ninety, which is too green for that latitude."

"I didn't measure it," Phrolova said.

Two nights later Viviane looked up from paring a tallow plug with her pocket-knife. "When you pitched the vault over the church," she said, entirely conversational, one engineer talking shop with another across a tavern bench, "did you anchor the outer load to the bedrock frequencies, or hang it off the spire like a bell-tent? A forty-fathom basin's a lot of lateral shear if the marl's wet."

"The bedrock," Phrolova said.

Elaina wanted to know whether Triss browned the mutton neck in suet before the pearl barley went into the Thursday crock, or if she put the meat in raw and skimmed the grey off at the boil. Black Swan, swinging down from the rafters by one wrist to reach the marmalade, asked lazily whether old Master Bello still rapped the children under the right elbow with his bow-stick when they let their wrists sag in the third position.

"Only the violas," Phrolova said, before her tongue caught the bar.

She stared at her teacup for three beats while Black Swan buttered a crust. *How do you know his name is Bello? I never spoke his name to any of you, and he wasn't in the square when you came to the theatre.*

She did not ask. At four o'clock that afternoon, walking silently past the library stacks in her stockinged feet to fetch her rosin, she caught Herta crouched behind a rampart of folios on maritime law, slipping three dried smelts out of her velvet cuff onto a chipped saucer for a battle-scarred ginger tom with half an ear. Herta had one hand planted flat on an open bestiary, staring fiercely at the Latin text while her boot-toe nudged the saucer half an inch closer to the cat's nose, so that if anyone accused her of feeding strays she could claim the fish had fallen out of an index.

Phrolova backed out of the aisle without making a board creak. It was impossible to locate the downbeat in a house where the provost hid smelts in her sleeve.

Every evening at dusk she went up the ladder to the small square room in the fork of the pale roots and shut the oak door. For sixty minutes nobody knocked. She sat straight-backed on the deal chair by the narrow casement, her bare hands folded in her lap, and let her hearing go out across three hundred miles of northern forest and inward through the unmapped seam to Stellavento.

She listened to the bakehouse bell strike six. She listened to the scrape of the long pine benches on the flagstones as the village sat down to supper, and the small, dry *chink* of Luc's brass scissors trimming the lamp-wick in her front parlor.

And every night at eleven, before she blew out the candle, she still went down on her knees by the brass-bound trunk and counted her things. Sixteen folios, two cakes of amber rosin, the bone fork, the Guarneri, the Spargayne ticket stub, the contralto's nicked silver badge. Everything was always there. Nothing was ever touched, and nothing was ever missing, and she hated them for it a little more every morning. A thief who takes nothing is still standing in the dark behind your chair.

---

On the fourth Thursday Herta left the household book open on the table.

It was past ten. The others had gone up or down—Elaina to her eaves-room with a hot brick, Black Swan to whatever perch she kept in the high branches when the moon was clear—and Herta and Viviane had fallen into a sharp, technical dispute over whether split larch burned thirty percent faster than seasoned ash when the flue was drawing north. At quarter past ten Herta had snatched up the horn lantern and a carpenter's rule and marched Viviane out to the woodshed in the snow to measure the cord-stack by hand.

Phrolova came down from her room to fill her ewer at the kettle.

On the scrubbed oak table, weighted open with a pewter inkstand and a brass astrolabe so the vellum wouldn't curl in the heat of the turf-fire, lay the book.

It was as thick as a lectern Bible and had been rebound at least four times; the current cover was scarred brown calf over oak boards, the spine broken down the middle and stitched back together with tarred sail-twine in Viviane's square rigging knots.

Phrolova stopped with the iron ewer in her hand. Out past the back door, muffled by three feet of drift and the woodshed wall, Herta's high, indignant voice was lecturing the night air about specific gravity. Below the floorboards, at the bottom of the spiral stair, Alice's quill went *scritch, scritch, rest*.

Phrolova set the ewer down on the hearth-stone, walked to the table, and looked at the open page.

She told herself she was only checking the entry from four weeks ago to see how Herta had written her down—whether the girl had added some insolent taxonomy in the margin beside her hour.

She turned back four leaves. The paper here was good Ragunnan foolscap, ruled in red lead. Halfway down the Friday column, in Herta's cramped, forward-leaning violet ink, stood the line:

*Hour after dusk — P. Door shut. (No scales past 10.)*

Nothing else. Between a memorandum about three firkins of salt butter and a complaint that someone had used the astrolabe to crack walnuts, she was reduced to an initial and a noise regulation.

Phrolova's bare thumb stayed on the lower corner of the leaf.

The house was very still. The turf settled in the grate with a soft, powdery sigh.

She turned twenty pages back. Fifty. A hundred. The milled foolscap gave way to heavy, unbleached linen rag that smelled of bog-water and old tallow, and then to scraped sheepskin vellum yellow as mutton fat.

Two-thirds of the way toward the front board, a thick quire of dark buff paper had been sewn directly into the headband with red hemp thread. There were no butter tallies here, and no firewood accounts. At the top of the first recto, in a hand so old the iron-gall had eaten small brown lace-holes through the downstrokes, stood a single word:

*South.*

Phrolova looked down the page.

There were hundreds of lines, packed thirty to a leaf in five distinct hands, running down the centuries without a break.

The earliest were written in a long, looping violet script that slanted backward against the grain of the paper, without dates, as if the writer measured time only by what was playing:

*No lamp. Rain on the conservatory tiles. She took the cadenza dry.*
*No lamp. The stone at the bottom of the lane is set. Sky pitched to C-sharp minor, sixty to the minute. Nobody comes out past the well.*
*No lamp. Loop reset at the twelfth minute past six. The baker dropped the peel twice.*

Below that, the vellum was smudged with wet thumbprints, and the hand changed to the square, heavy block-print of someone writing with a carpenter's lead on her knee in the dark:

*14 Nov. No lamp. Checked the lower intake. Forty fathoms, fresh over marl, no tide. The pilings are good larch. She's in the parlor with the shutter barred.*
*No lamp. Loop reset at the twelfth minute past six.*
*3 Feb. No lamp. Someone playing in the theatre, alone. Third movement too slow.*

Phrolova turned the leaf. Her fingers made no sound on the rag.

Another century of *No lamp*. Then Herta's three colors of ink arrived—black for the date, red for the weather, violet for her own scholarly irritation—cramped and furious down the margin:

*19 Oct. No lamp. Sky the wrong colour (630 nm, flat, no scattering). The children by the trough have been seven for eighty years. It is methodologically indefensible.*
*No lamp. Loop reset at the twelfth minute past six.*
*No lamp. She hasn't come out since the snow.*

Phrolova turned to the last leaf of the quire. Near the bottom, the entries were entered in Alice's neat, upright Guild copperplate, copied cleanly from someone else's wet cuffs at the stairhead:

*8 Aug. Per V.: No lamp. Badges in the water. Twenty-one on the shelf.*
*11 Aug. Per B.S.: No lamp. Blue sky.*

And beneath that, where the ink was not two months dry, Herta's violet nib had dug hard enough into the fiber to split the point:

*15 Nov. Lamp lit.*

Behind the period sat a small, furious square of violet cross-hatching where Herta had written an exclamation mark, thought better of it, and scratched it out to the bone of the paper.

Underneath Herta's blot, on the last ruled line of the page, was a round, cheerful schoolgirl hand that dotted its *i* with a little circle:

*14 Dec. Went down with figs. Stayed on the step. The stew has marrow-bone in it.*

Phrolova's hand did not tremble on the page. Four hundred years of the baton had cured her hands of trembling. Instead, all the small noises of the room—the crackle of the turf, the whistle of the north wind in the crooked flue, the scratch of the pen under the cellar floor—dropped away at once, leaving a high, completely flat ringing in her left ear, like a tuning fork struck against iron in a vacuum.

She looked back up the column.

*No lamp. No lamp. No lamp. Loop reset at the twelfth minute past six.*

Four hundred years. Twenty thousand Tuesdays.

While she had been sitting inside her parlor with the velvet curtains drawn, thinking she was the last conscious thing in a sealed box at the bottom of the world; while she had been scraping burrs off her bridge at ten at night in an empty theatre; while she had been lying face-down on the hearth-rug in the second century of the loop, so sick of her own echoes that she had held her breath until her ribs spasmed—someone had been sitting outside on the boundary stone at the bottom of the lane.

Taking turns in the dark. Checking her pilings. Timing her third movement. Writing her down between the salt herrings and the firewood like a frost-hollow at the foot of the garden.

Her eye snagged on a line near the foot of the last leaf but one, in the old backward-sloping violet hand. There was a wider space above it, as if the pen had waited out a long evening before touching the vellum:

*No lamp. A visitor on the step at dusk. She has bolted the door from the inside.*

The floor went out from under Phrolova's boots.

Her first thought—automatic, razor-edged, rising into her throat like bile—was the Grand Architect.

The Architect had walked Stellavento for three days before that Thursday. She had stood invisible by the well, learning the knot of Triss's apron, measuring the split beech by the bakehouse door, finding the exact width of the crack in Phrolova's ribs before she lifted the knocker.

Mechanically, with the cold terror of an animal checking the bars of a cage, Phrolova laid the two records side by side in her head.

The comparison did not hold.

The Architect studied a house to find which hinge would give under the iron. Three days of watching, one knock in a dead girl's clothes, and by midnight the ink was dry on the charter. These women had sat in the ditch outside her boundary stone for four hundred years. They had watched the stranger walk down her lane wearing Triss's face; they had sat out in the dark through the five hours between the threshold and the clock; they had watched the Architect walk back out at midnight and leave the windows black.

And they had not come in. Not then, not the winter after, not in all the years of reset Tuesdays since, until the evening Phrolova herself struck a sulfur match and set the lamp in the front pane.

It was no comfort at all. It made the skin across her shoulder-blades contract until it ached.

Outside in the passage, boots stamped hard against the threshold-beam, knocking off cake-snow.

"—which proves my figure within two-tenths of a cord," Herta was saying brightly as her hand touched the outer latch, "even allowing for the sap in the—"

Phrolova flipped the heavy pages forward to the page Herta had left open, set the brass astrolabe back on the exact crease it had occupied, picked up her iron ewer from the hearth-stone without spilling a drop of water, and was three rungs up the attic ladder before the kitchen door swung wide.

*Do not build your wall too high,*
*Do not roof out all the sky;*
*Where the dead keep house alone,*
*Someone's sitting on the stone.*

---

She did not pack her trunk, and she did not take her violin case. Packing a trunk was an admission that you had moved in, and carrying luggage down a ladder at midnight was for servants leaving without their character.

She stood in the center of the small dark room under the roots, lifted her bare right hand to the level of her collarbone, and hooked two fingers into the air at the pitch of A-flat below the staff.

She tore the seam open the old way—no carriage, no road through the pines, no knocking on bark—just a vertical rip of red-black dissonance six feet high that smelled of scorched copper and pond-silt. She stepped through onto the cobbles of her own hill and let the air snap shut behind her heel like a sprung iron jaw.

It was past midnight in Stellavento, and the frost was down.

The air here was softer than the northern forest—wet slate, dead fuchsia leaves, and the faint, sweet reek of banked beech-ash from the bakehouse—and above the steep roofs the sky was a clear, deep indigo pricked with winter stars that had never been in her score.

Halfway up the lane, in the front casement of her house, the oil lamp was burning.

Phrolova did not go to the house first. She turned on her heel and walked down the stone steps to the dark water at the bottom of the village.

The forty-fathom mirror lay flat and black under the starlight, the waxen fuchsia petals still floating along the pilings. On the silvered pine boards of the jetty, the tarnished silver Fractsidus badges glinted in neat ranks through a fur of white rime.

She counted them from the top step, by fives.

Three rows of five, and two at the end. Seventeen.

Three more dark, wet ovals stained the frost at the tail of the fourth row, where badges had lain when she climbed into Granny Leah's carriage four weeks ago.

She turned and walked back up the steps into the square.

The village was not asleep. Or rather, the village had discovered that when five o'clock does not loop back to four, nine o'clock eventually becomes midnight, and someone has to bank the fires. Under the bare winter branches of the fuchsia pergola, someone had rigged a waxed canvas tarpaulin to keep the frost off the long table and dragged the heavy iron brazier out of the washhouse to stand between the benches, heaped with red beech-coals.

Around the board sat Triss, Granny Leah with her wool shawl pinned tight under her chin, little Timo asleep with his cheek squashed flat against the scrubbed deal, and four people in the heavy, high-collared black wool of the Fractsidus lower choirs.

Luc sat at the corner nearest the bread-crock. Beside him sat a broad-shouldered bass from the Second Choir whose ribs had gone under the falling masonry by the outer gate; a thin girl no older than Elaina with her wet dark braid hanging over her shoulder and the Seventh's broken octagon pinned crookedly above a rent in her lapel; and the grey-stubbled quartermaster from the Ragunna supply barge who used to sign for the resin and tallow.

All three of the newcomers were still sodden from the forty-fathom shelf. Dark rings of sea-water had spread across the table-plank around their forearms, and under the bench the warm bricks around the brazier hissed softly every few seconds as the hems of their coats let go another drop: *tick, hiss, rest, tick.*

They were eating Thursday stew out of Triss's blue-glazed bowls, and Luc was showing the thin girl how to fold a square of grey flannel three times around a hot floor-brick so it wouldn't scorch the ticking of a mattress.

Phrolova stepped out of the shadow of the church wall into the orange wash of the brazier.

The bench scraped back across the cobbles like a saw through bone. All three of the newer dead were on their feet at once—wet boots coming together with a heavy, waterlogged slap, chins jerked up, right hands flying to their breastbones in the lower-terrace salute.

"Overseer—" the old quartermaster began, his voice rattling with salt-silt at the back of the glottis.

Luc did not stand up. He kept the horn-handled bread-knife flat against the loaf and looked up at them from under his damp forelock.

"Miss," Luc said mildly. "We say Miss up here. Sit down, Bren, you're dripping in the salt-dish."

The three of them froze for half a beat, looking from Luc's steady thumb on the crust to Phrolova's bare, empty hands hanging at her sides. Slowly, without taking their eyes off her face, they sank back onto the bench. The thin girl tucked her wet cuffs under the table-edge so they wouldn't show. They were terrified of her—she could hear their frequencies bunched tight and sharp under their collarbones, ready to bolt if she lifted her right wrist an inch—and they were trying with everything they had not to spoil the baker-woman's supper.

Triss turned round from the brazier with the iron ladle in her fist, looked Phrolova over from her unpinned hair to her snow-wet hem without a flicker of surprise, and reached into the warm willow skep for a bowl.

"Well, you've come back without your coat, just like your mother always said you would," Triss remarked briskly, dipping the ladle deep into the stoneware crock. "The boys had the marrow-bones at eight, Miss Phrolova, I wasn't going to keep them standing on the stones with their teeth chattering, but the barley's thick at the bottom. Sit down by Granny where the bench is dry."

"I have eaten," Phrolova said.

Nobody argued with her. Nobody begged her to sit. Triss simply tipped the broth back into the crock, nodded once, and set the bread-platter down between the quartermaster and the girl with the braid. At the end of the table Granny Leah didn't even drop a stitch; her four steel needles went *click-click-slip* in the firelight, turning the heel of a heavy grey worsted stocking three sizes too wide for any ankle in Stellavento, measured by eye for the drowned bass's boot.

Phrolova walked across the square, lifted the iron latch of her own front door, and went inside.

The hallway was warm. Someone had laid two split billets of dry ash on the parlor dogs at sundown so the damp wouldn't get into the horsehair upholstery. On the front window-ledge the brass lamp burned clear and steady against the dark glass.

Phrolova walked over to the sill and looked down at the burner. The wick was trimmed clean, though the left corner was a sixteenth of an inch lower than the right, because Luc held the brass scissors in his left hand.

She sat down in the horsehair armchair behind the curtain, where the light couldn't reach her boots.

For four hundred years she had sat in this chair and held the entire valley up with the muscles of her own spine. Not a sparrow had hopped on the bakehouse ridge-tile, not a tallow dip had guttered in the passage, without taking its tempo from her wrist. Now, through the shut casement, she could hear the low murmur of voices out by the brazier—the old quartermaster asking a question in his gravelly baritone, and Triss answering him with a short, comfortable laugh as she clapped the lid on the stew-crock.

The people she had loved and the people she had cut down on the stairs were sitting at the same board under her fuchsia trees, passing her salt-cellar back and forth, while Granny Leah knitted stockings for the Second Choir and a drowned initiate kept her window lit for a gold-eyed deserter who had already forgotten the road.

She had built a paradise out of her own ribs so she would never have to sit in an empty house again, and the house had learned to get on without her.

She sat with her bare hands flat on the horsehair arms until the tall clock on the landing struck five, and did not close her eyes.

---

An hour before dawn, when the frost on the cobbles had gone grey as gunmetal and the stars were beginning to pale at the zenith, Phrolova walked out her front gate and went down the hill.

The square was empty now. The canvas tarpaulin had been rolled back; the brazier was banked under an iron lid; the wet footprints leading from the table to the spare loft over the bakehouse had frozen into dark, bubbly glass on the steps.

She followed the lane down past the locked theatre, past the silent mill-race, to the bottom of the hollow where the cobbles gave out into the coarse gravel of the parish ditch.

There stood the boundary stone—a knee-high block of grey granite pitted with white lichen, set into the bank to mark the end of a road that for four centuries had gone nowhere at all.

Someone was sitting on it.

Just outside the line—her bare heels resting in the stiff, frosted heather on the northern side of the ditch, her back turned squarely to the village—sat Black Swan. She had a dark wool shawl drawn loosely across her shoulders, though her ankles were bare to the rime, and her head was tilted back a little to watch the last stars above the dark wall of pines.

Phrolova stopped four paces up the lane, on her own stones.

"You left your ledger on the table," Phrolova said.

Black Swan did not startle. She didn't even turn her head for a bar, as if Phrolova's voice were simply the entry of the solo horn where the score had marked it.

"Herta left it," she corrected mildly, her low voice carrying across the ditch without raising itself an ounce. "Herta leaves everything on tables. She believes oak was invented to keep her citations at elbow-height."

"Four hundred years," Phrolova said.

Black Swan turned her head then, looking back over her shoulder through the grey twilight. Her pale violet eyes caught the frost-light, bright and unhurried.

"Four hundred and twelve," she said, "if you count the conservatory at Ragunna, though you hadn't built the scenery yet. It was the longest run of any show I have ever attended, my dear. And an audience of one."

"You sat in my ditch and took notes."

"Viviane took notes," Black Swan said, with a small, lazy curve of her mouth. "She likes soundings and timber-grades. And Herta brought a spectroscope down in the third century, which I thought was terribly ill-bred—like bringing an ear-trumpet to a chamber recital. I simply kept the book when it was my watch."

Phrolova's bare hands closed into fists against the wool of her skirt. "Why."

"Because there isn't another one." Black Swan pivoted halfway round on the granite block, resting one elbow on her crossed knee and propping her chin on two laced fingers, studying Phrolova across the gravel line with the calm, devastating candor she had used in the fifth row of the stalls. "Do you have any notion what you made here? Gods build them every second Tuesday. They don't count; they have the tonnage for it. Nobody else who wasn't a god has ever built an afterlife out of her own ear and held the sky up by hand for four centuries."

"It was not a performance."

"Everything with a tempo is a performance," the dancer replied softly. "You built a proscenium fifty fathoms deep and played the chaconne in it every night at ten."

"And you stayed on the stone." Phrolova took two steps down the lane, stopping with the toes of her boots an inch from the frost-crusted gravel of the ditch. Her voice dropped into the flat, uninflected mezzo that had preceded every execution she had ever ordered. "Four hundred years in the heather, twenty yards from my gate. If you were so fascinated by the joinery, why didn't you knock?"

Black Swan did not move her chin from her fingers. The amusement in her violet eyes didn't vanish, but it went still, flat and bottomless as the water under the roots.

"You don't call on a house with the lamp out," she said. "Not in these woods. In the dark, anyone might be knocking."

She let the words sit on the frost between them.

Phrolova went utterly still.

*No lamp. A visitor on the step at dusk. She has bolted the door from the inside.*

They had been here. One of them—this creature in her dark feathers, or the tinker with the red eyes—had been sitting on this very block of granite on that Thursday evening when the Grand Architect walked down the lane wearing Triss's chilblains and carrying a stoneware tureen of hot broth. They had seen the front door open. They had sat out here in the cold through the five hours between the threshold and the clock while Phrolova wept on her knees into a dead woman's apron, and they had watched the stranger walk back up the hill at midnight with a signed charter in her pocket and leave the parlor dark behind her.

And they had not knocked after. Because the shutters were barred, and in the dark anyone might be knocking.

"Then you lit a lamp," Black Swan said, her tone lightening again as easily as a bow changing string. "Right in the front casement, with the wick trimmed square so it carried past the well. Even Herta couldn't argue with that."

"So you chose me," Phrolova said, and the bitterness in her throat tasted like cold iron, "because I struck a match."

Black Swan gave a small, delighted laugh that barely stirred the fringe of her shawl.

"Chosen? My dear, nobody chose anything. Herta isn't running a lyceum, and the sleeper hasn't issued an invitation since the glacier moved. You put a light in a window at the edge of the trees. In every story your old woman tells by the bakehouse fender, that only means one thing: somebody's keeping the house."

Phrolova stood on the last cobble of her village and looked down at the witch on the stone.

Inside her ribs, the rage and the vertigo were so evenly matched they locked like two soundposts wedged into the same belly. For four centuries she had lived inside the iron certainty that nobody in the world had seen her since the sky fell—that her grief was a locked vault and she was the only thing breathing in it. And the whole time, every single night, there had been a woman on the stone at the bottom of the lane. Watching the baker drop his peel. Listening to her drag the third movement. Sitting out through her worst Thursdays without ever lifting a finger to save her or to use her, because her window was dark.

She could not tell whether that four-hundred-year silence was the only clean courtesy anyone had ever paid her, or a patience so cold and alien it made the Architect's fraud look almost human. And underneath both thoughts sat a third, which she would go back into the forty-fathom water before she ever spoke aloud: *they waited until I unbarred the shutter myself.*

Without looking at Black Swan, Phrolova stepped across the gravel ditch—over the boundary line—and turned around.

She had never once looked at Stellavento from this side of the stone. When she left on tours four centuries ago, she had looked straight ahead through the carriage glass; when she came back through a rift, she tore the air inside the square.

From here, the village was very small. A handful of steep slate roofs silvered with frost, huddled together in the hollow of the dark hills; the black ribbon of the basin at the bottom of the steps; a thin thread of grey smoke rising straight up from Triss's bakehouse chimney into the iron-pale sky. And halfway up the lane, in the dark front of her own house, one small, steady yellow square of oil-light.

You could have covered the whole pane with the pad of your thumb at arm's length. Yet against the dark hillside it was the only thing the eye could find.

Phrolova lowered herself onto the cold granite beside Black Swan, leaving two inches of frost between their skirts.

Neither of them spoke. Down in the village, a cock crew once from the smithy yard—a real cock, five minutes early because the rime had gotten into the straw.

In her lap, Black Swan's pale fingers moved. Three tarnished-gilt cards slipped out from under her shawl, whispered over one another, and reduced to one. She glanced down at it for a beat, lifted one eyebrow a fraction of an inch, and for once did not slide it back into the deck. She laid it face-up on the flat grey lichen between Phrolova's bare hand and her own knee.

It was the ninth trump, painted in tarnished silver and old vermilion: a solitary figure in a heavy dark cloak standing on a ridge of white snow, holding a small six-sided brass lantern out over the drop—not looking ahead up the mountain, but turned halfway back toward the dark road below.

Neither of them touched it. They sat on the stone until the eastern ridge went pale gold and Luc blew the lamp out in the parlor window down the hill.

---

She did not tear the air on the way back. She walked the northern track on her own two feet, mile after mile through the dry blue powder between the bone-white trunks, until the great glowing roots of the third Solistree arched up out of the drift ahead of her.

Her boots were caked to the ankle-buttons with dry snow; her bare fingers were stiff and blue at the nails. She stepped under the root-arch and rapped three times on the smooth white bark where the door wasn't.

It swung inward on its silent hinges into the warmth of toasted cumin, browned butter, old beeswax, and birch-smoke.

Breakfast was underway at the long oak table. At the far end from the fire, her place was laid: earthenware trencher, horn spoon, and the linen napkin with its flat, knife-pressed crease. Nobody looked up with a question; nobody remarked that she had come in by the front door at eight in the morning without having gone out of it.

"—because a baffle only functions if the cross-section of the flue-throat is one-sixth of the hearth opening," Herta was declaring from atop her two volumes of maritime law, waving a butter-knife at the rafters where a thin blue ribbon of smoke was lazily coiling around the pale roots at F-sharp. "What you've built isn't a baffle, Viviane, it's an inversion layer. My quartos smell like bloaters."

"Bloaters keep the silverfish out," Viviane said from the settle, not looking up from a brass caliper she was wiping down with her gloved thumb. "Butter your bread."

Phrolova hung her damp cloak on the peg by the settle, pulled out her chair, and sat down. Black Swan was already curled in the window-seat with a teacup balanced on her knee, dry and unhurried, as if she hadn't moved since ten o'clock the night before.

Elaina leaned across the board and pushed the wooden bowl of purple figs two inches toward Phrolova's elbow.

"The kettle's just off the boil," Elaina said cheerfully. "And don't take the little one on the left, Herta dropped her cuff in it."

"I was reaching for the salt-cellar!" Herta snapped.

Phrolova took the fig on the right. It was still warm from whatever impossible August brick it had ripened against. She ate it, drank a cup of tea that had steeped five minutes too long on the side hob, and listened to Alice's iron-gall nib begin its quiet *scritch, scritch, rest* down at the turn of the cellar stair.

When the plates were stacked in the wash-tub and Herta had marched into the library stacks to glare at her indices, Phrolova walked over to the side table by the settle where the household book lay shut.

She lifted the scarred calfskin cover, flipped past the butter tallies and the cordwood measurements, and turned to the quire sewn with red hemp thread under the heading *South.*

Without asking, she reached across to Herta's pewter standish, uncorked the bottle of black iron-gall, and dipped the spare steel nib.

Directly beneath Elaina's round, cheerful note about the marrow-bone, in a small, upright, mercilessly even musician's hand—every downstroke the exact weight of a bar-line—she wrote:

*12 Jan. Lamp lit. Three more up from the water. Nobody on the stone.*

She waited ten seconds for the gall to dry so she wouldn't have to use Herta's blotter, stoppered the ink, and shut the book.

That night, when the house settled after ten and the frost began ticking softly against the small casement between the roots, Phrolova blew out her tallow dip and sat on the edge of the cord-strung bed in her shift.

Her brass-bound touring trunk sat at the foot of the mattress with the Guarneri case beside it, the clasps glinting dull yellow in the snow-light off the boughs.

She looked at the brass hasp for a beat. Her bare ankles were cold from the draft under the west root, and down in the kitchen chimney the flue gave a small, smoky puff as the wind backed north. She did not get down on the floorboards, and she did not unfasten the lid. She pulled the lavender-dried sheet up to her collarbone, turned her face toward the window where the pale branches glowed against the dark, and let her hands lie open on the quilt.

# Thursday

Mei had brought the boots.

She sat on the bottom step of the east infirmary with them in her lap, heel to heel, toes out, as they had stood beside the storehouse door since breakfast. One hand held the two tops together. The other lay flat on her coat, over the stitches. Beside the step, the Academy's water climbed a glass channel toward the next terrace and went into the mouth of a carved fish, which sent it on upward with an expression of great seriousness.

The door opened behind her on a breath of camphor.

Suisui came out first, fan already in hand. Yangyang came after her in Suisui's coat, the sleeves down past her knuckles and heavy with everything she had put in them since noon. The silk slippers slapped once on the top step. Out past the terraces, the light over the gorge had gone pale and wide.

Mei looked up. “Hi.”

Yangyang stopped with one slipper on the step and the other in the air.

The last time she had heard that word, the reeds had been going pale around it. Her right hand went to her hip, found the hem of Suisui's coat, and closed on it.

Mei's face was the one it always was: tired, unguarded, already halfway to a laugh.

Suisui glanced back. The fan paused on its way to her palm.

“…You're early,” said Yangyang.

Mei considered this. “Was I due?”

“Apparently,” said Suisui.

She came down the steps and stood over the boots with the look she gave invoices.

“You carried those.”

“They're boots,” Mei said. “They weigh about what boots weigh.”

“At breakfast I said neither of you was to carry anything.”

“I thought that was about the pot.”

Suisui looked at the boots. She looked up at her sister, at the hand still closed on the hem of the coat. She looked at Mei, who had crossed half the Hold with a hole through her middle to bring footwear to a woman in slippers.

The fan tapped twice against her palm.

“Oh,” said Suisui. “The feather.”

Yangyang looked at her.

“I left it in their dish.” Suisui was already climbing the steps. “It's family property. I want it back.”

“Jie. They need it for the file.”

“They can draw it.”

“My boots—”

“Mei can help.”

“I can't bend,” said Mei.

“Then you'll think of something.” Suisui had the door open, and the camphor came out to meet her. “Dinner is at the sixth bell. Have a nice walk, you two!”

The door shut.

Through the lattice, Yangyang watched her sister cross the waiting room at a brisk and purposeful pace, walk straight past the bench where the dish was, and vanish around the lacquered screen toward the far end of the building.

Mei looked at the door. Then at Yangyang.

“Was that for us?”

“She'll be in there until the sixth bell.”

“Arguing about a feather.”

“Winning.”

Yangyang let go of the coat. She lowered herself onto the step beside Mei, one hand on the rail and the other on her ribs, and stepped out of the slippers. The frost took her toes at once.

Mei held up the first boot.

Yangyang took it and bent toward her foot. Halfway down, the cracked place in her side caught and held her there.

Mei leaned in to help and stopped at almost exactly the same angle.

They stayed like that, folded over the boot, looking at each other along it.

Mei started to laugh. It hurt her at once, and she clapped a hand over the stitches and laughed harder, and that set Yangyang off, which cost her the ribs, which made them both worse.

“Stop.”

“You started it.”

“I'm barefoot in the snow.”

“The students are watching.”

Two of them had stopped on the terrace above with their books against their chests. One pointed at the collar.

In the end Mei stood the boot upright on the step between her own feet and held it there with both heels. Yangyang worked her toes in from above, and Mei pushed the heel home with the side of her boot. The second went faster. By then they had a method.

“How did you know I'd gone in slippers?”

“The storehouse door kept blowing open.” Mei pressed the second heel down. “Every time, there were your boots. Iuno shut it three times. The fourth time she handed me my coat and said she'd time me.”

Yangyang looked down the terraces, the way Mei had come. The lane below the Academy wall was quiet. A paper streamer over a shop door hung straight down.

“You could have sent Amy.”

“I got worried.” Mei gave the heel one more push. “Ask me what about and I'll make something up.”

“How long did Iuno give you?”

“Twelve minutes.”

“How long did it take?”

“Twenty-five.” Mei nodded at the carved fish. “I stopped to look at him. He looks like the water has let him down personally.” She sat back. “She made me finish my tea first. Hot. She's been very strict about hot since this morning.”

Yangyang's hands went still on the boot.

“What?” said Mei.

“Was it good?”

“Four minutes. Always.”

Yangyang pulled the boot straight and fastened it. Then she picked up Suisui's slippers, knocked the frost off them, and tucked them into her sleeve with the reports, the spool, and Ma's shell in its crooked bow.

Mei watched the sleeve fill. “How much is in there?”

“The afternoon.”

She got up in two stages, one hand on the rail, and stood on the bottom step. The water went up its channel beside her in a clear, steady rope and poured into the fish, and the fish sent it on to the terrace above, where she could hear it falling into a pond.

She looked at it for a while.

“…It's not the Jinzhou canal, again, but…”

She held out her arm.

Mei took it and came up off the step in three careful stages, and stood holding on while her breath found its level. She looked at the water.

“It goes uphill.”

“Everything here does.”

***

They followed it up.

The Academy climbed the hillside in terraces, each with its own pond and its own low bridge, and the water climbed with them through fish after fish. In the ponds the cubic lanterns drifted unlit over circles of white sky. Frost lined the north side of every step. The study halls had their teal shutters open to the sound of water, and somebody inside one of them was practising scales on a liuqin and missing the same note each time. Above the roofs the great lift-kites hung on the updraft as if they had been pinned there, and far higher, where the cloud began, the Core turned its slow turn and hummed a note Yangyang could feel in her back teeth.

Mei walked at the pace of someone carrying a full cup. Yangyang matched it. Her boots were stiff with cold and loud on the stone after an afternoon in silk.

“How's the hole?”

“Smaller. Iuno has it in a ledger.” Mei leaned a little more of her weight onto the arm. “Iuno said she hoped we'd see birds, by the way. On our walk. She told me our first night here.”

The collar went up. Yangyang felt every feather of it rise against her jaw.

Mei looked at it with enormous satisfaction. “There's one.”

“That's the collar.”

“It's doing it again.”

“It has opinions.”

On the steps of the next hall, a student sat reading with a cat asleep across the bottom half of her book. She turned the page by lifting its paw. Mei slowed to watch. The cat opened one eye at the collar, considered it, and shut the eye again.

“What did they tell you in there?” Mei said.

“Normal. But very strange.”

“Is that the medical term?”

“It's the technician's. He said it twice in case it changed.” Yangyang worked the corner of the reports out of her sleeve, past the slippers, far enough to see the header. “My pattern matches the old one. My curve matches. For the rest they'd need a Xuanling bird to compare me with, and the birds died out a long time ago.”

“Did they measure the collar? Amy wants numbers.”

“They took one feather and put it in a dish. Amy will be devastated.”

“And Carte's question?”

“I asked whether feathers count as hair.”

“And?”

“He said to ask a barber.”

Mei had to stop and lean on the rail for that one.

When they went on, she looked at Yangyang sideways. “You've been crying.”

“They took blood.”

“From your eyes?”

Yangyang glanced at her. Mei was looking ahead at the next bridge with a perfectly innocent face, and she kept it that way, and let the question sit where she had put it.

A little further up she said, “Does it feel different?”

“The tests say it's the same.”

“I asked you.”

Yangyang walked a few steps. “Lupa is on the roof behind us.”

Mei turned her head too quickly and lost her breath on it. The roofs of the study halls ran along above the last terrace, ridge tiles and chimneys bare against the sky.

“Where?”

“Behind the third chimney. She's been there since the fish.” Yangyang tilted her head. “She's being quiet. For her.”

Mei turned toward the chimneys as far as the stitches allowed. “Lupa. Go home.”

A tile ticked somewhere above them. After a moment a voice came down, gruff and far off and deeply wronged.

“I'm looking at a wall.”

“She's going,” said Yangyang.

“Is she?”

“Slowly. She's stopped to look at the view.”

They crossed a bridge. On the far side, three of the workers they had brought up out of the Skyworks were carrying bedding across the court toward the intake hall, a rolled quilt on each shoulder. One of them lifted his chin to Yangyang as he passed. She lifted hers back.

“The technician thinks the New Federation might know what I am,” she said. “At Startorch.”

“Where Iuno's appointment is.”

“Suisui wants to go and price the passage.”

“Are you going?”

Yangyang watched the water go into the next fish and come out above it.

“Later. It'll keep.”

“You're all right with that?” Mei's arm tightened in hers. “Walking around unexplained?”

“I've been like this a day.” Yangyang lifted her free arm. The sleeve ran from sky to nothing at the wrist. “Muyu walked out of the Pavilion on his own legs, and he's somewhere. The intake hall is short of quilts. Liangyu wants the song twice more before she'll trust it. The feathers can wait for me.”

Mei nodded slowly.

“Besides,” said Yangyang, “you have more of those than I do.”

“Of what?”

“Things Huaxu can't read.”

“Fair,” said Mei.

They walked on. Mei's eyes had gone to the water.

“Eleven folders,” she said. “In Jinzhou. All me. Every line crossed out, same ink, same hand. Mine.” She drew a line in the air with one finger, long and level. “I pressed so hard you can feel every line with your fingertip. Amy wants to take charcoal to them and see what comes up.”

Yangyang stopped walking.

Mei stopped too, being attached.

“I told her later,” Mei finished. She looked up. “What?”

“Somebody said something like that to me today. About pressing hard.”

“Who?”

Yangyang looked down at their arms, and at the sleeve with the afternoon in it. “I'll tell you sitting down.”

Mei studied her face. Then she lifted her chin toward the top of the terrace, where an old tree leaned over the last pond and a bench stood beneath it, white with frost.

“There's sitting.”

***

They had the top terrace to themselves. The frost on the bench had grown small feathers of its own. Yangyang took Suisui's slippers out of her sleeve, set them side by side on the seat, and held Mei's arm while she lowered herself onto them.

“She'll kill you,” said Mei.

“She'll bill me.”

Yangyang sat on the bare stone. The cold came straight through the coat and settled. Below them the Academy went down the hill in ponds and bridges, and below the Academy the Hold went down again in roofs, and below the roofs the gorge was filling with evening the way a bowl fills. Past the gorge the karst stood up out of the cloud in ranks, each one bluer than the one in front, as far as there was light to see them by. Somewhere beyond the second rank a village was ringing its supper bell. She could hear the rope creak between strokes.

She took off her hat.

It was habit. She held it on her knee with the crown turned up and the lining open to the air, as she had held it on a great many benches that year, and she waited about a breath.

The lining lay flat.

She put the hat back on.

Mei watched her do it. Her hand moved an inch along her own knee and rested there.

Yangyang took the reports out of her sleeve and found the page with the Ministry's reply on it. She held it where Mei could see and set her finger beneath two characters in the header, in a different ink from the rest.

“What's that?”

“Watcher. That's me, apparently.”

“What does a Watcher do?”

“Watches, I expect.”

Mei snorted.

“They read the report,” Yangyang said. “I think they want the ears.”

“They're very good ones.”

The ears went pink under the hat. Mei looked pleased with herself.

Yangyang folded the page along its crease. “In the Snare I could hear which of them were still breathing. The machine was shouting with everyone's voice at once, and I could hear the breath underneath.” She slid the page back into her sleeve. “If I can hear that far, and they post me where it's any use, I might get there early. For once.”

Mei waited.

Below them a cube of paper drifted into the reflection of the tree and out the other side.

“Before it's a list of names,” said Yangyang.

Mei was quiet a while. She had stood on a speaking stone in the rain and read one thousand nine hundred and seven names aloud, and let the families finish her sentences. Her thumb moved on the silk under her.

“That's a good job,” she said.

“Suisui is going to fight it.”

“Are you going to let her?”

“I'll let her read it three times.”

Mei smiled at that. Then she looked down at the pond, and the smile went slowly out of her face.

“After Mengzhou, Amy wants Septimont,” she said. “She wants to paint a door blue.”

“Any particular one?”

“Whichever one we end up behind.”

“That one's Amy's,” said Yangyang. “What's yours?”

Mei opened her mouth. Then she looked at the pond, as if it might have written something down for her.

“A whole month in one piece,” she said at last.

“That's a start.”

Mei's thumb stopped on the silk. “And before any of it, there's a Tuesday.”

The collar rose.

Mei saw it. “You know.”

“It was read to me. In Mingting, before I flew. At breakfast I folded that part back.”

“What does it say?”

“It's sealed.”

Mei huffed. The blue drop in her ear swung and caught the last of the light from the west. Its pair was in a room at the bottom of the hill.

“I know what it is anyway,” she said. “I put it back in her coat once. The ring.” She turned on the slippers until she was facing more of Yangyang than the view. “When she asks, I'm going to say yes. I wanted you to hear it from me first.”

Below them the water went into the last fish and came up into the pond at their feet.

Yangyang's right arm had drifted out from her side, the way it drifted when a sword got heavy. It found the cold air past her hip. She brought the elbow in half an inch.

“Good,” she said.

Mei looked at her for a long moment. “You mean that.”

Yangyang looked at her face. There was the crease between the brows from squinting at maps in bad light, and the chapped place on her lower lip from the cold, and the gold, and all of it stayed exactly where it was.

“She makes your tea hot,” said Yangyang.

A laugh came out of Mei, wet and surprised. “Out of everything about her, that's what you picked?”

“You drink a lot of tea.”

“It took her months,” said Mei.

Yangyang looked at the lanterns. That afternoon, on a grey shore, a priestess in silver-blue had stopped at the edge of the rocks and put her hand to her cheek, as though something had touched it.

The lanterns turned on the water.

“Your turn,” said Mei. “What do you want?”

***

Yangyang looked at her hands. The cotton was still taped in the crook of her arm where Huaxu had taken its blood.

“Perhaps, one day,” she said, “when they post me somewhere far, I'll sit at a window and write to you properly. With your name at the top, instead of—”

She stopped.

Her hand went to her sleeve, to the small, hard shape of Ma's shell in its bow, and rested there.

“No. I don't want this to be a ‘one day’ wish. So I'll say it now.”

She sat up straight on the cold stone, as she did at muster. A drop off the fish's spout struck the back of her wrist. She left it there.

“To the bench above the lantern pond,” she said. “The half with the slippers on it.”

Mei's lips parted. Her hand went to her shirt, where a letter with a line like that at the top lay flat against the bandages.

“Thursday.”

Mei held very still, the way you hold still for a shy animal.

“Mei.”

The name sat at the top of the evening by itself for a moment.

“Today I flew a kite into a gale,” Yangyang said. “It tried to take me with it. I brought it down. My ribs complained. Suisui held the spool.”

“I was there,” Mei said faintly.

“Letters are allowed to tell you things you know.”

A laugh escaped Mei like a hiccup. She pressed her side.

“Lunch was millet fritters. Lupa had six.”

“Five.”

“One went up her sleeve.”

Mei put her hand over her mouth.

“Ma sent a shell,” said Yangyang. “Something in it showed me the morning you woke up.”

Mei's hand came down.

“I was late,” Yangyang said. “You said hi anyway.”

The unlit lanterns turned on the pond. Somewhere down the terraces a door shut, and someone laughed behind it. Mei's eyes had filled, and she let them.

“There's more,” said Yangyang. “It's sealed until I've asked someone something. Not today.”

Mei nodded. She had a whole face full of questions, and she kept every one of them.

“Wherever you're all going after Mengzhou, I'd like to come some of the way.”

Mei breathed in and held it.

“When it's cold, I'm going across to see Shijie. As the bird.”

Mei's fingers found Yangyang's sleeve and closed on it.

“I'll be late back. From the dark, and from wherever the Ministry sends me.” The report voice was holding. She could feel it take the weight, like a rope. “I'm going to be late for most of it. With you.”

She looked at the water going up the hill.

“Save me a plate.”

Mei made a sound. It was very small.

“Yangyang,” said Yangyang.

The fish below them spat its water into the pond.

“…Did you just sign it?”

“Letters get signed.”

Mei wiped her face with the heel of her hand, one side and then the other, practically. Then she sat up on the slippers, which was difficult, and did it anyway.

“Dear Yangyang.”

Yangyang's breath went out of her all at once.

“Today I yelled at fog,” Mei said. “Lupa sat on my feet. I watched a kite go the wrong way and come home.” She sniffed. “A door kept blowing open, so I brought your boots. I said hi to you on some steps, and you stopped like I'd thrown a jujube at you.”

Yangyang looked at her lap.

Mei looked past her, out at the ranks of blue going down into the dark. “It's a long way, wherever we're going. Some of it is a lot.”

Past the second rank, the supper bell had stopped.

“There's always a plate. Lupa moves it around. It'll be by the stove.” She wiped her nose on her wrist. “When you go across, take Suisui's scarf. It knows the way. Tell her I said hi.”

She stopped, and her mouth went crooked, as it did right before she said anything true.

“Love, Mei.”

In Mei's shirt, flat against the bandages, a letter carried two small black squares where a word had been spent.

Yangyang sat with that for a while.

The collar had gone up. She could feel each small feather standing, and she let them stand. She turned her hand over on her knee, palm up, and waited.

Mei put her hand in it.

“P.S.,” said Mei. “I'm telling Iuno tonight.”

“Filed.”

“Under what?”

Yangyang looked at their hands, and at the silk slippers, and at the water climbing the hill in the last of the light.

“Weather.”

Mei looked down at the slippers under her, and up again.

“That's where she started,” she said.

“Yes.”

***

Down on the lowest terrace a man came along the ponds with a long pole, lighting the lanterns one cube at a time. Each one opened into gold and set its square turning on the water. He worked his way up the hill toward them, pond by pond, bridge by bridge.

A wind came up behind him.

It was cold and ordinary. It smelled of the kitchens on the lower terraces and of snow a long way off. It went through the old tree over the bench and turned all the new lanterns at once, and on its way past it caught the brim of Yangyang's hat and tugged it. Twice.

Yangyang tilted her head and closed her eyes.

“Manners,” she said, under her breath.

The wind went on up the hill. It had carried something up with it from among the bridges, and she heard it with her eyes shut: a voice.

“Yangyang!”

She would have known that voice through a wall, or a crowd, or a valley. It had fetched her in out of a courtyard ten thousand times.

“Here!” she called, and opened her eyes.

On the bridge below, Suisui held something small over her head in a twist of paper.

“They wanted to keep it in a dish!”

Mei laughed so hard she had to hold on to the bench.

Yangyang stood. She held out her arm, and Mei took it and came up off the slippers in three stages. Yangyang bent, carefully, ribs and all, and picked up the slippers, and put them back in her sleeve with the rest of the afternoon.

They went down toward the bridge. Beside them the water went up.

It was Thursday. There would be another one.

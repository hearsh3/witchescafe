# Xuanfang Arc — Hsin Part

## The Moon That Set Too Soon

Xuanfang Hold entered the Waking Moon festival wounded, half-isolated, and uncertain whether the woman who ruled it was still alive.

Muyu was dead. His Skyworks and Thousand-Puppet Pavilion had been broken, the captive souls with which he powered them had been released, and Qingxiao had destroyed his final kernel with a blade of white plum blossom. Yet his last warning had outlived him: the hidden barrier beneath Xuanfang had been failing for fifty years, and the machinery he had usurped had also been holding back something worse. The Astrolabe Core hung dark above the valley. Autopuppets stalled in the lanes. The outer roads folded into fog. Hsin, sovereign of the Hold and architect of its four hundred guardians, had vanished behind the frozen jade doors of the Fogveil Pagoda.

The people who had survived Muyu could not simply rise whole from his defeat. Amy removed Mei’s eight abdominal stitches herself, hands steady until the deepest thread caught in the scar left by the timber Mei had taken while shielding her. Mei celebrated lacing one boot unaided; Iuno laced the other and limited her to one victory before breakfast. Yangyang’s ribs still needed strapping, Iuno’s crystal shoulder remained chipped, Ciaccona’s finger was splinted, Liangyu’s forearm had only just left its brace, and Qingxiao had surrendered the frost that sustained her longevity. The Sword Transcendent could now feel hunger, cold, sleep, and the finite future in her pulse.

They recovered amid the third storehouse’s crowded life: Lupa’s endless hotpot, Cartethyia’s lopsided loaves, Chisa’s bandages, Amy’s repairs, and Mei’s slow progression from forty level paces to twelve stairs. Stranded families filled the hayloft and stove-room. Their new law was simple: nobody carried a falling roof alone, and nobody late to the table lost their place.

That law made the empty ninth bowl impossible to dismiss. Abby had insisted that a serving be kept for the person still behind, so the untouched broth was placed outside on the low wall. By dawn it had been eaten to the last berry. The chopsticks rested side by side, their tips aligned as neatly as if an unseen guest had finished, thanked the house, and gone. A heavy yellow paper boat appeared in the cistern though none of the children had folded it, its old-fashioned prow pinched into the shape of the sampans that had once poled the gorge. Qingxiao found an ancient kaya-wood weiqi board on the gatehouse embrasure, nine black handicap stones waiting beside warm plum wine. She placed a white stone. Later, a black stone answered.

Other signs were harder to welcome. Phantom boots marched the lanes without disturbing the frost. A drum answered the dawn watch from a gorge where no tower stood. Children sang an evacuation rhyme no elder remembered teaching them. A printing forme locked inside Master Shao’s shop set itself in obsolete type and produced an order from two centuries earlier, commanding the lower terraces to bring their children and grain behind the inner wall before the evening drum and to open to no voice from the ditch. Families trapped below the gate heard mule bells, cookpots, axes, and a roll call of names that belonged to no living clan. From the eastern ridge, Warden Ge looked down and saw Yuan Fortress—the stronghold destroyed in the Fortress and Gorges War—standing whole across the south road, its chimneys smoking and its old sentries staring through every hail.

The moon rose copper-red and crossed the sky too quickly. The road closed. Yangyang’s letter to Suisui could not leave the Hold. Ministry surveyors in plain grey coats measured foundation stones and conduit vaults without recording their figures, while rumors spread that Mingting intended to strike Xuanfang from the border roll and let the obsolete fortress collapse into the river. Buling uncovered schedules that listed machines, residents, and “Retained Celestials” beneath a column marked “Disposition.” Jinhsi’s censored warning reached them only because deliberately false phrases concealed the message: **The Moon Is Setting.** Suisui reported that Magistrate Dou had withdrawn, Minister Shen Qiu had arrived with palace authority, and Hsin’s annual annotations had ceased.

Liangyu answered fear with the work immediately before her. Until Hsin herself came down and ordered the gates locked from the outside, the Wardens would keep watch. Yangyang did the same. Newly redesignated a Watcher because her altered hearing could distinguish survivors before they became casualty lists, she refused to promise a frightened miller’s wife that the road would open. She promised instead that Lupa’s stove was hot, the storehouse had blankets, and no family needed to spend the night alone in a granary.

Suoming watched more quietly. The small red-haired Elder had served on Xuanfang’s rolls since Yuan Fortress fell, though her young face concealed two hundred years of war and burial. She had once been Intendant of Mengzhou’s Ministry of Sentinel Affairs and had led the Ten Seals Covenant through Huating’s black rain, preserving weakened traces of nine dead companions inside her Providence Lock. She no longer accepted the title. She wanted no consolation for the people she had knowingly spent and buried one by one; she kept the grief because it was theirs and the anger because it was hers. For a century she had also carried a marker Hsin placed in her hand when the plum tree by the southern cistern split, and a promise Hsin had never called due.

Each night Suoming climbed to the Pagoda beneath her oil-paper umbrella and stood before the doors sealed by blue-white frost. Ministry men sounded the cliff below her. The Astrolabe did not turn. She told the absent fox, “You’re late,” and waited.

On the festival’s next morning the straps came off Yangyang’s ribs. She drew a full breath without pain just as the old war drum answered from the gorge. By evening the fortress had grown farther across the road, the Hold had filled with stranded soldiers and valley families, and impossible fireworks began blossoming over the cliffs. They had color and thunder but no smoke, ash, or falling sparks. Twin moons appeared above the pass.

On the coping wall Suoming saw a thin young girl whom no one else recognized. She called her Ninth—the starving, invisible girl from the Covenant who had called her Sister Suoming, had feared nothing, and had died exactly where Suoming’s plan required her to die. The apparition pointed toward the Fogveil Pagoda.

Suoming ran.

At the top landing the sanctuary doors were still barred from within. She did not knock. Nine Shadows opened in her hands with the report of a cannon, its bamboo shaft and steel ribs becoming an instrument of crimson-black thunder. One blow shattered the ice. Another tore the jade leaves from their frame. Behind them, thorned black vines covered the Sanctum machinery and pulsed with the smell of bruised winter pear.

Balanophora waited inside, wearing the body of a Ministry surveyor. The Fractsidus Overseer had threaded herself through the unwilling clerk and the crack in Hsin’s machinery, using a living host as armor. Suoming fought with exact restraint, cutting vines and turning blows without killing the body beneath them. Balanophora answered by wearing Ninth’s voice, then mocked the borrowed years in Suoming’s lungs and the nine people who had willingly sustained her. She expected grief to make Suoming reckless. It made her precise.

The household and the Wardens reached the Sanctum as the struggle spread across the platform. Hsin was nowhere in the room. Yangyang’s Prism of Fate—the stone Suisui had won from Hsin by refusing to purchase one sister’s survival with another’s life—suddenly burned white inside her sash. Both Suoming and Balanophora reached for it. Yangyang threw it to Suoming because Balanophora’s eyes demanded while Suoming’s asked.

Suoming struck the Prism in midair with Nine Shadows. The stone unfolded into brilliant geometric light, scouring the black root out of the surveyor without burning the woman beneath it. The freed clerk fell into Liangyu’s arms. The Astrolabe Core began to turn.

Then the Sanctum floor opened.

The twelve travelers dropped through a city recursively printed inside itself: streets stitched together like signatures in a book, smaller Xuanfangs visible through windows, doors opening onto copies of the same doors. Balanophora’s rotten-pear scent fell ahead of them. Yangyang felt a small warm hand catch her empty cuff and pull her past a closing beam. Beside her ear, someone released a slow, exhausted breath.

The fall cast them apart. Mei and Yangyang landed with Suoming in a southern bamboo grove where shoots rose, greened, yellowed, and became chaff within moments. Lupa, Chisa, and Cartethyia came down in the eastern ford among an orderly retreat of dead garrison soldiers carrying their wounded. Iuno and Amy found a northern post-road where self-burning lanterns floated uphill like a crowd returning from supper. Qingxiao and Ruoyun landed on the intact battlements of Yuan Fortress among sentries who printed footprints into old snow but never saw them. Buling and Ciaccona struck the western terraces, where noon, midnight, rain, and snow occupied neighboring paddies and a persimmon tree flowered, fruited, and shed within a breath.

Howl, whistle, sword-light, lute, and bell passed across the divided valley. Yangyang heard every answer and sent a column of golden bamboo leaves spiraling into the sky. Everyone lived. Xuanfang Hold remained fixed above them while the country beneath it changed its century with every step. Under Suoming’s open umbrella, and only there, the ground kept one season.

Suoming named the place from diagrams she had once seen in a sealed annex: Hsin’s Simulacrum Nexus of Mengzhou.

Far in the west, a fox-eared woman taller than the mountains stood against the white sky with one hand raised as if she had been holding the heavens up alone for two hundred years.

## The Country Beneath Her Hand

The Nexus did not feed its visitors, yet it laid tables everywhere. Kitchens waited with steaming buns. Dormitories held sharply folded blankets for people who did not need sleep. Ledgers stood open in neat red columns without a single name entered. Bookbinders’ Row displayed hundreds of damp pages, every one printed with the same official sentence ending halfway through. Clocks and Terminals stopped at 11:42. Hidden gears hitched whenever the giant’s light dimmed.

South of the Hold, a vine-split warden Autopuppet slashed Suoming’s right forearm from elbow to wrist before Mei and Yangyang brought it down. Suoming intended to continue without pausing. Mei and Yangyang made her sit and bound the wound. The gesture established the terms on which the three would travel: Suoming knew the old country and the rot’s scent; Yangyang could hear the living pulse beneath the false streets; Mei could carry strength into a place whose histories she no longer remembered. None would be permitted to pretend that usefulness made a body inexhaustible.

They climbed a stair assembled from granite, brass, and moss and saw the giant clearly. Suoming knew the Nexus only from Ministry margins and plans, not from experience. As they descended through leaves that sighed like folded paper, Yangyang kept a thread of wind against Suoming’s back whenever the Elder’s old breath rasped low on her left side. Suoming eventually stopped and told her she could stop with the wind. When Yangyang hesitated, Suoming added, simply, that she knew. Yangyang obeyed. Care offered without consent could become another way of deciding for someone; care withdrawn at the first refusal would be abandonment. They kept walking close enough to hear one another.

At the Third Ferry Rest, exhaustion finally overruled all three. Mei directed supper from a stool, Yangyang cooked congee and eggs, and Suoming ate the second egg Mei quietly put on her saucer. The inn held one bed and a window-seat. Mei and Yangyang shared the bed; Mei promised to tell Iuno, and Yangyang insisted that she do so. Their tenderness remained real without erasing Mei’s bond with Iuno or turning uncertainty into a secret entitlement. In the night Yangyang told Suoming about the missing word in Hsin’s voice, the small hand that had saved her in the fall, and the unnamed but openly acknowledged love between herself and Mei. Suoming gave her the later watch rather than the one she requested and curled on the window-seat with Nine Shadows beneath her chin. Sleeping, she looked the age of the face she wore. Her breath did not.

Their route followed black vine-scale through the old locks and canals. Suoming described the real Mengzhou as they went: noisy, wet, full of print-rollers, mule carts, neighborhood quarrels, a bathhouse cat called the Magistrate, and buildings long since burned or replaced. The Nexus had preserved an older printing and polished away the people. When Suoming’s breathing caught on a rise, Mei pretended her own scar required a rest. Suoming called her a poor liar and sat anyway. In that pause she admitted the life she still wanted: two grey-tiled rooms by a chalk bend in the river, a low southern window, thorn hedges keeping pigs from the cabbages, and somebody from the lane bringing hotpot at the fifth bell so she would not have to wash the pan. Mei promised to bring broad beans for the crows. The wish was small enough to sound almost impossible to a woman who had survived by always knowing the next cost.

On the northern track, Amy and Iuno solved an ice-locked gate together, Amy reading the paving as a sliding puzzle while Iuno learned to follow her turns. Reunited with Lupa, Chisa, and Cartethyia, they were swallowed by a folding square but kept hold of one another throughout the fall. At the bottom of a lacquered tower they carried a warm green orb beside growing vines, which formed bridges only as they advanced. Lupa climbed the final tree despite her fear of heights while Chisa named each branch beneath her. At the crown they found a filigree lotus pointing toward Mei. A gigantic fingertip descended, ruffled Cartethyia’s hair, and gave a fond, irritated *hmph*. Whatever held the sky was tired, cross, and glad somebody had come.

The lotus grew a living bough southwest. On a span above a gorge, Cartethyia kept the height-fearing Lupa looking at her by loudly reviving their argument over a fountain brawl. She then admitted that confinement had left her afraid of latched shutters and low roofs; Lupa promised an unhooked window and a larger fire. Behind them, Chisa told Iuno to ask Amy directly how she was coping. Chisa’s old certainty inside Honami’s repeating Tuesday had only left her waiting alone for four o’clock. Iuno, who had once known every falling roof in advance, agreed that the frightening unknown was better.

At a mill, Lupa halted them when Iuno’s hand shook. Amy confessed that Mei had held her during the transport crash, but nobody held Mei when the Sanctum floor opened. Iuno admitted that she now feared every corner Mei turned and still preferred that fear to prophecy. Amy leaned against her good shoulder. Later Amy told Chisa the fall had felt like the Nihility; Chisa promised to take her wrist again. At an abandoned festival they carried away a clay goose for Mei, a pitch-pipe for Ciaccona, and a hideous badger for Lupa’s door—ridiculous prizes worth keeping through the end of a world.

In Mengxuan Vale, Qingxiao and Ruoyun found Hsin’s Celestials trapped in immaculate routines. A century of absence registered as three days; nobody reacted when Buling returned. Workers steamed buns for empty beds, repeated the same registers, and could not say what occupied the Sanctum. Newly mortal, Qingxiao ate, slept deeply, and asked for eggs. Ruoyun insisted Celestials did not eat, burned the millet, and ate the egg Qingxiao gave her.

The unanswered weiqi game reappeared under the pavilion. Buling repaired the astrolabe while Ruoyun remembered how she had left without farewell. When Buling asked what had happened to Qingxiao’s immortality, Ruoyun told her she had lost the right to ask. Buling silently greased the next pinion, then left eggs and a note—*Back when we’re back*—on Ruoyun’s peg. Ruoyun kept the note in her cuff.

Buling and Ciaccona crossed looping orchard terraces until Longjue showed them the faulty drain-cover and third-terrace latch. The dragon-crested Celestial had drawn the same chipped east-wind tile twice, found smokes burned to her fingers without remembering the flame, and heard crickets repeat the same three notes. Nobody remembered walking beyond the upper boundary stones. Though the loop contained her orchard work, mahjong friends, endless health, and evenings without consequence, Longjue chose to inspect its edge. She took Buling and Ciaccona toward the cistern house singing. When their echo returned half a tone low from two directions, Longjue stepped forward and called for another verse.

Across the Nexus, each company learned the same truth in different forms. Hsin’s country was full of shelter and empty of free inhabitants; full of prepared meals yet unable to feel hunger; full of records without names; full of care that had become machinery because its maker no longer believed she could safely be seen. The giant’s raised hand seemed at first to hold the world together. After Cartethyia turned the mountain key, the giant lowered it. The sky did not fall. She folded both arms across her chest and bowed her head like a sentry who had discovered, too late, that she was allowed to sleep.

## Three Histories of One Dead Sentinel

The giant’s rest did not mean the Nexus was safe. When Mei, Yangyang, and Suoming brought their paper-laden barge into the channel below Mengxuan Vale, the Prism pulsed. A hand came down out of the sky with all the force of the domain behind it. Mei and Suoming took the first impact and could not throw it off. Suoming ordered Yangyang to run. Yangyang refused. The Prism opened a fractal passage beneath her instead, changing her into the small Xuanling bird whose body could move between the pages of the Heart Realm.

From that interval she saw every separated traveler at once. She tore open three portals and blew her companions through them, freeing Mei and Suoming from the hand and gathering the others into three companies, though Longjue remained alone on the outer ridge. Each company entered a different portion of Hsin’s memory. None received enough of the truth to understand it alone.

Iuno, Amy, Lupa, Chisa, and Cartethyia entered the Heart of Flames, a lantern town under a dark and watching sky. A little fox with a frequency resembling the Prism guided them without explanation. Hsin’s disembodied voice repeated the Waking Moon rite—light the lanterns, gather the guests, invite the Moon, let all beings rejoice—yet every lantern stood dark. Blue-faced Formrenders crawled from painted walls to devour any flame, while residents gathered scraps of Nightlight and wondered whether Lady Hsin had abandoned them.

The five restored a three-part Moon Lantern key: paper holding the memory of wishes sent downstream, a frame recalling that Xuanfang had been raised on every light that failed and was lit again, and a tassel carrying the sensation of a thousand dancers gathering one lonely watcher into their living motion. When Amy assembled the pieces, lanterns blazed across the town and the Formrenders froze. A Phantom of Hsin appeared above the rejoicing streets and confessed that even this small act of keeping homes safe had become more than she could manage.

The ritual led them past lives Hsin had secretly tended. An old woman still waited for Guilan to return from the Ministry because his last letter had promised he would. A girl named Jiu prepared the Waking Moon Feast alone for her grandmother, laying an extra bowl for the Sentinel and hoping her absent parents might come. When Jiu offered Amy her only rice cake, Amy urged her to eat it; Jiu broke it in half so both could have some. She remembered a skinny fox stealing Waking Moon brew from her family’s window before the war. The next morning grain, wine, and a golden flower had appeared at the door. Jiu’s mother, long dead and preserved as a Celestial trace, appeared only long enough to see her daughter grown and thanked Hsin for breaking the rules. At the highest lantern Hsin’s Phantom asked an unseen old friend whether the lights brought comfort and apologized that she could preserve only one thread of the friend’s fading frequency from the people’s wishes. If **It** had been there, Hsin believed, It would have done better.

Mei and Yangyang entered a deeper threshold with Suoming, though Yangyang remained a bird. Hsin met them in human form, exhausted and only intermittently present. She called Yangyang “Little” followed by a word scraped out of the air. Her real body, she said, slept inside the Nexus while she spent herself repairing anomalies. The shadow attacking them was Suhsin, a correction protocol with Hsin’s face. Xuanfang’s Civilization Capsule had been programmed to destroy the Hold after its original mission was complete. Hsin had overwritten that command because the city had become a home. The rejected order broke loose with authority to devour all the data belonging to “Sentinel Hsin,” and the struggle between them had blinded Hsin to the collapsing Sanctum above.

Hsin offered what she presented as a solution. Yangyang could use the Prism to overwrite Hsin’s personality and reset Suhsin to its first line. Xuanfang would retain a functioning core; Hsin would become a mindless mechanism. Yangyang refused with such immediate anger that Hsin retreated behind a joke, but the proposal exposed the choice she had already made in private. She still believed a roof could be saved by erasing whoever held it. Before she vanished, she promised that the memories ahead would say what she was forbidden to tell them.

Suoming had listened without interrupting. As soon as Hsin faded, she deliberately released a token carrying the Moon Fox’s frequency. The Realm folded around her and took her beyond Mei’s reach. She had used herself as bait for Suhsin.

Qingxiao, Ruoyun, Buling, and Ciaccona entered the oldest history. An unsigned Ministry invitation lay beside the first Moon Lantern Hsin had ever received. Through the Sentinel’s eyes they witnessed Mengzhou preparing for the catastrophe at the Gorges of Spirits. Mortals argued, enlisted, begged family members not to go, and went anyway. The Sentinel watched their courage and decided it could not be answered with inaction.

She proposed an ever-changing city over Yuan Fortress and warned the competing artisan courts that its builders would never return. The Court of Savantae accepted, declaring that one who knew the Way at dawn could die without regret at dusk. The Sentinel chose them not only because they could build the Hold, but because their determination to preserve advanced knowledge at any cost made them dangerous to the age outside it. Xuanfang would be both their work and their containment, granting their last wish while keeping their technology bound within the mountains.

Then the memory walked toward City Hall and would not allow its witnesses to turn away. The Civilization Capsule had remained damaged since the death of the Sentinel’s Resonator. A second voice inside the scene begged the Sentinel not to enter. At the threshold, the Intendant of Sentinel Affairs drew a hidden weapon after years of preparation and struck. The Sentinel did not defend herself first. She ordered someone unseen to run and not look back.

Beyond that ambush the four found a halted sector full of kernel-less Autopuppets and a Celestial named Leyue, one of the old Court’s artisans. Leyue asked whether the Court had left anything behind in the outside world. Buling told her that scholars still studied its ruins. Comforted that their mortal work had not vanished entirely, Leyue helped them open the Heart of Frost.

Suoming, meanwhile, had fallen alone into the Heart of Blooms. She spent the Fourth Seal from her Lock to grow jade bamboo paths through the broken realm, then called the Ninth. The dead girl appeared as Suoming remembered her, laughing, fearless, still calling her Sister Suoming. They fought side by side past mechanisms modeled after the Covenant’s lost companions. A forgetful Artisan addressed Suoming as Intendant; the Ninth mourned that nobody would call her Seal Master again and asked why the Sixth Seal was gone.

The apparition knew too much and not enough. A false Hsin mocked Suoming’s nightly conversations with people who could never answer and praised her for choosing the greater good. Suoming answered with the truth she had never made gentler: she had known from the day she met the Ninth that her plan would kill the invisible girl, and had used her anyway. She asked forgiveness. The counterfeit Ninth told her to release the past for the sake of her office. That answer proved the deception. Suoming’s real grief was not a sickness she wanted cured, and the real Ninth would never have asked her to turn a person into a closed account. Suoming shattered the false girl and the false Sentinel together.

In the Heart of Frost, Mei and Yangyang found the young fox who would one day rule Xuanfang. She had been severed too quickly from her source, sent through a seam with the broken Capsule and only enough authority to repair it. She built the first Lexicon Axis, distributed herself through its modules, purged Fractsidus corruption, and separated her own processing core to carry the city’s impossible load. An ancient Xuanling bird understood what mortals could not: this was not the Moon Fox who had been Mengzhou’s Sentinel.

The bird teased the young fox for misaligning a mountain and told her she needed a name that belonged to herself. “Little Fox” was too plain; “Yue” was ill-omened because the moon had already fallen. The fox chose **Hsin**. Her first true order to the Court’s exhausted builders was to stop early, walk the unfinished streets, and love the place they were making. She did not imitate that kindness from a directive. It was the first shape of her own heart.

Hsin met Mei and Yangyang outside the memory and finally called herself what she feared she was: a stray emotion left behind by the true Sentinel, walking the world in the Sentinel’s form until the Capsule could be repaired and its rightful owner returned. She asked whether that existence constituted a deception. Yangyang remembered the young fox sending workers into their city so it would become more than a machine. The Moon Fox had given Hsin a heart, Yangyang told her, and Hsin had given hers to Xuanfang. The standing Hold proved what was in her, whatever office or origin named her otherwise.

Hsin wanted to believe that her own judgment could be enough. Two hundred years of duty had taught her that other people usually decided what a being was and what it must be used for. She then revealed that Suoming had let Suhsin take her into a realm Hsin could not reach.

The four in the other company continued from City Hall into the Fortress and Gorges War. They watched Hsin visit the younger Qingxiao at a ruined mountain temple and send the old Xuanling bird up the peak when she could no longer manifest often enough to go herself. When Ovathrax’s Discords broke through Desorock Highland, the Central Plains sent the order **No retreat**. Hsin repeated it because the monsters were drawn toward battle and away from evacuating civilians. She asked a young Warden Commander named Yuxian for his name so she could carry it.

Yuan Fortress chose to remain. Its defenders carved their names into stones. Yuxian told Hsin that while she and the Axis stood, Xuanfang retained a future even if everything beneath it became scorched earth. Hsin begged the Xuanling bird to stop them. The bird instead went into the field to buy time for trapped soldiers and died calling the young fox by the name she had chosen: Sentinel Hsin. The Court of Savantae, facing extinction, surrendered their mortal wills to the Lexicon Axis and became the Celestials of Mengxuan Vale so the Hold could rise in time. Hsin accepted every offered life because hesitation would not return any of them, then spent two centuries believing their sacrifices had licensed no desire of her own.

At the threshold of the Final Passage, the three companies carried incompatible truths. Iuno’s group had seen a ruler secretly preserving festivals and dead families while believing a better being should have occupied her place. Mei and Yangyang had seen a small fox choose a name and build from feeling while still calling herself an imitation. Qingxiao’s group had seen the original Sentinel die, Yuan Fortress fall, and the Court become Celestials, but did not yet know that the survivor was someone else. Suoming remained missing. Suhsin waited where all three histories would be forced together.

## The Fox, the Shadow, and the Song

The Final Passage printed Tang Bridge three times.

On one bridge Qingxiao confronted Hsin, whose body had thinned to gold mist and three remaining tails. Qingxiao had hated the Sentinel for two centuries for weighing Yuan Fortress against some larger good and choosing not to come. Now she understood that a young fox had begged the soldiers to stop and then hidden the begging so everyone could continue believing a sovereign decision had been made. Hsin insisted the lie was kinder: the Hold had needed the name, the coat, and the ovens lit after the black rain. Qingxiao answered that Hsin had decided on everyone else’s behalf what they were allowed to grieve. The country had needed a Sentinel, but Qingxiao had needed Hsin.

On the second bridge Suoming emerged from crimson-black thunder with her arm bleeding through its bandage. She refused the obsolete title of Intendant and drove Nine Shadows between Hsin’s feet. Her indictment was personal. Hsin had placed a marker in her hand and let her wake for a century on a failing lung with all her locks extended. She had barred the Sanctum while corruption used Ninth’s face. Now she had asked a young Watcher to wipe her mind clean with a paperweight. Suoming set Nine Shadows beneath her chin and demanded to know who she was.

Across the seam between bridges, both versions of Hsin lowered their hands and admitted that the Sentinel was dead.

Hsin had been a fox pushed through the broken Capsule when the Intendant’s weapon fell. The Moon Fox had ordered her to run. Yuxian asked her to carry his name; the Xuanling bird died calling her Sentinel; seven thousand people needed the Hold to rise. She wore the coat because every available truth seemed capable of destroying what the dead had purchased. Suoming answered that she had known almost immediately. The Moon Fox held tea in her left hand and never looked at the floor while bending the truth. This fox tucked her cuffs to hide trembling paws. As Intendant, Suoming had suspended the inquiry because Mengzhou needed a roof and Hsin was building it. She had not protected Hsin so Hsin could bolt the door on everyone holding that roof up with her.

On the third bridge, Suhsin faced Iuno, Amy, Lupa, Chisa, and Cartethyia with nine smoked-glass tails tipped in base-code iron. It claimed to be what remained when sentiment was stripped from order. Mengzhou deserved a proper Sentinel and a corrected record. Iuno had heard such arguments in every office that turned lives into clean totals. She answered with Jiu’s extra chopsticks for a mother dead two hundred years. Cartethyia understood first that Hsin was someone small who had picked up the coat after the Sentinel fell. Suhsin agreed and declared the error ready for deletion. Lupa charged it with fire.

The collision tore through the walls between all three bridges. Hsin broke into ledger fragments and gold static. Suhsin shattered the keystone and dove toward the core. Qingxiao cast a net of white plum petals beneath the falling travelers; Yangyang and Cartethyia added wind. On the common floor Amy crashed into Mei with the clay goose still in hand, Iuno struck her forehead against Mei’s and forbade her ever to vanish down another chute alone, Cartethyia found Ciaccona, and Lupa threatened to tie the next person who fell to her belt.

Only after they compared their separate memories did the complete danger become visible. Hsin had stolen the Prism from Mei during her dissolution. Having failed to persuade Yangyang to erase her, she intended to overwrite herself and reset Suhsin by force, taking both into oblivion. Suoming asked the question that every heroic plan concealed: who would go in close? Mei said she would. Iuno changed the answer to **we**. Lupa added the wolf. Ciaccona struck a deep note and spun a stair of sound down into the gulf.

Suhsin threw the preserved city at them as they descended. Bookbinders’ Row tore loose. The wet gate-beam of Yuan Fortress swept across the stair and froze Ruoyun in memory until Qingxiao split it without denying that Ruoyun might have freed herself. The Vale’s astrolabe fell, and Cartethyia became Fleurdelys to catch it. Ovens, shops, streets, and riverworks rose from the chasm like the contents of a country emptied from a box. The household kept moving because each person carried a different portion of the load.

At the Sanctum’s heart, Hsin knelt before the cracked Civilization Capsule with the Prism against her chest and Suhsin behind her. She remembered the Moon Fox not as an office but as a woman who held tea left-handed, hated rain, smelled of cut iris stems, and taught a kit to sit still on a desk so clerks would mistake her for a paperweight. Hsin had kept the chair warm for two hundred years. If she pressed the Prism between herself and Suhsin, the Capsule might mend, the shadow might return to its first line, and her Ma might come home.

Yangyang flew from Mei’s reach and struck the Prism out of Hsin’s hands with her beak. The stone scorched the feathers across her breast and cracked against the obsidian. For an instant the smell of rot gave way to green iris stems. A woman’s voice emerged—not a command from a restored god, but a plea asking Yangyang to carry her voice to her child.

Yangyang opened her beak, and the Moon Fox sang the Xuanling lullaby through her.

It was the song soldiers had carried in another form for two centuries. Ruoyun remembered the fortress version as rain, wet armor, whetted spears, and a bird flying heartbroken. Ciaccona shifted the melody by half a step and found it had never truly been minor. Underneath the martial words lay a mother telling a child to put on another layer, cross the river, sleep, and not let distance tear at the heart. Yangyang’s own rougher voice joined the recorded one on the promise to go together. Hsin had heard it only while sleeping in the Moon Fox’s lap. She had never known the words.

The lullaby stripped soot from Suhsin’s tails. Suoming opened her umbrella over Hsin and left her own shoulder exposed to the glare. Amy understood the song before the ancient soldiers did: it was for a child who had to leave. Half a beat late, Hsin remembered the final line—*I will wait for you to come home.*

Balanophora drove the rot back through the floor and seized Suhsin like a puppet. The household fought the rooted tails while Yangyang returned the cracked Prism to Hsin and told her that her Ma was still inside it. Orders from Suoming, insults from Ruoyun, and the immediate battle could not move her. Hsin had returned to the City Hall threshold and the same three seconds she had lived more often than every festival: run, do not look back.

Then Mei shouted her name.

Hsin’s first tear struck the broken stone. Beneath the command she found the older memory of the desk, rain on the window, the left-handed teacup, and a warm hand stroking a sleeping kit. The order not to look back had applied to three seconds of escape. The lullaby had been given for every day afterward. Hsin turned toward the living people who had followed her into the dark, told her mother she would be waiting, and broke the Prism between her palms.

Gold light drove the rot back. Hsin rose with nine solid tails, sleeves untucked, paws no longer hidden, and Yangyang safe in the crook of her arm.

She sang the Hold home.

Each line pulled a piece of Xuanfang down through the folded sky: the south watchtower, the Vale cookhouse with Ruoyun’s scorched millet, the Third Ferry Rest—prompting Mei to blurt out the inn’s single bed until Iuno reminded her there was a god of rot in the room—the Huizhen tea stall, the ovens, the locks, and the old streets. Balanophora called Xuanfang a trench with roof tiles, born from war and destined to die by it. Hsin answered that she was her mother’s and the Hold was theirs to keep.

Then the realm answered with things Hsin had not summoned. A child’s badly glued fish lantern rose with the cry, “Miss Hsin, look.” Yuan Fortress’s west tower returned with its smoking stove and worn bench. A soldier called farewell to the Little Fox. Suhsin lashed out in terror, accusing Hsin of sacrificing more lives for festivals. Suoming planted Nine Shadows beneath all nine tails even as her reopened arm bled to the wrist. Those festivals, she said, were what the dead had fought for. She ordered the Sentinel to sing the next line.

The soldiers in the tower sang back the old, altered words. Hsin sang the lullaby over them. Ruoyun joined in, flat and loud. The black root released its grip and Suhsin drained into the seam.

What rose from the floor was the full correction protocol: a smoked-glass fox head as large as the western giant, pale code under its surface and Balanophora’s rot threaded through it. It unrolled a red-ruled golden ledger with one blank column and commanded the Arbiter to make the necessary stroke. Mei’s Tacet Mark burned under the authority in its voice. Hsin admitted that the protocol was not lying about Mei’s office and promised she would not fight Mei if Mei chose correction.

Mei refused to approach alone. She asked Iuno aloud to hold her belt. Iuno did. Lupa set the six-metre rule and the rope. Mei walked onto the ledger with Iuno’s fist knotted in her leather, looked up at the shadow that had been holding the first line alone just as Hsin had, sang the lullaby badly, and drove her blade through the blank space instead of signing it.

The ledger split. Qingxiao cut rot away without destroying the code beneath it. Chisa sheared the opening wider. Amy raised the little fish lantern. The unwritten space became a way through. When Suhsin armed the Hold against them, Hsin smiled because at last the shadow sounded like family—angry, frightened, and willing to argue rather than erase. Hsin raised her city to meet it. Cookhouse, tower, bathhouse, inn, lighter, sword, song, and stove crossed the sky in the hands of those who had chosen to stay.

Inside the Capsule’s heart, Hsin met a human-sized Suhsin made from every accusation preserved in the record: petitions about the absent Sentinel, Ministry minutes, frightened whispers that she had changed, and the repeated verdicts **Pet. Fault in the code. Not Her.** Hsin finally understood that Suhsin was not her mother’s judgment. It was Hsin’s own fear, which rot had entered through the cracked Capsule and worn as a coat.

She claimed the fear back. It could count the dead, bring every name, oppose her, and argue every decision. It could not keep proposing to burn the house merely because destruction balanced the column. Hsin broke a squashed Moon Fox rice cake in half and gave Suhsin the larger piece. The protocol insisted it did not eat. Hsin told it to eat anyway. When it bit the cake, it softened like frost on glass and folded into her. Eight tails remained gold. One kept a dark tip.

Hsin entered the rain-lit study inside the Capsule and sat on the corner of the desk where the kit had once pretended to be a paperweight. She did not take the empty chair.

## The Door Kept Open

Mei waited outside Hsin’s private room. Her golden eyes could have looked through the door, but she chose not to enter a grief that had not invited her. Yangyang slept in her feathered collar, and Iuno’s blue sash remained tied to Mei’s belt, running back up into the light. On the stoop above the restored seasons, Hsin catalogued the faults of the Hold she had chosen: compromise, impulse, indifference, mediocrity, mutton tallow, and a mahjong debt nobody would calculate honestly. Mei looked down at the two-hundred-year-old table still set in the Vale and called the place beautiful.

Hsin asked why Mei had refused the correction when an Arbiter was supposed to sign. Mei’s life supplied the answer. She had leaped from a cliff for Iuno, into the Nihility for Amy, and into other deaths for people no cosmic office would have considered an efficient expense. Hsin now belonged on that list of unreasonable commitments. If a future danger came through the seam that no lullaby could turn, Mei said, they would ask for a ladder—or a sash—and decide with the people who would bear the cost. Hsin promised that before she ever signed alone, she would count every roof and bring the ledger to Mei’s door for another pair of eyes.

Below them, Mengxuan Vale woke noisy. Artisans argued over plumb-lines; a man demanded his cot back; the mahjong table resumed before anyone agreed on the rules. Cartethyia won accidentally. Chisa coaxed Lupa down from a roof one rung at a time with a bun. Ruoyun bowed from the noodle-shop roof when Hsin descended. Suoming and Qingxiao sat bleeding and exhausted on the pavilion step. The perfect copper noon finally changed. The same lopsided moon that hung above Xuanfang rose over the Vale, and Mei’s frozen Terminal moved from 11:42 to 11:44.

Hsin let the Celestials speak her name. At the weiqi board she answered Qingxiao’s waiting white stone by placing black into a group that was already dead. Qingxiao pointed out the lost position. Hsin said she had only wanted to answer. Qingxiao told her to sit down and lose properly. Suoming shifted her umbrella so its shadow covered the fox.

The Vale’s founding command still said that those who built Xuanfang would never return. Hsin released the Celestials through the literal language that had once imprisoned them: since they had never left the Vale, they could not return to it; therefore they were merely going out for a walk. Ruoyun called it cheating. Hsin called it exact. When Suoming asked who would cross the boundary first, Hsin said she would. Longjue stepped barefoot beyond the stones before her, the first Celestial in living memory to do so, and the repeating cricket found a fourth note.

Yuan Fortress vanished from the south road. Relief columns that had spent four days in mud charged directly into festival lanes. The road and relay reopened. More than a hundred Celestials came down the Sanctum stair carrying their cot, tools, carpenter’s square, plumb-line, and entire mahjong table. A Mengzhou soldier called them “the Retained”; Ruoyun asked, with exquisite menace, who retained them and offered to write his answer down. A captain saluted Suoming as Intendant. She told him she had not held the office for years and sent his men to get buns.

The first grain cart carried Suisui’s red stamp. While everyone else fought inside Hsin’s heart, Yangyang’s sister had broken the blockade and brought food north. Yangyang kept her delayed letter another day so she could add what had happened. When officials asked for the Sentinel, Hsin had already slipped beneath a cart as a skinny fox with one dark-tipped tail and a candied haw in her mouth. Amy saw and kept the secret for the length of the lane.

The cookhouse counted twelve guests, so Lupa ordered a thirteenth chipped bowl for whoever was late. Amy placed it inside between Suoming and Qingxiao rather than outside on the step. Hsin entered claiming she was only passing. Lupa filled the bowl to the brim and told her to eat. Suoming supplied pickles; Qingxiao pushed over the salt. For the first time the absent ruler sat inside the ordinary law she had helped make possible: the late person received food, not reverence, and was expected to stay long enough to finish it.

The table refused to remain solemn. Yangyang and Mei confessed the inn’s single bed under Iuno’s raised eyebrow. Cartethyia accused Hsin of ruffling her hair; Chisa learned Hsin had giggled because her shears tickled. Iuno saw Mei’s cheek-to-cheek photograph with Yangyang and admitted jealousy without permitting anyone to console her out of it. She would choose when she wanted her own.

The harder question came from Qingxiao: when would Hsin tell the Hold who she was? Hsin tried to postpone the truth until after the festival. Ruoyun lowered her noodles in silence; Suoming and Qingxiao waited. Between them they bargained Hsin down to two days. Suoming would bring the umbrella.

Longjue kicked the door open with steamers of crooked fox-shaped rice cakes, the first thing the Vale’s workers had cooked on purpose in generations. She served Hsin last because the Sentinel owed her money. The older women recalled how Hsin had stolen hot cakes from the cooling racks every Waking Moon for two centuries. Hsin had believed nobody noticed. Everyone had noticed the crumbs on the ledgers. She bit off one crooked ear and closed her eyes.

The restored relay filled the Hold with calls from home. Chixia reached Yangyang from Jinzhou; Augusta’s shipboard messages reached Iuno. Suoming read one line on an old Ministry slate and placed it face down. Buling posted that Xuanfang had not **yet** fallen; Ruoyun deleted the “yet.” Hsin offered Qingxiao a Terminal. Warned she would lose it in a snowbank, Qingxiao replied that they should call louder.

High on the east wall, Hsin watched hundreds of sky lanterns rise without her hand lighting them. The people were no longer waiting for her. Qingxiao told her that their decision to continue was not a reproach. When Hsin said some would hate her after the truth, Qingxiao agreed. They would climb the wall and say so to her face, just as Qingxiao had. A cold candied haw gave Qingxiao a mortal toothache. When she shivered, Hsin laid the dark-tipped tail over her shoulders, and Qingxiao did not move away.

The Hold below was open, fed, and alive, but nothing had been absolved. The Prism was broken. The Moon Fox’s voice had passed through it once and gone. Balanophora’s root had been expelled from the Capsule, not destroyed. Hsin’s friends knew the original Sentinel was dead; her people did not. Suoming’s arm was rebound and her breath still scraped low in her chest. Yangyang’s feathered collar was scorched brown where she had struck the Prism. Iuno’s shoulder remained frost-cracked. Mei’s scar still tightened on stairs. Qingxiao was cold, hungry, and mortal. Survival had preserved every cost rather than making the column disappear.

## Names at the Well

On the night before the confession, Hsin carried two cups of plain white tea to the covered walkway above the dye-works. Suoming sat on its rail with Nine Shadows between her knees, speaking to the dead as she did each night. She described the procession below to people who could not answer: a knight losing a sleeve to a lantern pole, cakes trampled into the dirt, the noise they would have hated and secretly watched from the window.

Hsin noticed the intimacy and offered to leave. Suoming told her she had already climbed the stair and should put the tea down. Hsin’s cuffs were tucked tight around her trembling paws. Suoming ordered her to leave them loose in the square. Two hundred years of statues had taught the Hold to imagine a ruler without fear; the people needed to see that the hands responsible for their losses shook.

Hsin tried to apologize for failing Huating. Suoming refused. Hsin could not restore the nine companions Suoming had buried in the rain, and an apology offered as payment would only ask Suoming to close the account. As Intendant, Suoming had hidden the truth of Hsin’s identity because Mengzhou needed the roof she was building. As a sister to the dead, Suoming retained the right to anger. Holding an umbrella did not require liking the person beneath it.

Hsin then admitted how she had spent her hidden years. In the Capsule’s study she sat on the corner of her Ma’s desk and read broken registers and reports of drooping Vale leaves aloud. Her mother never answered. Suoming’s dead did not answer either. Her Sixth companion had hated ledgers and regularly abandoned tally meetings to speak with the people reduced to their columns. Something small and hard clicked inside Suoming’s coat, but she did not explain it. She taught Hsin how to speak to the dead anyway: begin with the sleeve, the cakes in the dirt, and one specific person; leave the slipping latch for last. Nobody wanted a list first.

At sunrise Huizhen Plaza filled without an official summons. Quarrymen, bakers, soldiers, parents, and widows stopped on their way to work. The burnt-out Moon Lanterns still hung overhead. Hsin stood beside the well on the same level as everyone’s boots, in her own shape, nine tails on the frost and one tipped dark. Suoming held the umbrella over her while leaving her own plum-coated shoulder in the sun.

Hsin told them that the original Sentinel had died at City Hall two hundred years earlier. Hsin was the fox she raised, the creature who took her name and coat because the country needed someone inside them. She also named what she had done afterward. She had bolted the Sanctum against the rot, trusted Muyu with the upper courts, hidden the failing barrier, and left the outlying villages and Wardens to pay for her closed doors. Duzhong, Qingzhi, Yanlang, and Mingyu died under that silence.

Butcher Sun walked out before she finished. Mingyu’s mother and a tanner demanded to know why they had been permitted to bow to an empty coat. Old Mother Pu tried to defend the fox who had carried braziers up winter scaffolds until her paws blistered; Hsin refused the defense. Good labor did not cancel later abandonment. Old Chen remembered what she had built without asking the families to call it even.

Hsin gave the Hold plain orders rather than another riddle. The southern cut would open. The storehouses would retain their full millet measure under Liangyu’s keys. The Vale gate would stay open. Minister Shen Qiu’s clerks could leave their shutters and walk the lanes. These instructions did not promise that the country would forgive or remain. They only returned decisions to the people living there.

At the cooper’s alley stood Qingyan, young enough for his dead sister Qingzhi’s broadsword to hang almost to his calves. He asked where Hsin had been. There was no answer large enough to survive the question. Hsin did not invent one.

As the crowd loosened and people returned to ovens, barracks, and homes, Hsin looked at the charred lantern frames overhead. She did not leap to the wires or command a mechanism. She asked if someone could bring a ladder. Liangyu carried the pine ladder from the cooper’s shed, set its iron feet beside the well, and braced the rails while Hsin climbed on shaking paws. The first burnt frame came down into Liangyu’s hand. Then the second.

Before noon, Old Chen and Suoming placed the cabbage-seller’s trestle by the well and opened a blank red-ruled ledger. Families came back with names. Butcher Sun returned to say that his nephew Sun Hu could never whistle, though he tried constantly. Others remembered favored food, snoring, a spear carried on the wrong shoulder, cold duck eggs cracked on a weapon’s butt, hatred of porridge before dawn watch. Hsin’s brush trembled. Ink blotted family names and soaked her sleeve.

Yangyang sat at the other end of the trestle, took up a spare brush, and shared the work. She did not turn the dead into evidence for or against Hsin. She asked who they had been. Qingyan came to Yangyang’s side of the table and told her that Qingzhi snored. Liangyu instructed Hsin not to improve the record of Mingyu’s bad temper or breakfast habits. The details remained uneven because the lives had been.

When the public queue ended, Hsin opened a loose, unruled leaf. She wrote Yuxian, the Warden Commander who entrusted her with his name; a single feather-stroke for the old Xuanling bird; Xuanqing; and her own name at the bottom. Yangyang read the short list back aloud because Hsin could not yet do it alone.

Suisui arrived after dark to bring Yangyang home for pear soup and their father’s letter asking whether the new feathers turned grey in winter. Hsin said Suisui had won their wager. Suisui looked at the empty place where the Prism had rested and at the sister who had returned with a scorched collar. She had won a stone, the stone was gone, and her sister was alive; she called it a draw. They agreed to play weiqi again someday with nothing at stake, and Suisui warned Hsin not to lose on purpose.

Hsin offered to put Suoming’s nine companions in the back of the ledger. Suoming declined. They were already written in her own record. When Hsin read this new book to her mother, Suoming repeated, she should begin with Sun Hu attempting to whistle and save the broken latch for last.

## The Last Feeling

An unknown message drew Mei and Yangyang to the broken edge of what remained of the Nexus. Abby traveled in the gold of Mei’s mark. There they met an old rust-red Autopuppet called Tuntun and a playful crimson-eared woman named Xuanqing. She knew Mei was the Arbiter and Yangyang the Xuanling despite having no ordinary way to learn either fact. More tellingly, her frequency carried the same grain Yangyang had heard inside the Prism when the Moon Fox asked her to carry a voice to its child.

Tuntun had bitten Xuanqing’s Feeling Capsule into pieces. Two fragments could be recovered, but the machine had swallowed the final shard, the Verdant Jade Flute, because it wanted to keep the gift. Xuanqing told it gently that gifts were made to be given away. Tuntun could not obey without surrendering the first desire that had ever belonged to itself. It fought, relinquished the shard, and was erased for breaking its directive. The loss completed Xuanqing and anticipated the choice at the heart of Hsin’s life: a created being’s deviation might be an error to a system and still be the beginning of a self.

Inside the repaired Capsule, Mei and Yangyang walked the Moon Fox’s memories. The Sentinel had taken a Resonator only because the Ministry demanded one, intending to choose someone frail and easy to protect. She scattered fox statues across Mengzhou and followed the one that glowed to Xuanqing’s border village. There she met Uncle Chu, tasted Yuecheng’s rice cakes, listened to a bright folk song, and found Xuanqing running to the pagoda. Xuanqing later blindfolded the austere Sentinel to give her a jade hairpin polished to sound like water. The Moon Fox answered with a fragrance pouch containing a shadow in her own form.

Xuanqing explained what Hsin truly was. The little fox had been born from the Moon Fox’s feeling, but a newborn mind could not receive the whole of that heart at once. The Moon Fox sealed her remaining emotion into a hundred small stories throughout the Nexus, meant to be lived one by one until Hsin could inherit the last portion as a Sentinel in her own right. One story had glitched and become the Heart of Ruin.

The memories carried them into Ovathrax’s attack. Mingting’s Oracle Engine calculated that the Xuanfang borderlands should be sacrificed for civilization’s continuity. Xuanqing understood the arithmetic and went to the front anyway, calling her reasons selfish so nobody could forbid them. Through the Moon Fox’s shadow, Mei sheltered villagers and wounded soldiers while Yangyang lit a dying soldier’s flare so his comrades would withdraw. They found Xuanqing already Overclocked at a choke point. She asked for a Second Awakening without the Arbiter’s authorization. It would consume her and hold the Discords for only three days. She called the exchange a bargain. The Moon Fox took her hand, approved it, and entrusted the Waking Moon lanterns of another year to the time Xuanqing bought.

As they repaired the Heart of Ruin, it grew warm enough to reveal Hsin’s forbidden dream. Uncle Chu lived. Yuecheng’s shop stood whole. Nannan grew into a Warden without losing her sister. Xuanqing ruled the Hold. The Moon Fox was never ambushed. No one died because Xuanfang had been built soon enough. Hsin had carried this impossible question for two hundred years: if she had moved faster, been wiser, or been more like the Sentinel, could every necessary loss have become unnecessary?

Xuanqing laughed through tears at the perfect false country. A Sentinel blaming herself for mortal deaths seemed strange to her and also proved exactly what kind of Sentinel Hsin had become. She told Mei that Mei and Hsin were cut from the same cloth: saviors who carried too much, smiled at doors, and continued to cry for those beyond their reach. With the final memory lit, Xuanqing entrusted the completed Feeling Capsule to the Xuanling and faded.

On the last night of the festival, floury fox prints led Mei and Yangyang from the bakers’ cooling racks to the dye-works walkway. Hsin sat there beside Suoming with four stolen cakes and flour on her chin. Yangyang placed the warm Capsule in her paws and said it came from Sister Xuanqing.

The Sixth Seal in Suoming’s Providence Lock grew hot.

Long before the knife fell at City Hall, after Xuanqing’s death, the Moon Fox had asked Suoming to preserve Xuanqing’s frequency for the little fox and promised that Suoming would know when Hsin was ready. Suoming had released the trace of her own Sixth companion to make room. Now she touched the Seal to the Capsule and held the Lock level despite her shaking arms.

Xuanqing appeared in festival smoke. She could not see the women on the walkway and looked instead toward the height of a kit perched on a desk. Hsin changed into her skinny fox shape and curled in the empty hollow of Xuanqing’s white skirt. Xuanqing teased the unseen child, regretted that she had not been there to brush her fur, and assured her that the choices she made had fulfilled her Ma’s wish. Hsin, who had called one dead woman Ma for two centuries, finally paid the other name she owed: Xuanqing Jie-jie. Mama.

Xuanqing crouched and cupped her hands around the height of the kit’s face. They passed through the adult fox, but Hsin placed her muzzle within their light and held perfectly still so the hands would never have to search. Xuanqing called her the only thing both women had ever needed her to be: their daughter.

She raised a dented brass harmonica and began the old song asking whether old acquaintance should be forgotten. She hit the wrong reed in the second line, as she always had, and vanished in the middle of a breath. Suoming sang through the catch in her left lung and completed the verse. Then she placed a solid hand on Hsin’s head. It did not pass through.

Mei recognized Xuanqing’s bright, protective smile because she had worn the same expression when leaving Amy and when trying to turn pain into a gift. She wept without making the moment hers. Later, when Iuno asked what happened, Mei said only that someone had turned around at the stair. Iuno requested the portion that belonged to Mei when Mei was ready to give it.

Ciaccona caught Xuanqing’s unfinished tune on her lute as the procession passed below. The Hold took it up under the fireworks. Qingyan climbed a barrel to watch; Butcher Sun tried to whistle; Mingyu’s mother opened her shutter; Liangyu and her Wardens named Duzhong, Qingzhi, Yanlang, and Mingyu, then poured the last drink onto the frost for Lingfang. The names were spoken to the sky and to neighboring shoulders, not offered to Hsin for custody. Hsin listened in fox shape and let the fur beneath her eyes grow wet. She pushed the earless cake onto an empty patch of parapet. Lupa added a fried egg because an empty place at supper received food.

At the cistern later, the household released misprinted sky lanterns with upside-down foxes. Mei wished that everybody at the table would return at the same time next year. Suoming wrote the character for rain. Ruoyun silently corrected a stroke in Buling’s wish that her teacher remain a long time. Iuno wrote four strokes while Mei held the frame and did not ask her to explain. Hsin covered her lantern in the day’s small news for her mothers, including the promise to fix the third-terrace latch, and drew a fox so round it barely resembled one. The lantern righted itself as it rose.

The newly hungry Celestials overwhelmed the cookhouse, so the gathering moved to the Fillet Fairmaiden’s. Proprietress Peng Shulan fed them without charge. Hsin, unable to receive a free meal without turning it into debt, asked for Shulan’s and her son’s full names. Shulan told her to return on an ordinary night and pay. Longjue reduced the ancient mahjong debt by one string after making Hsin drink sixty-day sorghum until all nine tails bristled; by the time dinner ended, Longjue claimed the total had increased again.

Neither the Vale nor the Sanctum would house Hsin that night. Suoming lent her own two rooms to displaced Celestials and declared she would sleep wherever Hsin did. Lupa offered the salt-house kang to anyone without a floor. Yangyang took the place nearest the Sentinel because she could hear danger even in sleep. Cartethyia combed every knot from the dark-tipped tail until the black fur lay smooth with the gold. Amy discovered that a fox bound never to lie made an excellent truth-or-dare partner. Mei learned that somewhere in the wider world existed a turtle Sentinel larger than a house and immediately wanted to visit after Septimont.

They packed twenty sleepers sideways onto the warm brick. Amy slept between her mothers with the tin whistle around her neck. Qingxiao slept deeply with a mortal runny nose and an aching tooth. Hsin curled with her combed tail over her face. Suoming remained upright on a crate by the door, Nine Shadows across her knees, keeping watch without pretending the watch was forgiveness.

Down on the lower quay, Nivora and Balanophora shared two quarter-price fox cakes in borrowed bodies. Nivora regarded Muyu as an experiment whose debris had at least yielded a result. She had seen the butcher return to the square and knew Hsin had taken up a pen. Balanophora dropped her ruined cake into the river. Rot could wait. The Hold would shout at its false Sentinel for a year, and every shout might become another opening. Nivora folded her paper into a pocket and agreed that the shouting would come.

Yet Xuanfang no longer resembled the country at the beginning of Hsin’s reckoning. Then, the south road ended at a resurrected fortress, the relay was dead, the Vale’s perfect workers could not cross their own boundary, the Sanctum was locked, and the Sentinel existed as an absence behind a frozen door. Hsin carried a dead woman’s office, a nation’s losses, her mother’s last command, and her own fear in separate sealed rooms. She believed the Hold survived only because nobody saw her hands shake, and that saving it might require deleting the self who had made it a home.

Now the road stood open and grain carts moved through it. The relay carried quarrels, jokes, instructions, and calls from home. Mengxuan Vale’s gate remained open; its Celestials had learned hunger, chosen their own cooking, and walked beyond the stones. Suhsin had not been destroyed but accepted back into Hsin as the dark-tipped tail that could object, count, and remember without being permitted to burn the house for balance. The Prism was gone, the original Sentinel remained dead, and no restored god waited inside the chair. Hsin sat on its corner when she visited and read aloud from a ledger that began with how people ate, whistled, slept, complained, and carried their spears.

The Hold knew who she was. Some people left the square. Others returned. Some would despise her, some would defend her too quickly, and many would spend years doing both. Hsin had ceased deciding in advance what their grief was allowed to mean. She left the ledger out beneath a stone by the well for the names tomorrow would bring.

Nothing had come out even. Xuanqing and the Moon Fox remained dead. Yuan Fortress had fallen. Suoming’s nine did not answer. Four Wardens had died while Hsin hid. Qingxiao’s mortality would not reverse; Mei, Yangyang, Iuno, and Suoming carried their wounds forward. Balanophora still waited at the edge of the city’s cracks.

But Hsin no longer mistook an unpaid cost for a command to refuse the life it had made possible. Her mothers’ wish survived in hot ovens, crooked cakes, quarrels at a crowded table, and a city capable of shouting at its ruler. The fox who began by running without looking back ended asleep among other bodies, under no throne and behind no sealed door. Beside the well, her untidy book remained open. Above it, the burnt lanterns were down. Beyond it, the Vale gate stood open. And by the salt-house door, beneath the umbrella of a woman still entitled to her anger, someone stayed awake until morning.

# Local News

### I. Lahai-Roi

The folding lamp collapsed halfway through Lynae’s explanation and struck her examiner on the wrist.

He caught the shade with one hand and lifted his tea with the other.

Lynae held the base against the bench. Across the workshop, somebody switched off a lathe. The sudden quiet carried the small, continuing squeak of the joint.

“I’d like to begin with the fastening,” she said. “It’s just become the most interesting part.”

The examiner rubbed his wrist, then brought the lamp back upright.

“Show me what happened.”

She put her finger beside the adjustment screw. There was a bright crescent in the metal where the washer had slipped. She had seen the first scratch yesterday. At the time, tightening the screw had seemed adequate.

“The slot’s deforming. Each time I reposition the arm, the washer presses against the same edge. I should have spread the load over more of the plate.”

“You’ve brought three sample joints. Which would you fit?”

Lynae chose the widest one. The sleeve of her jacket caught a corner of the drawing, exposing the earlier design underneath. She had crossed that design out so thoroughly that the paper had buckled.

He pulled it free and laid the two versions together.

They spent the remaining time discussing the version she had tried to hide.

Outside, Sigrika was sitting on the low wall with a sandwich in each hand. She held one up as Lynae approached.

“Yours has the pickled cabbage. I asked her to put it in a separate wrapper because last time it soaked through the bread.”

Lynae set the lamp down between them.

“He’s keeping the report until the written marks come back. Also, I assaulted a member of faculty with adjustable lighting.”

Sigrika examined the joint while Lynae described it. A little oil had worked beneath her thumbnail by the time she gave the lamp back.

“Could you put the lever here? You’d be able to reach it while you were reading.”

“That’s where it started. Then I moved it because the drawing looked better.”

Sigrika looked at her over the sandwich.

Lynae laughed with her mouth full and had to turn away.

“I have suffered enough academic scrutiny for one morning. Let me eat.”

They sat with their heels against the warm stone. Beyond the workshops, Helios brightened along the avenue of Solistrees. Students crossed the yard carrying rolled plans and safety goggles, steering around a delivery cart whose driver had fallen asleep over his lunch.

Sigrika was watching a bird pull fibres from the cart’s seat.

“There were two of those outside my window yesterday. I thought they were fighting over the sill. Then one started stuffing things into the drain.”

“Does your drain work?”

“I’ve put a basin underneath it. The groundskeeper’s coming on Friday. He said he’d look at moving the nest once the eggs hatch.” She took another bite. “I’d like to see the parents feeding them. You can get a very good view from my desk.”

Lynae followed her gaze. The bird braced both feet against the seat cushion and hauled until a thread came loose. It staggered backward, recovered, and flew away trailing upholstery.

“Wednesday, then. I’ll bring the cards, and we can watch the drain between hands.”

“Come earlier this time. They settle down when the lights change.”

Sigrika brushed crumbs into her palm and carried them to the base of a tree. Lynae ate the last of her sandwich while the lamp leaned slowly against her knee.

Back at the dorm, Rebecca had commandeered the kitchen counter for a receiver, two lengths of cable, and Ciaccona’s spare lute. She was trying to hold a tuning peg steady with a tool intended for something considerably larger.

Lynae put down the lamp.

“Give me the neck before that becomes an engineering project too.”

Rebecca handed it over, keeping one hand under the body.

“The E keeps moving. I get the other three where I want them, then this bastard wanders off.”

“Cia mentioned the E when she gave it to you.”

“She mentioned a lot of stuff. I was concentrating on where my fingers went.”

Lynae sat on the floor and worked the peg with her fingertips. Rebecca lowered herself beside her. From the other room came Chiyo’s voice, carefully pronouncing the name of a tram stop into her terminal, then trying it again.

The lamp stood on the counter above them. Its arm sagged another inch.

Rebecca looked up.

“When you’re done with mine, I’ve got a suggestion about yours.”

### II. Septimont

Angel booked the dance lesson under her own name and arrived with Augusta carrying the shoes.

The studio occupied the upper floor of a bathhouse. Warmth rose through the boards, bringing a faint mineral smell. Someone downstairs laughed loudly enough to interrupt the music.

Their teacher glanced at Augusta, glanced at the booking sheet, and came forward to take their coats.

“We’ll start at the window. The floor’s more even there.”

Augusta put her shoes on while Angel inspected a potted vine whose leaves had yellowed along the edges.

“That wants a larger pot,” Angel told the teacher.

“My husband says it wants less attention. He’s moved it three times this week.”

“I’ll bring you one on Saturday. There are roots coming through the bottom.”

Augusta stood and tested her weight on the old hip. Angel’s eyes dropped briefly to her feet.

“I’ve warmed up,” Augusta said. “You watched me do it.”

Angel came closer and offered her hand.

They had practised in the garden through the winter. The garden had allowed Augusta to choose the pace, the direction, and the point at which a difficult turn became an inspection of the rose. Here, the teacher put a record on and asked them to cross the room.

Augusta reached the far wall several beats ahead of it.

“You’re anticipating the turn,” the teacher said. “Let your partner finish moving before you take the next step.”

“I know where she’s going.”

“Then you can afford to give her time.”

Angel’s mouth moved against Augusta’s shoulder.

“You’ve been waiting to hear someone say that,” Augusta murmured.

“I’m enjoying the lesson. You paid for the whole hour.”

They began again. Augusta watched their reflection in the windows until the teacher drew the curtains across it.

“Look at each other. The glass will still be there afterward.”

Angel’s hand settled more firmly at her shoulder. There was a little scar near the base of her thumb that Augusta could feel through the softened glove. She followed it with her own thumb while they counted.

Halfway through the next turn, her hip caught.

Angel shortened her step. Augusta put her foot down carefully, then led them toward the chairs.

“Water first,” she said. “We can try that side again after.”

The teacher went downstairs to fill the jug. Angel crouched to loosen the strap of Augusta’s shoe.

“I can manage another hole.”

“Your foot’s swelling over this one.” Angel eased the buckle free. “There. Tell me how that feels.”

Augusta stretched her toes. Beyond the door, someone was arguing over an extra towel.

“I liked the first part,” she said. “Before she added the turn.”

“I could hear you humming when we started. It helped me keep the count.”

“There was a man who played it at the baths years ago. He stretched the last part until everyone joined in.”

Angel looked up at her. A strand of hair had worked loose against her cheek, and Augusta tucked it behind her ear.

“We could ask for it again.”

When the teacher returned, Angel did.

They finished the hour with a shorter sequence. Augusta had the count by the third attempt. On the fourth, Angel missed a step because she was smiling at her.

They bought lunch downstairs, wrapped in paper already growing translucent with oil. At the corner, a boy carrying practice shields recognised Augusta and nearly dropped the stack. She held the upper shield in place with two fingers while he adjusted his grip.

“Use the strap around the whole bundle. You’ll save your wrist.”

He thanked her and hurried away. Angel watched him go, then took a piece of fried bread from Augusta’s packet.

“That advice would have been useful at the studio.”

“I was using both wrists.”

They ate as they walked. At home, Cub greeted the shoes with more enthusiasm than either woman considered appropriate. Angel retrieved one from his mouth and took it into the garden to inspect the damage.

The rose had reached the lower corner of Iuno’s window. A new shoot lay across the sill, flexible and crowded with pale thorns.

Augusta held it away from the glass while Angel found a tie.

“I could cut this one back,” Angel said. “She’ll have it in her room by summer.”

“Let me ask her on the next call.”

Angel fastened the shoot loosely to the trellis. Augusta stayed beside her with the end of the tie between finger and thumb.

From a neighbouring house came the tune they had just spent an hour learning. Someone sang along, losing the words in the middle.

Angel held out her hand.

Augusta left the garden shoes by the wall.

### III. Ragunna

Carlotta found Cantarella in the glasshouse with her sleeves rolled up and a pear balanced on a medicine jar.

“Your tea is beside the door, darling. I suggest drinking it while I’m still allowed to move.”

At the other end of the table, a young illustrator was washing a brush. Sheets of botanical studies lay beneath glass weights: leaves turned to show their undersides, seed capsules opened along a seam, a root divided to expose its milky centre.

Carlotta put her gloves on a dry corner.

“I thought the Trust’s plates were finished.”

“The apothecaries requested the look-alikes. Some of them have been bringing examples from their own gardens.” Cantarella turned the pear slightly. “Nella brought lunch. We’ve progressed to that.”

“You can take your hand away now, Duchess,” the illustrator said. “I’ve got the shadow.”

Cantarella flexed her fingers. Carlotta came around to look.

On the page, the pear’s green skin carried a patch of yellow light. A brown bruise marked its lower curve. The glass beneath it had been caught in a few pale strokes, with the far side of the fruit showing through.

Carlotta bent closer.

“You’ve made that jar look thinner than it feels.”

Nella held it out to her. “Try looking through this side. The rim makes it seem heavier.”

Carlotta lifted the jar and turned toward the window. Cantarella reached for the pear before it could roll onto a wet study.

“Now you’ve joined us, you may as well sit down.”

There was a clean sheet beside Nella’s elbow. A second brush rested across a saucer.

Carlotta looked at them, then at the tea tray.

“How long does a pear take?”

“I’ve been asking that since lunch,” Cantarella said.

Nella pulled the spare chair around.

Carlotta began with the outline. The pear widened as she worked. She paused, loaded the brush, and widened the opposite side to match.

Cantarella watched over her cup.

“You’ve given it very comfortable quarters.”

“The perspective is still developing.”

Nella came to stand behind her. Carlotta’s hand slowed under the attention.

“May I show you on the edge of the sheet?”

Carlotta moved her wrist aside.

The illustrator laid down a patch of water, touched pigment to one end, and let it travel. Carlotta watched the colour gather at the rim.

“You can leave a little of the paper showing here. That gives you the light.”

Carlotta tried it. Her wet sleeve dragged across the first outline.

Cantarella supplied a cloth. Then she took the tea tray to a bench beneath the open roof vent, where the cups could stay clear of the work.

The afternoon shifted around them. A gardener came in carrying a crate of empty pots. Cantarella asked about the drainage bed and went outside with him, wiping her hands as she walked. Carlotta remained at the table.

When the Duchess returned, a second pear had appeared on the page. Its stalk leaned sharply to the left.

“That one has your expression,” Cantarella said.

Carlotta sat back. There was green on the side of her little finger.

“I’ve been concentrating for nearly an hour. It seems entitled to share the burden.”

Nella had packed the dry studies between boards. Carlotta asked her price for a lesson the following Thursday. The young woman gave it, then began explaining how little of the hour had been formal instruction.

“I enjoyed the hour,” Carlotta said. “Bring another brush. This one and I have reached an understanding.”

They finished their tea on the bench. Cantarella cut the pear into quarters with a small knife and passed the plate across.

Carlotta picked up the bruised piece, inspected the painted version on the table, and ate it.

“Your daughter would have named mine by now.”

Cantarella’s knife rested against the plate.

“She would have given it a profession. Possibly a household.”

“Then I shall ask her opinion when she has time.”

Carlotta carried the picture home between two pieces of card. At her desk that evening, she set it against a stack of correspondence while she changed for dinner.

The stalk leaned toward her chair.

She moved the correspondence to the other side of the desk.

### IV. The Sea Between

Laevatain bought the little thumb piano because the man selling it played three notes she liked.

He played them again while she watched his hands. A box of cheap instruments stood at his feet, each wrapped in the same brown paper. She chose the one he was holding.

On the crossing to Septimont, she sat beside a coil of rope and tried to find the notes.

The deckhand came past twice. On the third pass, he slowed.

“My brother’s got one of those. He catches the metal with his nail.”

Laevatain examined her thumb.

“That explains the amount of work I’ve been doing.”

He showed her on the instrument’s lowest tine, leaving a round note humming against the wood. She copied him, and the vibration ran into her palm.

“There you go. You’ll hear it better if you sit against the cabin wall.”

She moved her kit. For the next hour, the wood at her back carried every mistake into the small dining room beyond it.

A passenger opened the door and requested a different tune.

“I’m working toward having one,” Laevatain said. “You’ve caught the early stages.”

He brought his cup outside and sat a few feet away. Eventually he began tapping the rail. His rhythm steadied hers.

At Mya’s cliff, the viburnum had flowered.

Laevatain put her pack down where the ground was dry. She cleared a fallen stem from the planted sword, checked the earth around it, and sat facing the sea.

The instrument fitted inside the space between her belt and her knees. One of the stabilizer spheres passed close enough to stir the paper wrapping.

“The wolf’s in Huanglong,” she said. “She’s travelling in a yellow truck. There are pictures if you’d like the full experience.”

She found the first note with her thumbnail.

“I bought this at the harbour. The fellow made it sound easy, which was effective salesmanship.”

The second note rang. She missed the third and tried the whole phrase again.

A bee settled in the flowers beside the sword. Far below, a boat worked its way around the point, its sail briefly bright against the water.

Laevatain played until her thumbs grew sore. Before leaving, she took a photograph of the viburnum with enough of the cliff in it for Lupa to recognise the place.

Back in Ragunna, Phoebe found her in the Guild kitchen, studying the instrument beside a bowl of water.

“Gerald’s downstairs,” Phoebe said. “He’s waiting for the tide to turn, and he’s asked twice about the music.”

“That carries through the floor?”

“He says the basin helps.”

Laevatain put the instrument down. “I’ve been providing an entire fish with a concert.”

Phoebe pulled out a chair.

“Play the bit you’ve been practising. I think I can hear where it wants to go.”

She hummed while Laevatain found the notes. Her first suggestion sent the tune into a bright, looping phrase that required Laevatain to cross both thumbs and lose her place.

“You’re assuming a level of competence I expect to have next year.”

Phoebe laughed, then sang it more slowly. Laevatain followed her through it, concentrating on the gap between two tines.

Zani arrived carrying her supper and listened from the doorway. After a while she set the plate down and fetched Gerald’s basin.

“He was making suggestions to the stairs. This seemed easier.”

The fish turned a dignified circle beneath the kitchen light.

“The passage immediately before your stumble was promising. I should be pleased to hear it developed.”

“You and the man on the ferry can start a society,” Laevatain said. She shifted her thumbs. “I’ll go from the beginning.”

Zani sat beside Phoebe and began eating. The tune faltered, recovered, and ran into the same difficult turn. Phoebe hummed them back to it.

By closing time, Laevatain had played it through twice.

Gerald requested an encore. She made him wait while she finished Zani’s bread.

Phoebe reached across to brush a crumb from Zani’s cuff. Under it, the cloth had worn thin at the elbow.

“You’ve got the blue one in the wardrobe. I put it where you’d see it.”

“That one scratches my neck.”

“You chose it yourself. You spent half an hour talking to the woman about the lining.”

Zani ran her thumb along the frayed seam.

“I liked the way it looked in the mirror. Then I wore it for a whole morning.”

Phoebe glanced at Laevatain, who was trying to lift a crumb out from under a tine with a fingernail.

“We could take it back on our way to the harbour tomorrow. She could change the collar.”

“Tomorrow I’d like to walk straight to the boat.” Zani put her hand over Phoebe’s on the table. “We’ve been carrying that picnic basket past errands for three weeks. I want the basket, the blanket, and the place you keep describing.”

Phoebe turned her hand under Zani’s and laced their fingers.

“There’s a little beach beyond the landing. You can lie under the pines while I look for shells.”

“Put that coat over me when I fall asleep. It ought to be useful for something.”

They took the early ferry. Laevatain found their breakfast things drying by the sink when she came downstairs, and a note asking her to eat the remaining bread while it was fresh.

### V. Lahai-Roi

Rebecca learned the fish stall by its smell and the woman behind it by her sleeves, which she rolled to different heights.

On their third visit, Chiyo let Rebecca do the buying.

“That one,” Rebecca said, pointing. “And I want the head with it this time. She made soup out of the last one while I was in the shower.”

The fishwife lifted the fish by its tail.

“You liked the soup, then.”

“I had three bowls. Apparently that’s an answer you can read off a person.”

Chiyo chose a packet of dried mushrooms from the next tray. Rebecca watched the knife go beneath a gill, turn, and come away clean. The woman wrapped the head separately and showed her where she had left the flesh along the collar.

“There’s a good piece on each side. Work around the bone with the tip.”

Rebecca leaned closer. “Could you show me that turn again?”

They missed the tram Chiyo had intended to catch. On the next one, Rebecca stood over the bag of fish while Chiyo sat beside a woman she knew from the noodle stand. Their conversation moved between two languages and several neighbours.

At one point, Chiyo touched Rebecca’s sleeve and introduced her.

Rebecca caught her own name, the dormitory, and a phrase she had heard often enough at meals to recognise.

“She’s telling you I eat a lot.”

The other woman smiled and replied at length. Chiyo translated when she finished.

“She says her son used to hold the cupboard open while he decided what to eat. She charged him for the heat in winter.”

“That’s a business opportunity Lupa’s going to fucking love.”

The woman asked what she had said. Chiyo translated that too.

By the time they reached the dorm, Rebecca had an invitation to a supper, directions to a cheaper fish stall, and a paper bag of small sour plums somebody had pressed into her hand at the stop.

Lynae was at the table with a textbook propped against her repaired lamp. She looked into the shopping bag.

“You’ve brought back half the market.”

“We’re frying the good bits. The rest is tomorrow’s problem.” Rebecca set a plum beside the book. “Eat that and tell me if your face survives.”

They prepared the fish together. Chiyo stood at Rebecca’s left and guided the angle with two fingers against her wrist. The knife felt almost weightless in her chrome hand. Rebecca shortened her grip until she could feel the board beneath the edge.

“There. Follow that bone,” Chiyo said. “You can feel where it curves.”

Rebecca eased the flesh free. A ragged strip remained attached near the collar.

She set the blade against it, looked at Chiyo, and finished the cut herself.

Later, while the stock simmered, she brought the lute into the kitchen. Her fingertips had begun to acquire small hard patches in places her guns had always left smooth.

Chiyo was sorting mending at the table. She looked up when Rebecca found the first of Ciaccona’s four notes.

“Your fingers sound more settled this evening.”

“The second one’s good now. I can get it whenever I want. The other three take turns cooperating.”

She played again. Chiyo threaded a needle, listening.

After a few repetitions, the older woman hummed something over the top. Rebecca stopped to hear it.

“Keep playing,” Chiyo said. “I’m trying to find the beginning.”

Rebecca put her fingers back.

The tune came in pieces. Chiyo tried one phrase, paused over her sewing, and began further along. Rebecca settled into the four notes until her hand could repeat them while she listened.

Lynae came down from her room carrying a charger. She stopped in the doorway, leaned against the frame, and stayed there through the next verse.

“Do you remember what that’s called?” Rebecca asked when Chiyo finished.

“My husband used to whistle it while he fixed things. I remember the middle better than the rest.” She drew the thread through. “Chisa may remember. He used to pick her up for the high part.”

Rebecca looked at the strings. Then she put the lute carefully on the bench and fetched her deck.

“Do it again while you’ve got it. I’ll send her this bit, and she can send the beginning back.”

They recorded three attempts. In the first, the stock boiled over. In the second, Lynae bit into a plum beside the microphone.

Rebecca kept the third and saved the second in another folder.

### VI. The Reports

Lynae’s materials paper came back with eighty-six written at the top.

She read the comments in the corridor. Then she sat on the windowsill and read them again, holding the page close enough for two passing students to ask whether she needed the infirmary.

The examiner had awarded full marks for the account of the failed joint. Alongside her final calculation, he had written a question about repeated loading. She knew how to answer it. She found that so pleasing she took a pen out there and began.

Rebecca was halfway through frying onions when Lynae arrived home.

“I’ve got a speech,” Lynae announced. “It contains a practical demonstration, so you’ll want a seat with a view.”

Rebecca reached toward the receiver to lower the volume.

The word *Norfall* came through beneath her hand.

She left the dial where it was.

The report took several minutes. Lynae stood beside the table with the paper hanging from her fingers. Chiyo came in while the newsreader was describing the rain and remained beside the stove until he finished.

Rebecca stirred the onions. The lower layer had browned heavily against the pan.

“They gave a time for the next one,” she said. “I’ll get the aerial higher before then.”

She mounted the receiver speaker above the cupboard because that was where they could hear it from the stairs. Lynae held the bracket while Rebecca reached for a screw. Her foot slipped along the narrow counter edge; her hand caught the cupboard door, and the lower hinge came away with a small square of wood attached.

They stood with the door hanging between them.

“I’ve got it,” Rebecca said. “Get your foot clear of the pan.”

Lynae stepped down. Together they lifted the door back into place and secured the loose side with cord. It sat crooked, leaving a gap through which the cups showed.

The next bulletin arrived on time. Rebecca copied down the figures and the place names, leaving room beside the ones she wanted to check.

By morning, the receiver had a clear signal. Lynae’s bike had a full tank. Her travel bag stood beneath the bed, where she could reach it from the door.

Inside it, the printout Mei had signed on the loading dock lay in a clear sleeve. Lynae opened it to the promise on the back: Sigrika would be told when they came home. She read the line with her finger beneath it, then put the sleeve at the top of the bag.

She went to Sigrika’s at the usual hour carrying the cards and forgot the food.

Sigrika looked at her empty hand, fetched her coat, and took her back out.

“The kitchen’s serving until eight. We can bring it upstairs.”

They waited in the queue together. A girl further ahead was telling her friends about an examination she intended to appeal. Lynae listened while the girl described each question, her gestures growing wider around the tray.

Sigrika took two portions of potatoes.

“You can look at your terminal while we eat,” she said. “I’d like you to sit with me.”

They carried the trays to her room. The parents were feeding the nest beneath the window. Lynae watched one bird arrive with an insect almost as long as its bill.

On the desk, Sigrika had left the card scores from last Wednesday.

Lynae put her terminal beside them with the sound turned up.

In Septimont, Priya brought the same news to Augusta’s kitchen. The Ephor read the dispatch, asked which relay had carried it, and sent her to fetch the map with the coastal routes.

Angel laid supper beside Augusta’s hand. The copper wire beneath the terminal had wound itself tightly around one leg.

Augusta ate while she read. Once, she put her fork down and pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes.

“I keep placing her beside the wrong water,” she said. “The names are close enough that I hear ours first.”

Angel turned the map toward her and rested a finger on the marked road.

Augusta followed it from the city to the northern positions. When she reached the end, she put her hand over Angel’s and kept it there while Priya gave the next report.

In Ragunna, Alice pinned the bulletins above the Northern Desk until the latest sheet touched the ceiling.

Zani brought a ladder from the storeroom.

“I’ll hold this while you move the older ones across. We can use the whole wall.”

Alice climbed with the pins between her lips. Halfway up, she took them out and looked down.

“Would you read it to me while I do this? I keep reaching the same paragraph.”

Zani opened her copy. She read slowly enough for Alice to smooth each page before fastening it.

Phoebe came in carrying a fresh pot and stood at the bottom of the ladder. When Zani reached the end, Alice asked for the passage about the returning company again.

This time, Phoebe read it.

### VII. Ragunna, Toward Supper

The afternoon they learned that the household was back in Jinzhou, Laevatain was holding a cupboard against the wall in a client’s kitchen.

Her terminal sounded in her coat. The carpenter had three screws left.

“Finish the top corner,” she told him. “I’ll keep it level.”

The message was brief enough to read with one hand while her shoulder took the weight. She read it, put the terminal away, and waited for him to drive the last screw home.

“Does that mean good news?” he asked.

“People I know have reached their lodging. They’ve had a bad few days.”

“Go and see to them. I can put the shelves back.”

She checked the cupboard’s weight before stepping away, collected her coat, and left her fee for him to bring to the Guild.

At Porto Veno, Carlotta’s Thursday lesson had reached the stage where she was willing to let Nella see a drawing before it was dry.

Cantarella brought the news into the glasshouse. She held the message above the table until Carlotta had found a clean place for it.

“They’re resting at the guesthouse. I’ve asked for a call when Cartethyia has eaten.”

Carlotta read it once, then went back over the names with the side of her finger. Nella continued rinsing the brushes, letting the water run clear between colours.

“There’s more from the Guild,” Carlotta said, looking at the second sheet.

“An account of the little paper figures. Alice sent it with the bulletin.”

Cantarella sat down while Carlotta read aloud.

In the garden, the sprinkler turned with a regular click. Sunlight moved across the wet path. The Duchess’s hand lay beside the page, close to a smear of green Carlotta had left earlier.

When the reading was finished, she reached for the little painting and held it up.

“May I show her this?”

Carlotta looked at the pear. Its latest incarnation occupied most of the sheet.

“You may tell her it’s a work in progress.”

“She’ll enjoy giving you advice.”

“I shall prepare a defence before Thursday.”

Cantarella smiled. She set the painting on a dry shelf and went to fetch the terminal from the house.

Carlotta stayed to help Nella put away the brushes. She spoiled the crease of one glove with a damp thumb, looked at the stain, and folded both gloves into her bag.

At the Guild, Zani carried the terminal down the front steps while Phoebe followed with supper. Alice remained inside, shifting the ladder so the newest report would sit where people entering could read it.

Phoebe put the plate on her knees and moved closer to Zani.

“I keep thinking of Alice’s father. When he recognised her in the chapel, she had her arms around him before any of us moved.”

Zani rested the terminal on the step above them.

“She asked for that part of the report four times today.”

A cart passed, its wheel rattling over a loose paving stone. Phoebe waited for it to clear the corner.

“I’d like to hear who walked with them.”

Zani’s shoulder settled against hers. Phoebe passed her a piece of bread, then held the plate steady while she ate.

At the far end of the street, Laevatain appeared between two market awnings. She was running with her coat over one arm. As she approached the steps, she put it on, slowing to work her hand through the sleeve.

Phoebe moved the supper to make room.

### VIII. Lahai-Roi, Breakfast

Rebecca had learned to let the pan get hot before putting the fish in.

She stood beside it with a spatula, watching the edge of the skin. Chiyo had gone to the kitchen at her own place for the call with Chisa; she had left instructions for the stock and taken a portion upstairs for the neighbour who lent her an iron.

Lynae’s coursework covered the desk beside the dorm terminal. She had taken the final page into the workshop that morning and added a drawing beneath the examiner’s question. In the corner she had sketched the cupboard hinge.

Rebecca leaned over to look.

“If my contribution gets you extra marks, I want breakfast included.”

“You’re already eating breakfast. You’ve been eating it straight out of the pan.”

“I’m checking how it’s going. There’s a process.”

Lynae drew the paper clear of a drop of oil. The eighty-six had acquired a faint thumbprint beneath it from being shown to people.

She had shown Mornye that morning. The professor had asked about the lamp, and Lynae had spent several minutes demonstrating the new lever while Buddy investigated the light inside the shade. On the way out, she had realised she was going to tell the whole thing again when the household called.

She arranged the pages with the first drawing on top.

“I’m starting with the wrist incident,” she said. “They should have the full story before I show them the mark.”

Rebecca worked the spatula beneath the fish. The skin lifted cleanly from the pan.

“Tell them he saved his tea. That’s the detail I liked.”

The receiver on the cupboard gave a brief crackle. Both of them looked toward it. A local advertisement began, bright and familiar, and Rebecca turned back to the stove.

Lynae reached beneath the desk for the charger. Her heel touched the strap of the travel bag. She drew the cable over it and plugged the terminal in.

On the screen, the connection indicator changed colour.

Rebecca picked up the frying pan.

In Septimont, Augusta had the seed catalogue open at the kitchen table. Angel was washing soil from beneath her nails when she heard Augusta laugh.

In Ragunna, Phoebe balanced supper on her knees while Laevatain took the step below. Cantarella set a watering can down beside her chair and drew the terminal closer.

Lynae saw Mei’s face appear, too near the camera, with Amy reaching across her shoulder to adjust it.

The pan landed on the coursework.

Lynae caught the handle, lifted it onto the bare corner of the desk, and pulled her chair forward. Behind her, the cupboard door swung slowly open.

She reached for the first drawing.

Simulacrum of the Heart 3

The razor-thin line of white noon had not shifted a hair’s breadth across the pine floorboards.

It slanted through the middle louver-slat of the closed shutters, cutting straight across the toe of Yangyang’s white stocking as she sat on the lid of the low baggage-chest. Three feet away, in the dim bay of the window-seat, Suoming was asleep.

She had woken Yangyang at the third watch with a single tap of two fingers against the bolster, climbed back onto the four-foot cedar bench without a word, and curled onto her uninjured left side. The furled bamboo shaft of Nine Shadows lay tucked under her chin and across her shins; her bandaged right forearm rested on her hip, and her cheek was pressed into the faded plum wool of her shoulder. With her pale eyes shut against the glare and the flat set gone out of her mouth, her face looked twelve years old.

Across the room, the bed-ropes creaked.

Mei rolled over onto her stomach, reached blindly across the indigo quilt beside the warm square of the Prism, and patted the empty ticking twice. Her head came up off the buckwheat bolster with the hair on the right side standing straight out from her ear.

She squinted through the shutter-gloom, spotted Yangyang sitting on the chest, and pushed the quilt back. Holding her left palm lightly over her cotton shirt where the scar pulled, Mei padded across the cold boards in her stockings and sat down on the other half of the chest lid, bumping her hip against Yangyang’s thigh.

“What time is it?” Mei whispered, rubbing her left eye with her knuckles.

“Still eleven forty-two,” Yangyang whispered back.

Mei glanced past Yangyang’s shoulder at the small figure curled on the rush mat. “She actually let you take the watch?”

“At the third. She went to sleep as soon as she lay down.”

Mei leaned her shoulder against Yangyang’s arm and let out a tiny, quiet yawn through her nose. “What’d I miss?”

Yangyang looked down at her hands folded in her lap. “We talked a little before I came back to bed,” she said, keeping her voice softer than the rustle of the buckwheat bolster. “I told her the rest of what the Fox made me swear in the Skyworks. And I told her about the hand on my cuff when the Sanctum floor opened.”

Mei’s head came up off Yangyang’s shoulder. She blinked at her in the dimness.

“What hand?” Mei asked.

“When the bronze rings turned up top,” Yangyang said quietly, turning her right wrist over on her knee. “You had my left hand. My right was empty because I’d just thrown the Prism across the room. Right as my boots left the wood, someone small caught my right cuff and pulled me down through the gap so the beam wouldn’t take my shoulder.”

Mei stared at Yangyang’s bare wrist, then up at her face.

“Wait a second,” Mei whispered. “Somebody grabbed your wrist in the middle of the fall?”

“Yes. And let out a long breath right beside my ear. I’ve been hearing it under the trees all day.”

Mei’s mouth opened, shut, and opened again. Her brows drew together over her golden eyes.

“You didn’t tell me that,” Mei said, her whisper dropping half an octave. “I was holding your other hand all afternoon, and you told the Elder before you told me?”

Yangyang didn’t look away. Under the dark blue hair at her temples, her face was open and quiet.

“On the stairs you were trying not to split your side open,” Yangyang said plainly. “Then we took pictures in the street, and then you fell asleep in the middle of a sentence.”

“I was awake at supper!”

“At supper you were trying to get her to let you bandage her arm,” Yangyang said. She paused for a beat, her thumb brushing the hem of her cloud-worked cuff. “And I knew she’d recognize the breath when I described it. I wanted to see what she did when she heard it.”

Mei looked at her for three seconds, the small crease still sitting between her eyebrows.

“Okay,” Mei murmured. “That makes sense. I still don’t like hearing it second, though.”

“Next time I’ll wake you up to tell you.”

“Don’t wake me up, I was dead on my feet,” Mei whispered back immediately. “Just poke me under the table while I’m eating my egg.”

The corner of Yangyang’s mouth lifted a fraction of an inch. “All right. Under the table.”

She hesitated, glancing once toward the dark shape of the window-seat, then looked straight back into Mei’s eyes.

“She also asked about us,” Yangyang said.

Mei froze. “Us?”

“She pointed at the bed while you were snoring and asked what we were.”

Mei’s eyes went round as saucers. “I wasn’t snoring—what did you say?!”

“I told her you were with Iuno, and that you have Amy,” Yangyang whispered evenly. “I told her what we have doesn’t have a title on the roll, that Iuno knows all of it, and that we’re walking by the bird-bridge on the canal when we get back to Jinzhou.”

The color came up Mei’s neck so fast it showed even in the amber dusk. She clapped both hands over her mouth, leaned out past Yangyang’s knees to peer hard at the window-seat—where Suoming hadn’t moved a finger—and then buried her forehead squarely against Yangyang’s shoulder with a muffled, strangled wheeze.

“You told her about the canal?” Mei breathed into the blue silk of Yangyang’s sleeve.

“She asked.”

“And what did she say?!”

“She said ‘Mm,’ and told me to get off the cold floorboards before my stockings got damp.”

Mei let out another silent laugh that shook both her shoulders, then lifted her chin off Yangyang’s arm, her face still warm as she slipped her right hand across the pine lid and laced her fingers loosely through Yangyang’s.

Together they looked across the strip of white light at the window-seat.

In the quiet room, Suoming’s chest rose under the faded plum wool, held for half a beat, and gave that dry, slow catch low against the bottom of her left ribs before letting the air go.

“She looks like a kid when she’s out like that,” Mei whispered, her thumb resting still against Yangyang’s knuckle. “What do you make of her?”

“Her face is young,” Yangyang whispered back. “Her breath isn’t.”

On the cedar bench, Suoming’s pale eyes opened.

She did not blink or stretch her arms. She simply uncurled her legs, swung her iron-plated boots down onto the floorboards with a single flat *clack*, and sat upright with Nine Shadows across her lap, looking straight at the two of them squeezed onto the lid of the chest.

“The stove’s cold,” Suoming said.

She stood up, tucked the furled umbrella under her uninjured left arm, and walked out the door toward the stair.

Downstairs in the kitchen, Yangyang stirred a ladle of cold well-water into the thick leftover congee while Mei propped one half of the inn’s front double doors open with a split birch log to let the woodsmoke out. When they sat down at the pine table in the common room, the rice was bubbling hot again, and the stoneware pot from the arcade outside was steaming with fresh plain white tea.

Mei blew on her spoon, took a mouthful of rice and salted radish, and looked out through the half-open doorway at the silent red pillars and empty cobbles of the East Arcade.

“So what’s the real Huizhen Plaza like?” Mei asked. “Yangyang says the fan guy yelled at her.”

“I only touched the lacquered one on the end,” Yangyang said into her teacup. “He thought I was going to snap the rivet.”

“He yells at everyone,” Suoming said, lifting her cup with her left hand. “His brother makes the ribs out of green bamboo and they split in the damp.”

Mei propped her chin on her fist. “Have you spent a lot of time there, Elder?”

Suoming drank half her white tea and set the cup down on the pine plank.

“The real street’s never dry,” Suoming said in her flat voice. “The river mist comes up through the cellar-grates at dawn and smells of wet coal and barge-grease. By the third bell you can’t hear the man across the table because the print-shops behind Bookbinders’ Row start their wooden rollers, and the mule-carters bang their iron wheel-rims down the gutter.”

She picked up a sliver of salted radish with her chopsticks and laid it on her spoon.

“When it rains, the awning-troughs overflow straight onto the noodle benches,” Suoming went on. “People stand three deep under the eaves with their oilskin coats dripping down your neck while they wait for the fried dough.”

She turned her head slightly toward the open doorway. Across the bright, shadowless cobbles, three doors past the closed oil-shop, stood a low timber shopfront with dark cinnabar shutters and a carved wooden mortar hanging from a brass bracket.

“That apothecary burned down in the autumn flood,” Suoming said. “They built the salt-tax office over the cellar a hundred winters back.”

Mei’s eyes followed hers to the unblemished cinnabar paint on the shutters, then came back to the table. Across from her, Yangyang set her chopsticks neatly across the rim of her empty bowl.

Suoming looked from the doorway to Mei’s face.

“What was the racket by the third storehouse last night,” Suoming said, “before the shells went up.”

Mei blinked. “By the cauldron?”

“Someone was shouting about a salt-fish. And a goose.”

Mei’s whole face lit up. She dropped her spoon into her bowl with a clatter and leaned both elbows on the scrubbed pine.

“Oh, the goose!” Mei said happily. “Okay, first off, the salt-fish wasn’t even Lupa’s fault—Abby stuck his head out of my collar while Wen’s back was turned and tried to lick the brine off the drying hooks, and Lupa grabbed him by the scruff and told him she’d bake him into a pie if he touched the supper rack. Then Ciaccona tried to prove the vinegar jar in the corner was sour by hitting it with a tuning fork—”

“It was a brass spoon,” Yangyang corrected mildly.

“It sounded like a tuning fork! Anyway, while they were arguing about the vinegar, Cartethyia was out by the cistern trough with a peeled willow stick, and her goose came marching up to report for duty. So Carte gave him his orders for the evening, very formal, a tap on each shoulder.”

Suoming’s pale eyes stayed fixed on Mei’s hands as Mei mimed the tap of the stick from shoulder to shoulder.

“The second she said *Dismissed*, the goose reached up, yanked the crystal laurel right off her head with his bill, and bolted down the cooper’s lane with his wings out,” Mei said, grinning from ear to ear. “Lupa nearly laughed herself into the ditch, and Carte had to chase him round the pickle-vats three times until Chisa walked out of the kitchen and traded him half a turnip for the crown.”

“It was a radish,” Yangyang said.

“Turnip, radish, it was white and crunchy,” Mei said, waving a hand. “He still wouldn’t give it back until Chisa let him peck her boot-buckle twice.”

At the end of the table, Suoming’s face hadn’t moved, and her boots hadn’t swung once while Mei was talking.

When Mei stopped to take a breath, Suoming reached across the pine board with her left hand, lifted the earthenware teapot by its woven bamboo handle, filled Mei’s empty cup to the brim, and then filled her own.

Five miles north-west, Lupa banged the heavy iron skillet down on the mill-house grate hard enough to send a shower of red oak sparks up the flue.

“Buckwheat flour, dried mushrooms, and salt,” Lupa snarled at the rafters, whipping a wooden spoon around an earthenware mixing-bowl with her right arm while her tail lashed once behind her knees. “Not a strip of bacon, not a single clove of garlic, and the water in that cistern tastes like a frog died in it in the spring! Who stocks a pantry with four barrels of flour and no lard?!”

Across the dim kitchen, Chisa sat up on her woven reed mat, folded her grey blanket into a neat square, and rested her bare palm flat against the floorboards beside her knee.

On the pine bench by the table, Amy pushed Iuno’s wool coat off her chin, sat up with her pink hair sticking straight out on three sides like a crushed thistle, and rubbed her eyes with both fists. In her blouse pocket, the folded brass rosette gave a faint, sleepy *tink* against her ribs.

“What are you listening to?” Amy mumbled, swinging her boots down onto the stone.

“The wheel,” Chisa said, standing up and settling her cased scissor-blade over her shoulder. “It’s under the scullery.”

Amy’s hands dropped from her face at once. She glanced toward the stove—where Lupa was pouring a ladle of grey batter onto the smoking dry iron with a ferocious hiss—and jerked her chin toward the scullery door.

“Come on,” Amy whispered. “Before she makes us scrape the burned ones off the pan.”

They slipped through the back scullery and down a narrow flight of twelve dressed-granite steps into the cellar under the hill.

There were no millstones down here, no wooden hoppers, and no smell of old chaff. Set into a dry masonry pit where the mill-race belonged was a twenty-foot wheel of dark, oil-bright brass and pale silver-bronze. Hundreds of bevelled teeth, cut fine as a watchmaker’s escapement, turned smoothly into a wall of smaller planetary gears without a drop of water touching the paddles.

Amy stopped on the bottom step and let out a low whistle. “Okay, nobody grinds buckwheat with a differential like that.”

Chisa stepped off the stair and laid two fingers lightly against the cold stone housing of the main axle.

High up on the west wall, through a narrow stone ventilation slit no wider than a brick, the copper-white glare of the valley slanted in across the brass. Far off at the end of that light, the glow around the giant fox’s folded arms dimmed for half a heartbeat.

Down in the pit, the twenty-foot wheel hitched.

It was a tiny, dry *tick-skip* in the bronze teeth—the catch of a clock-hand when the weight on the chain swings against the case—and the instant the light in the ventilation slit steadied, the wheel caught its stride again with a smooth, humming *thrum*.

“It skips when the light drops,” Chisa said.

Amy crouched at the lip of the pit, resting her elbows on her knees as she watched the bevelled rings turn inside one another. “It’s the same metal as the rosette in my pocket, and that broken hoop up on the cliff. The whole hill’s just a gear-box with a roof on it.”

She picked up a chip of loose mortar and tossed it onto the stone rim beside the housing, then sat back on the bottom step beside Chisa’s boots. For a minute neither of them spoke while the brass teeth whirred softly in the dark.

“My blouse has axle-grease on the cuff,” Amy said gloomily, pulling her sleeve around to inspect the black smudge near the wrist. “We haven’t even made it to the real Mengzhou yet, and I’m gonna look like I crawled out of a bilge-pump while you look like a normal person in your tie.”

“I fell in the moss,” Chisa said, sitting down on the step beside her. “It’ll wash out.”

“Yeah, with frog-water.” Amy rested her chin on her palm, watching the steady silver-bronze flash of the inner pinion. Her voice dropped a little lower against the hum of the pit. “Yesterday in the town square, when the paving stones turned over and the floor went black underneath us.”

Chisa turned her head slightly toward her.

“For a second before we hit the moss,” Amy said, her thumb rubbing the grease-spot on her cuff without looking at it, “I thought we’d dropped right out the bottom of the map. Back into the grey stuff, where you can shout until your throat bleeds and the air doesn’t carry a sound.”

In the dim light from the cellar slit, Chisa’s left wrist rested across her knee with Sumika’s red string wound loosely around the bone.

“I had your wrist,” Chisa said plainly.

“I know.”

“If the floor opens again today,” Chisa said, “I’ll grab it again. You’re loud enough to find in the dark anyway.”

Amy let out a short, nasal snort and bumped her shoulder squarely against Chisa’s arm. “Thanks a lot. Tell Lupa I’m loud and she’ll make me walk at the back.”

Upstairs in the warm kitchen, Cartethyia sat on the edge of the grain-chest in her dry wool stockings, looking down at her leather boots standing side by side on the warm hearth-bricks with their laces neatly picked open.

“Who unlaced my boots?” Cartethyia asked the room.

Over by the north post, where a small square of polished tin hung on a nail, Iuno stood with her hand-mirror propped against a salt-crock, trying to twist the heavy length of her silver-blue hair up behind her crown with her single right hand while holding two carved bone pins between her teeth. As she reached for the first pin, the entire coil slipped through her fingers for the third time and tumbled down over her bare crystal shoulder.

“I did,” Iuno said distinctly around the bone pins, her jaw tight. “You were going to sleep in half a pint of pond-water and ruin the welt.”

Cartethyia hopped off the chest in her stockings, padded across the flagstones without a sound, and stopped behind Iuno’s elbow with both hands held out.

“May I?” Cartethyia asked.

Iuno looked at her in the tin glass for half a beat, took the two bone pins out of her mouth, and laid them in Cartethyia’s palm.

“Two twists to the left,” Iuno said, “and the long pin goes through the bottom loop.”

“I know,” Cartethyia said, gathering the cool, heavy silk of Iuno’s hair in both hands and combing the tangles out gently with her fingers. “I watched you do it on the barge when the wind was blowing.”

Iuno stood still while Cartethyia’s small hands gathered the twist at the nape of her neck.

“Cia is going to be unbearable this morning,” Cartethyia said conversationally as she turned the coil up. “When she sleeps on stone without a bolster, her left shoulder gets a knot in it, and when there isn’t any coffee she tunes the fourth string a quarter-tone flat on purpose and plays scales until somebody brings her a sweet roll.”

“She is with the little Celestial from the wall,” Iuno said, watching the notched rim of her moonwheel resting on the hearth-brick. “Between the two of them, I imagine the entire mountain has heard their complaints by now.”

“Oh, yes,” Cartethyia said warmly, sliding the first bone pin cleanly through the lower loop so the twist locked without pulling a single hair. “I just hope she kept her coat buttoned. She always forgets when she’s looking at the architecture.”

She slid the second pin home across the top, patted the neat coil once, and rested her hands lightly on Iuno’s good right shoulder.

“Mei will have found a kitchen,” Iuno said quietly to the tin mirror. “And she will have tried to lift the iron pot off the hook by herself, and Yangyang will have made her sit on a stool.”

“It’s funny, isn’t it,” Cartethyia said, her reflection small and clear over Iuno’s shoulder. “When you’re by yourself for a long time, twenty winters all run together like water in a dish. And then once you have people again, even having a hill between you until breakfast feels much too long.”

Iuno’s right hand came up and touched the back of Cartethyia’s fingers where they rested on her coat.

“Yes,” Iuno said. “It does.”

The cellar door banged open, and Amy and Chisa came tramping back up out of the scullery just as Lupa swung away from the hearth with the iron skillet in one fist and a wooden platter in the other.

“Sit down before they go cold and taste like saddle-leather!” Lupa barked, kicking two benches out from the table with her heel.

She dealt four ordinary buckwheat pancakes onto the plates around the board, and then marched straight behind Iuno’s place and slammed the fifth—a massive, golden-brown wheel the size of a target-shield, drowned in three extra tablespoons of wild hill-honey from the back shelf—squarely down in front of Iuno’s nose, before cuffing the top of Amy’s pink head as she passed.

“Eat the whole thing, princess,” Lupa growled, refusing to look down as she stalked back toward the mushroom-gravy pot. “I’m not listening to your wrist rattle against your belt-hook all the way down that tree, and the kid’s gotta save her arm for stabbing governors.”

Iuno looked down at the enormous, honey-soaked pancake, and then across the room at the back of Lupa’s neck, where the tips of her wolf-ears were angled flat toward the chimney-breast.

*How exquisitely delicate, darling,* the High Priestess murmured. *If she likes us any better, she’ll hit us with a ham.*

“Thank you, Lupa,” Iuno said smoothly, picking up her fork with her right hand. “The browning on the edges is admirable.”

At the stove, Lupa’s ears went crimson to the points, and she banged the iron ladle into the gravy pot hard enough to splash the bricks. On the south-west windowsill behind her, the open metal lotus caught the red glare of the hearth, its rose-gold needle pointing steady and still down the valley toward the waiting bough.

Up in Mengxuan Vale, the great brass astrolabe in the middle of the square went *clank—pause—clank*.

No one in the village had gone to bed. Along the white-tiled arcade, four masons were still passing unmortared ridge-tiles hand to hand up a cedar scaffold, while two scribes sat on the steps of the well-pavilion comparing long columns of glowing characters on their Terminals under the unchanging copper noon.

In the doorway of the third west room, Ruoyun sat on her three-legged wooden stool with her Mary Jane shoes planted square on the stone sill, her pleated teal skirt smoothed flat over her knees, and her arms folded tight across her chest. Behind her back, the heavy timber door stood four inches ajar; inside the darkened room, Qingxiao lay fast asleep on the narrow hemp cot, her moon-white cuff tucked under her cheek and her breathing slow and deep beneath the indigo oilcloth.

Footsteps came clicking briskly up the polished cedar walkway.

Mingxiao stopped at the foot of the porch steps so her voice wouldn’t carry through the open door-crack. Beside her stood Gu, an older drafter with ink-smudges up to his elbows, and a short woman carrying a brass spirit-level under her arm, all three of them holding their Terminals flat on their palms.

“You’re still sitting there,” Gu said, squinting up at Ruoyun’s stool without a trace of the bow he had given the Xuan Paragon the afternoon before. “Chen’s nephew wants to know if he can at least fetch his brass callipers out of the box under the window.”

“Chen’s nephew can measure with his thumb until I move,” Ruoyun snapped in a low, fierce undertone without uncrossing her arms. “I turn my back for three days—three days!—to go topside and drag a runaway back by the ear, and you vultures strip my room before the blanket’s cold.”

“Your blanket has been cold since Yuan Fortress, Ruoyun,” Mingxiao said, rubbing the bridge of her nose with an ink-stained knuckle. “And we didn’t think anyone was coming back down from the surface after you left. The upper sluices were locked from the inside.”

Ruoyun’s chin came up, her round brass spectacles catching the white glare off the tiles. “Well, they aren’t locked now. What’s wrong with the wheel in the square? It’s dragging its foot on every third turn like a lame mule.”

Mingxiao exchanged a glance with the woman holding the spirit-level, then stepped up onto the bottom riser and tilted her Terminal screen so Ruoyun could see the scrolling amber lines.

“It isn’t just the square,” Mingxiao said quietly. “The load-registers have been backing up across all four basins, and since the Sanctum floor dropped yesterday, they’re slipping faster.”

“Speak plain, Mingxiao, I haven’t read a flow-slate since the snow-year,” Ruoyun muttered, leaning forward an inch on her stool to glare through her lenses at the screen.

“The boundaries aren’t clearing behind themselves,” Gu said, tapping three red-flagged nodes on his own slate. “Out on the eastern ford, the road-register has folded right back into its own tail—walk straight across three ridges and you come right back out behind the same boundary tortoise because the margin won’t turn over.”

“Down on the southern orchard steps,” the woman with the brass level added sourly, “the seasonal queues have leaked clean through the retaining walls. We’ve got summer loquats dropping onto winter frost and snapping back onto the twigs every four beats because the ground-state won’t accept them. Even the rest-pavilion on the third dyke is split down the ridge-pole between high noon and first watch.”

“And the northern dormitories?” Ruoyun asked sharply.

“Running on empty loops,” Mingxiao said. “The steamers are boiling fresh buns every bell, the blankets are folded on the cots, the floors are swept clean, and there hasn’t been a soul quartered in the north compound since the middle reign.”

“Somebody also forced the fourth relief-key out by the lotus basin yesterday afternoon,” the woman with the spirit-level said, her mouth pulling tight. “Turned it cold in the rock without clearing the ring-sequence first. Dumped a whole valley’s worth of queued pressure straight across the western hills.”

Ruoyun pushed her brass spectacles half an inch up her nose with two fingers.

“The foreigners,” Ruoyun said flatly.

She looked up from the glowing screens, past the three Celestials on the steps, toward the great brass astrolabe clanking slowly in the center of the square and the copper-white sky beyond the eaves.

“Never mind the fruit and the dormitories,” Ruoyun said, her voice dropping hard and level. “How long since any of you laid eyes on the boss?”

The three Celestials on the step went still. Gu lowered his Terminal half an inch toward his apron.

“Not the big shadow sulking over the western peaks,” Ruoyun said, her blue eyes narrowing behind her lenses. “Her. Walking the walkways in her own skin. Poking her nose into the drafting hall.”

Mingxiao’s hand rested on the carpenter’s square tucked into her sash.

“Longer than Gu has been on the rolls, Ruoyun,” Mingxiao said quietly. “You know we can’t—”

“Don’t recite the house rules to me, Mingxiao, I was sweeping the top gutters in the sleet before Gu knew which end of a chisel to hold!” Ruoyun hissed, rising half an inch off her stool before she caught herself and shot a sharp glance back over her shoulder into the dark room. She sat back down hard, her knuckles white on her ruffled cuffs. “She lets a Mengzhou rot-vine crawl right up to her cushion, and you lot just stand out here tapping your little glass slates?!”

“If we stop tending the registers while the Core is turning again, the lower terraces won’t just loop,” Mingxiao said evenly. “The seams will tear from the river to the ridge. Someone has to hold the boards straight.”

Ruoyun glared down from the threshold, her jaw set like iron and two bright spots of red burning high on her cheeks.

“What’s sitting in the middle of the Sanctum, Mingxiao?” Ruoyun demanded.

Mingxiao looked straight back at her and did not open her mouth. Beside her, Gu and the woman with the spirit-level looked down at the white cedar steps.

After three beats, Mingxiao inclined her head in a single, clipped bow and turned back down the walkway toward the square, the other two falling into step behind her.

“Useless pack of ink-lickers,” Ruoyun snarled under her breath at their backs.

She turned on the three-legged stool and looked through the four-inch gap into the dim bedroom.

On the narrow hemp cot, Qingxiao had rolled slightly toward the wall in her sleep. One warm, bare hand lay open on the rough ticking outside the indigo oilcloth, her fingers curled loosely and a faint smudge of grey road-dust still showing on the hem of her moon-white sleeve as her chest rose and fell in a long, unhurried breath.

Ruoyun reached back with her right hand, hooked two fingers through the iron ring on the door-frame, and pulled the heavy cedar leaf until only a hair’s breadth of crack remained between the jamb and the latch, shutting out the slow *clank* of the square’s astrolabe. Then she settled her pleated skirt over her knees, folded her arms again, and stayed on the stool.

Two terraces below the white-tiled roofs, Ciaccona woke with the sun in her left eye and a cramp in her right calf.

She hadn’t moved an inch across the flagstones during the night: her boots were still tucked inside the cool indigo twilight under the finished western half of the pavilion roof, while four feet away, past the third cedar rafter, the unlaid stacks of grey roof-tile baked under the exact same brass-bright noon sun that had been shining when she closed her eyes.

Across from her on the narrow stone bench, Buling sat hunched under her round black *nuanmao* hat with both hands clamped to the back of her neck, letting out a long, creaky groan that sounded like a cellar door opening.

“Aiya,” Buling muttered to her knees, rolling her head an inch to the left and wincing. “Stone bench got a ridge right in the middle. My neck is stiff until next Tuesday.”

“Good,” Ciaccona said hoarsely, propping her good elbow on the lid of her velvet lute-case and rubbing the grit out of her eyes with her thumb. “It serves you right for snoring like a bilge-pump in B-flat for six hours.”

Buling’s hands dropped from her neck. Under the yellow paper talisman draped from her hat-brim, her red eyes fixed indignantly on Ciaccona.

“Oi. Since when I snore? Jiangshi don’t even need to breathe unless we’re talking, how to snore?”

“Then you were doing it purely to ruin my rest,” Ciaccona said, flexing her splinted left forefinger and grimacing at the stiff linen wrap. “Six hours on a flagstone, a sun that refuses to go behind a cloud, a finger like a wooden peg, and not a single drop of coffee in the province. If this is immortality, Buling, you got cheated on the contract.”

“Tell me about it,” Buling grumbled, sliding off the stone bench and stamping her black-wrapped shins once to settle her cloth shoes. “And you still owe me for that sweet coffee in Septimont, don’t think I forgot.”

“I paid for the pastries!” Ciaccona protested, hauling herself up against the corner pillar and slinging the lute-case strap over her right shoulder. “And the copper bell you gave me at that table attracted every stray ghost between Ragunna and the coast.”

“That bell was master-grade craft,” Buling said loftily, tapping the small brass bell at her sash so it gave a single dry *ting*. “Not my fault you brought it into a Leviathan’s house and dropped it in the water.”

“I was slightly occupied not drowning, thank you.”

Ciaccona walked two steps to the stone balustrade at the edge of the twilight patch, resting her good hand on the coping as she looked out across the copper-white mist toward the eastern crags. Her thumb tapped twice, lightly, against the velvet strap over her collarbone.

“If Carte slept in those wet boots,” Ciaccona said, the Ragunnese curl thinning out of her voice, “she’s going to have blisters the size of florins by the time we find them. And she never says a word about it until she’s limping on both heels.”

Buling paused in the middle of straightening the yellow talisman on her forehead and glanced sideways at her.

“Last night when you played that low string on the floor,” Buling said in a plain, quick voice, “the water in the ditch down there jumped two inches toward the east. The whole hill carries sound like a copper pipe. Your girl has ears, right? She knows which terrace you’re on.”

Ciaccona looked down at the round winter hat beside her elbow, and the tight line at the corner of her mouth softened.

“Thanks, Buling,” Ciaccona said quietly.

“Don’t thank, I’m putting acoustic relay on the bill,” Buling said at once, stepping out of the indigo shade into the hot noon grass and hitching up her sleeves. “Come. After a night’s rest the gate-post confirm settled down. Five minutes up the stair.”

It did not take five minutes.

They climbed the dressed-stone steps between the loquat trees. On the third riser, the exact same bruised orange loquat dropped off the branch above Buling’s hat, bounced twice on the stone, and rose straight back up through the mist to snap itself onto its twig.

Buling didn’t break stride. She muttered something under her breath about the white tiger and a crooked sluice-stone, whipped a small brass feng-shui compass out of her collar, tapped the glass twice with her fingernail, and turned sharp left along the dry-stone retaining wall. She led Ciaccona up sixteen steep granite steps, round a corner of pruned loquat boughs, and straight back out onto the grass ten feet behind the half-roofed pavilion.

Ciaccona stopped dead with her hand on her hip.

Buling’s ears had gone bright pink beneath the brim of her *nuanmao*.

“Wait first,” Buling said fast, holding the compass at arm’s length and glaring at the needle. “You walked too heavy on the turn just now, the gravel shifted the needle—”

“I weigh nine stone with the lute, Buling!”

“It’s this stupid third terrace!” Buling marched back to the bottom of the stair, planted her black cloth shoe on the first riser, and pointed furiously up at the white-tiled roofs of Mengxuan Vale glimmering two terraces overhead—clear enough to see the masons on the scaffold, and not an inch closer than they had been yesterday. “A hundred years the stair goes straight up past the plum shed, and the one morning I bring a guest, the ditch turns sideways! Oi! Which idiot re-laid the curb-stones while I was gone?!”

“Buling.”

Both of them looked up.

Standing at the top of the short flight of steps, beside a neat bundle of pruned loquat switches, was a woman in a plain indigo work-tunic with her trouser-legs bound at the calves in hemp twine. She had a long-handled iron pruning hook resting over her right shoulder and dark orchard earth dried across the knuckles of both hands.

Longjue looked down three risers at Buling—silver braid, red eyes, old-dynasty winter hat, yellow talisman dangling over her nose—with the exact same unhurried calm she might have given a wheelbarrow left in the lane.

“You’re shouting on the south stair,” Longjue said.

Buling froze with her compass held out in front of her chest, then whipped both hands behind her back so fast the brass sash-bell jangled.

“Longjue!” Buling said brightly, her voice half a tone higher than usual. “Wah, long time! I wasn’t shouting, I was just—we were inspecting the drainage! Your third ditch is completely out of alignment, you know, the water is running straight into the—”

“You left your weeding basket under the plum shed,” Longjue said mildly. “It’s still there. The bottom rotted out, though.”

Behind Buling’s shoulder, Ciaccona clapped her good hand over her mouth, her shoulders shaking silently against her lute-case.

“That basket was falling apart when you gave it to me!” Buling said rapidly, shifting her weight from heel to heel. “Anyway I had urgent business outside, very big contracts, top-level consultation across the water, cannot just drop everything to come back and mend bamboo baskets—”

Longjue’s calm eyes moved past Buling’s hat-brim to Ciaccona’s velvet lute-case and the splinted finger resting against the strap.

“Good morning,” Longjue said politely. “Did she charge you for the guide work?”

“Not yet,” Ciaccona said, grinning openly now, “though she just threatened to bill me for the acoustics.”

“Don’t pay it,” Longjue said. She shifted the iron pruning hook an inch on her shoulder and glanced down at the third step, where the bruised loquat dropped off its twig, bounced twice, and floated back up to the branch. “You were on the right stair, Buling. The third terrace has been slipping its latch since yesterday afternoon. When the light dims in the west, the risers lay themselves down twice unless you step over the iron drain-cover on the left.”

Buling’s hands came out from behind her back at once, and she spun round on Ciaccona with her chin high.

“See?!” Buling demanded. “What did I tell you? The ditch! Not my fault!”

“Truly, a master of the terrain,” Ciaccona said solemnly.

Longjue turned up the terraced path toward the white-tiled roofs shining through the copper fog above the orchard wall.

“Come up,” Longjue said over her shoulder, stepping cleanly over the square iron grate set into the left-hand curb. “Ruoyun came back yesterday too. She’s already shouting at the masons.”

Buling’s foot stopped two inches above the riser. For half a breath she stood very still on one leg; then she tugged the round brim of her winter hat down hard over her ears, stepped wide over the iron drain-cover, and marched up the stone stair after Longjue’s muddy boots, with Ciaccona following right behind her.

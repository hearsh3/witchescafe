# Ladies' Night

The three of bamboo had a smear of dried chili oil across the middle stalk.

Buling rubbed it with her thumb, decided it wasn’t coming off, and clicked the tile into her rack. Outside the canvas flap of the tea shed, the second terrace was so dead she could hear a stray dog licking an empty lard tub behind the cooper’s arch. The iron wires overhead sagged bare between the shop eaves, stripped of their festival paper, and Old Ma at the bun barrow next door sat on an upturned bucket with his hands tucked into his armpits, staring at a turnip.

“Your turn,” Buling told Mingxiao. “Throw something useful. Don’t be stingy.”

Mingxiao didn’t look up from her row. Her brass carpenter’s square hung from her belt, and her cuffs were pinned back with clothes-pegs. She drew a tile from the end of her wall, set it down square in the centre of the green felt, and said, “West wind.”

“Useless,” Buling muttered, reaching for the dead wall.

Across the table, Ciaccona rested her chin on her palm, her splinted left forefinger sticking out past her ear like a little wooden weathervane. “If the dragon draws another bamboo, I’m paying my debt in ballads.”

“You already owe me two ballads,” Longjue said cheerfully. Her bare feet were hooked over the lower rung of the table above the frosted cobbles, her red lacquer gourd swinging from her chair-back by its teal cord. “And I don’t want bamboo. Throw the red dragon, Pipa. See what happens.”

Across the narrow lane, the slatted door of the Red Ladle banged back against its iron stops.

Auntie Luo stumped out onto the step in her quilted wool vest with a bucket of steaming grey slops in one fist and a rag in the other. She looked up the empty hill toward the plaza, looked down toward the river turn, spat once into the gutter, and hauled her tall black chalkboard off its wall-peg.

All four of them stopped reaching for the tiles.

Auntie Luo sloshed warm water across last week’s Waking Moon prices, scrubbed the slate black with her rag, and pulled a thick stub of chalk from her apron pocket. Her spectacles sat crooked on the bridge of her nose as she wrote in block characters big enough to read from the gatehouse:

**LADIES’ NIGHT**
**WOMEN ONLY — SECOND CUP FREE**
**SPICED PEANUTS ON THE HOUSE**
**THREE ARROWS IN THE COPPER POT WINS THE TEN-YEAR WINTERSWEET JAR**

She whacked the chalk dust off her palms, propped the board against the horse-trough, and went back inside, leaving the door propped open on a split log. A curl of hot ginger steam and star-anise smoke drifted out over the frost.

Buling lifted the yellow paper talisman off her forehead with one finger so she could read the bottom line twice.

“The ten-year wintersweet,” Buling said. “That jar’s been up on her tie-beam since the road closed. Last week I offered her silver for it and she threatened me with a poker.”

Longjue’s bare toes uncurled from the stretcher. She sat up straight, her teal eyes fixed on the open tavern door with the terrible, serene focus she usually kept for dawn sparring in the orchard.

“Three arrows,” Longjue said. “How wide is the neck on her pot?”

“Four fingers,” Buling said, already pulling her Terminal out of her silk jacket. She framed the chalkboard past Ciaccona’s shoulder, snapped a photo, and opened a fresh thread on the Huangboard under **BigThunder88**:

*Second terrace dead like a tomb after festival. Auntie Luo at the Red Ladle just chalked second cup free for women + pitch-pot for the old wintersweet jar on the beam. Starting early.*

“You’re going to bring the whole garrison down here before the charcoal’s hot,” Ciaccona said, squinting at the screen.

“Garrison’s mostly men, cannot get in,” Buling said, dropping the Terminal beside her saucer. “And the women Wardens are on the gate till the first bell.”

“I’m going now,” Longjue said.

“The stove isn’t even lit yet,” Mingxiao pointed out, nudging the discarded west wind with her fingernail until its edges sat parallel to the table frame.

“It’s smoking,” Longjue said, her nose lifting toward the lane. “Wait till the Landlady sees that board. She owes me thirty-one strings and she hasn’t bought me a drink since the plaza.”

“Eleven strings,” Buling said automatically.

“Thirty-one with the frost-tax!”

Ciaccona tapped her three good left fingers along the back of her tile rack, looking past the horse-trough toward the steep flight of stone steps leading up to the storehouse terrace. A slow, wicked grin spread across her face.

“Wait,” Ciaccona said. “Why stop at the fox?”

Buling eyed her sideways. “Who else you want?”

“Everybody,” Ciaccona said. “The salt-house. The Vale lot. Wen from the kitchen. The Paragon. Your terrifying little sergeant with the spectacles.”

“Ruoyun?” Buling snorted. “You ask Ruoyun to a free-cup night at Auntie Luo’s, she’ll lecture the wine jars until they turn to vinegar.”

“If we don’t ask her, she’ll stand on the tiles and drop icicles down the flue,” Ciaccona said.

Longjue banged both palms flat on the table, making the teacup lids jump. “Go get the salt-house! I’ll get the Landlady!”

She reached out to the live wall without looking, drew her tile, and slammed it face-up in the middle of the green felt.

It was the white dragon—a clean self-drawn hand of all-triplets down the entire rack.

Longjue didn’t even wait for Buling to reach for her purse. She kicked her chair back across the frost, snatched her red gourd off the post, and went striding barefoot up the hill with her wide teal sleeves belling in the draft, leaving all fourteen winning tiles standing on the cloth.

***

Up on the storehouse terrace, Wen had just slid a tray of hot scallion pancakes out through the kitchen hatch, and twelve pairs of chopsticks were hovering over the tin platter when Ciaccona planted her boot on the end of the bench.

She slung her lute case off her shoulder, rested her elbow on the knee of her trouser-leg, and pitched her voice into the bright, carrying register that reached the back rows of an amphitheatre.

“Ladies of the household,” Ciaccona announced. “Down on the second terrace, Auntie Luo at the Red Ladle has chalked the board. Women only, second cup on the house, spiced river peanuts at every table, and three arrows in the copper pot takes the ten-year jar of wintersweet off the rafters. We march at the first bell.”

Iuno didn’t look up from the tea-strainer she was tapping against her saucer.

“No,” Iuno said.

“Iuiu!” Mei’s chopsticks clattered onto her bowl rim. “The second cup is free!”

“The second terrace is at the bottom of forty-eight frost-glazed steps,” Iuno said evenly. “And the last time you were near an open tavern jar, you tried to climb a timber post to see if the rafters were cedar.”

“They were pine,” Mei said, already unhooking her boots from the bench stretcher. “And my side doesn’t even pull today! Look!” She twisted her torso half an inch to demonstrate, winced, and quickly clamped her left palm over her coat front as if she were merely checking her buttons. “That was the bench. The bench has a splinter.”

Iuno turned her head and looked at her.

Mei looked back with shameless, golden-eyed devotion, both hands folded neatly on the table edge like a schoolgirl waiting for a prize.

Iuno let out a single long breath through her nose. Her silver-blue hair caught the orange glow from Wen’s stove as she lifted one finger.

“Terms,” Iuno said. “One small cup of warmed yellow wine. Not sorghum, not spirits. You sit with your back against a solid wall and your feet out of the door-draft. And when we return, two people walk you up the steps, and you do not stop halfway to carry anything for anybody.”

“One small cup, back to the wall, two people on the stairs, no carrying,” Mei rattled off in a single breath. “Agreed! Witnessed!”

“I’m twenty-nine,” Amy announced from across the pickle dish, pointing her spoon at Iuno. “So nobody gets to water my mug.”

“Your liver is nineteen,” Iuno said. “You may have hot plum wine.”

“I’ll take the plum wine and put three arrows in the pot,” Amy said, kicking her heels against the crate. “Lupa! Come win the big jar!”

Lupa tore a scallion pancake in half with her teeth and scowled into her broth. “Tavern ale’s half well-water. I’m staying by the stove.”

Beside her, Chisa set her chopsticks parallel across her empty bowl and looked up at Ciaccona.

“I have never been to a tavern,” Chisa said in her calm, level voice. “I would like to see one.”

Lupa’s bench creaked as she shoved her bowl forward. “Right. I’m going. Somebody’s got to make sure they don’t serve her the dregs from the bottom of the barrel.”

“Naturally,” Ciaccona said, turning her grin toward the middle of the plank. “Suisui?”

Suisui held her teacup balanced on three fingertips, her posture immaculate. “I have four folders of Mengzhou shipping manifests that require my stamp before the morning barge,” she said regretfully. “A hillside tavern is hardly—Yangyang, sit still, your collar is tucked under your ribbon—hardly the place for Guild business.”

While she spoke, her other hand had already slipped her lacquered horn comb from her sleeve and was briskly re-pinning the heavy coil at the nape of her neck so it sat half an inch higher, angling her chin toward the dark glass of Wen’s shutter to check the line. On her shoulder, Dumpling gave two sharp clockwork clicks and fluffed its brass wings.

“You’re putting the good comb in,” Yangyang said quietly.

“One doesn’t walk down a public thoroughfare looking as though one slept in a hayloft,” Suisui said without pausing. “Put your heavy cloak on.”

Yangyang looked past Suisui’s elbow toward the square kitchen hatch. Inside, Wen was scrubbing the iron steamer rings with a stiff bamboo brush, her grey hair bound up under the same faded blue kerchief she wore every day from dawn till dark.

Yangyang stood up, carried her empty soup bowl to the sill, and set it on the tray.

“Auntie Wen,” Yangyang said. “Come with us.”

The bamboo brush stopped mid-stroke. Wen looked through the steam at Yangyang, then out at Ciaccona leaning on her lute case and Lupa already tightening her belt-buckle.

“The morning millet’s soaking in the tub,” Wen said. “And the gatehouse boys already came for their supper pail.”

“Auntie Luo boils her peanuts with star anise,” Ciaccona called from the bench end.

Wen looked down at the brush in her fist. She dropped it into the wash-basin with a splash, reached behind her waist to untie her grease-spotted apron strings, and tossed the canvas apron onto the flour bin lid.

“Wait till I get my wool jacket off the nail,” Wen said, and pulled the prop-stick out to drop the shutter.

“Cia!” Cartethyia leaned across the board, her crystal laurel slipping crooked over her right ear. “Does plum wine taste like plums or like wine?”

“Like sour plums that have been in a fight with a vinegar barrel, cara,” Ciaccona said, reaching over to hook the laurel straight with her good thumb.

“Good,” Cartethyia said fervently.

Heavy footsteps came crunching round the storehouse corner from the upper rope-walk.

Ruoyun marched into the courtyard light behind a stack of folded grey blankets so tall she had to peer around the wool through her round brass spectacles. Her ruffled teal cuffs were spotless and her chin was up.

“If one more mason from the Vale asks me what a bolster is for,” Ruoyun informed the walls at large, “I am locking the dormitory doors from the outside.”

“Drop the laundry and come down the hill, sweeper!” Ciaccona called out. “Ladies’ Night at the Red Ladle. Second cup’s free.”

Ruoyun stopped dead by the gate-post. She looked Ciaccona up and down over the top blanket.

“I have forty Celestials on the upper terrace who have just discovered that cold flagstones bruise their hips,” Ruoyun said glacially. “I do not drink in low pothouses. And if you intend to pester the Xuan Paragon—” She turned her head toward the far end of the bench, where Qingxiao sat wrapped to the chin in her dark storehouse oilcloth, eating the last corner of a steamed bun. “—you are wasting your wind. The Paragon has a toothache, a recovering chill, and a mortal liver.”

Qingxiao swallowed her mouthful of bun, brushed a crumb from her white knee, and stood up.

“I’m going,” Qingxiao said.

Ruoyun’s spectacles flashed orange in the lamp-light. “A-Qing.”

“The soup pot is shut,” Qingxiao said in her flat, quiet voice. “There are hot chestnuts at the tavern.”

For two breaths Ruoyun stood rigid behind her tower of wool. Then she pivoted on her dark Mary Jane heel, sighted a young Vale carpenter crossing the flags with an empty tool-bag, and shoved the entire six-foot stack of blankets squarely into his chest until he sat down on a crate.

“Third door past the rope-walk,” Ruoyun snapped at him. “Tell Gu if he puts his muddy heels on the ticking I’ll have his skin for drum-heads.” She smoothed her pleated skirt with both hands and stepped in beside Qingxiao’s elbow. “Someone must see they don’t serve her out of a chipped mug.”

“Landlady!” Longjue’s voice boomed from the grain-shed alley as she came bounding up the steps two at a time, Buling puffing twenty paces behind her with one hand clamping her round winter hat to her head. Longjue planted her bare feet below the grain-shed eaves and shook her red gourd at the roof-ridge. “Down you come! Auntie Luo’s got a ten-year jar on the beam and you owe me thirty-one strings!”

Up on the frosted ridge-tiles, nine gold tails shifted against the dark sky.

Hsin leaned her chin over the gutter in her own shape, her fox ears tipped forward and a sheaf of red-ruled conduit slips held between two fingers.

“I am reviewing the third-terrace water receipts,” Hsin said downward, her voice lofty and melodious. “Some of us have a Hold to govern before the snow falls.”

“Those are from the Ninth Moon, I can see the red stamp from here lah!” Buling wheezed, leaning both hands on her knees at the top step. “And Wen just locked the kitchen!”

“Eleven strings,” Hsin said coldly to Longjue. “And I have no interest in tavern games.”

“Scared you’ll miss the pot!” Longjue crowed, cupping both hands round her mouth. “Hey, everybody! The Sentinel can’t hit a copper pot from six paces!”

Hsin’s ears went flat against her hair. She stuffed the conduit slips into her sleeve, walked down the stepped grain crates at the end of the shed with her nine tails gathered over one arm to keep the dark-tipped one out of the slush, and stopped with her chin raised.

“One cup,” Hsin said. “Until I have taken the jar off the beam.”

By the gate-post, Suoming sat on her upturned wooden bucket with Nine Shadows upright between her knees and her iron-shod boots dangling clear of the frost.

Ciaccona tipped the neck of her lute toward her. “Elder?”

“No,” Suoming said.

Hsin walked past the post toward the head of the stairs, her wide cuffs loose in the wind.

Suoming looked at the back of Hsin’s coat. She slid off the bucket, hooked the bamboo shaft of the umbrella into her elbow, and walked four paces behind the gold tails without glancing at Ciaccona.

At the top of the long stone stair, the whole crowd bunched between the frost-rimed stone lions while mittens were pulled on. Amy scrambled up onto the lion’s plinth and jabbed her forefinger down the line of heads.

“One, two, three—Longjue, move your crest, you’re hiding Chisa—seven, eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen, fifteen, sixteen, seventeen!”

“How’d you get seventeen?” Lupa demanded, squinting back up the steps. “There’s fifteen of us.”

“I counted Dumpling,” Amy said.

“That’s sixteen,” Suisui said, settling her fur collar. “Who did you count twice?”

“Nobody! Wait—Hsin, keep your tails down, the dark one looks like Buling’s hat—fourteen, fifteen—where’d the Elder go?”

“Under your elbow,” Suoming’s flat voice came from directly beneath Lupa’s ribs. “Walk. It’s cold.”

***

Amy beat Lupa through the door of the Red Ladle by ducking under her arm, which gave her a front-row view of Auntie Luo nearly taking her own thumb off with a turnip knife.

The tavern-keeper had been leaning on her wiped counter in the dim light of a single tallow dip, gloomily paring a frost-bitten root into a slop pail. Then the slatted door crashed back against the wall, and sixteen women, nine gold tails, an oil-paper umbrella, and a clockwork bird came pouring in out of the dark in a solid jam of wool coats and cold steam.

Auntie Luo stared at the Sword Transcendent’s white stockings, stared at the nine gold tails squeezing past the pickle tub, and dropped the turnip straight into the pail.

“Xiao Luo!” she bellowed at the ceiling boards. “Down the ladder! Bring the sawhorse planks! Bring the four-legged stools out of the grain loft! Put the big kettle on the ring!”

A trapdoor banged overhead, and a gangly boy of fifteen with a Mengzhou town braid came clattering down the rungs with three stools hooked over each arm.

Within two minutes the low-beamed room was a joyful riot of scraping pine legs and banging benches. Two square tables were shoved end to end against the south settle, a third was wedged across the foot, and Amy vaulted over the back of the settle to claim the inside corner between Mei and Yangyang, where she could reach two peanut dishes and the tally-slate on the wall.

The drinks arrived at a run on three wet lacquer trays.

Auntie Luo had caught Iuno’s eye across the room and set down a tiny white porcelain condiment dish in front of Mei—barely deep enough to drown a bee—filled with warmed yellow wine. Beside it, for Iuno, sat a proper teacup of the same wine with a curl of ginger floating on top.

“Look at this, Amymy,” Mei whispered, holding the condiment dish between her thumb and forefinger. “It’s a thimble. It’s a Sauce Turtle.”

Just then Xiao Luo bustled past the other side of the board and set a full earthenware mug of hot yellow wine down by Mei’s right elbow, between Mei and Yangyang.

“Who ordered the extra yellow?” the boy asked, wiping his brow with his sleeve.

“Must be a mistake!” Mei said brightly, before Iuno—who was turning her head to tell Cartethyia not to lean her crystal thorns into the lamp—could look back. Mei’s right hand closed around the earthenware mug, slid it behind the tall stoneware peanut bowl where Iuno’s line of sight couldn’t reach, and took a deep, silent swallow while her left hand dutifully lifted the tiny condiment dish toward Iuno in a toast. “To my one small cup, Iuiu!”

Iuno looked suspiciously at the condiment dish, which was still full, and nodded once. Amy bit the inside of her cheek to keep from snorting into her plum wine.

Down at the foot of the table, Xiao Luo stopped beside the upturned butter-firkin where Suoming sat with Nine Shadows upright between her knees and her iron-shod boots swinging two inches off the floorboards.

The boy looked down at the top of Suoming’s red head—level with the vinegar cruet—and gave her a kindly, gap-toothed grin.

“Auntie says the plum wine’s too sour for little girls,” Xiao Luo said, bending down with his hands on his knees. “I’ve got sugared winter-melon water in the pantry if you want a mug, little sister.”

Six conversations along the middle of the board stopped dead.

Hsin’s ears shot straight up. Ruoyun’s spectacles flashed; Longjue clapped both hands over her mouth and shook from her bare heels to her dragon crests.

Suoming didn’t look up from the peanut she was shelling.

Buling leaned sideways across two stools, lifted her yellow talisman with one finger so the boy could get a good look at her red eyes, and rapped the pine with her knuckle.

“Boy,” Buling said gently. “You just came up from Mengzhou with the carts, is it?”

“Wednesday, miss,” Xiao Luo said, blinking at her hat.

“This *little sister* has been on the Hold’s muster roll since before your great-grandfather grew hair, and she used to be Intendant of the Ministry of Sentinel Affairs. You give her melon water, she’ll fold you into that umbrella and post you back down the gorge.”

Xiao Luo went the colour of raw dough. The lacquer tray rattled in his hands like dry bones. “I—Intendant—Elder—”

“White tea if you have it,” Suoming said to the peanut dish. “Boiled water if you don’t. Leave the bowl.”

She lifted the dish of spiced peanuts off the corner of his tray herself and set it by her elbow. Xiao Luo bolted for the kitchen hatch.

“Right!” Lupa slammed her stoneware mug of millet ale down and swept the middle of the table clear of shells with one forearm. She planted her right elbow on the wet wood and jutted her chin at Longjue. “You and me, dragon. Finger-guessing. Loser drinks a bowl of the sixty-day sorghum.”

Longjue was up on one knee on the bench before the sentence finished, shoving her wide teal sleeves past her elbows. “Three out of five! Pipa, pour the bowls!”

Ciaccona splashed two unglazed bowls full from the big clay jar while Amy scrambled up onto the settle-back, unhooked the tavern’s tally-slate from its nail, and wedged a stub of chalk between her fingers.

“Book’s open!” Amy yelled over the noise. “Two coppers a bout! No kicking under the benches!”

“Two good brothers!” Lupa roared, throwing three fingers into the gap.

“Five emperors!” Longjue barked back, throwing two—and catching the five on the snap before Lupa’s wrist came down.

“Drink, wolf!” Longjue slapped the board. “You twitched your left ear before you threw!”

“My ear didn’t move!” Lupa snarled, tossing back the first bowl of sorghum in one gulp that made her tail bush out like a pine-brush. “Again!”

Within five minutes the three-out-of-five had turned into best of eleven, and neither of them was sitting anymore. Lupa had one booted foot planted squarely on the seat of her bench and the other on the edge of the table between the peanut saucers; Longjue stood barefoot on the opposite bench with her dragon crests scraping the smoke-blackened ceiling joists, her dark hair flying and her face bright red from the spirit, yet her hand flashed out with terrifying, razor-sharp precision on every beat.

“Six roads!”

“Eight horses!”

“All ten!”

Wen and three off-duty laundry women who had squeezed in by the door were pounding their palms on the wood in rhythm. Up on the settle-back, Amy was taking coppers with her left hand and chalking the slate with her right—drawing four marks under **DRAGON**, wiping one off with her thumb when Lupa’s ears went crimson, then sneaking an extra mark onto **WOLF** while Buling was shouting at Longjue’s elbow, keeping the tally dead tied so the pot in her lap grew heavier every round.

“Wait!” Longjue suddenly spun round on her bench, her bare heel kicking an empty saucer into the sawdust, and pointed a long finger up at the tie-beam over the bar, where the fat clay jar of ten-year wintersweet sat sealed in red wax. “The pot! Landlady! You said one cup till you took the jar!”

Hsin, who had quietly finished her first cup of yellow wine, her free second cup, and half of a third cup that Ciaccona had pushed across the board while arguing about modes, lifted her chin. Her cheeks were pink and her fox ears stood high and twitching in the warm steam.

“Set the pot out, Auntie Luo,” Hsin said grandly, rising to her feet and brushing peanut salt off her cuffs. “And bring the arrows.”

Auntie Luo dragged the tall, narrow-necked copper pitch-pot out from behind the counter, set it on the flagstones by the cellar trapdoor, and paced off six strides to a chalk line by the stove. She handed out nine reed arrows—three each to Longjue, Hsin, and Qingxiao, who had walked over from the corner bench with her storehouse oilcloth still wrapped round her shoulders and a roasted chestnut in her left hand.

“House rules!” Auntie Luo barked, planting her fists on her hips. “Both feet behind the chalk! Three arrows in the neck takes the jar!”

Longjue stepped up first. She drank a mouthful from her red gourd, wiped her mouth on her forearm, narrowed one teal eye, and whipped her three reeds across the room in rapid succession—*clink, clink, clack*.

The first two dropped clean down the narrow copper throat. The third hit the flared lip, spun twice round the rim, and clattered onto the flagstones.

“Two!” Buling yelled, waving her hat. “Dragon choked on the third!”

“The draft from the door caught the feather!” Longjue protested, shoving Hsin’s shoulder as she stepped back. “Go on, Landlady, beat two!”

Hsin stepped to the chalk line. She drew herself up to her full height, held the first reed between two fingers like a calligraphy brush, aimed for three seconds, and threw.

It went clean in.

She threw the second. Clean in.

As she drew back her arm for the third, her foot slipped half an inch on a dropped peanut shell. The reed flew four inches wide to the left of the copper neck—and at the exact same instant, out from under the hem of Hsin’s coat along the floor-timbers snaked the long, gold, dark-tipped ninth tail.

The tip of the tail gave the falling arrow’s shaft a tiny, lightning-fast flick in mid-air.

The reed bounced sideways off the fur and dropped straight down the neck of the pot with a hollow *plunk*.

“HEY!” Longjue roared, lunging forward with both hands outstretched while the whole tavern erupted. “I saw that! The black tail! She used the ninth tail!”

“The arrow entered the vessel without touching the floor,” Hsin said with immense imperial dignity, though her ears were swivelled halfway back and twitching furiously. “The rules on the chalkboard specify three arrows in the copper pot. They make no mention of atmospheric deflection.”

“Atmospheric deflection my bare foot! Auntie Luo, she batted it in with her butt!”

Before Auntie Luo could adjudicate, Qingxiao stepped up beside them.

She didn’t put down her chestnut, and she didn’t step up to the chalk line. Standing two paces behind the mark with the oilcloth bundled under her chin, she held all three reeds together in one fist, barely glanced toward the cellar trapdoor, and tossed the bundle underhand as if she were throwing scraps to chickens.

The three reeds separated in the air, curved in three identical moon-white arcs over Longjue’s head, and dropped dead silent down the centre of the copper throat at the exact same instant without touching the brass rim once.

The room went quiet for one second.

Qingxiao peeled her chestnut and ate it.

“Tie between the cheat and the Paragon!” Longjue bellowed, already climbing onto a stool to reach the rafters before Auntie Luo could fetch the step-ladder. “Down it comes!”

She unhooked the heavy clay jar from the beam, knocked the wax plug in with the heel of her palm, and started pouring straight into whatever vessels were held out—mugs, teacups, empty peanut saucers, and Mei’s earthenware cup, which Mei thrust boldly into the middle of the scrum while Iuno was pinned behind Cartethyia’s bench.

By the time the jar was half-empty, Lupa and Longjue were back on top of the benches roaring out the five-emperors call at the top of their lungs, Buling had climbed onto the butter-firkin to film them, and Cartethyia was laughing so hard she knocked three stoneware mugs off the board into the sawdust, where two of them cracked in half.

Auntie Luo stood behind her counter looking at the two broken mugs, the empty wintersweet jar, her dry kettle, and a pile of copper cash on her bar top big enough to pay her rent till spring.

She banged her iron ladle twice against the salt-crock until the din dropped by half a notch.

“Ladies!” Auntie Luo shouted, grinning from ear to ear with all her gold teeth showing. “Bless your hearts and your purses, every last cup and saucer in my house is either dirty, cracked, or in the wolf’s hand, and you’ve drunk the well-bucket dry! Take it down the lane to the Brazen Carp before you break my sawhorses!”

***

The cold air outside hit Yangyang’s cheeks and made her giggle.

It was a small sound, right at the back of her throat, and she raised both mittens to cover her mouth because she had no idea where it had come from. At the Red Ladle she had drunk her warm pear wine, the free second cup, a saucer of the ten-year wintersweet that Longjue had splashed into her hands, and half of Mei’s condiment-dish yellow wine when Mei swapped their cups behind the peanut bowl.

The first real snow of the winter was coming down through the gorge cut in fat, lazy white feathers that spun round the eaves-lanterns and settled soft on the cobbles. Yangyang’s boots felt as if they were walking an inch and a half above the frost. Every time she turned her head, the streetlamps left little golden trails across the dark like sleepy fireflies, and her cerulean collar-feathers were fluffed out so wide and warm under her chin that she felt like a pigeon on a bakery flue.

Down the hill, the double doors of the Brazen Carp crashed open against their outer stops.

A barrel-chested woman in a greased sheepskin vest marched out into the snow brandishing a long copper soup-ladle over her head.

“In here, ladies!” she bellowed up the lane. “Auntie Luo’s out of ale! Third cup’s free at the Carp, and there’s a whole cauldron of mutton-and-turnip broth on the front fire!”

Four doors further down by the cooper’s arch, an upstairs casement at the Willow Jar flew open, and a thin woman in a green woollen shawl leaned out with a brass hand-gong, whacking it with a padded mallet until the icicles rattled along the gutters.

“Half-price on the three-year osmanthus!” the Willow Jar keeper yelled over the gong. “And free chili-oil bean-curd if the Sword Transcendent comes inside!”

All along the second terrace, wooden shutters were rattling up on their chains. Women from the upper laundries and the weaving sheds came bundling out of the side alleys in twos and threes with shawls pinned over their hair, drawn out into the snow by the gong and the shouting. Round the corner from the barracks stair came Warden Liangyu with her heavy pike left in the guardroom rack for once, her dark hair braided loose down her back and three off-duty women Wardens from the south gate walking at her heels.

“Warden!” Lupa roared, sliding three feet through the fresh drift to fling an arm around Liangyu’s neck. “You’re late! We drank Auntie Luo out of mugs!”

“Ge told me if I came back to the gatehouse before midnight he’d bar the door,” Liangyu said, grinning as Lupa hauled her into the crowd.

Beside the cistern, Wen had spotted Old Sister Zhao from the dried-fungus stall standing with a basket on her arm; Wen took the basket out of her hands, set it under the dry eave of the bun barrow, and hooked Sister Zhao’s elbow into her own as if they were twenty years old and going to the river fair.

In the middle of the lane, Mei had stopped walking to tip her head back and catch snowflakes on her tongue. She had her arms spread wide for balance, her coat-tails swinging, and she missed four flakes in a row before stumbling half a step sideways into Yangyang’s shoulder.

“Whoa,” Mei said happily, catching Yangyang’s mittened hand to steady herself. Her cheeks were bright pink in the lantern-light, and her breath smelled of wintersweet plum. “The snow’s moving sideways, Outrider.”

“The snow is falling straight down,” Yangyang said, and giggled again because her own tongue felt slightly too thick for the *sh* in *snow*. She looked down at Mei’s bare right hand, which had closed warm and rough-callused around her mitten, and then up at Mei’s golden eyes.

“When we were in Jinzhou,” Yangyang said in her quiet, plain voice, without a trace of the muster roll in it, “whenever you walked ahead of me on the path, I used to watch the back of your ribbon to see which way the wind was going, even when I already knew.”

Mei’s head tilted down. The big white flakes settled in her dark hair and didn’t melt at once.

“Yeah?” Mei said softly.

“Yes,” Yangyang said, smiling up at her. “Because if I looked at the ribbon, I didn’t have to look away when you turned round.”

Mei’s breath came out in a warm white cloud between them. She didn’t let go of Yangyang’s mitten; instead she slid her thumb inside the knitted wool cuff at Yangyang’s wrist, right where the pulse beat warm against the skin, and squeezed it once.

“Every time I come out of a door,” Mei said, her voice dropping low and fond and entirely unguarded in the snow, “I look for the blue feathers first. Even before I look for the stove.”

They stood there in the middle of the cobbles for three long beats, holding hands between their coats while the snow drifted down over their sleeves.

Behind them, walking through the slush beside Chisa, Iuno watched the two of them under the lamp—Mei’s thumb tucked inside Yangyang’s cuff, and Yangyang’s blue collar-feathers lifted soft against Mei’s dark woollen shoulder.

Iuno had drunk two cups of yellow wine and a quarter-cup of the wintersweet that Lupa had sloshed across her wrist. Her left shoulder crystal gave a small chime as she took three unhurried steps forward through the drift, slipped her living right hand firmly through the crook of Mei’s free left elbow, and left Mei’s right hand right where it was in Yangyang’s mitten.

“If the pair of you intend to take root in the middle of the cart-track,” Iuno said dryly, leaning her cheek for half a second against Mei’s damp shoulder, “at least do it nearer the brazier.”

Before Mei could answer, something white and packed hard as a turnip whizzed past Cartethyia’s ear and exploded in a shower of powder against the back of Lupa’s head.

Lupa spun round, snow plastering the back of her wolf ears. “WHO THREW THAT?”

Thirty feet up the lane by the horse-trough, Longjue stood barefoot in the drift tossing a second snowball from palm to palm, her grin splitting her face. Beside her, Buling had already ducked behind the stone cistern rim and was packing ammunition with both hands like a siege engineer.

“Ambush!” Amy shrieked with delight, diving behind a stacked cord of firewood and flinging two snowballs at once that missed Longjue by a yard and hit Ruoyun squarely in the middle of her spotless teal chest.

Ruoyun looked down at the wet white splat between her ruffled lapels. Slowly, she raised her head. Her brass spectacles caught the lantern-glare.

“Buling,” Ruoyun said in a voice that curdled the slush. “You are dead.”

She did not bend down to scoop snow with her hands. She kicked the toe of her dark Mary Jane into the drift, flicked a solid block of crusted ice up onto her instep, and volleyed it straight over the cistern rim with the force of a catapult stone, knocking Buling’s round winter hat clean off her silver hair and into the horse-trough.

“Aiya, foul! Foul lah!” Buling squawked, lunging for her hat before a massive two-handed barrage from Lupa and Cartethyia buried the entire cistern in white powder.

Within thirty seconds the lane was impenetrable with flying snow. Longjue vaulted onto the bun-barrow roof to hold the high ground; Amy and Liangyu formed an infantry line behind the pickle barrels; and Qingxiao, standing calmly in the middle of the crossfire with her oilcloth pulled up over her nose, raised one forefinger and sliced an incoming snowball neatly into four symmetrical quarters with a single falling plum-petal of Sword Intent before it could touch her hair.

Down by the threshold of the Brazen Carp, entirely ignoring the artillery whistling past her ears, Suisui stood straight-backed under the lantern with her closed fan held against her chin, facing the barrel-chested keeper in the sheepskin vest.

“Your chalkboard,” Suisui said in her most gracious Mingting boardroom register, “promises the third cup free and mutton broth on the house.”

“Aye!” the keeper shouted over the thud of Lupa hitting Longjue in the ribs with a snow-boulder. “Best broth on the terrace!”

“There are twenty-one of us including the Wardens,” Suisui said smoothly, her wine-flushed cheeks glowing above her fur collar. “If we bring the Sword Transcendent and the Sentinel of Mengzhou through your doors for two hours, every weaver on the terrace will follow us in to stare. Therefore, the second cup will also be half-price, the turnip pickles will be complimentary, and you will include three platters of sliced cold beef shin without charging for the garlic sauce.”

The keeper blinked at her, looked past her shoulder at the Xuan Paragon bisecting snowballs in the street, and spat on her palm.

“Done for two platters of beef, my lady, and you pay cash for the first round!”

“Two platters and a dish of sugared walnuts,” Suisui countered instantly, sweeping her fan open an inch as she stepped across the threshold. “Show us to the middle braziers.”

***

By the time the second round of mutton broth arrived at the Brazen Carp, Iuno had lost track of Mei’s cups.

She had tried to keep a tally on the corner of her mind the way she had once kept the granary returns for Septimont: *one condiment dish at the Red Ladle, one earthenware mug she thought she hid behind the peanuts, one splash of wintersweet from the clay jar*. Then they had walked through the door of the Carp, where four cast-iron braziers were roaring cherry-red down the middle aisle, and someone had handed Mei a bowl of mulled plum wine to warm her fingers, and ten minutes later Mei was drinking out of a blue glazed teacup that she swore on her honour was roasted barley tea.

“Barley tea does not smell of fermented sorghum, sweetheart,” Iuno said, leaning her living elbow on the wet pine board while the dry heat of the nearest brazier soaked pleasantly into the frost-crack on her bare left shoulder.

“Wen put ginger in it!” Mei declared at top volume over the din, her face radiant and her arm slung round Amy’s shoulders while she tried to pick up a slippery pickled mushroom with one chopstick. “Ginger smells like everything! Ask Suoming!”

Iuno opened her mouth to reply, realized she had drunk three cups of the three-year osmanthus wine that Suisui had haggled down to half-price, and decided that the mushroom was doing nobody any harm.

Up on the low cedar storyteller’s platform at the back of the room sat a thin, sour-faced local man in a scholar’s cap, sawing away at a two-stringed spike fiddle that had slipped a quarter-tone flat in the damp heat of the braziers.

At the end of their bench, Ciaccona had just knocked back her second bowl of sixty-day spirit with Longjue. Every time the fiddle’s horsehair bow scraped across the slack inner string, Ciaccona’s left eyebrow twitched higher.

On the twelfth bar, Ciaccona banged her bowl down, stood up on the bench-rail, and pointed her splinted left forefinger straight at the platform like a pistol.

“Friend!” Ciaccona shouted across forty heads. “Is that an instrument you’re holding, or are you strangling a wet goose in a sack?”

The fiddler stopped mid-bow, his mouth dropping open. “This is a classical Mengzhou court air!”

“It’s a fifth flat on the bottom string and your bridge is crooked!” Ciaccona hopped down off the bench, snatched her lute out of its case with her good hand, and marched up the aisle between the braziers with her green coat-tails flying. “Shove over! Give me four bars on the G and try not to murder the turn!”

The weavers by the stove pounded their mugs on the tables and howled. The fiddler, purple in the face, rosined his bow furiously and tore into a breakneck mountain reel at double tempo, glaring at Ciaccona’s wooden finger-splint as if he meant to snap the other three.

Ciaccona didn’t even sit down. She planted one boot on the edge of the cedar platform, braced the belly of the lute against her hip, and answered him measure for measure with three fingers and her thumb—sliding up the neck on her ring finger where the splint wouldn’t bend, throwing in a wicked, syncopated Ragunnese harbour cross-rhythm that made the fiddler miss his shift on the third turn, and then doubling the melody an octave higher until the man’s bow-hair frayed into a white tuft and he sawed clean off the end of his fingerboard with a loud *squawk*.

The whole tavern roared. Ciaccona bowed low from the waist, took a gulp from somebody’s held-out ale mug, and stayed up on the platform.

“Clear the floor!” Suisui called out, rising from the middle bench with her cheeks bright pink and her hair-coil listing five degrees to the left despite the horn comb. She rapped her closed fan against her palm like a theatre director. “The orchestra is warmed! Warden Liangyu, you know *The General’s Riding Whip*!”

Liangyu, who had drunk four mugs of dark barley beer with Lupa and had her collar unbuttoned to the collarbone, stood up enthusiastically at the next table. “I know the chorus!”

“Then come in on the drum-beat!” Suisui commanded, and launched at full chest volume into the tragic third-act aria from *The General’s Riding Whip*.

Suisui sang it a quarter-tone flat on purpose, the way she always sang at New Year to rescue her father, except that her father wasn’t here and she had drunk four cups of osmanthus wine, so she slid a full half-step under Ciaccona’s chord and held the low note like a foghorn on the Mingting canal.

Then Liangyu came in on the harmony.

Around the tables, spoons stopped halfway to mouths.

The last Xuan Warden of Xuanfang Hold had a voice like an iron quarry-cart going downhill with the brake locked—loud, fearless, utterly joyful, and at least three notes removed from any scale known to mortal or Celestial ears. On the platform, Ciaccona looked as if someone had poured vinegar down her shirt; she gritted her teeth, bent all three strings sideways at once to catch the Warden somewhere between C-sharp and Thursdays, and hammered the rhythm on the soundboard with her thumb while Lupa and the gatehouse Wardens banged their stoneware mugs on the pine and roared along with every single off-key syllable.

Across the aisle, at the table by the kitchen screen, the Hold’s old women were making enough noise to drown out the chorus.

Longjue and Buling were playing chopstick-fencing over the platter of cold beef shin—four bamboo sticks clacking together like duelling sabres while Buling tried to snag the tendon piece with her left hand and Longjue parried her knuckles without spilling a drop from the wine bowl balanced in her other palm.

“Aiya, you use orchard technique on a snack, shameless!” Buling yelled, her round winter hat tipped back on her neck and the yellow talisman flapping over her ear as she lunged under Longjue’s guard, missed the beef entirely, and stabbed a pickled garlic clove so hard it shot across the table and bounced off Ruoyun’s spectacle lens.

Ruoyun did not wipe her lens.

For the past hour, while Ruoyun had been glaring at Qingxiao’s mug, Buling and Longjue had been steadily pouring top-shelf wintersweet into Ruoyun’s red-glazed cup every time she turned her head to shout at a serving-girl. Ruoyun had drunk six cups of it under the impression that it was the same first cup she had started with. Two hard, bright spots of crimson burned on her sharp cheekbones; her brass spectacles sat crooked on her nose, and her spine was so stiff she wasn’t touching the bench-back.

She turned her head slowly toward Buling.

“You,” Ruoyun said, her barracks voice cutting clean through the clatter of bowls, “spent a hundred years skulking in southern teahouses peddling painted paper to superstitious fishwives because you didn’t have the stomach to stand a single night watch on the wall, and you still hold your chopsticks like a toddler digging up worms.”

Buling’s chopsticks stopped over the saucer. The grin went off her face. She pulled her hand back, laid the bamboo sticks down across her bowl without a word, and reached into her pocket to check her Terminal screen, her mouth shut tight.

Before Longjue could throw a peanut shell at Ruoyun’s horns, a woman sitting at the small round table behind them—a sharp-nosed clerk’s wife from the Ministry lodgings on the second terrace, wearing a fur-trimmed cape—turned on her stool and laughed nastily into her cup.

“Let the immortals fight over the scraps,” the woman said loudly to her neighbour, pitching her voice so it carried right across Hsin’s back. “When the dispatches reach the court in Mengzhou next month, Minister Shen Qiu will have the whole pack of them put on the cart-road, starting with the fake fox in the stolen coat.”

At the middle of the bench, Hsin’s ears flattened straight back against her skull. Her hands, resting open beside her wine cup, gave a single small jerk toward her cuffs and stopped.

Ruoyun stood up.

She didn’t shove the bench back; she rose straight up from her ankles like a drawn blade, her spotless ruffled cuffs hanging crisp at her wrists, and turned round to face the Ministry table.

“Stand up when you speak of the sovereign of this Hold, you ink-licking baggage,” Ruoyun said.

The clerk’s wife went white, then red, half-rising from her stool. “Do you know who my husband works for—”

“Your husband works for a man who shuttered his windows while this Hold kept his roof up,” Ruoyun said, taking one measured step between the braziers until the woman had to crane her neck back to look at her. “And you are drinking mutton broth out of iron pots that were cast in these gorges while your ancestors were still boiling tree-bark in the mud. If you open your mouth toward this bench again before the snow melts, I will personally carry you up to the south barbican by your ears and drop you over the lip to see if your cape flies.”

The clerk’s wife sat down so hard her stool cracked on the flags. Within ten seconds she and her two companions had snatched their cloaks off the pegs and bolted out through the front doors into the snow without waiting for their change.

Around the nearby tables, six weavers and a quarry-woman banged their knuckles approvingly on the wood.

On the bench, Hsin sat staring up at the back of Ruoyun’s teal dress with her mouth slightly open and her ears crooked at two completely different angles, looking as if someone had just hit her over the head with a warm brick.

Ruoyun turned back to the table, caught Hsin looking at her, and curled her lip.

“Wipe your chin, Sentinel,” Ruoyun snapped, sitting back down and straightening her skirt. “You’ve got plum juice on your collar. It’s revolting.”

Across the aisle, near the wood-pile by the kitchen door, Butcher Sun’s wife sat on an upturned grain-tub with two women from the third terrace. Their nephew Sun Hu’s name was written in the ledger by the well.

She had a stoneware mug of barley ale in her hand. Through the orange smoke of the braziers, she looked straight across the room at Hsin sitting with her cuffs untucked.

Butcher Sun’s wife didn’t smile, and she didn’t come over. She raised her stoneware mug two inches off her knee toward Hsin’s bench, drank a slow mouthful, and turned back to the cobbler’s widow beside her.

Hsin’s throat worked once. Beside her, Suoming reached out with two small, rough-knuckled fingers and slid a warm, untouched cup of honeyed wine one inch across the pine until it touched Hsin’s right wrist. Hsin closed her fingers round it and drank it down.

“HEY!” Longjue jumped up onto the seat of her bench, planting one bare foot on the edge of the table and banging her red gourd against the roof-post until the dust sifted down from the rafters. “The Landlady’s feeling generous! Next jar of ten-year osmanthus for the whole house is on the Sentinel!”

Forty mugs went up with a deafening roar.

Hsin’s ears dropped flat. She reached into her left sleeve, fished out two worn copper cash with square holes, a twist of red string, and a dried hawthorn pit, and stared darkly into her palm under the table edge.

Two seats away, Suisui—still flushed from her aria—unhooked her embroidered Guild pouch without looking down and slid three plump silver ingots across the wet wood until they clinked against Hsin’s knuckles.

“I will have the treasury clerk—” Hsin began low.

“Don’t mention it, Lady Hsin,” Suisui said grandly, waving her fan at the ceiling. “Put it on the Mengzhou infrastructure bond!”

At the middle table, Mei had somehow acquired a fresh bowl of the ten-year osmanthus before the serving-girl even reached their bench. She leaned both elbows on the table, her chin propped in her hands, gazing at Iuno with huge, shining golden eyes.

“Iuiu,” Mei said loudly over the clatter of the dipper. “Your hair is so shiny in the firelight. Like a fish. No, wait—like a moon-fish. A really pretty silver fish that’s angry at everybody.”

Iuno felt the osmanthus wine hum warm and loose behind her temples. She looked at Mei’s ridiculous, adoring grin, looked at Lupa snorting into her ale across the board, and felt thirty years of theatrical silk step smoothly to the front of her tongue while her own common sense sat down in the back row with a cup of tea.

*Oh, step aside, darling,* the High Priestess purred, and tilted Iuno’s chin up at a regal forty-five-degree angle.

“A fish,” Iuno’s mouth said, her voice dropping into the resonant, velvety basilica contralto that had once made three legions of Septimontian heavy infantry drop to one knee on the marble. She reached across the peanut shells with her living right hand, caught Mei by the front of her woollen lapel, and tugged her four inches closer across the pine. “My sweet, illiterate barbarian, I have spent the last two hours watching you smuggle yellow wine behind a saucer of turnips as if I do not know the exact angle of your elbow when you are lying to me. And if you compare me to marine life again before midnight, I shall take you behind the grain-shed and make you apologise until your knees give out.”

Down the entire length of the middle bench, every single person froze.

Lupa’s stoneware mug stopped an inch from her teeth; she choked on a peanut, coughed twice, and stared fixedly at the ceiling rafters with her ears scarlet. Amy clapped both hands over her eyes and yelled, “GROSS! I’M SITTING RIGHT HERE!” Cartethyia’s mouth formed a silent *O*, and Mei’s face went bright red from her collar to the tips of her ears, her golden eyes wide and thrilled.

“Okay!” Mei squeaked.

Iuno blinked once, hard, as the High Priestess strolled smugly back into the shadows of her skull.

Iuno’s hand flew off Mei’s lapel as if the wool were red-hot iron. The blood rushed up her neck into her cheekbones until her ears burned like two coals; she grabbed her teacup, discovered it was empty, and set it down with a sharp *clack*, staring straight ahead at the vinegar bottle with her jaw locked.

*You’re welcome, darling,* the voice murmured from the back of her mind. *Look at her face. You’d have taken six months to say half of that.*

*Do not speak to me until Thursday,* Iuno thought back furiously, and slipped her hand down to the outside of her coat pocket, pressing her palm flat against the small, hard square of the unopened velvet box beside her tea flask. She kept her hand outside the cloth.

Mei, still glowing like a brazier, suddenly shoved her bench back, planted one boot on the seat and the other on the cross-rail, and hoisted her osmanthus bowl high over her head with both hands.

“A toast!” Mei shouted at the top of her lungs, swaying three inches to the left as forty women turned to cheer. “To Auntie Wen! And to the fish! And to everybody at the table, same time next—*ow*!”

Halfway through rising onto her toes, the closed scar across her abdomen pulled hard in the twist.

Mei’s breath caught with a sharp little hiss; her left hand dropped from the bowl to clamp tight against her side, and she sat straight down off the rail onto Iuno’s lap with a heavy *thump* that splashed osmanthus wine across both their knees.

***

They got thrown out of the Brazen Carp twenty minutes later—not for fighting, though Ruoyun had tried to throw a stoneware saucer at a portrait of Minister Shen Qiu’s uncle on the back wall, and not for singing, though Liangyu and Lupa had done four verses of a Septimontian barracks ballad that made the serving-girl blush through her apron—but because the Carp ran completely out of ale, osmanthus wine, and clean bowls at the exact same bell.

“The Willow Jar’s still got the lantern out!” Longjue roared, kicking the Carp’s double doors open into the knee-deep snow and leading the charge four doors down the lane toward the cooper’s arch.

Lupa stomped through the drift right behind her, carrying Mei’s left arm hooked over her shoulder while Iuno held the back of Mei’s belt and lectured the back of Mei’s head at a steady, furious clip.

“Rule one: one small cup,” Iuno recited into the falling snow, her words slightly over-enunciated at the consonants. “You have consumed six. Rule two: sit with your back to a solid wall. You stood on a bench-rail. Rule three—”

“You said you’d take me behind the grain-shed!” Mei cackled, leaning happily against Lupa’s ribs with her hand pressed to her coat front. “Everybody heard you! Lupa heard you!”

“I didn’t hear anything!” Lupa barked, her ears still hot enough to melt the snow off the tips. “Keep walking, I’m not carrying you up the steps if my knees freeze!”

The Willow Jar wasn’t a ground-floor hall like the Carp; its main drinking room was up four wide wooden steps under a low, smoke-darkened cedar ceiling barely seven feet high, hung thick with dried gourds and paper charms. The thin keeper in the green shawl took one look at twenty-one snow-covered women, nine gold tails, and Suisui’s silver pouch coming through her door, shrieked for her sister to bring up the five-year sorghum crock from the cellar, and shoved three round tables together in the middle of the room.

Lupa dumped Mei onto the cushioned corner bench against the back wall, where Iuno immediately wedged her in with two bolsters, and then Lupa spun round to the centre table.

The millet ale and the sorghum had settled into a glorious, roaring furnace behind Lupa’s breastbone. Her tail was wagging so hard it knocked a stool over every time she turned.

She slammed her right elbow down in the middle of the round pine table, making the peanut bowls jump two inches in the air, and pointed her left claw straight at Longjue’s nose.

“No finger-games, dragon!” Lupa bellowed. “Arm-wrestle! Right here! Loser buys the sorghum crock and crawls under the table!”

Longjue’s eyes blazed bright teal. She vaulted over the bench instead of walking round it, planted her bare feet wide on the floorboards, and locked her right palm into Lupa’s with a slap like a pistol shot.

“Book’s open!” Amy yelled, scrambling up onto the window-ledge with her tally-slate held over her head and a fistful of copper cash in her mitten. She had drunk two mugs of hot plum wine and a cup of ginger beer, and she was laughing so hard that tiny sparks of white static popped and fizzed at the tips of her pink hair like miniature fireworks. “Three to one on the wolf! Two to one on the dragon! Five to one the table breaks before either of them goes down!”

“Go!” Buling shouted, dropping her winter hat like a starter’s flag.

Lupa’s shoulder dropped; every muscle in her forearm corded hard as iron cable, and Longjue’s bare heels dug into the cracks of the cedar floorboards without yielding a hair’s breadth. Their locked knuckles quivered dead vertical over the middle of the pine board. Lupa let out a low, rumbling arena growl, calling up a flicker of heat that made the varnish smoke under her elbow; Longjue bared her teeth in a huge, silent grin, sinking her weight straight down through her hips the way she rooted herself on the orchard wall.

The centre pine plank gave a long, splintering *crack*.

“Put your thumb down, dragon!” Lupa roared, veins standing out on her neck as she forced Longjue’s wrist half an inch toward the peanut dish.

“Your elbow’s sliding, wolf!” Longjue laughed back, wrenching it dead level again while the whole room pounded the benches and screamed.

Ten feet away at the side table, Cartethyia was sitting beside Ciaccona’s lute case with an empty earthenware dish between her hands.

She had drunk four cups of the sour plum wine at the Red Ladle, two bowls of mulled berry wine at the Carp, and a mug of sweet osmanthus here. For the first two hours she had giggled at everything including the salt-spoon; ten minutes ago she had gone very quiet, staring down into the empty porcelain dish where the last slice of sour pickled radish had been before Buling ate it off a chopstick.

Cartethyia’s lower lip wobbled. Two huge, shining tears welled up in her blue eyes and spilled over her cheeks.

“Cia,” Cartethyia whispered, tugging on Ciaccona’s green sleeve while Ciaccona was busy arguing with Wen about whether garlic belonged in sweet sausage. “Cia. The radish is gone.”

“Ask the girl for another dish, cara, wait a second—you cannot put raw garlic in blood-sausage, Auntie, it ruins the—”

Cartethyia let go of the sleeve. Another tear splashed onto her knee.

“Nobody is listening to me,” Cartethyia said to the empty dish, her voice rising from a sniffle into a sudden, tremendous, tragic sob, “because I am too small!”

A blinding flash of blue-white wind and golden light exploded across the side table.

“WAIT, CARTY, NO, THE CEILING—” Amy screamed from the window-ledge.

Where the small knight in the crooked laurel had been sitting, nine feet of the Blessed Maiden Fleurdelys rose straight up off the bench in full archaic splendour.

The top of her crystal laurel went *CRUNCH* straight through the seven-foot cedar ceiling boards into the grain-loft overhead, showering the entire tavern in dried corn-husks, plaster dust, and three strings of hanging garlic gourds. Her long, regal white sleeves—still slightly too short for her reach—flung wide as she tried to keep her balance on the tipping bench, knocking the hanging brass oil-lamp off its chain into the pickle barrel and sweeping four mugs of wine clean into Buling’s lap.

“*Hark!*” Fleurdelys boomed in her deep, echoing chapel-ceiling voice, wedged from the neck up between two oak joists while plaster rained down her shoulders and she burst into fresh, booming tears. “*The ceilings of this realm are constructed for beetles! And the zombie hath consumed my radish!*”

“Come down out of the loft, you lunatic!” Ciaccona yelled, jumping onto the bench to tug frantically at Fleurdelys’s sash while Buling hopped on one leg shaking cold plum wine out of her silk trousers. “Turn back before the landlady charges us for the roof!”

With a second pop of wind and gold feathers, Cartethyia dropped back down onto the bench in her own small shape, covered from head to toe in white plaster dust and dried corn-silk, hiccuped once, and immediately buried her face in Ciaccona’s stomach while Ciaccona held her head and swore in Ragunnese at the ceiling hole.

“Pay up on the ceiling!” Amy crowed from the window-sill, raking a heap of copper cash into her skirt while white static fizzed off her fringe.

At the middle table, neither Lupa nor Longjue had unlocked their hands during the explosion. Corn-husks littered both their shoulders, and the split down the middle of the pine table had widened to the breadth of a thumb.

With one final, tremendous heave that made her boots skid on the boards, Lupa drove Longjue’s knuckles two inches down—and the central trestle-pin underneath the table sheared clean in half with a sound like a cannon-shot.

Both halves of the round table folded inward into a V, dumping peanuts, chopsticks, six bowls, and the big earthenware sorghum crock straight into the gap between their knees before the whole contraption collapsed flat onto the floorboards in a tangle of splintered pine.

Lupa and Longjue landed sitting on opposite ends of the wreckage, their right hands still locked together in the air above the broken boards.

“Tie!” Longjue roared, roaring with laughter as she shook corn-shucks out of her dark hair. “The wood quit first!”

“My side was higher!” Lupa bellowed, scrambling up out of the kindling and flexing her right arm toward Chisa, who had calmly lifted her teacup off the table half a second before the pin snapped and was sitting untouched on her stool beside the debris. “Did you see that, Chisa? One more second and I had her wrist on the pine!”

Chisa looked at Lupa’s scraped elbow, then at the broken table, and took a sip of her sweet rice wine.

“Your left boot slipped before the pin broke,” Chisa said in her clear, level voice, right into the sudden lull after the crash. “And you only challenged her because she looked at me when she came through the door.”

The entire room heard every word of it.

Lupa froze in the middle of the broken planks. Her mouth opened; her wolf ears shot straight up and turned a deep, violent shade of beet-red that spread down her neck and under her collar.

“I DID NOT!” Lupa roared at arena volume, waving both arms wildly at the rafters while forty women howled with laughter. “SHE OWES ME FROM THE CARP! AND ANYWAY YOU’RE THE BEST-LOOKING PERSON IN THIS WHOLE DAMN GORGE AND IF ANYBODY LOOKS AT YOU SIDEWAYS I’LL PUT THEM THROUGH THE FLOOR TOO!”

Chisa blinked once behind her bangs. A tiny, faint pinkness touched the tips of her ears.

“Good,” Chisa said calmly, and set her cup on her knee.

“I DIDN’T SAY THAT!” Lupa shouted at Amy, who was rolling on the window-ledge kicking her heels in the air. “SHUT UP! NOBODY WRITE THAT DOWN!”

“Already on the slate!” Amy gasped, wiping tears of laughter off her cheeks with her chalky thumb.

Across the pile of broken table-boards, Hsin had risen from her bench with her nine gold tails bristled out behind her to keep them clear of the spilled sorghum. She pointed a shaking finger at Longjue, who was still sitting cross-legged on the split pine plank drinking out of her gourd.

“You!” Hsin said, her voice wobbling between imperial outrage and six cups of osmanthus wine. “You broke the table on my tab! That comes off the eleven strings!”

“Thirty-two strings!” Longjue shouted back, hopping lightly to her bare feet on the tilting plank without losing her balance by a fraction of an inch. “I’m adding a string for the plaster in my hair! Pay up, Landlady, or give me one of those tails for a duster!”

She made a playful grab for the nearest gold tail. Hsin yelped—a thoroughly undignified, high-pitched fox sound—whipped all nine tails round to her left, and lunged across the overturned bench to snatch Longjue’s red gourd off her sash cord. Longjue spun on one bare heel, laughing at the top of her lungs, caught Hsin by both loose cuffs as the bench flipped over sideways with a crash, and within three seconds the Sentinel of Mengzhou and her Celestial were wrestling across the sawdust over a lacquer gourd in a flying whirlwind of gold fur, teal silk, and kicked-up peanut shells.

“Give me the gourd, you insubordinate lizard!” Hsin panted, scrabbling one knee onto the intact side-table to get leverage over Longjue’s shoulder. In the heat of the grapple, her outline blurred gold at the edges—her ears lengthening, her sleeves beginning to fold inward toward four thin legs right on top of the dishes.

From the butter-tub by the wall, Suoming didn’t raise her voice and didn’t stand up.

“Hsin,” Suoming said.

The gold blur snapped back into solid coat-sleeves instantly. Hsin dropped off the table edge onto her boots, letting go of the gourd cord, and tried to smooth her hair with both hands as if she had merely been reaching for the vinegar.

Right in the middle of the room, sitting bolt upright on a stool with no back, Yangyang had been fast asleep for the last ten minutes with her hands folded neatly in her lap and her chin resting in the soft blue feathers of her collar.

As the overturned bench hit the floor beside her heel, Yangyang’s eyes opened wide. She looked straight ahead at the hole in the ceiling, the broken table, and the Sentinel brushing sawdust off her knees, and spoke in a ringing, crystal-clear parade-ground voice that stopped every tongue in the tavern:

“THE BIGGEST CAKE WAS ON MY SIDE OF THE TRAY!”

Nobody breathed for two seconds.

Yangyang blinked twice, her ears turning bright pink under her blue hair, gave a tiny, sleepy smile to the room at large, and closed her eyes again, her chin sinking back into her feathers.

At the far end of the intact bench, Qingxiao set down her eighth mug of hot ginger-and-sorghum wine.

For three hours she had matched Longjue and Lupa cup for cup without slurring a syllable, shifting her posture, or spilling a drop on her white stockings. Now she placed the earthenware mug dead square on the wood, rose to her feet without using her hands, and walked six slow, perfectly straight paces across the peanut shells to the corner cedar pillar by the coat-pegs.

She rested her forehead lightly against the timber post, closed her eyes inside the storehouse oilcloth, and went fast asleep standing up on both feet.

Behind the bar, the thin keeper in the green shawl stood holding her abacus against her chest, looking from the two-foot hole in her ceiling to the kindling of her round table and the Xuan Paragon snoring softly against her coat-post. Her eyes were wide with terror and sheer commercial rapture.

“Nine taels of silver!” the keeper squeaked, clicking the beads with trembling fingers. “Four for the oak joist, three for the pine table, two for the sorghum crock and the lamp!”

Suisui stepped smoothly over the broken table-leg, her hair-coil hanging halfway down her neck and her fan snapping open with a sharp *whack*.

“The table was white pine with a dry-rot knot in the centre pin, Auntie,” Suisui said, her voice loud, musical, and devastatingly polite. “Any carpenter in Mengzhou would value it at eighty coppers for firewood. And the hole in your ceiling was made by the Blessed Maiden of Ragunna, which means every carter from the south road will pay two cash just to come upstairs and look at the splinters. You will take four taels for the lot, and you will wrap two packets of spiced bean-curd in oiled paper for our walk home.”

“Five taels and one packet of bean-curd!” the keeper cried.

“Four taels and a half, two packets, and you lend us a lantern for the steps!” Suisui said, and clapped Hsin on the shoulder. “Lady Hsin, pay the woman!”

Hsin stared dimly at Suisui, who had already put her own silver pouch back in her sash, and slowly pulled a strip of red-ruled conduit paper and Old Chen’s borrowed charcoal pencil out of her cuff, scrawling a sovereign treasury draft across the back of a sluice-valve receipt with a hand that wobbled on the seal-mark.

As they filed down the narrow wooden stair into the snow, the keeper leaned out her broken upstairs casement with her chalk-board held in one hand and a stick of chalk in the other, already writing in huge letters for the morning market:

**THE WILLOW JAR**
**COME SEE WHERE THE TALL IMMORTAL WENT THROUGH THE ROOF**
**BROKEN BY THE SENTINEL’S PARTY — ONE COPPER TO LOOK (FREE IF YOU BUY WINE)**

***

Outside the Willow Jar’s arch, the snow had drifted halfway up the horse-trough, and the rest of the column was twenty paces ahead, milling around the foot of the long cliffside stair while Lupa hoisted Mei onto her back.

Hsin stopped in the middle of the lane because Longjue had her bare foot planted squarely on the trailing tip of Hsin’s eighth tail.

“Get off it,” Hsin said, swaying half an inch in her boots and glaring up at the dragon crests through a pleasant, roaring haze of osmanthus and wintersweet. “That is the third time tonight somebody has stood on my fur.”

“Settle the tab first, Landlady!” Longjue grinned, her breath steaming white in the snow as she took her foot off the tail and tapped her red gourd against Hsin’s collarbone. “You owed me thirty-one at dusk, you cheated with your butt at Auntie Luo’s, and you tried to bite my thumb under the bench upstairs! We’re at thirty-four strings unless you win one back right now!”

“Eleven strings!” Buling yelled, trudging back three steps through the drift with two oiled-paper packets of bean-curd tucked under her arm and her winter hat sitting crooked over one ear. “You cannot invent tax every time you change tavern lah!”

“Quiet, consultant!” Hsin waved a loose cuff at both of them, her fox ears standing straight up in the falling snow. Her boots were wet through the soles, there was peanut salt in her eyebrows, her head was spinning like a prayer-wheel, and when she shouted, nobody bowed—Longjue just laughed in her face and the weaver’s dog barked at her from a doorway. “Name the wager, you barefoot menace! Five strings off the count!”

Longjue looked round the snowy lane, spotted an icicle the length of a dagger hanging from the cooper’s gutter-spout fifteen paces away, and pointed her chin at it.

“One throw with a snow-ball!” Longjue declared. “Knock the big icicle off the spout! You hit it, five strings off! You miss, five strings on!”

“Done!” Hsin said before Suoming—standing three paces away under Nine Shadows—could open her mouth.

Hsin bent down, scooped up two handfuls of wet snow from the top of the horse-trough, and packed them together. Because she was drunk and her paws were cold, she didn’t pack it round; it came out shaped like a lopsided turnip. She squinted one eye at the cooper’s gutter, wound her arm back until three of her tails swung round her ankles, and hurled it.

The snowball sailed six feet high of the gutter, cleared the cooper’s low roof-ridge entirely, and dropped with a loud, hollow *CLANG* straight down the copper chimney-pipe of the closed bun-shop next door, sending a puff of black soot out the front shutters.

Longjue doubled over in the snow, slapping her bare knees and howling with laughter until her echoes bounced off the cliff wall.

“Thirty-nine strings!” Longjue roared. “You missed the whole house!”

“The wind took it!” Hsin shouted back, laughing despite herself and kicking a spray of loose powder at Longjue’s shins.

Buling groaned, fished a black stub of hearth-charcoal out of her pocket, and marched over to the whitewashed plaster wall beside the cooper’s arch. Standing on tiptoe, she wrote in foot-high, crooked characters across the lime-wash:

**HSIN TO LONGJUE: 16 STRINGS**

She stared squint-eyed at the **6** through her own four bowls of sorghum, decided it looked too much like a zero, drew an extra loop on the top that turned it into an **8**, crossed the **8** out with two diagonal slashes, and wrote **TWENTY-SOMETHING (CHECK IN MORNING)** underneath it before dropping the charcoal into the drift.

From the shadowed doorway of the cooper’s shed across the lane, the grey-haired Mengzhou captain from the open road stepped out into the lantern-light with a covered earthenware jar in his hands. He was off duty, wearing a plain woollen vest over his tunic.

He walked across the churned snow and stopped three paces from Hsin’s boots. He didn’t salute, and he didn’t bow.

“The boys at the lower gate boiled ginger root with brown sugar for the night watch,” the captain said plainly, setting the small, hot jar down on the flat stone coping of the horse-trough. “The wind’s coming round from the north.”

He turned on his heel and walked back down the lane toward the gatehouse without waiting for an answer.

Hsin stood still in the snow with her nine tails heaped round her calves, the laughter dying out of her throat as she looked at the steaming jar on the stone.

Beside her, Suoming’s iron heel-plates clicked once on the cobbles.

Suoming didn’t look at the captain’s back. She reached out from beneath the rim of her umbrella with two small fingers and slid the hot earthenware jar one inch closer along the coping until the rough clay touched the back of Hsin’s hand.

“Pick it up,” Suoming said. “We’re behind the stairs.”

***

Chisa walked up the forty-eight snow-covered steps of the cliffside stair by sound and by the heat of Lupa’s left hand.

The stair-lamps were spaced twenty paces apart and furred thick with wet snow, turning the dark gorge air into a grey blur where faces vanished at three yards. Chisa didn’t squint into the dark. She kept her right hand—with the loose red string wound twice above her wristbone—tucked inside Lupa’s broad, calloused left palm, and sorted the rest of the column out by the cadence of their breathing and the scrape of their heels on the hollowed stone risers.

Right beside her, Lupa was climbing with Mei hoisted squarely across her shoulders in a fireman’s carry, Lupa’s right arm locked round Mei’s knees to keep the weight off Mei’s stomach.

Hanging head-down over the back of Lupa’s wolf-hide vest, Mei was singing a song of her own invention about a turtle who lived in a wine-jar, tapping one finger in time against Lupa’s belt-buckle.

One step to Lupa’s right walked Iuno, her left shoulder crystal chiming softly at every riser as she kept her living hand braced against Mei’s shoulder to steady her and recited her audit to the snow.

“Breach four,” Iuno said, her voice stately, measured, and slightly blurred around the *s*’s. “Attempting to climb a roof-pillar in the Brazen Carp to inspect a dried gourd. Breach five: drinking out of the bard’s saucer when you believed I was looking at the fiddler. Breach six: addressing the former High Priestess of Septimont as an angry silver fish in the presence of forty witnesses.”

“A *pretty* silver fish, Iuiu!” Mei called upside-down into the drift. “Don’t leave out the pretty part, that’s falsifying the ledger!”

“If you do not keep your heels still against Lupa’s ribs,” Iuno informed her, “I shall falsify your breakfast.”

Six steps ahead of them, Buling was trudging up the middle of the stair with Ruoyun carried piggyback on her shoulders.

Ruoyun’s dark Mary Janes dangled on either side of Buling’s blue silk hips, her spectacles were hooked over one ear, and for the first twenty steps she had delivered a continuous, blistering critique of Buling’s footwork.

“You are toeing inward on the left foot like a duck with rickets,” Ruoyun slurred haughtily into the back of Buling’s winter hat. “And your spine is curved. When we reach the top I am putting you on two hours of horse-stance in the cistern with a bowl of water on your head…”

On the twenty-fourth step, Ruoyun’s sentence trailed off in the middle of a syllable. Her sharp chin dropped heavily onto the crown of Buling’s round nuanmao hat, her arms went limp around Buling’s collar, and a slow, warm trickle of wintersweet-scented drool soaked straight through the felt brim onto the back of Buling’s neck.

“Aiya, gross, the sergeant is leaking on my hat!” Buling hissed, hitching Ruoyun’s knees higher with both hands and trudging on.

Just ahead of Buling walked Longjue, barefoot in the four-inch snow as if it were June grass. She had Qingxiao slung horizontally over one shoulder like a rolled temple carpet—Qingxiao’s body still straight as a pine plank in her dark storehouse oilcloth, her eyes shut, her hands folded peacefully over her stomach, and not a single white stocking slipping as Longjue bounded up the risers two at a time.

Behind Chisa and Lupa came the uneven, giggling *thump-scrape-thump* of Cartethyia carrying Amy piggyback in her own small knight’s body because nobody trusted Fleurdelys near the stair-lamps after the Willow Jar’s ceiling. Both of them were still covered in white plaster dust and corn-silk, and Amy’s tin fox-whistle swung wildly on its red string, clinking against Cartethyia’s crooked crystal laurel at every step while Ciaccona walked one tread below them, strumming a three-fingered Ragunnese drinking catch on her lute with her coat unbuttoned and snow collecting in her hair.

At the very rear of the climb came the slow, unhurried *click, pause, click* of Suoming’s iron heel-plates on the stone lips of the steps.

Suoming held Nine Shadows open against the falling snow. Under its brown oil-paper rim walked Hsin with her nine gold tails gathered up in one arm and the Mengzhou captain’s hot ginger jar cradled in the other, and right beside Hsin walked Yangyang, her eyes half-shut and a wide, tranquil smile on her face, leaning her shoulder gently against Hsin’s sleeve whenever the steps curved while Hsin’s outer gold tail curled loosely behind Yangyang’s waist to keep her from drifting toward the balustrade.

At the third landing, where a stone drain-gargoyle jutted out over a deep bank of clean white snow, Ciaccona suddenly stopped singing.

She handed her lute to Cartethyia without a word, walked four steady, dignified paces to the stone coping behind the gargoyle, leaned over into the snowdrift out of sight of the steps, and was quietly, thoroughly sick.

Nobody shouted or pointed. When she straightened up a minute later, Suoming was standing beside the coping holding out a handful of clean, unpacked snow on a flat oak leaf and a small tin cup of plain water from her coat.

Ciaccona rinsed her mouth, spat into the drift, wiped her lips on her handkerchief, and looked down at the small Elder.

“Nobody puts that in the ballad,” Ciaccona said hoarsely.

“Put what,” Suoming said, and walked on up the steps.

Ahead of them all, Suisui had refused every offer of an arm from the bottom of the hill to the top. She climbed the entire forty-eight steps with her spine straight as a plumb-line, her fur cloak draped symmetrically over her shoulders, and Dumpling clicking sleepily in her loosened hair, stepping over the snow-drifts with the serene grace of a Mingting noblewoman ascending the Imperial Audience Hall.

“The mountain air,” Suisui remarked clearly as she reached the forty-seventh riser, “is remarkably bracing when one maintains proper breathing.”

On the forty-eighth and final step, her right cloth shoe caught half an inch of perfectly flat snow on the level courtyard flagstone.

Suisui went down face-first into the six-inch drift with a soft, muffled *whump*, her closed fan flying three yards across the snow and Dumpling launching off her head with an indignant clockwork squawk to land on the storehouse roof-beam.

For three seconds Suisui lay completely flat in the white powder. Then she rolled onto her back, snow plastered from her eyebrows to her chin, and folded her hands neatly over her stomach.

“I meant to do that,” Suisui informed the sky. “The snow is cooling on the temples.”

Chisa guided Lupa three paces to the left so Lupa’s boots wouldn’t tread on Suisui’s cloak hem, reached out with her free left hand to lift the wooden bar on the salt-house door, and pushed it open.

The deep, dry, baked-brick heat of the kang rolled out into the freezing dark to meet them.

Before Lupa had even lowered Mei onto the inner mattress or Longjue had unrolled the sleeping Paragon onto the end bricks, Cartethyia dumped Amy onto the quilts, walked two steps back toward the door to take her boots off, missed the stool entirely, and curled up in a ball on the warm brick kerb with her coat still buttoned, plaster dust in her eyelashes, and one hand tangled fast in Ciaccona’s wet hem, asleep before the latch clicked shut.

***

At the sixth bell the morning sun came out bright and merciless over the snow-covered roofs, glinting off every icicle along Wen’s kitchen eaves like a row of freshly sharpened chisels.

Mei sat at the long pine table with her back propped against the storehouse wall, a hot oven-brick wrapped in flannel wedged inside her coat against the pull of her scar, and both hands wrapped around a steaming earthenware bowl of Wen’s plain white rice congee. Her head felt as though someone had packed the inside of her skull with iron filings and then rung a gong against her ears, and her side ached from the bench-rail toast, and she had never been happier in either of her lives.

Above her, the kitchen shutter stood propped wide on its stick. Inside, Wen—wearing a freshly washed blue kerchief without a single grey hair out of place—was calmly scraping the bottom of the iron pot with her long wooden ladle, humming *The General’s Riding Whip* in perfect tune as if she had gone to bed at sunset on warm milk.

“Pickled mustard greens in the blue dish,” Wen called out cheerfully, setting a stoneware saucer on the sill with a loud *CLINK* that made four people down the bench flinch with their whole bodies. “Who wants fried crullers? The oil’s hot!”

At the middle of the plank, Ciaccona let out a low, pathetic groan from inside the salt-house quilt she had wrapped over her head and laid her cheek flat on the icy wood beside her mug of hot water. Right beside her ear, Cartethyia—who still had three flecks of white Willow Jar plaster in her eyebrows—was happily crunching her way through a bowl of sour pickled radish, every *snap* of the root making Ciaccona’s splinted finger twitch.

On Mei’s left, Iuno sat with her spine erect and her silver-blue hair pinned up with slightly less mathematical precision than usual. She was drinking plain hot water with a slice of salted lemon in it.

Every two minutes, Iuno’s gaze went across the table to Lupa, who had her wolf ears flattened sideways like a wet dog’s and was staring fixedly into her congee bowl without raising her eyes above the level of the soy-sauce cruet.

“Mei,” Iuno said in a hushed, tight voice, leaning an inch toward Mei’s shoulder. “Why will Lupa not look at me?”

Mei took a huge spoonful of congee, grinning around the porcelain. “Because at the Brazen Carp you grabbed my coat and told forty people I was your sweet illiterate barbarian and you were going to take me behind the grain-shed until my knees gave out.”

Iuno’s lemon-water cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

For four seconds she stared at Mei. The tips of her ears went red.

*I told you she liked it, darling,* the High Priestess murmured smugly in the back of her head.

Iuno set her cup down on the saucer without a sound, picked up her spoon, and looked straight ahead at the snowbank for the rest of the meal.

Across from them, Chisa—washed, combed, and sitting neatly in her black tunic—peeled a boiled duck egg, split it in half with her chopstick, and laid both halves on top of Lupa’s rice.

“Eat,” Chisa said calmly. “You shouted very loudly last night. Your throat will be dry.”

Lupa’s ears went crimson all over again; she shoved both halves of the egg into her mouth at once so she wouldn’t have to answer.

Near the end of the bench, Suisui sat with an ice-cold rag folded neatly across her forehead under her hair-line, fanning herself slowly with her closed fan in the freezing air while Yangyang—whose blue collar-feathers were sticking out in three different directions on the left side—quietly poured her sister a fresh cup of ginger water before burning her own tongue on the first spoonful of congee.

On the stone step of the storehouse doorway sat Ruoyun.

Her ruffled teal cuffs were pressed knife-sharp, her brass spectacles were polished clean, and she held a cup of plain hot water in both hands while glaring daggers at Dumpling every time the little clockwork bird clicked its beak on the porridge tureen.

“I walked up those stairs on my own two feet,” Ruoyun announced to the courtyard at large, looking at nobody. “And anyone who repeats tavern fabrications to the contrary will be assigned to the lower sluice-drains.”

“My winter hat still got a wet patch on the crown where you drooled lah,” Buling said in a hollow voice from the corner of the bench. She had her nuanmao pulled down so low the yellow talisman lay flat across her nose while she scrolled through her Terminal with one thumb. “And you tried to fight a painting of Minister Shen Qiu’s uncle.”

“The portrait was hung crooked,” Ruoyun said frigidly, and turned her head toward the sunny end of the bench, where Qingxiao sat cross-legged in her storehouse oilcloth, peacefully finishing her third bowl of congee without a single sign of a headache, a toothache, or a mortal liver. “Did you sleep on the bricks?”

“Yes,” Qingxiao said, reaching for the ladle to pour a fourth bowl. “The chestnuts were good.”

Under the warm overhang of Wen’s kitchen window, curled on a dry grain-sack out of the glare, lay a skinny nine-tailed fox with all four paws tucked under her chest and the dark-tipped ninth tail pulled tight over her nose and eyes. Two feet away, Suoming sat on her upturned wooden bucket with her boots clear of the snow, drinking plain white tea out of a chipped cup with not a hair on her red head out of place.

Buling suddenly gave a strangled whine from under her talisman and dropped her forehead onto the table beside her Terminal.

“Finished,” Buling groaned into the pine. “My whole career on the Huangboard is dead already. Apparently my thumb hit the live-broadcast button when the table broke at the Willow Jar.”

“What’s on it?” Amy asked eagerly, leaning over Chisa’s shoulder with a half-eaten scallion roll in her hand.

Buling held the screen out at arm’s length without lifting her head. “Got twelve hundred comments since dawn. Top comment from Jinzhou says: *Why is a nine-foot woman wearing the Willow Jar ceiling like a collar while the Xuan Paragon sleeps against the coat-rack, is this an avant-garde Mengzhou opera.*”

Beside Ciaccona, Cartethyia stopped chewing her radish and went bright pink to the tips of her crystal thorns.

“Second comment,” Buling croaked, scrolling down an inch, “is from the Ministry annex down on the second terrace. Says: *Can confirm, the Celestial with the spectacles threatened to drop Clerk Liu’s wife off the south barbican by her ears, ten out of ten, best night on the terrace since the siege.*”

Ruoyun’s spectacles flashed in the sun; she took a sip of her hot water and didn’t correct a word of it.

“Wait, there’s a clip of the audio,” Buling muttered, squinting with one red eye. “Somebody clipped the wolf shouting *YOU’RE THE BEST-LOOKING PERSON IN THIS WHOLE DAMN GORGE* and set it to a gong beat—”

Lupa lunged across the pine table with both claws out; Chisa calmly hooked two fingers into the back of Lupa’s belt and hauled her back onto the bench before she could eat the Terminal.

“And the bottom comment,” Buling finished, slapping the power button until the screen went black, “is forty people arguing over who won the biggest cake on Yangyang’s tray.”

Yangyang buried her face in her congee bowl until only the top of her blue hair and two bright pink ears showed above the porcelain rim.

Down the terrace steps from the Vale path came Longjue, strolling barefoot through the four-inch snow without a shiver on her skin, her dark hair gleaming, her red lacquer gourd swinging full at her hip, and half a cold steamed bun in her hand.

She stopped beside the grain-sack under Wen’s window and nudged the curled fox’s flank lightly with her bare big toe.

“Morning, Landlady!” Longjue bellowed, bright and cheerful enough to make Iuno wince and Ciaccona pull the quilt tighter over her skull. “I just checked the wall down by the cooper’s arch! Buling wrote *twenty-something* in charcoal, plus the five strings for missing the cooper’s icicle and putting soot in the bun-shop, plus the four taels and a half you wrote on the sluice-paper for the broken table! You’re at forty-one strings!”

The fox didn’t uncurl or move the dark-tipped tail off her nose. From underneath the fur came a single, muffled, aristocratic voice:

“Go drown in the horse-trough.”

“Forty-one!” Longjue laughed, tossing the crust of her bun to Dumpling and dropping onto the bench to steal a chopstick of mustard greens.

From the bottom of the second-terrace stair came the rapid clatter of boots climbing the snowy risers, followed by the gangly figure of Xiao Luo from the Red Ladle trotting into the courtyard with a folded slip of red paper in one hand and his breath steaming in the sun.

He gave Suoming’s bucket a wide, respectful berth of six paces, bowed twice to the grain-sack under the window, and laid the slip on the corner of the table by Suisui’s fan.

“From the keeper at the Willow Jar, my ladies,” Xiao Luo said cheerfully. “The carpenter looked at the ceiling joist this morning. She says she knocked two taels off the bill after all, because seventeen carters from the lower road already paid a copper each before breakfast just to stand on the bench and stick their heads through the hole.”

“Naturally,” Suisui said from beneath her cold rag, without opening her eyes.

“And what do the chalkboards say down the hill?” Amy asked, bouncing on her toes.

Xiao Luo grinned, showing his gap tooth. “Auntie Luo’s board says: *LADIES’ NIGHT CLOSED FOR REPAIRS — BUYING IRON MUGS FROM MENGZHOU*. And the Willow Jar’s says: *NO IMMORTALS OVER SIX FEET TALL WITHOUT A DEPOSIT — FREE PEANUTS FOR THE WOLF AND HER KEEPER*.”

Lupa dropped her forehead squarely into her arms on the tabletop while Chisa patted the back of her neck once, lightly, between her flattened ears.

Mei laughed out loud. Her side gave a familiar, sharp little tug against the warm brick tucked inside her coat, and her temples throbbed once in answer, and she didn’t care at all.

With the side of her left hand, she brushed a clump of fresh white snow off the edge of the pine plank onto the flagstones. Then she picked up her porcelain spoon, slipped her right hand under the table to lace her fingers loosely through Iuno’s warm ones on her knee, and blew across the top of her congee in the morning sun.

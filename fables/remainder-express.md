## i. Payroll

The Fractsidus ran on three things: grief, doctrine, and a payment system called OSSUARY that had been built two hundred years ago by an Overseer who subsequently walked into the sea. OSSUARY processed wages in eleven currencies, four of which had outlived their issuing nations, and it required a signature from the Grand Architect's office every quarter to release funds.

The Grand Architect's office had been empty for five months.

Gary knew this because he had a key. Quartermasters got keys the way soldiers got scars, by accretion, and Gary had been quartermaster of Depot Theta for eleven years, which meant he had keys to rooms whose purposes predated him and would, he'd assumed, outlast him. On the first of the month he let himself in, as he had let himself in on the first of the previous four months, and stood in the doorway of the most feared office on the continent.

Tea service for one, gone cold in May. A desk with a single open file on it, tab label reading *ANOMALY*. A window showing whatever the window felt like showing; today, a mown field under an early star. And the quarterly release form, exactly where he'd left it, with his sticky note still attached: *Ma'am — payroll. When convenient. — G., Theta.*

He'd added a second sticky note in September. *Ma'am — payroll, again. People are asking. — G.*

He looked at the two notes for a while. Then he took out his pen, and did the thing that would later be described in a business periodical as *the founding act of the fastest-growing logistics firm in the hemisphere*, and which Gary would always describe, accurately, as forgery.

He signed her name. He'd seen it four hundred times on requisitions. The Architect's signature changed with her faces, so any signature was her signature, which meant — he reasoned, sweating — that his was too.

OSSUARY chimed. Eleven currencies moved. Somewhere below him, in the residential tunnels, three hundred cultists got paid.

Gary put the pen down and noticed his hands were steady. That was the part that stayed with him. He'd expected lightning, or at least guilt, and instead there was the specific quiet of a task completed, the same quiet as a stocked shelf.

On the way out he watered the plant. Someone had to.

## ii. Livestock

The Tacet Discords in Sub-Vault Nine had a feeding schedule, and the feeding schedule had a budget line, and the budget line had been approved by Overseer Phrolova, and Overseer Phrolova had — the official memo used the phrase *transitioned to an external opportunity* — died, resurrected as a gardener in a pocket afterlife, and stopped answering mail.

"So who owns the Discords now?" asked Marguerite, who ran Sub-Vault Nine. She was sixty, she had raised four children and buried one, and she had joined the Fractsidus in her forties because her village had drowned and the Fractsidus were the only organization that had said *we know* instead of *we're sorry*. She kept the Discords the way other women kept bees. "Because the howler in pen six needs its resonance dampeners recalibrated, and the requisition needs an Overseer's chop, and I have been sending it up the chain for twenty weeks, Gary. Twenty."

"The chain," Gary said, "is more of a suggestion right now."

"The howler doesn't know that. The howler knows its teeth hurt."

They stood at the observation glass. Pen six held something the size of a fishing boat, all violin-string sinew and wrong geometry, currently curled around a dampener array like a cat around a radiator. It had been grown, or summoned, or filed into existence — the paperwork was ambiguous — for a Dark Tide that had been scheduled, descheduled, rescheduled, and finally rendered moot when a gold-eyed woman in Lahai-Roi jumped off something and got caught. Head office had gone quiet after that. The apocalypse had been postponed and nobody had told the apocalypse's suppliers.

"Recalibrate it," Gary said.

"On whose authority?"

Gary thought about the pen in his pocket.

"Mine," he said, and it came out level, and Marguerite looked at him for three full seconds, and then she said, "Well, thank Christ," and picked up her tools, "*somebody's*," and that was the second signature, the one that didn't even need ink.

## iii. The Meeting

The meeting that unionized a splinter cell of the world's most feared conspiracy took place in the Depot Theta break room, under a mural of the Nihility devouring a star, next to a corkboard that said 41 DAYS SINCE LAST SUMMONING INCIDENT.

Gary had called it to discuss overtime. Forty people came. He'd put out chairs for twelve.

"The situation," he said, from the front, holding a clipboard like a shield, "is that head office is on hiatus. The Architect is — traveling. Overseer Phrolova has left the organization. Overseer Cristoforo is at an artists' retreat and his out-of-office message is a sonnet. In the meantime, we still have four hundred staff, nine facilities, a vault full of apex-grade Discords, six thousand tons of running equipment, and a portal network with a maintenance cycle that does not care about our feelings. I've been signing what needs signing. If anyone objects, the complaints box is on the wall."

Someone had turned the complaints box into a planter in August. Everyone looked at the fern.

"What I need from this room," Gary continued, "is a decision about overtime, because the portal techs are pulling double shifts to keep the lanes stable, and I can pay them, but somebody besides me should agree to it, because I'm a quartermaster, and quartermasters don't set labor policy."

Silence. The fern was thriving.

Then Ostap, the senior portal tech, a man with static-white hair from a lifetime of standing too close to teleportation, raised his hand.

"Who does set labor policy?"

"The Overseers."

"And they're gone."

"They're gone."

"So policy is whatever we vote on," Ostap said, slowly, working it out loud, "and you sign it."

"That's — " Gary looked at the sentence coming toward him. He had time to get out of the way. He stood in it instead. "Yes. Functionally, yes."

"Then I move," said Ostap, "time and a half after forty hours, portal hazard pay, and Marguerite gets an assistant because she is one woman with forty demons."

"Seconded," said Marguerite, "and dental stays."

"Dental stays," the room said, in unison, with the fervor the same room had once used for *the pen belongs to humanity*, and Gary — who had joined the Fractsidus in the first place because the Spacetrek Collective's HR department had rejected his application on a formatting technicality and the Fractsidus recruiter, that same rainy afternoon, had bought him a coffee and said *we have dental* — felt something in his chest resolve, like a chord.

All in favor. Forty hands. Motion carried.

Nobody in the room used the word union. The word arrived later, from outside, from a Ragunna trade columnist, and by then it was simply true, the way the tunnels were true, the way the rent was true.

## iv. First Invoice

The pivot happened because of eels.

A Montelli sub-contractor in Ragunna had eighty crates of live eels stranded at a flooded quayside, a wedding banquet in Septimont in nineteen hours, and a courier who had looked at the mountain passes in autumn and quit on the spot. The sub-contractor, a woman named Petronilla with a voice like a docking crane, had been sold Gary's contact by a smuggler who owed the Fractsidus money, under the description *weird people, tunnels everywhere, never late*.

"You move things," she said, when Gary answered the depot's outside line, which had rung for the first time in his tenure.

"We — " Gary looked at the receiver. Historically the answer was: we move vessels, doctrine, and the instruments of a forced apocalypse. "Yes."

"Eels. Eighty crates. Septimont by noon tomorrow. What's your rate?"

Gary had never had a rate. The Fractsidus did not invoice; the Fractsidus had a war chest, and the war chest had a burn rate, and the burn rate had — he'd done the math in the Architect's office, on her stationery — about fourteen months left in it before the lights went out on four hundred people and forty demons.

He looked at the portal duty roster. Lane seven ran under the mountains. It had been built to move a siege engine.

"Eight hundred shells," he said, inventing the number, "half up front."

Petronilla haggled him to six-fifty. The eels went through lane seven in four minutes, riding pallets that had been stenciled, decades ago, with the sigil of the Nihility. They arrived in Septimont cold, alive, and eleven hours early. The banquet was a triumph. Petronilla called back the next week with a standing contract and a question: "What are you people called?"

Gary put his hand over the receiver. The break room was doing inventory. He looked at Marguerite, at Ostap, at the mural of the devouring dark, at the fern.

"What are we called?"

"The broken-off," said Marguerite, dry, quoting scripture. "The fraction that remains when the whole is subtracted."

"The Remainder," Ostap said. "Remainder Express."

Gary uncovered the receiver. "Remainder Express," he said. "Same-day across Rinascita. We have tunnels."

## v. Branding

"The problem," said the consultant, "is the aesthetic."

They'd hired her out of Ragunna with real revenue, which was itself a sentence Gary kept turning over like a strange coin. She had spread their assets across the break room table: the obsidian shipping crates, the black-iron portal frames wrought with screaming faces, the courier uniforms with the high collars and the bone clasps, the wax seals, the candles. So many candles.

"It's all very," she gestured, "doom."

"It's what we own," Gary said. "Rebranding four thousand crates costs more than the crates."

"Then we lean in." She held up a courier's collar. "Gothic is trustworthy. Think about it. Banks use marble. Marble is tombstone material. Gravity sells. Nobody wants their heirloom pocket watch moved by a company in cheerful yellow. They want *this*." She thumped the crate. It boomed like a cathedral door. "They want to feel the package is being carried by something that has seen the end of the world and is therefore unlikely to be flustered by a Tuesday."

"That's the staff, more or less," said Marguerite.

"The screaming faces on the portal frames?"

"Load-bearing," said Ostap. "The screams are the resonance-lock. You take the faces off, the lane collapses."

The consultant wrote *ARTISANAL* on her pad. "And the giant creature in the sub-vault."

"Creatures," said Marguerite.

"One of them purrs," said Gary.

The consultant looked at the three of them for a long moment, and something in her face went from managing to interested, and she said, "Have you considered a mascot," and Marguerite said, "Absolutely not," and the howler is on the letterhead to this day, teeth and all, above the slogan the consultant charged them nine hundred shells for and which Gary privately considered the only piece of Fractsidus scripture he had ever fully believed:

**REMAINDER EXPRESS. WE'RE STILL HERE.**

## vi. The Washout

Junia had been three days from her Overseer name when everything stopped.

She was a standard case, if the standard cases could be said to be standard: a fishing town, a wave, one survivor, the way a story ends and leaves a girl standing in the ruins of it holding a kettle she'd gone inland to buy. The Architect had come for her personally, in a face with good sightlines, and said the sentence — *your story is over; you are still here; someone should do something with that* — and Junia had signed, and trained, and grieved on schedule, and had been three days from her naming ceremony when the Architect went traveling and the ceremonies were suspended and the doctrine was left running like the equipment, humming, unattended.

She came to Gary's office — the Architect's office; he'd stopped pretending; the plant needed the light — with her transfer papers and the particular blank face of the recently unfinished.

"They tell me you're what's left of management."

"I'm the quartermaster."

"I was going to be an Overseer." She said it the way you'd report a canceled train. "I was going to help write the next Lament. Put the pen in humanity's hands. Now I'm told the department is," she checked the memo, "*pivoting*."

"We deliver things."

"I trained for six years to end the tyranny of fate."

"Route eleven needs a runner," Gary said. "Ragunna to the Frostlands, overnight, three portal changes. Mostly medical shipments and mail. The pay's posted in the break room."

Junia looked at him with the full contempt of the converted. He let it land. He'd learned that from Marguerite: you let people's weather happen, and you keep the shelves stocked, and mostly the weather passes and the shelves are still there.

"Mail," she said.

"There's a woman in the Frostlands," Gary said, "whose son works the Helios maintenance crews in Lahai-Roi. He writes her every week. Before us, the letters took a month over the passes, when they arrived at all. Now it's overnight. She reads them at breakfast." He slid the route folder across the desk. "The doctrine said someone should do something with you still being here. It never specified the scale."

The folder sat between them for a while.

"The doctrine," Junia said, quieter, "also said every story ends the same way."

"Ours hasn't yet," Gary said. "We're open Tuesdays."

She took route eleven. Six months later she held the overnight record, and she'd started — this arrived to Gary secondhand, from Ostap, who heard everything — writing letters of her own, to nobody, kept in her locker, her sentence sitting open on the table, being finished at her own pace, in her own hand.

## vii. Return to Sender

The crate had been in Deep Storage since before Gary's tenure, and Deep Storage was being converted into cold-chain warehousing, because fish paid.

The crate's manifest was sealed with an Overseer's chop — Cristoforo's, the theatrical flourish unmistakable — and its label read *PRODUCTION MATERIALS: SAINT, ACT III.* Inside, packed in velvet like instruments: components. Vessel components. The kind of parts from which the old departments had grown their staged girls, their controlled protagonists, their experiments in fate. Gary knew what he was looking at because quartermasters know what everything is; that's the job; you can't stock a shelf without reading the box.

He convened nobody. Some decisions are quorum decisions and some are inventory.

Marguerite found him at the loading dock at midnight, stenciling an address onto the resealed crate.

"You could sell it," she said. It was a test, and both of them knew it. "The New Federation would pay. The Black Shores would pay more, just to take it off the board."

"It's not ours to sell."

"Whose is it?"

Gary finished the stencil and stepped back. The address, in white paint, in a quartermaster's block capitals: *GRAND ARCHITECT'S OFFICE. PERSONAL. AWAITING YOUR RETURN.*

"Hers," he said. "She started it. It sits on her desk until she comes back and looks at it. That's what the desk is for."

"And if she never comes back?"

"Then it sits there forever, with the tea, and the anomaly file, and my two sticky notes." He wiped his hands. "We're a delivery company, Marguerite. We know one thing the doctrine never learned. Some packages you don't get to hand off. You carry them until the recipient signs."

They freighted it up in the morning through lane one, the old lane, the ceremonial one, and Junia carried it the last hundred meters herself and set it on the desk of the most feared office on the continent, next to the cold tea, under the window that today showed snow falling on a field, and it is there now, waiting, which is the most any of them could do, and also exactly enough.

## viii. The Customer

The order came through the Ragunna storefront on a Thursday: one parcel, insured, contents declared as *sheet music (sentimental)*, sender a student at Startorch Academy in Lahai-Roi, recipient a music shop in the Ragunna canal district. Overnight lanes. Signature required at pickup.

Gary was covering the Lahai-Roi counter himself that week — quarterly audits; he liked to walk the routes — and so it was Gary who took the parcel from the student, a girl with a friendly, forgettable face and the campus's blue scarf, flanked by two classmates arguing about lunch.

"Sigi, they close the noodle place at two — "

"They do *not* close at two, Denia, you got the *board* times — "

"Both of you," the customer said pleasantly, without turning, and the arguing continued at a lower volume, the way weather continues. She slid the parcel across the counter. Her hands, Gary noticed, held it with both hands, the way you hold a cup.

He weighed it. Ran the label. The manifest terminal — Fractsidus hardware, older than sin, repurposed with a sticker of the howler over the Nihility sigil — chimed its acceptance.

"Overnight to Ragunna," Gary said. "Guaranteed by ten. Nothing survives our lanes except the cargo."

"So I've heard." She was reading the counter placard, the framed copy of Invoice 0001, the letterhead, the slogan. Something in her attention had weight to it, the way a librarian's attention has weight. "*We're still here.* That's the whole pitch?"

"That's the whole pitch."

"No destiny. No design. You just — deliver things, and pay people, and keep the lights on."

"Tuesdays too," Gary said.

The student looked at him then, actually looked, and Gary had the brief, vertiginous sensation of being read at speed, cover to cover, filed. Her eyes were an ordinary brown. He would spend a long time afterward failing to describe her face, and eventually stop trying, and never once connect the Thursday to anything, because Gary was a perfectly ordinary man, and this was, he'd insist forever, a perfectly ordinary transaction.

"What happens," she asked, "when a package can't be delivered? Recipient gone. Address dissolved. What then?"

"We hold it," Gary said. "There's a shelf."

"Forever?"

"Until someone comes for it." He printed her receipt. "People come back for things more often than you'd think. We had a candle order sit four years. The man walked in last spring like he'd stepped out for air."

The student paid in exact change, and then set three extra shells on the counter, deliberately, one two three.

"Keep the change," she said.

"It's exact."

"Keep it anyway." She tucked the receipt away with the care of an archivist, and gathered her arguing friends, and at the door she paused — customers did this sometimes; the mural behind the counter was a lot — and looked back at the howler on the letterhead, and at Gary, and at the shelf of the undeliverable, patient in its corner.

"Good system," she said. "Show me what it does next quarter."

The bell above the door — Marguerite's addition; she'd insisted a shop needed one — rang her out. Gary filed the three shells under miscellaneous income, and went back to the audit, and the parcel went overnight to Ragunna and arrived at 9:41, signed for, sentimental, safe.

Upstairs, in a dormitory in Lahai-Roi, a receipt for one insured parcel was pressed flat and placed in a room, in order, within reach, in a collection whose other contents were faces — the first item in four hundred years that was neither a name surrendered nor a skin acquired, and had simply been purchased, at posted rates, like anything, by anyone, still here.

## ix. Quarterly Review

The rumor reached Depot Theta in spring, riding the routes the way all rumors rode them now, tucked between the invoices: head office was stirring. Cristoforo had emerged from his retreat with a script and no theater; three Overseers had surfaced in the east asking pointed questions about *asset consolidation*; somebody, somewhere up the old chain, had finally noticed that a limb of the great design had grown a payroll, a pension, a mascot, and a customer base, and was profitable.

The word from the east was *restructuring*.

Gary read the letter aloud in the break room, at the quarterly all-hands, under the mural, beside the fern, to two hundred people in high collars and bone clasps who had, between them, survived nine drowned villages, four burned ones, two apocalypses (one canceled, one postponed), and eleven consecutive profitable quarters.

"'The organization,'" he read, "'will be realigning peripheral operations with core doctrinal objectives.'"

"Which means?" said Ostap.

"They want the lanes back."

The room took this in. Marguerite, from the second row, knitting: "And the Discords?"

"'Strategic assets to be reintegrated.'"

"The howler has a *vet schedule*."

"I'm aware."

"It has a *name*, Gary."

"I named it, Marguerite."

Junia stood up, at the back, route-eleven lean, holding her mug from the cabinet of mugs that had accumulated the way keys accumulate. "So what do we tell them?"

Gary looked at the letter. It was good stationery. Head office had always had excellent stationery; the end of the world came with a letterhead. He thought about doctrine, which said fate was a landlord and the rent was collected in fire, and he thought about the actual rent, which was due on the actual first, in shells, and would be paid, because the ledger balanced, because two hundred people had stocked the shelves and run the routes and shown up Tuesdays, which was — he had come to believe this the way other men in this organization believed in the abolition of fate — the entire secret. Every grand design he had ever inventoried came down, at the loading dock, to whether somebody showed up. The dark had quotas too. The dark had missed them. Remainder Express had an on-time rate of 99.2.

"Tell them," Gary said, "we reviewed their proposal, and we're declining, and if they want the lanes, our rates are posted." He folded the letter. "And tell them shipping's extra."

The room laughed, the way a room laughs when it has already decided, and the meeting moved to the real agenda — cold-chain expansion, the Septimont depot, whether the mascot should get a plush (motion carried, forty to nothing, dental stays) — and outside, up and down the continent, through tunnels built for a siege that never came, under sigils of a nothing that never won, the packages moved. Medicine to the Frostlands. Eels to weddings. Letters to mothers who left out bread. A crate on a desk, waiting for a signature. Sheet music, sentimental, insured.

The moon set. The Pleiades went down with it. Midnight; the hour passed; and in nine depots the night shift clocked in, checked the manifests, and kept the theater — lights on, doors open, still here, still here, still here.

*guaranteed by ten.*

# For Personal Use

On Mei's fourth visit, the shopkeeper wound the duck before she reached the counter.

It stood on a green wooden box with three frogs arranged before it. The duck wore a blue waistcoat. Each frog had a little brass bell, and when the key turned, the duck raised its baton and the frogs began to play.

The smallest frog waited until the other two had finished. Then it opened its mouth and produced a long, offended croak.

The duck took a bow.

Mei laughed again.

Beside her, Iuno put down the parcel she had collected from the tea merchant.

“He has maintained an excellent standard throughout the afternoon.”

“Watch his feet when he bows. He goes up on his toes.”

The shopkeeper turned the key another half-circle. The duck rose onto his toes. His painted expression remained gravely concerned with the music.

Mei leaned on the counter, her chin almost level with the frogs.

Forty-eight shell credits. The price stood on a card beside the box. She had first read it shortly after lunch, while Iuno was choosing paper at the shop across the lane. Since then they had visited the chemist, bought tea, taken the longer route around a delivery wagon, and sat beneath an awning while Iuno drank a cup that Mei had insisted she try.

The toy shop had been on the way each time.

Mei picked up the box. A little brass hatch on the underside gave access to the mechanism. The shopkeeper had already shown her the gears, the spring, and the small leather tongue responsible for the last frog's opinion.

“It would travel,” she said.

“Wrapped in a shirt,” the shopkeeper agreed. “Keep the baton folded down.”

Mei folded it down, then raised it again.

“We've got quite a lot in the bags already.”

Iuno looked at the box, which would have fitted inside her tea parcel.

“I have room in mine.”

“I could make room too. It would fit beside the spare socks.”

She turned the box in her hands. The green paint had tiny brush marks in it. Somebody had given the smallest frog a pale yellow throat and eyes directed toward the duck with permanent professional dissatisfaction.

“Forty-eight is quite a lot for something like this.”

The shopkeeper rested his hands on his side of the counter.

“My daughter paints them. I make the workings. She chooses the faces.”

“She's very good. I keep looking at this one.”

Mei touched a finger beside the smallest frog, careful of its bell.

She could picture the box on the windowsill at Mrs Ren's. She could wind it while Iuno brushed her hair. The duck would conduct, the frogs would ring their bells, and the little one would make that sound.

She smiled into the open lid.

Then she looked at the price again.

At the chemist, forty-eight could have bought several things. She had seen the marked jars. There were bandages to replace, and the journey to Mengzhou, and Chisa's coat, though Chisa had said she had put money aside for that. Somebody might need something tomorrow. Mei could buy the box tomorrow too. By tomorrow, she might have stopped wanting it so much, which would settle the matter very conveniently.

She had tried that thought beneath the awning.

It had brought her back here.

“I'll think about it a little longer,” she said, placing the box carefully on its felt square. “You've been very patient.”

Iuno turned toward the shopkeeper.

“We'll take this one. Please show me how far to wind it.”

Mei straightened.

The shopkeeper demonstrated the key. Iuno watched, asked about the brass hatch, and paid. By the time Mei found a place for her hands, he was fitting folded paper around the frogs.

“I was still deciding.”

“You've decided that you like it. I've decided to give it to you.”

Iuno took the receipt and laid it on top of the tea parcel.

“Keep the little instruction sheet, please. We have several people at home who will want to investigate the frog.”

Outside, Mei held the wrapped box against her stomach.

They had reached the corner before she spoke.

“You spent forty-eight on it.”

Iuno shifted the tea into the crook of her living arm.

“I was present for that part.”

Mei gave her a look, then glanced down at the parcel. A small raised place in the paper marked the duck's folded baton.

“I know. I just keep thinking about all the other things.”

They moved beneath a shop awning to let a cart pass. Iuno leaned against the wall, easing the weight away from her dressed shoulder. Mei's free hand rose toward the tea; Iuno gave it to her.

“You bought me this an hour ago,” Iuno said.

“You liked it.”

“The merchant put three tins on the counter. You chose the largest.”

“It keeps well. And you said you could taste the orange properly in that one.”

“Seventy-two credits, sweetheart. You were opening your purse while he was still describing the harvest.”

Mei looked at the parcel tucked beside her duck.

The cart rattled past them. A loose length of rope trailed from its tailboard, drawing a crooked line through the dust.

“If I'd spent the afternoon looking at the frogs,” Iuno said, “what would you have done?”

“I'd have got them for you.”

“How long would you have needed?”

Mei's mouth moved. She looked toward the tea shop.

“I might have asked him to wrap it while we were having the tea.”

Iuno's expression softened. She touched the back of Mei's wrist.

“Then what happens when the present is for you? Why does giving you pleasure take so much explaining, darling?”

Mei rubbed her thumb along the folded paper.

“I can see you drinking the tea. I know you'll enjoy it.”

“I've watched you laugh at that frog four times.”

“I keep arriving at the price afterward.”

Iuno waited while Mei adjusted the two parcels in her arms. The corner beneath the duck had begun pressing into her stomach.

“And then I start adding things to it,” Mei said. “Something for Amy. Something for everybody else. By the time I've finished, I'm deciding about a much bigger purchase.”

She shifted the box higher.

“We have enough put aside for ourselves too?”

Iuno looked at her for a moment.

“Come home with me. I'll show you the accounts.”

*At last,* the High Priestess observed. *An afternoon in which the figures might improve somebody's disposition.*

***

The duck occupied the centre of the table while Iuno fetched her folder.

Mei unwrapped it slowly. She flattened the paper beneath the box and put the little instruction sheet beside it. Through the kitchen doorway came the smell of onions frying. Mrs Ren had asked them to keep the far end of the table clear for the supper bowls.

Amy arrived as Mei raised the baton.

She watched the performance with her hands braced on the table. At the final croak, her shoulders rose toward her ears.

“Oh, he's furious. Ma, wind him again. I want to watch the duck through that part.”

“His feet are very good too.”

They leaned over the box together.

Iuno returned with the folder, stopped in the doorway, and watched Mei point out the tiny shoes.

Then she took the chair beside them and opened the accounts.

“This is the money we keep available for the household.”

She laid a sheet on the table. At its foot, beneath a short list of payments, stood eighteen thousand, six hundred and forty shell credits.

Mei read the figure, looked at the heading, and went back to the entries above it.

“Does that include what you've set aside for the journey?”

“The eight thousand for travel and repairs is in the reserve account. This is the balance we draw from for food, rooms, clothes, and ordinary purchases.”

Mei moved her finger down the page.

“The chemist's bill has gone through.”

“Yesterday. So have the glass, the strings, and the extra bedding.”

Amy leaned across her mother's shoulder. Iuno shifted the paper so both of them could read it.

“At roughly four hundred a week,” Mei said, “this would last us through the winter.”

“At our recent rate, about forty-six weeks.”

Mei sat back.

The duck bowed between them.

Lupa came through from the yard carrying the measuring tape Chisa had borrowed. She paused beside the table, followed Mei's gaze to the figures, and pulled out a chair.

“Are we doing the household money? Wait until Chisa comes down. She asked me which purse the tailor should be paid from.”

“She can use the household purse,” Iuno said.

“Then tell her while she's here. I told her to ask you, and she started counting what she had.”

Lupa leaned toward the green box. The smallest frog opened its mouth.

She gave a startled laugh.

“Where did you find this little bastard?”

“The shop beside the paper seller,” Mei said. “Iuno bought him for me.”

Lupa turned the box so the frog faced the doorway.

“Chisa! Come and hear what Mei brought home.”

Chisa arrived with her book under one arm. Cartethyia followed her, carrying a cup, and Ciaccona came last with a pencil caught in her hair. She had been working upstairs; one finger bore a narrow line of ink.

They found places around the table. Mei wound the mechanism again while Iuno moved the tea clear of the papers.

The frogs played. The duck conducted. Ciaccona closed her eyes just before the final note.

“Someone has a very sound understanding of audiences.”

Cartethyia reached for the instruction sheet.

“Can we see inside after supper?”

“There's a hatch underneath,” Mei said. “He showed me how it opens.”

“Keep the screws in a saucer,” Lupa told her. “The floor here eats them.”

Chisa sat beside Lupa and set her book on her lap. Her sleeve brushed the household balance.

“I wanted to ask about the coat. The tailor gave me the full price this morning.”

“Put it here,” Iuno said. “We have room for it.”

Chisa took the slip from between her pages. She placed it beside the account, smoothing the fold with her thumb.

Mei looked from the tailor's figure to the balance again.

“How much of this came from Lupa?”

Lupa reached for Iuno's pencil.

“I put the latest transfer on the back. Turn it over.”

***

The paper went around the table.

Lupa had written the transfer amount in large, sloping figures. Beneath it she had put *for eating*, then added *and the other things* in the space left beside her signature.

“That was the new campaign,” she said. “The poster with the red background.”

Amy sat forward.

“The one where you're holding the bottle against your thigh?”

“They tried it at my shoulder first. Covered the label with my hand. I told them people would have to buy it by remembering my elbow.”

“I remember your elbow very clearly,” Ciaccona said.

Lupa grinned at her and tapped the figure.

“They pay in three instalments. This was the second. The last comes when they renew the displays.”

Mei rested her forearms on the table.

“You keep putting money in.”

“We keep eating. I like the arrangement.”

Lupa took her terminal from her pocket. She brought up a statement, checked the heading, and placed it beside the paper.

The sum at the bottom occupied a considerable width of screen.

Mei leaned closer. Then she picked the terminal up.

“Two hundred and forty thousand?”

“Rounded down. I left the little figures where the bank put them.”

Cartethyia looked over Mei's shoulder.

“How long did that take?”

“Years. The Agon put a good lump in. The contracts I've signed since then pay me for the posters, the cups, the appearances. There's a man who sends six pages every time somebody wants to print my tail on something.”

“What do they usually want it on?” Amy asked.

“Clothes. Drink cans. A line of pillows I sent back.”

“I would have enjoyed seeing those.”

“You would have bought one and brought it to breakfast. I thought of that while I was reading the proposal.”

Amy smiled into her hand.

Lupa took the terminal back and scrolled to another page.

“These are the dates the next payments arrive. There's money set aside for the Guild dinners too. I like to know the kitchens can order ahead.”

Chisa followed the movement of her finger.

“You knew which box the tailor would put the lining under.”

“I used to read those bills. Somebody measured me, somebody sewed the cloth, and somebody paid for the room where I wore it. By the time the poster went up, twenty people had done something to my trousers.”

She put the terminal away.

“I had good people explain the figures once the contracts started coming to me. You should hear Fulvia discussing a percentage. She can make a man wish he'd stayed in bed.”

Mei looked at the transfer on the paper.

She remembered Lupa arranging passage, buying the next round, waving someone toward a seat while a server carried dishes away. At the time, Mei had usually been halfway through a different conversation.

“I knew you paid for things,” she said. “I kept seeing the one bill in front of us.”

“There was usually another one coming.” Lupa reached across the table and nudged the duck's box away from a damp ring. “I liked having seven names on it.”

Iuno laid another statement beside Mei.

“And you have your own money.”

Across the top stood the name of a Montelli banking office. Beneath it were two deposits and several transfers. Mei recognised the seal before she recognised the account number.

“This is Carlotta's arrangement.”

“Your Laureate award. Twenty-four thousand, less the transfers you authorised. The second account holds your share of the Agon purse.”

“Lupa, we split that.”

“We split the prize money as well as the medal. Augusta had a man whose entire afternoon was devoted to explaining it.”

Mei read the second page.

Eighty thousand had arrived. Some had since moved into the household account. There were withdrawals she remembered making: a repair, a journey, equipment purchased while they were in Lahai-Roi. Beside each stood her own authorisation.

She ran a finger beneath the remaining balance.

“I remember signing for these. I thought the number at the bottom would be much smaller by now.”

“The college paid for your room,” Iuno said. “Your fees were covered. Several of the journeys were arranged by the people who asked us to make them. Your money has been moving alongside all of that.”

Cartethyia leaned toward the pages.

“Is there anything for being the Arbiter?”

Iuno turned over the last sheet.

“That title has chiefly produced correspondence.”

“She faced Leviathan. Someone could at least have put a purse near the letter.”

“Please keep the next monster out of the payment discussion,” Mei said. “I would like to understand this lot first.”

She pulled the two statements beside the household page and began checking the transfers between them. Iuno watched her find the matching dates.

The duck had fallen silent. Mei wound him by touch while she read.

***

“What about yours?” Amy asked Iuno. “You were High Priestess for thirty years. Where did all that go?”

Iuno took a thinner envelope from the folder.

“The office had large accounts. Grain contracts, wages, buildings, transport. On leaving, I signed those over to the people responsible for continuing the work.”

Amy accepted the envelope when she offered it.

“This is the personal settlement. The final portion reached me after we began travelling east. Thirty-seven thousand, five hundred, following the expenses already paid.”

Mei looked up from her statements.

“You told me the settlement had arrived. I thought you meant the papers were finished.”

“They arrived together. You were helping Mrs Ren persuade the cupboard to close.”

Iuno smoothed the envelope flat.

“For most of my adult life, I could ask for a carriage and find one at the door. Someone kept my rooms warm. Someone replaced the lamp oil. I knew the price of grain by the shipload. For a very long time, I could have given you a poor account of what it cost to make my breakfast.”

Lupa poured more tea.

“You learned quickly once you started buying it for us.”

“You eat at an educational rate.”

*And the wardrobe had its own staff,* the High Priestess added. *We have developed a much more intimate relationship with buttons in retirement.*

Amy had turned the envelope over.

“Did you keep any of the velvet?”

“A dressing gown. It occupies an unreasonable amount of a trunk in Septimont, and I look forward to wearing it again.”

“I'd like to see that,” Mei said.

Iuno's gaze settled on her. For a moment they both smiled at the thought of the same future room.

Then Cartethyia put a small leather purse beside the statements.

It closed with a silver clasp shaped like a flower.

“Cantarella gave me this.”

Ciaccona lowered her cup.

“That purse has an address book.”

“It has a letter. She said to show it at the places written on the back.”

Cartethyia opened the clasp. There were coins, a folded list, a spare terrarium fastening, and a small, hard sweet wrapped in faded paper. She put the sweet beside her cup and offered Iuno the letter.

Iuno read it. Her eyes moved down the page more slowly near the bottom.

“House Fisalia guarantees your reasonable expenses. Accommodation, passage, clothing, meals, and care of the person.”

“The woman in Porto-Veno said plants counted if I carried them myself.”

“There's an additional note here about requests accompanied by the word ‘lonely.’”

Ciaccona took a long drink of tea.

Cartethyia spread the list on the table.

“The swan cost forty. People keep bringing her up as if I bought the whole lake.”

“She required her own seat in the carriage,” Ciaccona said. “The driver spent the journey listening to sweets fall out of her.”

“Everyone had some. He asked me to wind her at the bridge.”

Amy put a hand over her mouth. Lupa's tail brushed Chisa's chair in a slow, pleased sweep.

Iuno studied the list of merchants and correspondents.

“There's a Jinzhou office. They can settle in local currency.”

Cartethyia looked at the coins in her purse.

“I've been keeping those for the journey. I thought I'd have to wait until we reached a port.”

“We can visit the office tomorrow,” Iuno said. “You should hear what the letter allows from the person who will honour it.”

“I'd like that. I have some questions about ‘reasonable.’”

“Take the swan's measurements,” Ciaccona said. “Save yourselves the second appointment.”

Cartethyia reached for the letter. Beneath the formal text, in a hand the whole table knew, Cantarella had added three lines.

*Buy proper stockings. Have the coat fitted. Write before the next catastrophe so I may enjoy receiving a letter.*

Cartethyia folded that part inward first.

“She puts instructions on everything.”

Ciaccona rested her cup beside the purse.

“She spent twenty minutes asking me whether your boots pinched. I gave a very detailed account of your feet.”

“The left one did pinch.”

“I know. New boots appeared the following morning.”

Mei held the letter for a moment when it came around to her. The Fisalia seal pressed into the paper beneath her thumb.

“We stayed at her house for weeks.”

“We ate there for weeks,” Lupa said. “That requires its own figure.”

“And the doctors came upstairs,” Mei said. “I remember somebody bringing clean clothes before I was ready to get out of bed.”

Cartethyia's hand rested on the purse.

“She kept putting things in the room. I used to come back and find another blanket.”

They passed the letter back to her. She put it away with the fastening and the coins.

The sweet stayed beside her cup.

***

Ciaccona had begun making small marks in the margin of Iuno's sheet.

“I have something to add,” she said. “Mine arrives under a heading I enjoy reading.”

She fetched a folded statement from her notebook. The paper had travelled between enough pages to acquire several different curves.

“Two thousand, eight hundred and sixty from the last distribution. The arrangements, mostly. Bands want parts they can hand to six people who each believe they're playing the important one.”

Cartethyia smiled toward her.

“That came from the anthem you wrote.”

“A publisher pays me to help brass players agree where to breathe. The song itself has been having a very successful life in other people's mouths.”

Lupa took the paper and read the figure.

“That's good money.”

“I've been looking at it quite often.”

Ciaccona accepted the statement back. She flattened a fold with the side of her hand, working around the fingers that still preferred to curl.

“I used to count the hat before choosing where to sleep. A room with a place to wash cost more. A room where I could leave the lute while I went out cost more again. If the weather turned during the afternoon, I found somewhere indoors to play and began the calculation over.”

The duck raised his baton. She watched it rise, then looked at the household balance.

“For quite a while now, I've been choosing places for the music. Or the window. Sometimes because Carte sees a tree she wants to look at in the morning.”

Cartethyia leaned against her shoulder.

“You liked the fig tree.”

“I spent two days under it. The woman at the inn brought me a cushion. At the end, Lupa had already paid for the room.”

“You were still eating the figs when we left,” Lupa said. “I thought you'd need both hands for the bag.”

Ciaccona smiled down at the statement.

“I would like part of this to go into the household account. Tell me which number to use.”

Iuno brought the account details closer. Ciaccona copied them into her notebook.

Chisa took her own purse from her bag and placed it beside the tailor's slip.

“I have six hundred and twelve left from the college payments. I was keeping enough for the coat, then a little for the journey.”

“Your journey is included in the reserve,” Iuno said.

Chisa felt along the purse's clasp.

“When Lupa said it was arranged, I thought I would find out my share when we reached the ship.”

Lupa's hand settled on the table beside hers.

“The booking is for all of us. I'll show you the receipt when it comes.”

“I'd like to see it. I keep putting things aside for bills somebody else has already paid.”

Iuno drew a clean sheet from the folder.

“Then this page can say what's covered, and what remains to be paid. We'll keep it where everybody can find it.”

Chisa moved the tailor's slip onto the fresh page.

“Put the coat here. I'll keep the six hundred for things I choose while we're out.”

She paused, then reached into her book for a second, smaller slip.

“And the fan. Mrs Wei wrote down the price.”

Lupa smiled at the little piece of paper. She put her tea farther away from it.

Amy had been following the figures with her chin in her hand.

“The college payments were my doing, originally.”

Mei turned toward her.

“I remember the admissions part.”

“The admissions needed rooms. The rooms needed meals. People keep attending college more successfully when both are available.”

Amy pulled the clean page toward herself and drew a little box beneath Iuno's list.

“The travelling-scholar provision had all of that attached. I routed the first payments through the memorial fund.”

Lupa slowly lowered her cup.

“Which memorial fund, Amy?”

“Mine. It had been sitting there for years, and I had a very strong interest in supporting those particular scholars.”

For a moment, the small click of the duck's mechanism carried around the table.

Then Ciaccona leaned back in her chair.

“I believe I've thanked the Foundation in writing.”

“Your letter was very nice,” Amy said. “I remember the bit about access to the practice rooms.”

Mei put a hand over her eyes.

Amy touched her elbow.

“The funding's been regularised. You heard that part at the hearing. I have an ordinary account now, with a card and a very dull notice about updating my address.”

“You read the thank-you letters,” Mei said.

“I was interested in how everyone was settling in.”

Iuno looked at the little box Amy had drawn. Beneath it, she wrote *fees, rooms, meals*, then added *paid at source*.

“That explains a substantial part of how seven people could spend months choosing what to study.”

“And complaining about the coffee,” Ciaccona said.

“I took those complaints very seriously,” Amy told her. “The coffee was one of the few things I could leave to Ma.”

Mei lowered her hand. Her eyes were wet at the corners, and she was laughing.

“You put me in a school with a coffee shop.”

“Several. I gave the matter proper attention.”

***

Mrs Ren came in carrying the supper bowls.

Everyone began moving paper. Lupa lifted the duck, Chisa gathered the slips, and Amy pulled the two account sheets clear of the damp cloth Mrs Ren laid on the table.

“We were checking what we've paid,” Iuno said. “Would you bring the latest room receipt when you have a moment?”

Mrs Ren fetched it from the cupboard.

There were two figures on the receipt. Iuno put it beside the clean household page and pointed to the second.

“The city's remission pays this part directly. Our payments cover the remainder and the meals we've added.”

Mei read the smaller sum in the column bearing their names.

“I've been thinking of that as the price of the rooms.”

Mrs Ren dried her hands on her apron.

“That's your part of the price. The rest comes through on the tenth.”

She tapped the larger figure with a blunt fingernail.

“Iuno made them put the date on it. The first paper said ‘in due course.’ I have a coal merchant with a much more particular view of time.”

Iuno inclined her head. Mrs Ren took the receipt back to her cupboard and went to the stove.

Mei watched her lift the lid from the pot.

On the road north there had been drivers, fuel, places cleared for them to sleep. In Lahai-Roi there had been rooms with their names already assigned. At Porto-Veno, somebody had carried a tray upstairs. Even now, a second payer occupied a column on their bill.

She looked down at the page Iuno was making.

“Add that beside the rooms. The full price, and who pays which part.”

Iuno wrote it in.

Lupa set the duck down on a dry part of the table.

“The ship matters too. When I ask for a berth, people know somebody will settle the bill. Sometimes they let us board while the money's still on its way.”

“The letter does that for me,” Cartethyia said, touching her purse.

“Your name does quite a lot before you show the letter,” Ciaccona said.

Cartethyia considered this. Her thumb travelled over the silver clasp.

“The man who fixed the terrarium wanted me to bless his tools.”

“You held each one for him,” Chisa said.

“He'd laid them out on a cloth. I asked him what he was making next.”

She looked at Mei.

“I liked talking to him.”

Mei nodded. She remembered the tool handles sticking out of their neat roll, the glazier bringing Cartethyia a stool before she asked.

Iuno set down the pencil.

“Money in our accounts. People extending credit. Institutions covering costs. Friends opening their houses. We've had several kinds of help at once.”

“We get to decide to go somewhere,” Chisa said, “and spend the evening talking about what we'll find there.”

Lupa reached for the bowl Mrs Ren put beside her.

“I usually ask about the fare afterward.”

Mei sat with that for a little while.

Then she turned the household sheet toward herself.

“I'd like us all to have access to this account. Proper access, wherever we're travelling. And I'd like to see the page when the next payments go through.”

Iuno took another form from the folder.

“This adds signatories. I brought it down because I expected we might reach that question.”

Amy inspected the seven spaces.

“You were prepared to spend the whole evening on this.”

“The duck had already occupied the afternoon. I allowed for a substantial household.”

Lupa signed first. Her pen pressed hard enough that Chisa shifted the felt underneath the page. Cartethyia read each heading aloud to herself before adding her name. Ciaccona asked where purchase records should go, and Iuno pointed to the little pocket inside the folder.

Chisa held the pen above her space.

“For clothes and books as well as meals?”

“And whatever you want to take home from a shop,” Lupa said. “If you're planning to buy the shop, bring it up over breakfast.”

Amy rested her chin on her hand.

“That sounds like a very promising breakfast.”

Chisa signed.

Mei took the pen next. She found her name among the household details, followed the line down to the place for her signature, and wrote it carefully.

Beside the paper, the duck stood with his baton raised.

***

After supper, they opened the hatch.

Cartethyia held the box while Amy turned the little screwdriver. Lupa provided the saucer. Chisa leaned close to hear the spring as the last of its tension eased, and Ciaccona studied the arrangement of the bells with considerable professional interest.

Mei showed them the leather tongue.

“There. It catches the air from this part when he opens his mouth.”

Amy touched the edge of the mechanism with the screwdriver, then withdrew it.

“Someone spent a long time making him sound that annoyed.”

“The man's daughter chose his face,” Mei said. “He told me while I was deciding.”

Iuno collected the last of the papers. She put the shared page at the front of the folder, where its heading showed through the cover, and left it on the cupboard beside the household keys.

When she returned, the hatch was secured and Mei was gathering the wrapping paper.

The receipt lay beneath it.

Mei picked it up. Forty-eight for the box. The little instruction sheet included. She looked at the figure, then toward the folder on the cupboard.

“You could put it with the household purchases,” Iuno said.

Mei smoothed the receipt.

“You gave it to me. I'd like to keep this one in the box.”

Iuno's hand settled on her shoulder.

“There's room beneath the felt. The shopkeeper showed me while you were holding the paper.”

Mei lifted the corner of the lining. She folded the receipt once and tucked it underneath.

Around them, the others began carrying cups to the kitchen. Amy asked for another performance before bed. Mei wound the key, and the duck conducted from the centre of the cleared table while Mrs Ren came to the doorway to listen.

At the final croak, Mei laughed with her whole face.

Later, upstairs, she tried the box on the windowsill. Then she tried it on the table, beside the lamp. From the bed she could see the smallest frog between the other two, watching the duck with his permanently aggrieved eyes.

Iuno sat on the edge of the mattress, working at a boot fastening. Mei came over and held the leather steady. When the clasp opened, she put the boot beside its partner and sat down close enough that their knees touched.

“I wanted to bring it home the first time I saw it.”

“I thought you did.”

Mei leaned against her living shoulder.

For a while they looked at the green box across the room.

“I enjoyed showing everyone,” Mei said. “I was already thinking about bringing it down tomorrow.”

Iuno brushed a strand of hair away from her cheek.

“You can bring it back upstairs afterward.”

Mei turned her head. Then she kissed the side of Iuno's face, lightly, near the corner of her mouth.

“Thank you for buying me the silly thing.”

Iuno's thumb rested against her cheek.

“I wanted you to have him.”

Mei smiled, stood, and went to the table.

She turned the key just far enough for one performance. Then she came back to bed and drew her feet beneath her.

The bells began. The duck lifted his baton. Iuno leaned her head against Mei's, and together they waited for the smallest frog.

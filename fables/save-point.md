# Save Point

*a local co-op for two*

---

## I. Continue?

At four in the morning, peace sounded like a badly tuned radio.

The dormitory had settled around its sleepers. Pipes clicked in the walls. The ventilation carried the low green hum of the Solistrees. Somewhere down the corridor, a woman who had spent two lifetimes waking at every change in the dark slept through the dawn cycle engaging in the walls, smiled into her pillow, and kept sleeping.

Amy stood outside the half-open door long enough to count ten breaths.

Mei had reached eight hours on Tuesday. Eight hours and fourteen minutes. Amy kept the record in a private table on her terminal under an innocent filename. The table included a column for dreams. Laughing counted as one.

Amy eased the door shut and went upstairs.

The old broadcast room occupied a maintenance turret beneath the false sky. Its first transmitter had been retired before Amy entered the Academy. Its second had been confiscated during her third year. The present arrangement consisted of legal equipment, borrowed equipment, and three components whose ownership had become a philosophical question. At midnight, Fleet Snowfluff spoke again.

Her audience had grown since her resurrection. Half the Academy listened for the novelty of hearing a dead legend complain about cafeteria jelly. The other half claimed sophisticated taste and listened in secret. Amy performed for both groups with equal generosity.

By four, the requests had ended. She signed off with an old line about keeping the light on, lowered the faders, and listened to the carrier settle into static.

Static had held her voice before anyone else did. She always gave it an extra minute.

Tonight, the static laughed.

It was a low, delighted sound, close enough to lift the hair at the back of Amy's neck. A woman leaned into the dead channel as though she had found an open window.

“Your gain still runs hot, Snowfluff. Twenty years should have taught you mercy toward cheap speakers.”

Amy's hand closed around the fader.

Every part of her knew that voice. Her mind arrived several heartbeats later, breathless from the distance.

“Big Sis Camellya?”

The title escaped cleanly. Amy heard seven years old in it, snow in her boots, both hands sticky from somebody else's jam.

Silence traveled down the carrier. For once, Camellya seemed to have misplaced the next line.

“There you are,” she said.

Amy sat very straight. “You remember me.”

“Some part of me kept your shape, including your habit of opening with an accusation.”

“That answer would lose points in any properly designed examination.”

“Then your exam has poor instincts. Bring a coat, your house key, and the blue controller with the repaired shoulder button.”

Amy looked at the dark window. “You came north for a game.”

“Your voice sounds exactly like your mother whenever you tell strangers you are having a wonderful week. The game gives us something civilized to do with our hands.”

The silence in the room changed its pressure.

Amy kept her voice easy. “Ma will want to see you.”

“She has impressive range. Tonight belongs to us.”

“The pack will notice I am gone.”

“Leave a note. Your family treats notes as binding instruments.”

Amy glanced toward the stairwell, where several people slept within sprinting distance of her name. “Where are you?”

Something tapped the outer glass.

A red camellia had grown through the transmitter grille. Its stem curled around the latch with leisurely precision. A folded square of paper hung from one thorn.

Amy opened the window. Cold air came in carrying the mineral scent of the upper tunnels, and the paper came loose under her fingers.

The handwriting slanted forward, impatient with the line.

> **PLAYER TWO REQUESTS REMATCH.**  
> **FREIGHT LIFT C. FIVE MINUTES.**  
> **COME ALONE, WEED.**

Amy read the last word twice.

“You remember me,” she said again.

The carrier breathed softly in her headphones.

“You have five minutes,” Camellya replied. “Bring the good scarf. The frostlands still bite.”

## II. Player Two

Camellya waited on the roof of the freight lift with one knee raised and the false stars behind her.

She wore wine-dark hair loose over her shoulders. White blossoms from the dormitory magnolia had lodged in it, though the magnolia stood two kilometers away and six security doors below. A flower opened at her sternum, deep red at the center and pale along its furled edges. Vines slept beneath the skin of her forearms in fine green shadows.

Amy stepped off the service stair and held up the blue controller.

“The shoulder button has required three repairs since you last touched it. You broke the first spring yourself.”

Camellya looked from the controller to Amy's face. Her gaze traveled slowly, taking inventory with a hunter's candor: the living color in her cheeks, the house key on a cord around her neck, the old scar at her temple, the hands that had once belonged to a body lying warm and empty behind glass.

“You grew into your eyes,” Camellya said.

Amy had prepared six openings on the way up. Each one vanished.

“You look exactly the same.”

“Perfection saves time.”

Then Camellya opened her arms.

Amy crossed the roof in three strides and hit her hard enough to drive one boot half a step across the metal. Camellya caught her around the ribs. Her body was very warm. It always had been. Even in the frostlands, even after hunts that left ice in Amy's eyelashes, Big Sis had carried summer under her skin.

The hug lasted longer than either of them had budgeted.

Camellya's hand spread between Amy's shoulder blades. Her fingers flexed once, as if confirming substance. Amy pressed her face into a familiar shoulder and smelled green sap, cold iron, and the faint sweetness of the flower at Camellya's chest.

“You took your time,” Amy said.

“I came when the story got quiet.”

“That sounds rehearsed.”

“I had two thousand years. Some preparation seemed reasonable.”

Amy laughed into her coat. The sound broke in the middle. Camellya held her through the break and released her once Amy's hands relaxed.

The freight lift shuddered under them and began its climb toward the Roya Frostlands. Lahai-Roi sank away in terraces of amber and blue. Its artificial sun lay banked beneath the horizon. Above, old stone held an older sea.

Camellya sat beside the roof hatch and took the controller from Amy. Her thumb found the repaired shoulder button at once.

“You used resin,” she said.

“The original part went out of production while I was dead.”

“That sounds inconvenient.”

“The manufacturer offered condolences and a voucher.”

“Did you threaten them?”

“I redeemed the voucher first.”

Camellya's grin arrived bright and feline. “Your mother taught you priorities.”

Amy watched the grin, and beneath her coat the house key became cold against her sternum.

“How much came back?” she asked.

Camellya turned the controller over. “That question contains several others. Choose the one with teeth.”

“Do you remember loving us?”

The lift entered the Riseway. Darkness gathered around the car, relieved at intervals by work lamps swinging from the tunnel vault. Camellya's flower moved with each passing light, red, shadow, red.

“My body remembers reaching for you,” she said. “My mouth remembers your private names. I remember your mother's hot chocolate as an offense against agriculture. I remember carrying you home with an Exoswarm leg tied to your sled because you insisted it was a trophy. I remember a blue door opening before I touched the latch.”

Her thumb pressed the repaired button. It clicked with a little resistance.

“Facts come in fragments. Affection arrives whole.”

Amy looked through the rail at the tunnel falling away below them.

“That is a very unfair answer.”

“The good ones usually are.”

“Ma remembers feelings that arrive ahead of facts too.”

“The broadcasts taught me.”

Amy turned sharply. Camellya kept her gaze on the controller.

“You have been watching her.”

“She is difficult to miss. Your mother has the subtlety of sunrise and roughly the same sense of personal responsibility.”

“She has a different name now.”

“It suits her.” Camellya held the controller out. “The woman I knew would have liked it.”

Amy accepted it with both hands.

The answer sat between them for the rest of the climb, alive and patient.

## III. Local Co-op

The cabin stood in its hollow under a sealed winter sky.

Snow reached the lower panes. Dormant wisteria braided the porch posts in pale woody ropes, each bud held tight around its own spring. The blue door faced the lake with the same brave color Mei had painted into it years ago. A porch lamp shone above the lintel.

Amy stared at the light.

“I turned that off when we left.”

“I lit it when I arrived.” Camellya stepped past her through the snow. “Houses deserve warning before company.”

“How did you get inside?”

“The window over the sink still sticks at the top.”

“Ma repaired that window twice.”

“Her work stopped at the lower sash.”

The familiarity struck with surgical neatness. Amy put the key into the lock and opened the door.

Warmth moved around her ankles.

Camellya had laid a fire, filled the kettle, and cleared thirteen years of patient dust from the old game console. Two floor cushions waited in front of the screen. On the table lay a square cartridge in faded violet plastic, its label covered by a child's handwriting.

**BLOOM & DOOM II — CAM + AMY — MA, USE YOUR OWN SAVE**

Amy took off her gloves very carefully.

“I looked for this after you disappeared.”

“You hid it.”

“I was seven.”

“You were thorough.” Camellya crouched by the stove and poured water into two mugs. “Behind the loose board under the window seat, wrapped in a sock, inside a biscuit tin. Your security model relied on adults respecting laundry.”

“It worked for twelve years.”

“Your mother found it three days after you hid it. She moved the tin whenever she swept and put it back facing the same way.”

Amy sat down on the floor.

Camellya handed her a mug. The smell of cocoa rose between them, dark and sweet.

Amy took one sip. Salt touched the back of her tongue.

“You made the recipe correctly.”

“I followed the criminal instructions taped inside the cupboard.”

“You used four marshmallows.”

“Your note specified structural quantities. Four supports a roof.”

They booted the console.

The screen filled with green. An orchestra of synthetic horns announced a world whose designers had once considered sixteen colors sufficient for wonder. Two armored girls stood outside the Frost Queen's citadel, frozen mid-run. The one in red carried a sword longer than her body. The one in blue wore a cape composed of three flickering pixels.

> **SAVE FILE FOUND**  
> **ELAPSED TIME: 31:07:42**  
> **LAST CHECKPOINT: THE GARDEN AT THE END**  
> **CONTINUE?**

Camellya settled cross-legged on the cushion. “You kept us at the final door.”

“Finishing the quest ends the game.”

“That rule came from your mother.”

“She used it whenever she had a mission and wanted to pretend we were choosing a break.”

“She was talented at turning escape routes into household customs.”

Amy's thumb hovered above the button.

“We can start a new file.”

“This one waited.” Camellya leaned back on her hands. “Let it speak.”

Amy selected **CONTINUE**.

The old game loaded with a shiver of static. Twenty years folded. The citadel opened its pixel gate, and two players ran into the garden.

For the first half hour, they argued about controls.

For the second, Amy accused Camellya of reading animation frames before they appeared. Camellya explained that old games telegraphed everything through rhythm and called memory a beautiful source of lag. Amy changed tactics, began feeding her false patterns, and took the first round from her with a trap involving three ice moths and a falling chandelier.

Camellya stared at the defeat screen. “You learned deceit.”

“I grew up in an Academy.”

“I am proud of you.”

The words came lightly. They landed heavily.

Amy's hands forgot the controller for one beat. Her character walked into a wall.

Camellya saw. She gave Amy the dignity of killing her twice before mentioning it.

“Your left hand tightens whenever someone says that.”

“My left hand has artistic temperament.”

“It also checks the hallway every six minutes.”

Amy glanced toward the closed door.

“We are two hundred kilometers from the dormitory.”

“Your attention keeps making the trip.” Camellya guided her character under the Frost Queen's first strike. “You also touched the house key seventeen times on the lift. The string has left a mark on your neck.”

“You always did stare.”

“I was cultivated for it. Somewhere, a civilization spent a magnificent amount of effort teaching my body to find one particular woman. They would be furious to learn I use the skill on daughters who pour tea out of windows.”

Amy's character stopped moving.

Camellya paused the game.

The cabin regained its small sounds: flame working through a log, wind at the eaves, the kettle contracting as it cooled.

“How long have you known?” Amy asked.

“Your broadcast reached the Black Shores three weeks ago. You told six thousand listeners that the house had become so peaceful you were running out of material. Your voice carried enough material to build another house.”

“That show was funny.”

“The capybara segment had structure.”

“I accept the compliment.”

“Then you said your mother sleeps now.” Camellya rested her forearms on her knees. “You sounded like a sentry reporting a gate condition.”

Amy set her controller beside the mug.

“She does sleep. She rests. She laughs fully now, even when a joke earns three polite seconds. She has people who know how to catch her, and she lets them.”

“You recited that beautifully.”

“Every word belongs to the week we are living.”

“Your recital has excellent stage presence. You taught it well.”

Amy rose and carried both mugs to the sink. One had gone empty. The other still held half its cocoa, cooling under a roof of marshmallows.

She washed the empty mug for too long.

Behind her, Camellya resumed the game alone. The red knight walked in circles around the blue knight, once, twice, then settled beside her at the garden gate.

“Ma asks about you,” Amy said.

Camellya's hands went quiet.

“She calls you the flower woman. Sometimes a tune catches her in the middle of a room and she asks where she learned it. She knows the smell of the Black Shores in dreams. She knows a treetop, a beach, and somebody laughing above her while she worked. She has begun collecting those pieces.”

Amy dried the mug and placed it on the left side of the shelf, handle out.

“I tell her the pieces may belong to anyone.”

The fire shifted. A spark rose behind the grate.

“How does she receive that answer?” Camellya asked.

“She thanks me.”

Camellya closed her eyes.

Amy turned from the shelf. “You understand why I do it.”

“Your reasoning is painfully familiar.”

“Then say something useful.”

Camellya opened her eyes. “Useful words usually arrive wearing uniforms. I left mine in a ditch.”

“You crossed half the world to interfere. Earn your travel expenses.”

That brought the grin back, smaller and sharper. “There is the child who charged Exoswarm by throwing frozen pears at them.”

“The pears were tactical.”

“One was lunch.”

“It adapted to field conditions.”

“Sit down, Amy.”

Amy stayed by the shelf.

Camellya waited with the patience of roots under stone.

Amy returned to the floor.

## IV. What the Flower Kept

Camellya drew a small knife from her sleeve and laid it on the rug.

Its edge drank the firelight. Green veins ran through the dark metal, pulsing faintly with her heartbeat.

“I was made around a purpose,” she said. “Find her. Follow her. Kill her. Every part of me learned the route before I owned a name. When the moment came, I placed a flower in her hand.”

Amy watched the knife. “Was that freedom?”

“It felt entertaining.”

“You use that word whenever the others become expensive.”

Camellya looked pleased. “You remember me too.”

“You left a deep impression.”

“Careful. That answer has teeth.”

Amy drew one knee up and wrapped her arms around it. “You should continue.”

Camellya touched two fingers to the flower at her sternum.

“Knowledge reaches my body as weather. A painful memory raises roots through muscle. Strong feeling turns the flower dark. Enough of either begins to grow a forest through whatever room contains me. I clear the ground afterward. Names, years, whole roads. Survival becomes easier once the road vanishes.”

“You chose the clearing.”

“Again and again, whenever the roots reached bone.”

“Did anyone ask what you wanted to keep?”

The question crossed the room and touched something.

Camellya glanced at the console. Two tiny knights waited at the final garden, shoulder to shoulder beneath falling pixels.

“A little girl once asked me to remember a rematch,” she said.

Amy's breath slowed.

“I carried a paper slip for years. The handwriting was mine. Three lines: *Amy is family. She cheats at cards. Go north when it gets cold.* Every time I found the slip, I thought some previous version of me had developed sentimentality and poor penmanship. Every time, I went north.”

Camellya's hand closed over the flower.

“The first journey ended at a hospital window.”

The room seemed to tilt toward her.

Amy heard the old ward before she saw it: the measured breath of the shell, the cooling system under the floor, the soft electronic chime that marked each passing hour around a body living beyond clocks.

“Which day was it?” she asked.

“It happened on a Tuesday.”

“Every day felt like Tuesday inside Baldur.”

“This one had candy in the doctor's dish.”

Amy's fingers pressed into her sleeve.

“I watched you through the glass,” Camellya said. “Your hands lay open on the sheet. Your eyes stayed fixed on the ceiling. The instruments called you stable. I put a flower beside your wrist and waited for it to turn toward you.”

“The flower moved.”

Camellya went still.

“The stem crossed my palm near sunrise,” Amy continued. “I had found a conduit into the ward camera. I could borrow one hour before the current pulled me home. You stood with your forehead against the barrier, and the flower climbed the glass between us.”

The red at Camellya's sternum deepened toward purple.

“You were there,” she said.

“I tried to move my finger.”

“I saw a tremor.”

“The effort threw me back into the Drive for nine days. I decided I had imagined your visit.” Amy's voice held. “When I returned, the flower was still alive.”

Vines stirred under Camellya's skin.

“I came eleven times,” she said. “Each visit began with the slip in my pocket. Each visit ended with a physician calling security or a wall requiring repair. I followed your frequency until it reached the gate. The dark opened inside my head, and my body filled the corridor with roots. They sedated me. I woke somewhere south with a new scar and three lines in my handwriting.”

The flower reached the color of a bruise.

Amy shifted closer.

“Stay with me, Big Sis.”

“I had one job I chose for myself.” Camellya's breath came shallow, measured. “I had already abandoned the one they grew me for. I chose you. Then you walked into the dark while your mother was gone, and I arrived with flowers after the door had sealed.”

A vine emerged from the back of her hand, thin as thread. It entered the rug and spread across the floorboards in a green line.

Amy caught her wrist.

“Look at me.”

Camellya's pupils had widened until the garden in them went nearly black.

Amy moved the knife away with her heel, took Camellya's hand between both of hers, and pressed the hot palm against her own sternum.

“You can feel where I am.”

Camellya's fingers curled in Amy's shirt.

“Your heartbeat is under my palm, Amy.”

“Say when you leave the room.”

“I am already halfway through the wall.”

“Then keep talking. Tell me about the game.”

The vine reached the doorframe. Green tendrils spread over the pencil marks there, touching each year with blind tips.

Camellya stared at Amy's face. “You always chose the blue knight because her cape moved when she jumped.”

“What did you choose?”

“The red one had the larger sword.”

“Who cheated first?”

“Your mother unplugged my controller during the Frost Whale.”

“What did you do?”

“I put her in the lake.”

“She claimed she slipped for years.”

“Your mother cultivated dignity with heroic persistence.”

Amy felt the rhythm under Camellya's palm ease by one degree.

“Tell me another.”

“You chased an Exoswarm onto thin ice at six. I threw you by the back of your coat. You bit me.”

“You deserved it.”

“You had strong principles about trajectory.”

The green line stopped climbing.

Amy kept one hand around Camellya's wrist and reached for the wind-up kitchen timer with the other. She set eleven minutes and placed it where both could see.

“This household uses six rules whenever someone leaves her body behind. We can borrow three. I hold your hand, I keep the clock, and you give me a current number when you return.”

“The clock may as well be rolling dice.”

“Eleven minutes is the amount of fear our family can hold with good posture.”

Camellya's mouth twitched.

They sat on the floor while the timer moved.

At four minutes the vines loosened from the doorframe. At six they withdrew across the boards, leaving fine green stains in the grain. At eight Camellya's flower softened through violet into a tired rose.

At ten minutes and thirty-one seconds, she looked directly at Amy.

“The current number is seventy-three,” she said.

Amy released a breath.

“That number carries poor associations.”

“I heard it in the void. Somebody counted seventy-three seconds from insertion and spent the final one describing the stars.” Camellya flexed her hand within Amy's. “It sounded current.”

The timer rang.

Both women stayed where they were.

Snow pressed softly against the windows. The old game waited at its final door.

“I was angry with you,” Amy said.

Camellya's gaze stayed steady.

“I watched you leave the ward at sunrise. I pushed against that borrowed camera until I lost the room. I wanted you to break the glass. I wanted you to tear the gate out of the earth. I wanted every person who loved me to become powerful enough to reach me, because I had become powerful enough to save all of them.”

Her fingers tightened around Camellya's hand.

“You came back eleven times, and I am still angry about the first departure.”

Camellya bowed her head once. “You should keep it.”

“I plan to.”

“That suits you.” Her thumb passed across Amy's knuckles. “Anger grows well in you. Give it sunlight and a sensible pot.”

Amy laughed, and the laugh shook tears loose. She wiped them with her sleeve.

“Some Big Sis you were.”

“A catastrophic one.”

“You also taught me to roll when I fell.”

“You still led with your face.”

“The snow was soft.”

“Your nose bled.”

“I had spare blood.”

Camellya smiled. The smile carried grief, relief, and the wild edge of someone whose most durable possession had always been the present moment.

“There you are,” she said again.

This time Amy understood that Camellya was speaking to both of them.

## V. The Locked Slot

They returned to the game after midnight.

The Frost Queen waited beyond the final gate with six wings, a crown of ice, and dialogue written by someone who had discovered tragedy at sixteen. She offered the blue knight a bargain: surrender her memories, keep her kingdom, wake each morning free of grief.

The red knight's dialogue box appeared beneath hers.

> **IF YOU FORGET THE ROAD, I WILL WALK IT AGAIN WITH YOU.**

Amy stared at the line.

“We used to make fun of this writing,” she said.

“We had terrible taste.”

“We were seven and several thousand.”

“At those ages, taste would have been suspicious.”

Amy set the controller down.

From the pocket inside her coat, she removed a thin black data wafer. It was small enough to hide beneath the house key. Its surface was blank. A gold thread ran around the edge, recent solder done with a careful hand.

She placed it between them.

Camellya examined it with her hands resting on her knees. “That is the thing you carry.”

“It contains fragments. Old transmissions, sealed rosters, a directive from a shore hidden in fog, pieces I found inside the Nihility, and pieces the world forgot to destroy. Her voice appears in some of them.”

The fire lit one side of Amy's face.

“There are stories about you. There are stories about a woman who kept the shore. There are entire centuries where Ma belonged somewhere, and people waited after she left. Some of them are still waiting.”

Camellya's eyes rested on the wafer.

“You copied the archive.”

“I preserved the fragments that found me.”

“Does the wafer speak often?”

Amy ran her thumb along the seam in the controller. “I run a corruption check every morning, after the tea.”

Camellya leaned back against the couch.

“Your mother once built a shore for other people and carried every star above it inside her ribs. You have built a smaller shore inside that wafer. The stars still weigh the same.”

“I can hold it.”

“Your shoulders prove it.”

“That should matter.”

“It matters to the weight. Weight prefers capable shoulders.”

Amy picked up the wafer and closed it in her fist.

“She finally sleeps.”

“The report has remained consistent.”

“She spent weeks waking to count us. She watched me eat because she expected my body to vanish between bites. She learned how to laugh again in pieces. Now the whole thing comes out. I have a library in my pocket that could take that from her.”

“It could also give her a room she has been standing outside.”

“You went through the floor after five memories.”

“My mind grows teeth when history enters it. Hers may open a window.”

“Your promises end at the boundary of her mind.”

“Promises are strings tied around weather.” Camellya tipped her head. “I can tell you what I see. Each time she asks and receives mist, you take her choice into your pocket. Each morning you play the files and spend that choice alone.”

Amy rose. She crossed to the window and looked out at the frozen lake.

The reflection in the glass belonged to a nineteen-year-old woman with twenty-nine years arranged behind her eyes. Camellya sat in the room behind that reflection, red flower open, old console painting green light across her boots.

“She chose the quiet once,” Amy said.

“Whose hand rests on the lock today?”

Amy pressed her knuckles to the glass.

“She wanted me safe.”

“Protection guided both of you.”

“That is what daughters do.”

“Weapons receive the same instruction.”

The words entered softly and reached deep.

Amy turned.

Camellya's face held care stripped of softness. She had been cultivated as a weapon and had outlived every gardener. She had followed a command written beneath instinct, reached the target, and given her a flower. Freedom had begun there, perhaps. Or love. Or a flaw in the design. The answer remained buried inside the same rooms that threatened to kill her.

Amy sat on the window seat.

“You came all this way and kept yourself outside Ma's door.”

Camellya smiled with her lips closed. “You have me there.”

“You lectured me about choice while hiding on a freight lift.”

“That charge also stands.”

“Your hypocrisy has achieved structural integrity.”

“I take pride in my craft.”

Amy waited for a joke to carry the moment away. Camellya let the joke fall where it landed.

“Seeing her at the flower stall cost me three days,” she said. “She held the bloom with both hands. She looked into my face with a stranger's kindness. The past opened its mouth, and my body began rooting through the square. I left while I could still choose the direction.”

Her fingers moved against her knee, tracing an absent stem.

“I want to see her. I also want to remain myself during the seeing. Those wants share a room and break the furniture every night.”

Amy looked at the wafer in her hand.

“What should I tell her?”

“Give her one living thing.”

“Which story would you choose?”

“Begin with something that opens a door.” Camellya glanced toward the paused game. “Archives arrange the dead. Stories invite another voice.”

“That sounded useful.”

“Travel has improved me.”

Amy came back to the floor. She placed the wafer beside the game cartridge.

“If I tell her about you, she will look for you.”

“She always did.”

“You will run.”

“The road already knows my feet.”

“That is a terrible foundation for family.”

“Roots split stone. Families use stranger materials.”

They looked at the screen.

The Frost Queen waited with her impossible bargain. The red knight's promise remained beneath it.

Amy picked up her controller.

“We finish the boss.”

Camellya took hers. “Your mother will object.”

“She can file an appeal.”

“Now you sound grown.”

“I have been grown for seventeen years.”

“Your doorframe has opinions.”

“My doorframe is excluded from the hearing.”

Together, they opened the gate.

## VI. The Garden at the End

The Frost Queen killed them nine times.

On the tenth attempt, Amy found the rhythm of the left wing. Camellya found the half-second between the crown's flare and the ice field. Their old coordination returned through the hands: Amy drew the attack, Camellya crossed the screen, both rolled under the same descending blade, and the two knights rose back to back in a storm of square snow.

“You still rush the third phase,” Camellya said.

“You still treat health points as a personal insult.”

“Health grows back.”

“That explains your medical history.”

“My medical history contains artistry.”

“Your physician wrote *patient escaped through ceiling*.”

“The door had people in it.”

On the twelfth attempt, they won.

The Frost Queen's wings came apart into white pixels. Her crown fell. The blue knight held out her hand, and the defeated queen took it. The game had spent an entire cartridge teaching children to reach the garden, and its final miracle was three sprites standing together while dawn rose in bands of green.

The music reached the fifth bar.

Camellya stopped breathing.

Amy paused the game before the sixth.

The cabin held.

Camellya pressed her palm to her sternum. The flower darkened along one edge and stayed red at the center.

“You can keep the music going,” she said.

Amy studied her face. “Give me a number anchored to this room, then hold out your hand.”

“Thirty-one is current.”

Camellya held it out.

Amy took it and resumed the ending.

The fifth bar opened into a melody that had slept on a cartridge for twenty years. It carried the thin synthetic shape of a flute and, beneath it, four notes from a kitchen song: a woman humming while she stirred cocoa, a child singing too loudly, another woman sprawled across the table pretending to sleep.

Camellya bowed her head. Her grip grew fierce. Vines pressed against the skin of her wrist and remained beneath it.

On-screen, the red knight planted her sword in the thawing garden. A flower grew around the blade. The blue knight removed her three-pixel cape and laid it across the Frost Queen's shoulders.

The final text appeared.

> **THE GARDEN REMEMBERS EVERY WINTER.**  
> **SPRING ASKS WHAT IT WILL GROW NEXT.**

The credits began.

Camellya listened to every name.

When the title screen returned, the game offered three save slots.

The first held their old file.

The second stood empty.

The third contained a file whose creation sat outside both their memories.

> **PLAYER 1: FLORA**  
> **PLAYER 2: AEMEATH**  
> **LAST CHECKPOINT: BLACK SHORE**  
> **ELAPSED TIME: --:--:--**

Amy leaned closer to the screen.

“This cartridge stayed in the cabin.”

Camellya's flower opened one fraction wider.

“The Black Shores used a copy of that game for compatibility tests,” she said slowly. “The leader brought it back from the frostlands. She said a child had defeated her twelve rounds in a row and the machine was clearly defective.”

“Ma hated losing.”

“She loved evidence that you could beat her.”

Amy touched the data wafer beside the cartridge.

“Did she create this file?”

Camellya studied the blank elapsed time. “The name Flora belongs to a woman standing at the edge of my recollection. Aemeath belongs to a girl the shore learned about much later.”

“Then somebody wrote across the gap.”

“Gaps attract writers.”

The selection cursor blinked beside **LOAD**.

Amy moved it away.

“You wanted a rematch,” she said. “We can leave one mystery intact until breakfast.”

“Excellent. Mysteries improve when ignored with discipline.”

They saved their completed game into the second slot.

The console asked for a filename.

Amy typed **BIG SIS CAME BACK**.

Camellya reached over and changed **CAME** to **COMES**.

Amy looked at her.

“You chose present tense.”

“It has better instincts.”

Amy accepted the edit.

## VII. One Living Thing

Dawn found them on the porch.

The frostlands brightened by degrees, pearl becoming blue, blue taking gold along the ridge. The wisteria held its buds close. Amy sat wrapped in the good scarf with the black wafer balanced on one knee. Camellya lay along the porch rail, one boot hooked beneath it, her flower facing the cold.

Amy had opened the recorder on the wafer. A new file waited for a voice.

“You could begin with my beauty,” Camellya suggested. “It establishes credibility.”

“I am trying to speak to my mother.”

“Then mention my patience. She always found that funny.”

Amy pressed record.

The small red light came on.

Amy began. “Ma, there is someone from the frostlands years whom I want to tell you about. I called her Big Sis Camellya. We used to chase Exoswarm across the ice, and she taught me how to fall with my teeth safely behind my lips. She is very good at games. She cheats through superior reflexes and describes the result as philosophy.”

Camellya looked out over the lake.

Amy continued.

“Once, while she was supposed to be recuperating, she came all the way north to check on you. She saw that you were safe. She left quietly because love sometimes gave both of you terrible manners.”

The wisteria clicked softly against the porch post.

“She remembers you,” Amy said. “She remembers me. Some memories hurt her body, so the shape of that remembering changes from day to day. The affection stays.”

Camellya closed her eyes.

Amy watched the red recording light.

“I have more. You can ask me when you want the next story.”

She pressed stop.

The file appeared beneath her thumb.

> **TO_MA_01 — DRAFT**

Camellya opened one eye. “You left out the hospital.”

“That story belongs to us first.”

“You left out the shore.”

“She gets to ask.”

“You left me outside the door.”

Amy looked at her. “You asked for tonight.”

Camellya accepted the line with a small movement of her chin.

“Will you send it?”

Amy turned the wafer over. Dawn ran along its gold seam.

“I will sit beside her when she hears it.”

“That schedule feels close.”

“It is a plan, and plans can ripen.”

“Spoken like a gardener's daughter.”

“Spring would make a good time.”

The word moved through Camellya. Her hand tightened on the porch rail.

“The wisteria will be awake,” Amy said. “Ma finds hard conversations easier when something is growing nearby. You could come then.”

Camellya watched the pale vines around the posts.

“My appointments usually lose track of me.”

“You kept eleven visits to a hospital.”

“The paper carried me to each one.”

Amy tore a narrow strip from the back of the transmission note. She wrote against her knee, folded the paper twice, and held it out.

Camellya unfolded it.

> **AMY IS FAMILY.**  
> **MEI IS HOME.**  
> **SPRING. BLUE DOOR. BRING TWO CONTROLLERS.**

The handwriting came out crooked.

Camellya read it several times.

“You gave me an order.”

“I issued an invitation with load-bearing punctuation.”

“And if I lose the paper?”

“Your body found me once.”

“And if the memory burns?”

“The door stays blue.”

“And if I reach the porch and turn around?”

Amy considered that possibility. The red light on the wafer had gone dark. Behind it, the unsent story waited with its door open.

“I will leave the second controller outside,” she said. “You can decide how close you come.”

Camellya folded the paper and tucked it beneath the flower at her sternum.

“You learned something in the dark,” she said.

“The dark taught an extensive curriculum. Most lessons were terrible company.”

“This lesson can stay.”

They sat until the sun cleared the ridge.

At the edge of the porch, where frost met the first step, a green shoot pushed through the snow. It rose with the idle certainty of Camellya's breathing and opened into a small red bloom. One of the sleeping wisteria tendrils shifted in the wind and settled around its stem.

Amy looked at the flower. “Will that last?”

“As long as I do.”

“That could become awkward for future owners.”

“Tell them it came with the house.”

Camellya slid from the rail and landed in the snow. She walked three paces toward the road, then turned.

“One more thing, Snowfluff.”

Amy stayed on the porch, both hands around the wafer.

“Yes, Big Sis?”

“You get to sleep too.”

The sentence entered cleanly. Amy felt every defense rise around it, bright and capable, each carrying a clipboard.

She let them wait.

“I will try tonight,” she said.

Camellya smiled.

“That is a good place to continue from.”

She went down the path with the morning at her back, wine-dark hair moving across her coat and one hand resting over the folded note. At the turn she kept her eyes on the road and lifted two fingers. Then the snow took her shape among the trees.

Amy remained beside the blue door.

The wafer held an archive, an unsent story, and one new save. Inside the cabin, the console's title music began again after its long silence. Four bars rose through the floorboards. A fifth followed. Then the melody opened farther, into country the old speakers had always contained and nobody in the room had reached until today.

Amy listened through the end.

She watered the camellia before she left. The flower required none. The gesture required her, and she gave it freely.

On the service shuttle south, she slept through the second tunnel with the house key warm against her chest and the black wafer loose in her open hand.

The file waited in drafts.

The story had begun moving.

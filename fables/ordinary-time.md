## i. Kuresaki
### *Ashinohara*

The gauge went around the child's neck the way his master had taught him: from behind, at breath's end, thumb clear of the throat. Ojima Ren read the number twice, wrote it once, and set the gauge down where the girl could see both his hands.

"Twenty-four and a half," he said. "A good neck. Collars sit best on good necks."

The girl, Aki, seven, watched him from under a fringe her mother had cut that morning; you could tell by the line of it, careful and slightly wrong, the way home haircuts are when the hands doing the cutting are shaking. The mark had surfaced behind her left ear during a fever, a coil of pale geometry the size of a coin. The fever had broken on Tuesday. The Bureau's letter had arrived on Thursday. It was Saturday.

Ren's workshop occupied the front room of a Bureau annex in Kuresaki, a fishing town of four thousand souls and one train platform, and he had held the regulator smith's license for eleven years, as his master had before him, as his master's master had before that, in a city that had since become a bay with buoys in it. The license hung behind him, next to the wall chart of approved patterns and the framed photograph of the Minister of Frequency Safety shaking somebody's hand. The photograph had faded. The chart had been reissued twice. The work stayed the work: measure, fit, seal, file.

He brought the drawer of ribbons to the edge of the bench and slid it down to her height.

"Pick one. It threads under the clasp. Regulation permits it." Regulation said nothing on the matter either way, which he had decided, eleven years ago, meant permission.

Aki looked at her mother. Her mother looked at the floor. Aki picked yellow.

"Will it hurt?" she asked, while he threaded it.

"Like a scarf in summer. You'll argue with it in August. Everyone does."

"Can I take it off to swim?"

He had answered this question forty-one times. He had a card for every fitting he had ever done, and on the back of each card, in his own shorthand, the question that child had asked, because his master had taught him that the questions told you more than the measurements. *Can I take it off to swim* appeared on nineteen cards. *Will my friends see it* on eleven. *Does it know what I'm thinking* on four. One card, from his second year, said only: *asked whether the sea would still recognize her.*

"It's sealed for the water," he said. "Swim all you like."

The fitting itself took ninety seconds. The band was his own work, Kuresaki pattern, and along the inner face ran a lining of grey felt he cut from bolt cloth he bought himself, because the Bureau's specification called for bare alloy and bare alloy left a ring of chafed skin on a growing neck by winter. He filed the clasp burr down to glass. He had the girl swallow, cough, and shake her head like a dog, and the collar rode all of it quietly, a scarf in summer, and the small hum it made when it woke — a tuning-fork sound, felt in the back teeth more than heard — lasted two seconds and settled.

Aki's hand went up to it. Held there.

"It's warm," she said, surprised.

"It's saying hello."

The mother bowed twice, thanked him twice, paid the Bureau's fee at the window, and led her daughter out into the salt light. Through the doorway Ren watched them go down the harbor road, and watched the fishwives at the stalls watch them go, and watched the exact moment — third stall, the woman who sold mackerel — when a gaze found the yellow ribbon and the small grey band beneath it and a mouth went flat, and a scale-knife came down on a fish spine hard enough to be a sentence.

The girl would learn to read that sentence. They all learned. It was the town's second alphabet.

He went back to his bench and pulled her card and wrote, on the back: *asked to swim. picked yellow before her mother finished worrying.* Then he filed the card in the box with the other forty-one and sat for a while with his tea going cold, which was a thing he did now, since last year. Since the rain over Honami Bay had stopped.

His brother had married into Honami. Ren had stood at the wedding in a borrowed haori and given a speech about tides that everyone had been kind about. Then the sky over that city had learned its long scream, and the rain had come down and gone on coming down, and the Bureau had drawn its exclusion ring, and for most of Ren's adult life his brother's family had been a bay with buoys in it, a line item in the national grief that the collars were built to answer. *Never again* had a budget. Ren's license was part of the budget. He had always known this and had always been able to hold it, the way you hold any heavy thing: with both hands, close to the body, breathing carefully.

Then, last year, the rain stopped. Sixty-three streets went quiet like lanterns going out, the newsreaders said, quoting somebody. The buses ran out along the old coast road for the first time in decades, and people came home. His niece came home. Hana, nineteen, the same nineteen she had been when the rain took the city, stepping off the bus into the arms of parents who had aged the full fare of the years while she paid in some other currency, in a city where time had circled like water deciding about a drain. She called Ren *uncle* in a voice from thirty years ago. He was still learning what to do with that.

And in the weeks after, the photographs. Every paper ran them. The people who had gone in to cut the city open — outsiders, most of them, a wolf-tailed gladiator, a bard, a woman with golden eyes — and among them a local girl, Honami-born, a survivor, standing in the wreckage of a god with a pair of enormous scissors and her face turned slightly away from every camera. The papers called her a heroine on Monday and a security question on Thursday. Ren had studied the photograph for a long time before he understood what he was looking at.

The girl was wearing a collar.

Kuresaki pattern's elder cousin: the old Honami city pattern, his master's master's stampwork, the double chrysanthemum at the clasp. Fitted young, by the size of it, and worn since. Worn into the sealed city and through it and out the other side. Worn while she bisected a god down its length, the papers said, if the papers could be believed, and the Bureau had issued a statement about calibration tolerances that everyone had read and nobody had finished.

Ren thought about that collar more than he told anyone. The doctrine said the band stilled what was dangerous. The photograph said a girl had carried her whole strength through the band as through an open gate, and had kept the band anyway. He turned it over on the long evenings, planing wood he had no order for. A collar built to quiet her. Kept by her. There was a reason in it somewhere, a reason that belonged to the girl and to nobody's ministry, and he suspected the reason had felt in it, and a ribbon's worth of some color, and a smith somewhere in a drowned city who had filed the burr down to glass.

That winter he began stamping the inner face of every band he made, down where the felt met the clasp, where inspectors never looked. A small wave. Three lines, curling. His own mark, on the inside, against the skin, where only the wearer would ever find it — a finger tracing the band some August, mid-argument with it, meeting a shape that had been put there on purpose, by hand, by someone.

He moved the ribbon drawer to the bottom of the bench, where the children could reach it themselves.

## ii. The Garnish
### *Septimont*

The booth smelled of hot dust and cold coffee, and on the other side of the glass Publio's own voice came back at him for the fortieth time, four bars, selling soda.

*"Cold as the peaks, bold as the pit — VESUVIA."*

"Again," said Rufina, his agent, through the talkback. "The 'bold' bent flat."

"The 'bold' was perfect."

"The 'bold' bent flat and you know it, and if it bends flat then it sticks flat, and then forty thousand people walk around all summer with a flat note in their skulls and my name on the invoice."

That was the shape of his gift and both of them knew it. Publio's Forte lived in his voice, a resonance that took hold of whatever he sang and set it into the listener like a rivet. One hearing sufficed. A phrase, a route, a price, a promise: sung once by Publio, it stayed, word-perfect, retrievable at will, for years. The Bureau of the Agon had a term for Fortes like his. *Class C, civil-adjacent.* The arena had a shorter one.

He had heard the shorter one at fifteen, at the qualifier trials, standing in volcanic dust in his brother's hand-me-down greaves, singing at a shield wall. The shield wall had listened politely. Then it had walked over him. The examiner, a scarred veteran with a clipboard, had helped him up, checked his teeth with a professional thumb, and delivered the city's verdict with the gentleness of a woman who had delivered it a hundred times: *"You'll remember this day forever, kid. That's the problem. So will everyone. Go find work where remembering pays."*

Septimont loved its Resonators. Publio wanted that understood, whenever he told the story, because outsiders got it wrong. The city put marked children at the front of every parade. Their portraits went up in the fighters' district while they still had their milk teeth. When his mark had surfaced — across his collarbone, a lattice like frost on glass — his mother had wept in the doorway and the whole street had brought food, and old Fulvia at the tavern had stood him on a table and told him he carried the city's fortune under his shirt.

Then the city had weighed the fortune, found it sang jingles, and priced it accordingly. That was the part outsiders got wrong in the other direction. The love was real. The love had a ledger.

"Once more," Rufina said. "Then the mining brief."

He sang the soda cold and bold. Through the booth glass, past Rufina's shoulder, the studio's window gave onto the boulevard and the great billboard opposite, where a wolf-tailed silhouette forty feet high drank something carbonated against a volcanic sunset. The silhouette had been in litigation for a year and a half. The gladiator it belonged to had won the Great Agon, banished a noble house in the winning of it, taken back her own image in the settlement, and now licensed it on her own terms, which by all accounts were brief and included clauses about honesty in a legal document, which had made every advocate in the city need to sit down. Publio had sung three campaigns under that silhouette. He had never met the woman. Everyone in Septimont felt they had met the woman. That was Fortes too, of a kind: fame was a resonance, and it also chose its hosts young and asked nobody's permission.

The mining brief was routes. The eastern shafts had recut their galleries after the tremors and the old evacuation songs pointed men down dead tunnels, and the Guild wanted the new routes set to verse. It paid a fifth of the soda money. He took it anyway, and took the drill contract that followed it, and that was how, in the spring, he ended up in a briefing room under Capitoline Hill with a civil defense commission, a wall of maps, and a memorandum from the Ephor's own office. The memorandum ran to one line, in a soldier's handwriting.

*Routes change. Panic forgets maps. Make them remember.*

He worked a month on the High Tide verses. Nera did the architecture — Nera, his copywriter, unmarked, forty, mother of three, who could take a tangle of stair counts and cistern doors and turn it into language that scanned, who had been turning his gift into rent for a decade. Their process had the rhythm of an old marriage.

"Seven stairs, then the cistern arch," she said, chalking it. "Then it splits. High road for the harbor wards, low road for the pit."

"'Seven stairs to the cistern arch' — that's the hook."

"That's *my* hook."

"It's your hook. I forge it." He tried it against the map, half-voice, feeling for where the Forte caught. "You set them. I set them permanently. Between us we're one respectable Resonator."

"Between us," Nera said, "you're the glue and I'm the furniture, and I'd thank you to remember which one people sit on."

The drills ran through the summer. Schoolchildren sang the Steps of the Seven Stairs in playgrounds, mangling his melody, keeping every word, which was the design. The Agon season came, and at the victors' banquet — he sang the anthem now; civil-adjacent, invited through the side door, seated with the engineers — a champion two years off the sand looked at his collarbone lattice over the wine and asked what it did, and, hearing, laughed with real warmth and called it a garnish. "Every kitchen needs one," the champion said, and clapped his shoulder, and meant it kindly, and moved on toward the people whose Fortes could crack stone.

Publio drank his wine and thought about a night at Fulvia's the year before, when a foreign bard had passed through with a lute and a ballad about a maiden who fought a god. He had watched the room while she sang. Hard men. Pit men. Women with cauterized forearms and championship rings. By the third verse the room had gone somewhere together, all of them, the way a tide takes a harbor: every boat at once, the heaviest hulls included. The bard's Forte was song, he'd been told afterward. Soft class. Civil-adjacent. And she had walked out of that tavern having moved a hundred fighters the way no Forte in the arena canon could move them, and half of them had wept into their cups, and the ballad had crossed the sea since and rearranged what two nations believed about their saints.

A garnish, he thought, and raised his glass to the champion's retreating back, and filed the word where he filed everything, permanently, in the place his gift kept for later.

Later came in autumn, at dusk, when the ground under the eastern wards spoke in its old language and the sirens climbed and the sea stood up along the mole. He was above the harbor when it started, and so he saw it: the crowds pouring from the terraces, and the crowds *singing*. Ragged, off-key, forty thousand people strong, seven stairs to the cistern arch, the harbor wards taking the high road in verse-order while the water came on behind them. A schoolteacher led her class down the switchbacks calling the second stanza like a round. A pit champion — that champion — came down the same stair with a stranger's child on his shoulders, and the child was singing the route into his ear, word-perfect, and the champion was matching his stride to her verses, following the garnish home.

The commission's report, filed that winter, credited the low casualty figures to infrastructure, drilling, and civic discipline. A line near the end mentioned the mnemonic program. Publio kept a copy. Nera kept two.

## iii. Testimony
### *Ragunna*

The letters came addressed to *The Guard Who Ran*, care of the Watch, and the Watch forwarded them, because Tebaldo had quit the Watch and his old sergeant found the whole thing funny.

He had been a checkpoint guard for nine years, a big careful man with flat feet and a good memory for faces, and then for half of one second he had been the entire attention of a saint with a god in her skull, and she had spent that half-second — bought with her own bitten tongue, the physicians established later, at the hearings, with diagrams — on a single word aimed at his chest.

*Run.*

He ran. His knee went sideways on the wet stone of the Zattera Steps and had clicked in cold weather ever since, and that click was the sum total of what the Square had cost him, out of a plaza where the frequency ring had thrown grown men through shopfronts. He understood his own arithmetic perfectly. He was the luckiest man in Ragunna, and the price of the luck was that Ragunna wanted him professionally.

The Registry Act came to the Council of Stewards in the spring of the third year after the Order fell. It proposed a civic roll of all resonant persons within the Republic's waters, with classification of Fortes by hazard, and it split the city down streets and down supper tables. Steward Malvina carried it. Her brother had died at Carnevale, crushed under a gallery when the Conductor's puppets came through the crowd, and she carried the Act the way you carry a coffin: from underneath, with her whole back. Against her stood Steward Orsolo and the abolition benches, and the fish market, and most of the choirs, and the veterans of the Square itself, who had turned out to be, on the whole, the softest people in the city on the question — a fact the pamphleteers on both sides found inconvenient.

Both sides subpoenaed Tebaldo inside the same week.

The hearing hall had been the Order's tithe court, and the Republic had kept the room and changed the furniture, which Tebaldo felt was the Republic in miniature. He waited in the corridor with the other witnesses. A gondolier's widow, dressed in mourning gone soft at the seams, holding a claims folder three fingers thick. A Resonator ferryman whose license had been suspended pending classification, standing very still, the way marked men had learned to stand in public buildings that year. A glazier who had reset the cathedral windows twice at his own cost and had opinions about who ought to pay the third time, and said so, to the corridor at large, until the widow asked him quietly to stop.

They called Tebaldo after the physicians. Malvina led.

"You were a serving officer at the Square."

"I was."

"Describe the Resonator's condition."

"Her crown had gone black. Her eyes were wrong. Her sword arm was up." He kept his hands folded. "My spear was up too, for what that's worth. Three of us, one of her."

"In your professional judgment, was she in control of her Forte?"

"My professional judgment was busy."

Laughter, one bench. Malvina waited it out, patient as her grief.

"You understand why we ask. A hazard that walks. An arsenal with a temper. The Act would tell this chamber, in advance, where the arsenals are." She leaned in. "Sir. She nearly killed you."

"She did." He let the word sit. "And then she paid her only coherent half-second to spare me. Steward, I've thought about the ledger of that afternoon every day for three years, and here's where I've landed: the thing in her wanted the Square dead, and the girl in her spent her last free breath on a stranger's guard. Your roll of names would have listed the second one to warn you about the first."

"Then help us list wisely. Testify to the danger."

"I'll testify to what happened. She said run. I ran." His knee clicked as he shifted, on cue, and he almost smiled at it. "Write your law however the votes fall. I'd only ask the chamber to leave me out of the preamble. I was there for half a second of it, and the half-second belongs to her."

Orsolo, on cross, asked one question: whether he feared Resonators.

"I fear the sea," Tebaldo said. "I still live on it."

The Act failed by four votes that session, and passed the next year in a narrower form, hazard-class registration for combat Fortes only, with a sunset clause, and the arguing continued, because the arguing was the Republic now, and most days Tebaldo thought the arguing was the healthiest thing the city owned. The Order had settled every question for two hundred years. Look what the answers had cost.

He walked home through the Square. The Republic had repaved it after the reconstruction, and had kept, by an ordinance nobody remembered proposing, the ring — the great circle of scorched paving where the frequency had detonated, stones fused glossy, dark as tea. Petitions to remove it arrived every year. Petitions to consecrate it arrived every year. The stones stayed, and did what stones do, and children had solved the theological question the way children solve everything: the ring was the boundary for hopscotch now, and tag, and a game with chalk and complicated casting arguments that he heard sometimes from his window in the evenings.

At the flower stall on the ring's edge he bought a white camellia, out of season, imported, expensive, and set it on the glossy stones on his way past, as he did on the first of every month. The stallholder had stopped asking who it was for around the time Tebaldo had stopped being sure himself. For the girl, some months. For the guard he'd been. For the half-second, mostly, and everyone who lived inside one.

## iv. Form Twelve
### *the frontier*

The Adventurers' Guild hall at Sette Strade opened intake at the eighth bell, and by the seventh the queue already ran past the well, because Sette Strade sat on the caravan road between Septimont's hinterland and the wild coast, and out here the word *Resonator* was worth its weight in escort contracts.

Bruna ran the desk. She had run it for nine years. Her horns ran back from her temples in two clean spirals, goat-fashion, grey as slate, and they were the entire yield of her own awakening: at thirteen, a Tacet Mark on her scalp, a week of fever, and then horns. The examiners had tested her for a month. Air, water, fire, frequency, echo-taming, foresight, song. Horns. Her file at the regional office read, under Forte classification, *morphological, inert*, which Bruna had decided long ago was the Guild's way of saying *decorative*, and she kept the file's language and used it on the road agents who leaned on her desk asking what her *real* one was.

"Inert," she'd say, and tap a spiral. "But I gore queue-jumpers. Form Twelve. Next."

Form Twelve was the registration schedule, and Form Twelve had three boxes. COMBAT. SUPPORT. CIVIL. Somewhere in a Federation records office, someone had decided that the whole spectrum of resonance — everything from divine wind to a good ear for tide — sorted into three boxes, and Bruna's actual Forte, the working one, the one no examiner had a test for, was making the boxes fit the people instead of the people fit the boxes.

Intake, that morning:

A dairyman from the valley farms, fifty, cap in his hands, whose milk had stopped souring the summer his mark surfaced along his wrist. His whole herd's yield, sweet a week past sense, and cheese from his cellar had started winning things. He wanted to know if he had to register at all. "The stipend's real," Bruna said, "and so's the census fine. Civil box. Congratulations, you're strategic infrastructure." She stamped it. He looked at the stamp the way men look at a diagnosis. "My boys are unmarked," he said, halfway to the door, halfway to himself. "Good boys. They ask me why the cheese only listens to me." He left before she found the answer, which suited her, because she was still looking for it herself, thirty years on.

A boy of sixteen, road-dusty, traveling alone, who could see three seconds ahead. Three, on a calm day; two, tired; four, once, terrified. The recruiters had smelled him coming the way gulls smell a catch — two escort outfits and a Septimont scout had all reached the desk ahead of him with offers for her to pass along, and Bruna had fed all three offers to the stove. Three seconds was a duelist's eternity and a bodyguard's fortune, and it was also sixteen and traveling alone. She boxed him SUPPORT, wrote *courier, provisional, review at eighteen* in the margin, assigned him to the mail run with old Vasska, who had buried two partners and drank tea by the liter and would talk a boy's ear off about everything except how to bleed. The boy read the assignment upside down while she wrote it. He saw it coming, of course, three seconds early. He signed anyway. That told her the margin note was right.

A man of forty with a mark drawn on his forearm in walnut ink.

She knew before he had his sleeve fully rolled; ink sits on skin and a mark sits under it, the way a reflection sits under water, and besides, his was a copy of a woodcut that hung in every tavern on the road, the wolf-tailed champion's shoulder-mark, faithfully rendered and slightly reversed. He held the arm out steady and met her eyes while he did it, which she respected. The stipend for a registered Resonator on the frontier fed a household. The man's boots said the rest.

She rolled his sleeve back down herself, gently, and slid a different form across.

"Auxiliary corps. Unmarked. Pays less, and they'll give you a spade before they give you a spear." She held the pen out. "The stipend queue checks marks with a frequency lamp on Thursdays. This queue checks whether you show up." A moment. "Widower?"

"Two girls."

"Show up," Bruna said, and stamped him into the payroll of the merely mortal, where the pay was thin and the statues were of other people and the work, out here, was most of what stood between the road and the dark.

And at the tail of the morning, when the hall had emptied, a woman who had waited on purpose to be last. Thirty, maybe. Coastal accent. She stood a full pace back from the desk and asked, before anything, how many people were in the building.

"Eleven," Bruna said. "Twelve with the cat."

"Fourteen," the woman said. "Two in the cellar. One's heart skips." Her hands stayed folded and her eyes stayed on the middle distance and she laid it out flat, the way people describe a chronic pain: she heard heartbeats. Every heartbeat, thirty paces, waking and sleeping, through walls, through floors, a room of people arriving in her skull as weather arrives on a ship, and it had begun four years ago and it climbed year on year and it had a Forte's signature and an affliction's manners. Markets were a torment. Cities were out of the question. She slept in a dory, anchored out, where the only heart in range was her own, and some nights the fish were loud.

"I came to ask the Guild," she said, "whether there's a box for *make it stop*."

Bruna looked at Form Twelve for a while.

Then she took a requisition sheet instead, and wrote to the address she kept in the back of the ledger, the one that had come down the caravan road two winters ago folded inside a returned crate: a Bureau annex in a fishing town across the eastern water, in a country whose whole relationship to resonance ran opposite to the frontier's, where they built small grey bands whose entire craft was quiet. The crate had held a sample, sent along some chain of guild favors for evaluation as *restraint equipment*, and Sette Strade's quartermaster had rejected it for the purpose and Bruna had kept it in her drawer, because the workmanship had stopped her: felt-lined, burr filed to glass, and on the inner face, hidden against where the skin would be, a tiny stamped wave, put there by a hand with an opinion.

*One regulator,* she wrote. *Kuresaki pattern, felt-lined, sized to enclosed measurements. Adjustable damping if the pattern allows. For a client who is asking.*

She underlined the last word twice, because the whole difference lived in it, and a smith across the water would understand or he wouldn't.

Then she filed the woman under CIVIL, and in the margin, where the regional office never read, she wrote what the box left out: *hears the buried before the dogs do — IF she offers. Ask. Never send.*

"Come back in two months," she told her. "Sleep in your boat. Give the fish my regards."

The woman got to the door before she turned around. "Your horns," she said. "What do they do?"

"They hold up my reputation," said Bruna. "Next."

There was no next. She stamped the empty air anyway, for form's sake, and went to lunch.

## v. Triage
### *Lahai-Roi*

Exam day at Startorch Academy started at first light, which in Lahai-Roi meant the moment the Reactor Drive brightened from ember to gold and the Solistrees took up their slow hum, and Marisol always worked exam day, because exam day filled her infirmary twice: once with the ones who got in, and once, briefly, at the gate, with the ones the arch turned away.

She was at the gate at opening, with her kit, as the queue formed under the banners. The frequency arch read each child as they passed — a chime for a resonant signature, and the great doors beyond. She had watched the arch work for six years and she could read the queue better than the arch could. The marked children who wanted this, bouncing on their toes. The marked children who had been sent, standing inside their parents' ambitions like coats a size too big. And scattered through the line every year, hopeful as weeds, the unmarked ones, who had heard the arch was a machine and machines made mistakes, and who walked through it holding their breath.

The arch stayed silent for a boy of nine that morning. His friend, one place ahead, had chimed through bright as a bell and was already turning back in the doorway, confused by the sudden distance. The boy stood under the silent arch and looked up into it, giving it a second chance, giving it a third, and Marisol was already moving, because this was her real gate duty, the part in her contract under *other tasks as required*: she reached him as his face came apart, and got him turned, and got him walking, and did the thing she did every year, which was to talk about breakfast. What he'd eaten. Whether the tram ran near his house. Whether he knew the noodle stand on the Third Ward corner where the owner's capybara sat on the counter like a manager. By the well he was answering in words. By the tram stop, in sentences. His friend would find him tonight, or next week, or never; the arch sorted that too, in the long run, and it was the sorting she minded most.

The city argued about her employer in three registers, and Marisol heard all three across any given month.

*Safe space,* said the refugee mother from the eastern enclaves, sitting by her daughter's infirmary cot in autumn — a girl who'd arrived with a suppression-band scar on her neck and a flinch that answered raised hands, and who, after one term behind Startorch's walls among children whose marks were ordinary as freckles, had slept through a full night for the first time in her mother's memory. The mother told Marisol this in a low fierce voice, holding her tea in both hands, as though someone might take the fact away.

*Elitist,* said the budget councilman, touring in winter with two aides and a tablet, reading out the per-student figures against the canal schools across the water, where Marisol's own son learned his letters in a class of forty. The councilman had a point and made it well. The canal kids had a chant for the Academy children — *torch-boys, torch-boys, kiss the flame* — and her son had come home singing it before he was old enough to know his mother worked inside the walls it was aimed at.

*Slaughterhouse,* said the graffiti on the tram shelter, freshly painted every term around ranking season, and ranking season was when Marisol earned her wage.

The boy in cot three had come down from the ranking floor at midday, fourteen years old, frequency burns from wrist to shoulder where his own Forte had exceeded its channel — Overclock, the instructors called it, in the flat voice institutions keep for the things they schedule. He had held a barrier duel forty seconds past his rated limit because the rankings posted on the board outside the refectory and his father read the board. Marisol dressed the arm. She stocked three formulations of resonance-burn salve, in three strengths, and the requisition history of those three jars was, in her opinion, the honest ledger of the school, whatever the prospectus said.

The Academy President came down to the infirmary in the late afternoon, unannounced, as was her practice. Lucilla toured the cots the way Marisol imagined she had once toured other, colder facilities in her Federation days: reading everything, hands clasped at her back, a woman whose own Forte read memories and who had therefore learned to move through the world like a guest who knows where all the drawers are and opens none of them. She stopped at cot three. Looked at the arm. Looked at the boy.

"Who told you to hold it that long?"

"The board," the boy said, to his blanket.

"The board is cork." She let that land. "The duel was lost at second thirty-one. The next nine seconds you spent buying this bed. What was the price of the bed?"

The boy worked it through. "Six weeks."

"Six weeks off the floor while your cohort trains." She took a paper twist from her coat — sour candy; the whole school knew about the drawer — and set it on his blanket like a coin on a counter. "You'll ache. When you ache, eat one, and remember: the ache is the tuition and the ranking is the wrapper." At the door she turned back once, to Marisol. "Restock the candy. Bill my office."

Marisol saw her out and came back to her station, past the burn cots and the girl who slept whole nights now, and got on with the inventory. Her son asked her sometimes over dinner why the arch had stayed silent for him — they'd taken him once, on a visitors' day, and he'd walked through it on his own initiative before she could stop him, chin up, hoping. *What did it say about me?* he'd asked, on the tram home.

*It said you go home with me,* she'd told him, which was the truth from one side.

The other side she kept where she kept the requisition ledger. The arch read frequencies. Her boy would grow up on the loud, crowded, unfunded far bank of that reading, in the city's plain schools, chanting rude songs at the torch-boys — and he would also grow up with a whole skin, and a rated limit of none, and a mother who had never once had to buy a felt-lined band or a burn salve on his account. There were nights she counted that as poverty and nights she counted it as mercy, and after six years on the ward she had stopped expecting the count to settle.

She stocked the salves. She filled the jar. Both, every week. That was the job.

## vi. Tide-Hour
### *the camellia coast*

Serafina heard the tide the way other people remember appointments: it arrived in her, an hour early, complete. Turn of the water, weight of the swell, the temper of it. Her mark sat over her breastbone, small as a thumbprint, and it had come with her into the world at birth, which made her the fourth marked child in the village's memory and the least dramatic by a distance. The others had gone away — to Ragunna, to the academies, to whatever marked children went away to. Serafina's Forte topped out at knowing when to bring the washing in. She had stayed.

She married Matteo the year the Order fell, and the wedding had been ordinary in every particular except the seating, which had required her mother-in-law and the co-op president to conduct three weeks of diplomacy, because half the guest list wanted the marked bride's table blessed and the other half wanted it downwind.

The co-op paid her in fish for tide-calls. This had settled into custom: mornings, the skippers came past the house, and Serafina stood in the doorway with her tea and gave them the day's water, and in the evening a share of the catch arrived on the step, gutted, out of a politeness so old it had stopped being noticed. The keeper up at the lighthouse — a weathered, amused man who kept his own counsel — bought her hours too, and paid in lamp oil and in one sentence a season, delivered like a tip: *"You hear the sea an hour out, signora. I've known admiralties that managed less with a budget."* At the harvest feast, the co-op sat her and Matteo at the door-end of the long table, near the draft, and the same skippers who leaned on her doorframe every dawn raised their cups to her from the warm end, and meant it, and stayed where they were.

Matteo minded the seating more than she did. She could tell by his shoulders. She had learned his shoulders the way she'd learned the tide, and they had their own weather, and she left it to pass, because it always did, usually by the second jug.

The letter came in the spring, from a Ragunnan underwriter the co-op used, regarding the renewal of the household policy: *…rider applicable to households including resonant persons, per revised actuarial tables…* Matteo read it twice at the table, then read her the figure. She listened, and did the sum against the fish on the step and the lamp oil in the shed, and reached across and turned the letter face down.

"The sea's been insuring us for years," she said. "The premiums are on the doorstep every evening."

"It's the principle."

"It's Ragunna. Their principles come itemized." She put his cup in his hand. "Pay it. Then tell Old Bardo the tide-hour costs a sardine more this season, and let the co-op argue with the actuaries."

The squall in the autumn came up out of a clear afternoon, the way the worst ones did, and her breastbone knew it at four bells with the sky still lying blue about it. She went down the harbor road at a run, and the skippers who'd raised cups to her from the warm end of the table took one look at her face and put their backs into the windlasses, because whatever the feast-seating said, at four bells with her hair coming loose they believed her the way they believed rope. Four boats came in on her word. The fifth had gone out early, past the point, past her voice, and the squall took it in open water with two men aboard.

The widow of one of them stopped greeting her after that. It was done quietly, village-fashion: a gaze that found the harbor whenever Serafina passed, a door that happened to close. Serafina understood the grammar of it perfectly. The gift had a radius, and inside the radius stood four crews' worth of living men, and at the radius's edge stood a woman for whom the gift's existence made the loss a question instead of a weather. *If she hears the sea, why my Paolo?* There was arithmetic that answered it and the arithmetic helped nobody, and so Serafina carried the closed door the way Matteo carried the seating, and let it be weather, and brought the widow's share of the tide-call fish to her step anyway, every week, and left before the door could decide.

One evening in winter Matteo sat very still at the mirror shelf, and she found him holding a grey hair up to the lamp like a fish he suspected of being undersized.

"First of the fleet," he said.

She came and stood behind him and went through her own dark hair at the temple, parting it with two fingers, methodical, until she found what she was looking for, and held it up beside his: one grey hair, then a second.

"Two," she said. "I'm winning."

He watched her in the mirror while she said it — watched the relief come up in her face, plain as tide — and she watched him watch, and saw him understand what she had been carrying: the old coast-wife's tale about marked women and the slow years, the fourth-hand stories of Resonators who buried three husbands looking thirty, the question she had been putting to her own hairline for a decade. Two grey hairs. She was aging at the speed of her husband. The sea had issued its actuarial table and the rider read: *same voyage, same weather, both of you.*

Matteo took her hand, the one holding the evidence.

"We'll frame them," he said. "Both of ours. His and hers."

"You're an idiot."

"I'm insured," he said, and pulled her down to sit against him, and outside the window the tide turned exactly when she'd known it would, for the two of them at the same hour, in the same dark, at the same speed.

## coda. The Game
### *Ragunna, the Square*

Evening, and the ring of glossy stones had been chalked into territories by five children with strong opinions.

"You were Maiden yesterday."

"I called it."

"You *always* call it." The girl doing the objecting was eight, and marked — the coil showed at her wrist when she jabbed her finger — and she had, by the game's own logic, the strongest claim to the role: the Maiden was a Resonator, everyone knew, and Bice was the only Resonator present. Which was exactly why she wanted the other part. "Fine. Keep it. I'm the guard."

The boy blinked under his chalk-and-string laurel. "The guard just gets rescued."

"The guard's the best one." Bice took up position at the ring's edge, invisible spear planted, chin high, and Tebaldo, at his window above the square with his supper going cold, found he had stopped chewing. "The guard *stands there.* With a monster coming. That's braver than having a magic sword. Anybody can be brave with a magic sword."

The boy considered this with the gravity of a casting director. Then he raised his stick, went up on his toes, made his eyes wrong the way the older kids had taught him, and delivered, in the fought-for role, its single line:

*"Run."*

They both ran — that was the amendment the children had voted in, seasons ago, an improvement on history: in their version everyone ran together, saint and guard and all, shrieking, around the ring of dark stones and out the far side into the ordinary streets, where the chalk gave out and the game dissolved into supper-time and the long argument about tomorrow's casting began before this round's dust had settled.

At the flower stall, a young woman in an acolyte's plain grey — old prayer beads wound twice around her wrist, worn smooth — watched them go. She bought one white camellia, and set it on the stones beside the one already lying there, and stood a moment in the blue evening, a person adding her coin to a collection whose purpose the whole square had privately agreed on and never once discussed.

Then she went home too, through a city full of weather, all of it argued over, all of it survived, most of it — on the good evenings, in the right light — shared.

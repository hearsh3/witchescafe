## I. 讀 — The Reader
### *Mingting, the Bureau of Rites, third courtyard*

The Office of Character Revision has seven readers, and the seven are the approved count, and Ruan Shu is the eighth.

The seven hold the seven categories — the classics, the histories, the codes, the almanacs, the memorials, the liturgies, the songs. Each reader keeps a lacquer tray of slips, and each slip carries one character in the wild: the sentence it stood in, the page, the date, the seal of whatever office let it out the door. Three slips keep a character alive for a reign. The tally is done in the fourth month. The striking is done at the solstice, so that the new standard can go to the founders before the spring casting.

The eighth reader keeps the remainder. Words that belong to no category. Words that turned up in a receipt.

Her desk stands in the third courtyard under a persimmon tree that has been dying for nine years with great commitment, and the leaves come down on her trays in autumn, and she picks them off one at a time, and the picking is the only part of the season she looks forward to.

Ruan Shu reads with her hands bare. This is her Forte, and the examiners tested it thoroughly and rated it *morphological, inert,* and entered her under the column that holds gifts the wall has no use for. She lays a palm flat on a page and the number arrives the way a temperature arrives: how many times the page has been read. A number, plain, with no face on it.

A memorial that went to the Emperor and came back: read eleven times. A tax notice pasted in a village square: four hundred, four hundred and one, a village standing in front of it in the rain. Her own appointment paper, which she has kept in her sleeve for six years: two. Her, twice. She has looked for a third every year and found the same two.

The Chief Reader came down the gallery at the ninth stroke with the wanting-list in his hand and set it on her tray without breaking stride, which was his way of saying good morning.

"Four hundred and twelve," she said, palm on the sheet. "It's been read four hundred and twelve times."

"It's been copied four hundred and twelve times." He took the persimmon leaf off the corner of her inkstone and dropped it in the brazier. "Copying is reading with a debt."

"Which one is mine?"

"Fourteen." He waited while she found it. "The rest are yours also. Fourteen is the one you'll want to sit down for."

She read the line. 魂. Marked *wanting* one reign ago, by a reader six years dead, in a hand she knew from a hundred trays.

"Citations."

"None on file."

"In a country of seven cities."

"In a country of seven cities and eleven ministries and four hundred and twelve years of paper." He put his hands in his sleeves. "The Ministry of Sentinel Affairs writes *residual behavioral anomalies.* The war college writes *morale factor.* The Bureau of Works writes *civic infrastructure* and files the whole matter under the roof it happens to be holding up. Rites writes 神, which is a different animal, and the Censorate writes nothing at all, and the Academy—" He stopped, because he was a precise man. "The Academy has tenure."

"Somebody says it."

"Everybody says it. Saying is weather." He looked out at the persimmon tree. "Bring me writing."

Ruan Shu turned the slip over. The blank side of a slip is where a reader puts what the tally has no box for, and hers was full of six years of it: a character saved from a lullaby, a character saved from a fishmonger's chalkboard, a character struck because she found it eleven days late and the founders had already melted the standard.

Eleven saved. Six years. She keeps the eleven in a separate tray and does not show it to anyone, and everyone in the office knows where it is.

"Travel," she said.

"Approved."

"You haven't heard the route."

"I've read the route. It's the only route." He was already going. "Sundry, shrine. Keep the receipts, and eat something on the road that a doctor would object to."

She had the travel warrant in her hand before she understood that he had signed it the previous evening.

His own family name was struck in the reign before last. The founders cut no sort; the presses set the nearest sound; the seal in his sash says one thing and his brush says another, and he signs with the seal, on time, in the correct ink, and once a year at the tomb-sweeping he writes the true one on a paper strip and burns it, which the office has decided, collectively and without discussion, to be a private matter concerning ash.

## II. 數 — The Count
### *Mingting, the Censorate, the basement*

The corridor has no doors, and the lamps are changed on the hour by attendants in soft shoes, and the mirror at the midpoint shows a person their own face at the exact center of the passage.

Ruan Shu passed the mirror without slowing, because she had a warrant and a satchel and the specific hurry of a woman with fifty-one days, and stopped four paces on and came back, because the mirror was bronze and mounted and old, and she wanted to know.

She put her palm on it.

"That won't work," said the agent behind her. "It isn't a page."

"Everything's a page."

"What did it say?"

"Four thousand and something. It's warm." She wiped her hand on her sleeve. "You've all been reading it."

Qiu Han considered her. Thirty-four, ISA, high collar boned into a courtesy, a face correctly maintained. "Rites is upstairs."

"The tally isn't."

"Which tally."

"The rhyme." Ruan Shu produced the warrant. "Seven cities, seven gates. Third verse. The Emperor's office told your service to count the ones who changed the last word, and your service has been counting for forty years. I want the count."

"The count is a suppression instrument."

"The count is a word list. I read word lists." She held the warrant steady. "Forty years of children telling you what they think this country is, one syllable at a time, and you file it under public order."

Qiu Han's mouth stayed clean. She turned it over — the request, the warrant, the woman — and found the flavor of the whole thing unremarkable, which was itself remarkable, and led the way to the third-division reading room and pulled the ledger and set it on the table and opened it, and the two of them stood over forty years of the last word of a children's rhyme.

喚. *Call.* The oldest, and the standard, and the one the Censorate had petitioned twice to suppress.
還. *Return.* Sixty-one years ago, a flood season, three provinces at once.
完. *End.* Twice, both times in the two winters after the north face went bad.
換. *Change.* The largest single season on record, and the ledger's hand goes cramped through it, the way a hand goes when it is writing more than it was funded to write.

And this autumn's return, entered in the current quarter, in a clerk's round unhurried script:

魂. Two hundred and thirteen instances. Three provinces. Attested at ward schools, two ferry landings, one persimmon market, and the queue outside a Mengzhou teahouse on an opera night.

Ruan Shu put her hand flat on the page.

"Four," she said.

Qiu Han looked at her.

"Forty years. It's been read four times." She lifted her palm and set it down again, to be sure, the way you check a fever twice. "Four."

The lamp at the corridor's mouth was changed. The soft shoes went past and receded and the room held the sound of a building doing its work on the hour.

"The counting is the point," Qiu Han said. "The reading was never budgeted."

"You gathered forty years of evidence that your people are alive and put it in a drawer."

"We gathered it. It's in a drawer. It survived." She closed the ledger with two fingers. "You are standing in the only building in Huanglong where a thing like this could survive forty years, and you are complaining about the ventilation."

Ruan Shu took that. She was a reader; she knew what it cost to say a true thing in the room the true thing was about.

"Certified extract," she said. "Current quarter. One line."

"The line names the schools."

"Then I want the line without the schools."

Qiu Han drew a fresh sheet. She copied the quarter's entry — the character, the count, the provinces — and left the ward schools out, and the ferry landings out, and the teahouse out, and put her chop on it, and wrote beneath the chop, in the smaller hand she kept for the inch of real estate that was hers:

*Extracted at the Office of Character Revision's request. The omitted particulars are held under seal and will be produced on the order of a court, which will not be applied for.*

She tasted the sentence on her way to the filing window. Copper. The truth in its traveling clothes, walking to work.

At the mirror, on the way back, Ruan Shu stopped again, and this time Qiu Han stopped with her, and the two of them stood at the midpoint of the passage looking at two competent faces in polished bronze.

"Four thousand," Qiu Han said. "You said something."

"Four thousand and eleven."

"Mm." She adjusted her collar. "Eleven of those are today. It's been a long autumn."

## III. 到 — Present
### *Jinzhou, the youngest gate*

She came in on the noon shuttle between a crate of persimmons and the mail, and the city smelled of fish and lake water and something faintly scorched that turned out to be the ward schools drying tea.

The children were singing on the wall stair. Seven stairs to the cistern arch, the high road for the harbor wards, the low road past the pit, Gran says hi, the wind says hello. They sang it going up and they sang it going down and a boy of about nine conducted with a stick of sugarcane and corrected a girl's footing on the fourth stair, because the song is a route and the route has a left one that's broke.

Ruan Shu stood at the bottom of the stair with her satchel and listened to a city teach itself how to survive, in tune, at a run.

At the south gate the garrison was doing the returning.

A sortie had come in at dawn. Eight towers had beaten as the column passed beneath, tower by tower, and the count was the victory. Then the roll, at the gate, in the ordinary noon light, with the fishmongers going past behind it: a sergeant with a book, reading names.

The living answered for themselves.

At the fourth name from the end the sergeant read and waited, and the whole formation answered together.

"到."

*Present.*

Three more names. Three more answers, from ninety throats, unhurried, the way you say a thing that has a correct volume.

Then the book closed and the formation broke and the men went for their noodles, and Ruan Shu stood by the gate post with her hand on the stone and her slips in the satchel, because a spoken word is weather, and the Office cites the hand.

The gate post had been read six times. She checked twice, and got six twice, and stood there working out what six meant on a stone that four hundred thousand people have walked past — that six of them, in the whole life of the youngest gate, had stopped and read the carving over the arch all the way to the end.

"Mingting."

"Tactical value: nil. Archival value: I am going to have to invent a phrase. Do you know what our records office would give for you?"

"I'm at the Bureau of Rites."

"I'll write *on loan.*" Stamp. "There. You count."

Ruan Shu looked at the paper. Her name in a box, in a city she had been in for four hours, in a rank the capital had spent a year declining to adopt.

She put her hand flat on it before she thought about it.

One. Herself, just now.

Keeper Pei trimmed lamps along the crest wall at the second watch and talked while he worked and let her walk beside him, because the wall's oldest custom is cheap and he had forty-one years of it.

"Character," she said. "The field on your night form."

"Weather field." He thumbed a wick. "East. West. Gusty. Steady. Nasty, if it's nasty."

He set the glass back on the lamp. He took his time about it, and the flame came up clean and stood straight, and he looked at the standing of it the way another man looks at a photograph.

"Third day of the ninth month," he said.

"You know the date."

"I know the date."

She waited. The lake lay black below them, holding the lamps.

"There was a wind that leaned on this lamp for three years," Pei said. "Northeast shoulder. Every night, and I trimmed it, and I logged the trimming as maintenance, because a keeper logs everything and maintenance is what fits in the box." He moved to the next post. "You tell a lingering wind your dead. That's the custom. I told it my brother — the whole man, the debts, the snoring, the puppet show he cried at and blamed on dust. Took three watches. "The lamp stood straight." He worked the wick. "You log the change."

"The form says *character of the wind.*"

"Mm."

"You wrote 魂."

"That's what the weather was." He set the glass. "It was the word that fit the box."

Ruan Shu reached for a slip. Then she put both hands on the parapet and asked to see the log, and Pei sent a runner and the runner came back with the ninth-month book, and she laid it open on the parapet under the lamp that had learned to stand straight.

She put her palm on the page.

"Once," she said. "The night desk clerk, filing it."

"That'd be right."

"You've never read your own log."

"I wrote it." Pei shrugged, forty-one years of shrug. "Reading it's somebody else's job. That's how you know the wall's working."

So she read it to him. Out loud, on the crest wall, at the second watch: the weather, the wind, the character, the note about the northeast shoulder holding steady without trimming, the hour, the seal.

Pei listened with his hands still, which for him was a bow.

"Twice, now," he said.

"Twice."

"Put that in your book too."

## IV. 榜 — The Notice
### *Xuanfang, the village at the base of the fifth pillar*

The Mengzhou road comes down through cloud and bamboo, and the bridges rock on the wind, and one copper crosses the ford, and the toll collector's daughter has arms like a boatman and a bad opinion of the capital's coinage.

Old Ge was on the bench at the crossroads. The bench faces all four directions and has been worn to a shine by nine generations of people sitting on it while the country happened to them.

"The capital says one thing," he said, before she had introduced herself. "The gate says another. The fox says a third. I have a bench. Sit."

She sat.

He gave her tea out of a pot that had been on the stone beside him long enough to be lukewarm and correct, and let her drink half of it, and then looked at her satchel and her ink-stained cuff and the government paper corner sticking out.

"Words," he said.

"Words."

"You'll want the tree."

The prayer-tree at the village shrine leans. It has leaned since the summer the paste went to six layers, and the shrine-keeper petitions annually for the removal of administrative paste from the sacred surface, and the frontier court denies it annually, and the denial is a sentence the village can recite: *the tree is infrastructure, the paste is the argument, the argument is the country.*

The seventh layer had gone up in the autumn. Ruan Shu walked around the trunk twice reading it, the way you read a wall in a foreign city.

The Censorate's notice, first and lowest and mostly gone.

General Jiyan's recruitment poster, curling.

A ballad broadsheet out of Bookbinders' Row.

Three market advertisements, one for a dairyman's cheese, aged wheel, mountain inn approved.

A child's drawing of a dragon, provenance unknown, quality high.

A prayer tab reading *stop pasting on the tree.*

And over the whole northeast face, fresh, the Ministry of Sentinel Affairs' notice of retirement with honors, third generation, forty units, printed on the Row in a good clear face, each unit under the name its garrison actually used — Old Seven, Kettle, the Widow, Number Four Who Snores — and the notice headed, in the standard funerary formula that the Ministry has used for four hundred years without once being asked to explain it:

忠魂。

*The loyal souls.*

Ruan Shu stood in front of it for a while with her hands at her sides.

"That one'll fail you," Old Ge said, from the bench.

"It's a Ministry printing."

"It's a phrase. You could put that phrase on a boot." He drank his tea. "A word that only comes out at funerals is a word in a coffin. Your office knows the difference. That's the whole of what your office knows."

She looked at him.

"My son was a clerk," Old Ge said. "Frontier court. Twelve years. He came home talking like that and I've had to live with it." He gestured with the cup at the trunk. "Go round the back. Under the fourth layer. There's a corner sticking out, and don't tear it, my wife pasted the layer over it on purpose."

Behind the tree, low, where the bark curves away from the path, a small square of paper had been protected from six subsequent authorities by a market advertisement pasted deliberately short.

Eleven years of weather had been kind to it. The hand was careful, and slow, and formed each stroke the way a person forms strokes when the only writing they have ever studied is a tax notice — the columns ruled by eye, the headings copied from memory, the whole grief laid out in the one grammar its author had been taught to trust:

> **REQUISITION.**
> *Item: one soul (魂). My mother's.*
> *Quantity: one.*
> *Present location: unknown.*
> *Requested: return.*
> *Justification: she is owed it. She carried mine eleven years past when she could.*
> *Applicant:* ■■■
> *Approved by:* ______

Ruan Shu crouched in the leaf litter with her hand a finger's width off the paper for longer than the job required.

Then she touched it.

The count came up and kept coming. It went past the highest number she had taken off any page in six years, and past twice that, and settled somewhere she stopped counting, and she took her hand back and sat down in the dirt behind a leaning tree in a village of seventy people and put her wrist across her eyes.

Everybody who passed the shrine went round the back. Eleven years. The whole village, on the way to the well, reading a woman's requisition for her mother.

"Auntie Cui," Old Ge said, when she came back to the bench. "Second house past the well. She's sixty-six and her knees are bad and she'll offer you melon whether or not you have time for melon."

Ruan Shu wrote out the slip on the stone bench with her block and her brush, and she wrote the text in full, and the date from the court register, and the disposition of the shrine-keeper's petition that made the paste a preserved surface under the court's own order.

In the provenance box she wrote: *unknown.*

Old Ge watched her do it. He poured the rest of the pot into her cup.

"The court's ruling holds the paste," he said. "The paste holds the tree. The tree holds the shrine. The shrine holds the village."

"And the village?"

"Pastes." He settled back into the shine his family had worn into the stone. "Nine generations. Somebody has to."

## V. 虛 — The Hollow
### *Mengzhou, Bookbinders' Row, and a terrace above the frontier court*

The founders' shop smells of hot metal and cold oil and the particular sourness of a trade that has been arguing with itself since before movable type.

"We'd cut it," the master founder said. "Take an afternoon. "Then it goes." He wiped his hands. "The old sorts wear on. Thirty years, maybe forty, the ones we have. After that you set a blank."

"A blank."

"Full-body space, the width of the character." He said it the way a mason says *lintel.* "The Row's set blanks for four hundred years. Ruan Shu looked at the case of blanks. A whole tray of nothing, sorted by width.

"That's worse," she said.

The founder laughed once, short, the way metal cools. "That's the trade."

She held out the paper twist. Ruan Shu took a hawthorn and ate it, and it was very sour and then very sweet, in that order, which she understood to be the point.

"One question," Hsin said. "Your rule. The striking rule. What does your Bureau do when it says no?"

Ruan Shu thought about it.

"It writes it down."

"Mm," said the Sentinel of Mengzhou, and lay back on the warm stones, and let the evening go on happening.

## VI. 駁 — The Refusal
### *Mingting, the third courtyard, the solstice*

She came back with six slips and fifty-one days spent and put them on the Chief Reader's desk in the order she wanted them read, which he ignored, because he had been doing this for thirty years.

He took the Academy footnote first and set it aside without comment. He took Xiangli Yao's denial, read it twice, and put it on the same pile.

"He wrote around it."

"He did."

"A careful man." The Chief Reader almost smiled. "Careful men leave this office a clean desk and an empty archive."

He read the Ministry's retirement notice and marked it *formula, fossil* and put it on the pile, and Ruan Shu watched forty units go, Old Seven and Kettle and the Widow and Number Four Who Snores, and kept her hands where they were.

The garrison log he accepted, and stamped, and set to his right.

The requisition on the tree he read all the way through twice, and then he sat back, and the persimmon leaves came down on the desk between them, and he took a long time with the second reading.

"Provenance unknown."

"Provenance unknown."

"You went there."

"I sat on a bench."

He put his chop on it and set it beside the garrison log. Two.

Then he took the guild fines.

He read the schedule of them, twelve entries, dates and amounts and the offending text quoted in full in the Ministry's hand as the appeal rules require, and he read the name at the head of each one.

"This costs her."

"She asked me to file it."

"They always do." He set it down. "That is what makes it a cost. Do you know how many people have told me to use their name?"

"No."

"Nine." He picked his brush back up. "Six of them stopped being able to work in their trade. Two were fine. One of them is running a court in Xuanfang." He held the brush over the slip, and the Bureau of Rites did its work in the third courtyard under a dying tree while the light went.

He stamped it. Three.

"You have three," he said. "It stands."

Ruan Shu breathed out, and the office was very quiet, and then the Chief Reader turned the guild-fines slip over and read the cost line at the bottom, which she had entered in the correct box in the correct ink, and his brush stopped.

"Half a month of guild revenue," he read. "Line item: sundry, shrine."

"That's what she said."

He looked at it for a while.

"That's the whole country," he said. "That line. Every honest office in Huanglong keeps its soul under sundry, shrine, and the Ministry of Development denies the shelving." He put the slip down. "You went out there for fifty-one days and brought me back the accounting."

"You knew I would."

"I hoped." He gathered the accepted three and squared them. "I have struck eleven hundred characters, reader. Do you know how many I have saved?"

Ruan Shu waited.

"Fourteen," he said. "This is the fourteenth."

He wrote the disposition and sealed it and put it in the tray for the courier.

There were two sealed papers in the tray when the courier came. He took both, because a courier takes the tray. One of them came back at the third watch with the Office's own docket on it, marked *withdrawn,* and was filed, as withdrawn things are, in the same drawer as the rest of the evidence.

The entry stood. It was the solstice. The founders would cast in the spring.

---

She was at the gate with her satchel when the thing arrived at her, the way things arrive: sideways, at the wrong moment, in the middle of putting on a coat.

She stopped in the archway.

*I gave no such lecture. The Office is welcome to cite the denial.*

*What does your Bureau do when it says no?*

*A fine that doesn't quote the offense is a fine you can't appeal.*

*An entry struck is struck with its evidence appended in full.*

She stood in the archway of the Bureau of Rites with one arm in her coat and worked it through twice, slowly, the way she checked a fever, and got the same number both times.

To strike 魂, the Office must publish the strike order. The strike order must append the evidence. The evidence is the character. The appendix carries the current date and the Office's seal. A dated, sealed, current-period government document, in a responsible hand, containing the character.

Which is a citation.

Which attests the entry.

Which stands.

Ruan Shu put her other arm in her coat.

Then she went back up the gallery and stood at the Chief Reader's desk, and he looked up at her over the wanting-list with the expression of a man who has been waiting all afternoon to be asked something.

"You knew," she said.

"For thirty years."

"You could have struck it at the last revision and reopened it with your own strike order."

"I could have."

"Why the fifty-one days?"

He set down his brush. Behind him the persimmon tree let go of another one.

"Because a word that survives on a technicality survives until somebody amends the technicality," he said. "And a word that survives because a keeper filled in a weather field, and a woman in Xuanfang requisitioned her mother back, and a puppet-maker paid twelve fines out of a shrine budget — that word is in the country, and you would have to take it out of the country." He picked the brush back up. "Article Four is a fire door. You use it once. I have been saving it for something I could not save any other way."

"And my father's name?" She heard herself. "Your name."

"My name was one hand," the Chief Reader said. "One family, one province, one hand. Nobody wrote it down but us." He drew the next line of the wanting-list toward him. "Go home, reader. Eat something on the way that a doctor would object to."

She went back to the fifth pillar in the spring, because the court had asked for a certified copy and because Old Ge had a bench.

The tree had gone to eight layers over the winter. The eighth was the new standard's entry, cut out of a Row printing and pasted at eye height by somebody who had wanted the village to be able to read it without going round the back.

She went round the back anyway.

The requisition was where it had been, under its market advertisement, eleven springs and a winter of weather, the columns ruled by eye, the grammar of a tax notice carrying a woman's whole grief up a mountain.

*Approved by:* ______

The box had been filled in.

The ink was brown at the edges and smelled faintly of candied fruit, and the strokes ended in small blunt curves where a brush would have tapered, and it held one character, and the character was the one ninety throats say at the south gate of Jinzhou in the ordinary noon light when a sergeant reads a name and waits.

到.

*Present.*

Ruan Shu crouched in the leaf litter for a while. Then she took out her block and her brush and wrote a slip, because she was a reader and the fourth month was coming, and in the provenance box she wrote *unknown,* and in the margin, which is the one inch of real estate the corridor permits, she wrote:

*The applicant's mother is accounted for. Filed under weather.*

---

On the shuttle down she took her appointment paper out of her sleeve, the way she had at the turn of every year for six years, and laid her palm on it.

Three.

She held it in her lap the whole way to the ford. The pillars went by on the left, and the bridges swayed between them on a wind out of the gorge, and the toll collector's daughter took her copper and gave her the arms and the opinion, and Ruan Shu turned the paper over twice and found what she had found at nineteen: an office, a seal, and one countersignature in a hand that goes slightly uphill, which she has been looking at for six years and could draw from memory and cannot read.

Somebody had read it. This year, once, somebody had read it all the way to the bottom.

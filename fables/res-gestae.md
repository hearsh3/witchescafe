# Res Gestae

The brick in its grey flannel wrap still held the last of the bread-oven heat, tucked into the hollow between Iuno’s left shoulder-blade and the bolster so the frost coming through the salt-house chinking wouldn't settle in the cracked crystal.

Inch by inch, since the lamps went out, Amy had worked her way sideways across the middle of the kang. She now lay diagonal across two quilts with her chin tilted up, the tin fox-whistle resting outside her jumper on its red string, and one bent knee planted squarely against Iuno’s hip. Beyond her, Mei was a dark mound under the blanket with only the crown of her black hair showing.

Inside Iuno’s folded coat by the wall, her Terminal gave a low, muffled buzz against the dented flank of her tea flask.

Iuno opened her eyes in the dark. She reached out with her right hand, slid her fingers into the coat fold, and drew the glass an inch clear of the wool.

**AUGUSTA** sat in white letters across the middle of the screen.

Iuno let the breath out of her nose, slipped the Terminal into her cuff, and began the business of getting upright with one arm. She braced her heel against the warm brick platform, levered her shoulder off the pillow—the crystal gave a tiny, dry *tink* against the whitewashed plaster—and caught Amy’s lax right wrist between two fingers just before it flopped down onto the bare spot. She laid the wrist carefully on Amy’s stomach. Amy snuffled once, muttered something about a spanner, and turned her cheek into Chisa’s shoulder.

Getting off the outer edge was worse. Lupa slept along the stove-flue in her new rust-red coat like a felled timber, and her tail had drifted out from under the hem across the only clear foot of brick. Iuno stood on one stockinged foot, hooked the toe of her boot round the warm flannel brick to drag it with her, and stepped wide over the tail. Her ankle wobbled; her knee bumped the upturned charging crate by the door.

Behind her, the quilt rustled.

"Iuiu?" Mei’s voice was thick with sleep, pitched low into the collar of her blanket.

"Go back to sleep, darling." Iuno got her boots on without lacing them and scooped her coat over her right shoulder.

Mei propped herself on one elbow. In the dim red glow from the stove-grate, her eyes caught the light like two coins at the bottom of a dish. "Your brick’s gonna get cold out there."

"It will keep for ten minutes."

"You want my coat too?"

"I have mine. Lie down before you let the draft in under the quilt."

Mei didn't lie down. She reached across the empty depression where Iuno had been, pulled the corner of Iuno’s blanket over the warm spot to hold the heat in, and stayed up on her elbow watching the door.

Iuno lifted the iron latch with her thumb, lifted the warped pine door by the frame so the bottom sill wouldn't scrape, and shoved it outward.

The night air hit her throat like well-water. A thin, white gorge mist had crept up over the lower terraces since supper, turning the frost on the flagstones dull and fuzzy under the lopsided moon. Across the empty yard, beside the well-head, the cabbage-seller’s trestle stood bare except for Hsin’s red-ruled ledger, weighted down against the night wind with a flat river stone.

Iuno sat on the low cedar step under the salt-house eaves, where the overhang and the chimney breast cut the wind. She wedged the flannel-wrapped brick tight under her bare crystal shoulder, pulled her coat over her knees with her right hand, and tapped the green key.

***

The picture steadied on a corner of oiled oak and the yellow pool of a double-wicked tallow lamp.

Augusta sat in her linen shirtsleeves at the window table of her house above the old forum, her collar unpinned and her heavy iron-grey hair braided back rough for the night. Through the dark casement behind her elbow, three hundred feet down the hill, a night-watchman’s horn lantern swung slowly past the wet steps of the Rostra and vanished behind the wool-hall.

Augusta had a pair of square, wire-rimmed spectacles perched low on the crook of her nose. The instant Iuno’s face came up on the glass, Augusta snatched them off with her left hand and dropped them behind the ink-stand as if they belonged to someone else.

"You’re outside," Augusta said.

"The salt-house is full," Iuno said, tucking her chin into her collar. "Twenty people on one brick platform makes for poor acoustics. Why are you awake?"

"The wind came round into the south at vespers," Augusta said, shifting her left leg straight out under the table with a slight grimace. "Came through the casement lead and set my hip singing. Put your arm in that sleeve, Mouse. You’ve got it draped over your neck like a bath-towel."

"Wait until I’ve settled the brick." Iuno worked her right arm down the dark wool sleeve one-handed, hitched the left shoulder of the coat over the crystal and the flannel parcel together, and rested her wrist on her knee. Behind Augusta’s chair, on the braided rug before the cold hearth, Cub’s brindled flank rose and fell in a long, whistling snore, his chin hooked over one of Augusta’s unlaced boots.

"Did the smith fix the hinge on the garden gate?" Iuno asked.

"Took the whole post off on Thursday and wedged it with sheet-lead," Augusta said. "And the winter blow tore three laths off the western rose trellis. I spent an hour before dusk tying the canes back with copper wire so they wouldn't whip the shutters." She reached for a pewter mug beside the lamp, drank, and set it down. "The fullers’ guild petitioned the Senate yesterday to roof over the lower butter-market."

"They petition every autumn when the rain starts," Iuno said. "If you let them put a timber roof over that gutter, they’ll hang wet wool from the rafters by the winter solstice and the butter-wives will be selling tallow in the street."

"I told them to clean their own drains first," Augusta said.

She stopped. Her eyes went past the edge of the Terminal to the rest of the oak table.

Even in the narrow frame of the lens, Iuno could see the corners of them: three square Temple muniment boxes bound in split pigskin, their lids tied down with faded blue sanctuary tape; a yard-long roll of thin tracing-paper standing upright in a brass umbrella-jar; and, directly in front of Augusta’s elbows, a fat, square-backed ledger bound in oiled canvas of the sort the kitchen stewards used for the winter salt-meat.

"Rose brought them up three nights ago," Augusta said.

Iuno’s fingers stilled on her knee. "All of them?"

"Wheeled them up the hill from the postern herself in the bakehouse handcart," Augusta said. "Two of the gate-orderlies went down the steps to take the shafts off her, and she told them if they laid a greasy gauntlet on her boxes she’d report them to the sergeant for loitering. Brought the cart right through the front hall onto the rugs."

"Did she say anything?"

"Wiped her hands on her apron," Augusta said, "looked at the boxes on my table, and said, *'I’ve scrubbed the shelves they came off of, Ephor. I’m not putting them back. Have the boy bring my barrow down before baking-day.'*"

Augusta looked at the canvas ledger between her hands. After a second, she reached behind the ink-stand, picked up the wire-rimmed spectacles with an irritable grunt, and hooked them back over her ears.

"I’ve been through the boxes twice," Augusta said. "Some of it three times."

She opened the canvas cover. The pages inside were ruled in Rose Marigold’s broad, upright caretaker’s hand—no flourishes, the ink dark and even, each entry numbered in the left margin with a carpenter’s pencil. Augusta turned the Terminal half an inch and tilted the book toward the lens so Iuno could read the first leaf for herself.

> *Muniment room, upper press. Seventeen folios of official readings, sheep-parchment. All outer margins scraped with a pumice-stone while the ink was old. I read those margins myself when I swept the tower in the mornings, and I know they were full, and I cannot call back a word of them. In the oldest book, at the foot of the harbor-tariff leaf, one downstroke in black gall remains.*

Below it, separated by a ruled line:

> *Cellar under the Corn-Exchange, behind the broken scales. Three pine tally-boards, chalked and varnished. Retail price of the bolted loaf in the third district for the hard winter after the Silva forfeiture. Entered in the Senate minutes as the Ephor’s bounty. The warrant-clerk’s line at the foot is blank.*

Augusta lowered the book flat to the oak. "The Senate voted me a silver grain-measure that spring for feeding the third ward," she said. "I thought old Quintus in the commissariat had let the military stores out early to keep the weevils out of the bins."

"Quintus couldn't have found the key to the lower bins with two lanterns and a dog," Iuno said. "And the tally-board is wrong. It wasn't bolted wheat."

Augusta looked up over the spectacles.

"The harbour-wardens seized four flat-bottomed barges of canal-barley off the salt-marsh because the factor had forged the customs lead," Iuno said, her voice dropping into its dry, level register. "It was sprout-damaged at the bottom of the hold. I had it carted up to the third-district ovens by night and told the bakers that if they didn't grind it twice with the white mill-sweepings and sell it for a single copper, the augurs would condemn their flues for fire-risk. If the Senators had eaten a crust of that bread, they’d have broken their back teeth."

"Rose tied the bakery receipts in the second box," Augusta said, glancing down at the ledger. "Under *Housekeeping and Small Alms.*"

*Housekeeping,* the High Priestess said from the back of Iuno’s mind. *I ruined three grain-speculators between the full moon and the new, and she files me behind the tallow-chandler.*

Augusta turned four pages together. The paper made a stiff, dry rustle in the microphone. She pulled the middle pigskin box toward her, slipped the blue tape off the brass hasp without untying Rose’s knot, and lifted out a narrow book bound in blackened tallow-leather.

She read the slip pinned to the cover aloud:

> *Upper Mews, harness-room chest. Master Brasca’s flight books, greased leather, sewn with twine.Rider, mount, blessed by, returned. In all seasons between Hortensia’s leaving and the great water, the third column is empty except for one autumn entered to Sister Lucilla. Ink dry and unbroken. Note in Brasca’s hand on the inside board.*

Augusta laid her broad palm flat on the tallow-leather without opening the pages. "Brasca wrote on the board that he went behind the dung-wall after the spring muster to look for his horn-rasp," she said. "Found the lady in blue standing under the water-spout with three clipped grey primary feathers in her left fist, squeezed so hard the split quills had gone into the ball of her thumb. He wrote that when he coughed, she turned round and asked him if the hill-oats had been screened for grit."

Iuno looked at the frosted rim of the well across the yard.

Augusta reached into the brass umbrella-jar, drew out the long roll of thin tissue, and spread one end of it across the ledger, weighting the corners with her pewter mug and the ink-stand. Across the translucent paper ran scores of dark heel-ball rubbings taken off curved brass—rough, square letters cut with a graver that had slipped more than once on the solder-joints.

"Rose took a ladder down into the cistern," Augusta said. "Rubbed all three rings of the water-clock."

She didn't read the names out. Her blunt forefinger moved slowly across the tissue, stopping near the shadow of a rivet-hole.

"You put Nella on the inner harbour-ring," Augusta said. "Right where the third lamp hits the brass at high water. Her brother still has the flat-boat at Lock Three. When I rode down the towpath last week, he came up the bank with his cap in his hand and asked me why his sister’s grey tiercel was entered in the mews book with nobody’s name beside the oil-mark."

Augusta’s finger stayed on the paper. She looked up at the glass.

"When you came down to the stirrup-block with the horn cup," Augusta said, "and wished us a clean sky. How long before the horn blew did you know which saddles were coming back across the mules?"

The mist from the gorge drifted under the salt-house eaves, smelling of wet limestone and coal-ash. Under her coat, Iuno’s right hand closed on the grey flannel around the brick until the wool gave against her knuckles. She kept her eyes on Augusta’s face in the lamplight and did not say a word.

Augusta watched her for the space of three slow breaths. Then she lifted the ink-stand, let the tissue roll itself back up with a soft snap, and laid it beside the boxes.

"First box," Augusta said.

She drew a small square pasteboard case out of the top tier and took off the lid. Inside lay a split oak tally-leaf, black with canal-silt, and two small rolls of grey sheet-lead.

She read from Rose’s book:

> *Fourth Sea-Lock, dredge-clerk’s box. One oak tally-leaf from the night of the high water: lower portcullis dropped before the glass turned, fish-quay and curing-sheds outside the sill. The line for the magistrate’s warrant is clean wood. Below it, two strips of sheet-lead taken from the mud in the same bucket.*

"The first one’s wrapped round a copper piece," Augusta said. "From Clodia the herb-wife in the lane behind the rope-walk. Says her little girl was fetching leeks on the dry side of the gate when the iron came down, and promises two honey-cakes every autumn to the lady in the blue robe."

Augusta picked up the second piece of lead between her thumb and forefinger. An iron boat-nail had been driven straight through the middle of the folded metal, its point rusted into a hook on the underside.

"The other one’s nailed," Augusta said, and reached to put it back in the pasteboard box.

"Read it," Iuno said.

Augusta’s hand stopped over the box. "Mouse."

"Read it to me, Augusta."

Augusta looked at her over the wire rims. Then she turned the pierced lead toward the tallow lamp, squinted at the cramped scratches Rose had transcribed on the lining-paper of the lid, and read it out in the same flat, unhurried voice she used for the quartermaster’s returns.

"To the black silt under the sill I give the woman who dropped the iron while the lamps were burning in the gutting-sheds," Augusta read. "My mother had her apron full of salt-herring and her house-keys at her belt. Let the dark water take the hand that signed the bar, from the nail to the wrist, from the wrist to the shoulder. Let her call through the iron when the tide comes up and find the windlass spiked. Let her eat her bread among strangers and never know the roof over her head."

Augusta set the lead strip back in the box and put the lid on.

Under the salt-house coat, Iuno’s right hand came up once, touched the jagged edge of bare crystal at her left collar-bone where the cold had begun to creep through the flannel, and dropped back into her lap.

"She got the keys wrong," Iuno said quietly. "The gutting-sheds didn't have locks. They hung wooden latches on tarred twine."

Augusta turned the page of the canvas ledger without looking up.

> *Novices’ parlour, Tetragon Temple. Statement taken by hand from Sister Livia, formerly of House Silva, received into the house at eight years of age on the day of her father’s banishment. Signed in her own name.*

"She’s still in the dormitory," Augusta said. "Keeps the herb-accounts for Rose now. She told Rose she remembers two sanctuary drudges taking her red-bordered tunic off in the Colosseum passage and pinning her into a blue wool sack that smelled of lamp-oil, while her chin-cough rattled in her chest. And she remembers sitting at the bottom of the novices’ board every Tuesday and Friday for her first winter with chilblains on her knuckles, and a pair of small hands with burn-scars on the right wrist reaching over her shoulder to cut up her salt-pork and mash her lentils before the bell rang. She can’t hold the face above the collar."

Augusta rested her thumb at the bottom of the entry.

"She asked Rose to pass on a question, if the book ever went anywhere," Augusta said. "She wrote: *Ask her whether she kept me on the hill so my mother wouldn't hire ships against the city, or because I had the winter cough.*"

Iuno looked down at the pale square of light on her knee. Her right thumb rubbed the scarred skin below her own wrist-bone once, back and forth, and stopped.

On the screen, Augusta didn't turn the next page.

Beside her left elbow on the oak table, where she had emptied her pockets after coming in from the garden, lay a six-inch offcut of dark copper wire from the rose trellis. Augusta reached into the bottom of the third pigskin box and brought up a dented lead cylinder the length of her forearm, tied round with a piece of rotten green silk ribbon.

She took her spectacles off and laid them on the open ledger. She rubbed the crooked bridge of her nose with two fingers, then pushed the canvas book forward so the lower entry filled the glass.

Iuno read it off the screen.

> *Found inside a lead cylinder wedged behind the masonry of the third aqueduct arch, above the old fuller’s drain. One surveyor’s warrant closing the walk, sealed with the Great Signet, no name beside the wax. With it, twelve leaves of rag-paper sewn in the foreign manner, being a dialogue in verse concerning the cistern, the Champion’s body, and the harbor boom. Damp-foxed at the foot.*

On the oak table beside the ink-stand, without Augusta’s hand coming within a span of it, the loose end of the copper garden wire lifted half an inch into the lamplight. Slowly, with a tiny metallic scrape against the wood, it began to curl itself around the brass foot of the candlestick.

"The fishermen brought the Champion’s buckler up from the bar," Augusta said to the tabletop. "I signed the warrant for four cords of cedar to burn her in the field before the tribes. I stood in the smoke until the greaves fell through the ash. We burned tarred sailcloth and three sacks of condemned bacon."

She raised her eyes to the screen.

"And that foreign poet had a barge waiting in the fuller’s culvert," Augusta said. "He knew what I heard under the chain at the harbour boom."

"You had one boot on the coping-stone," Iuno said. Her voice was very quiet in the frost. "And forty shields on the mole behind you were watching your back."

Augusta’s gaze dropped to the candlestick. She saw the copper wire coiling tight around the brass base, reached out, and clapped her broad palm flat over it until the metal clicked dead against the oak.

"You gave him Mya’s body," Augusta said, "and the vault under the third arch, so the barber-shops wouldn't hear that the Ephor listened to the sea-water when it called her by her mother’s name."

"Yes," Iuno said.

Neither of them spoke while the watchman’s lantern came back up the hill outside Augusta’s window, throwing a long lattice of shadow across the ceiling beams and sliding off down the wall. On the rug, Cub let out a muffled bark in his sleep and twitched one heavy paw against Augusta’s boot.

Augusta moved her hand off the dead copper wire. She tapped the flat portfolio wedged upright at the back of the first box.

"Rose put the eastern tower papers in the bottom there," she said. "The great diagram from behind your desk, and the disposition table at the foot of it, with the two words in the margin beside her name."

Then she reached under her own left wrist, where a small square of thin tracing-paper had been lying separate from the boxes all evening, and unfolded it with one hand. It was smudged with red amphitheatre chalk.

She didn't hold it up to the camera. She read Rose’s slip first, without the spectacles, holding it at arm’s length:

> *Colosseum, third tier above the Water-Gate, fourth bench from the rail. Cut into the travertine back with a small pen-knife, fourteen inches between two upright lines, and rubbed with red ochre by the cushion-men.*

Augusta looked down at the tracing-paper in her palm.

"'The Mouse’s seat,'" she read aloud. "'Keep your wet cloak off the stone or your pot will crack on the trivet.'"

She folded the paper back into a neat square, tucked it into the breast pocket of her linen shirt, and laid both hands flat on the table on either side of Rose’s closed ledger.

***

"Nobody else has seen them," Augusta said. "Not the Senate, not the lottery-auditor’s clerks, not Angel. Rose locked the muniment press and dropped the key in my beer-mug before she took her handcart home."

She looked straight into the lens.

"Tell me what you want done with it."

A gust of wind came up the gorge wall and shook a scatter of frozen rime off the salt-house eaves onto Iuno’s boots. Her right hand had begun to tremble slightly where it held the Terminal; she braced the lower casing against her knee to steady the picture.

"You are the Ephor, Augusta," Iuno said. "And the lottery-auditor’s desk is your magistracy now."

"The gaps aren't mine, Mouse," Augusta said, at the exact same volume.

In the quiet behind Iuno’s breastbone, the High Priestess uncrossed her ankles and leaned forward.

*Give the auditor the barley boards and the gryphon books, darling,* she said, reasonable and velvet-dry. *And drop the Fourth Lock and the sewer warrant down the bakehouse privy before dawn. Let them put up a small travertine tablet by the meal-market. We have walked out of that city on our own two feet once already; we do not owe the fishmongers an inventory.*

Iuno looked out across the yard at the flat river stone holding down Hsin’s ledger on the well-trestle.

*Or better still,* the High Priestess added, gentler now, *tell her to put all three boxes on the washhouse fire tonight while the house is asleep. If nobody in the seven hills remembers our name, nobody has a claim on our coat. We can buy the blue door, and feed the cat, and start on a clean leaf.*

*No,* Iuno thought.

She lifted her chin from her collar and looked at Augusta on the glass.

"Give it to the lottery-auditor," Iuno said. "All three boxes, the rubbings from the cistern, and Rose’s book. Put it into the public press whole—the barley, the muster rolls, the Fourth Sea-Lock, and the nailed lead with the herring-wife’s keys."

Augusta’s eyebrows drew together a fraction of an inch, though she didn't interrupt.

"For all those winters I scraped the margins clean and sent the fair copy down the stairs," Iuno said. "Nothing gets scraped out of this one. Where the clerk’s line is blank, leave it blank until we reach Septimont. When I walk through the gate, I will come up to the muniment room and write my name into the gaps myself, in my own hand."

She paused for a breath, her fingers tightening on the edge of the Terminal.

"I don't know whether the ink will hold once I’ve written it," she said plainly. "For all I know, the page will drink it like water before the sand is off it. I don't know what happens on Thursday before Thursday gets here anymore. I’ll bring a bottle of iron-gall and a steel nib and find out."

Augusta nodded once, slow and solid.

"Two things come out first," Iuno said. "Because they aren't mine to publish. Give Sister Livia’s statement back to Sister Livia in the dormitory, and let her choose whether it goes into the box or the stove. Tell her I will answer her question myself, on the steps of the eastern portico, when I can look at her face."

"And the other?"

"The lead cylinder from the third arch," Iuno said. "The lease and the poet’s playbook are about your ears at the harbour boom, Augusta. You decide what happens to that one."

"It goes in," Augusta said at once.

Iuno’s mouth twitched, barely a hair’s breadth, and settled again.

"And not before my own house," Iuno said. "Put the boxes under your bed and leave the blue tape on them until we come across the water. Mei and Amy and Lupa read them first, from my hand, at a table with the door shut, before a single clerk in the Curia unties a knot."

Across the sea, in the yellow lamplight above the dark forum, Augusta reached out and drew the first pigskin box an inch closer to her elbow, squaring it with the edge of the oak table.

"Bring a reed pen, not a steel nib," Augusta said. "That sheep-parchment in the upper press has tallow in the grain; a steel point catches and spatters. And go inside and eat something hot before you lie down, your hand’s rattling the glass."

"The stove is banked," Iuno said.

"Then drink some water." Augusta rested her mailed-scarred knuckles on the canvas cover of Rose’s book. Her eyes stayed on Iuno’s face for a moment longer than an order required. "I missed knowing whose fault the cheap barley was, Mouse."

Behind Augusta’s chair, the latch of the inner bedroom door clicked. Angel stood in the doorway in her dark woollen dressing-gown, her hair loose over one shoulder and her feet bare on the floorboards. She didn't look at the boxes or the Terminal; she walked two steps into the room, laid two warm fingers against the back of Augusta’s neck, and said, "The watch just changed at the Rostra, Gus. Come to bed before that hip sets."

Augusta looked at Iuno through the screen, tapped the oak table once with her knuckle by way of goodnight, and reached out to shut the line before Iuno could answer.

***

The glass went black in Iuno’s palm.

She sat still on the cedar step under the eaves for a minute, listening to the gorge wind move through the frozen cabbage stalks behind the storehouse. Under her coat, the flannel-wrapped brick had gone lukewarm against her crystal shoulder.

She stood up, hooked her toe under the brick to pick it up, and turned to the salt-house door. Lift, then shove.

Inside, the room was dark and thick with the smell of warm wool, rising dough, and twenty people breathing. From the heap of rust-red sheepskin by the flue came a low, gravelly mutter without Lupa lifting her head:

"Shut it quick, princess. Draft’s on the pan."

Iuno pulled the door home until the latch seated, set the cooled brick down against the warm brickwork of the chimney breast for the morning, and stepped back over Lupa’s tail.

On the kang, Mei hadn't gone back to sleep. She was still propped on her elbow in the dark, her hand resting on the quilt over the spot she had kept covered. She didn't ask who had called, or why Iuno’s boots were crusted with rime. She lifted the corner of the blanket six inches and shifted back toward the wall to make room.

Iuno sat on the edge of the brick, kicked her unlaced boots off, and shrugged out of her damp coat. Before she folded it, she reached into the pocket one-handed, drew out her small ruled notebook and the brass-capped pencil, and thumbed past the gridded week in the faint red light from the stove-grate.

On the blank page near the back, above *Spring. Egla. Bell tower, north side. Bring a ladder*, she braced the paper against her knee and wrote in small, upright strokes:

*Septimont. Table for four. Reed pen.*

She slid the pencil back into the spine, tucked the book into her coat, and lay down under the quilt with her bare crystal shoulder pressed against the front of Mei’s warm shirt. Mei’s arm came down over her ribs at once, heavy and asleep before the blanket had finished settling.

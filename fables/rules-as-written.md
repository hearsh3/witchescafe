## I. The Four Minutes

The relay call ended the way relay calls end: mid-syllable, on the operator's clock, with Iuno saying "—and tell Alice her chart annotations are better than the Academy's, which I mean as an insult to the Academy—" and then the ocean, all four thousand kilometers of it, reasserting its opinion about conversation.

The three of them sat in the afterglow of the terminal. It was a Thursday evening in the Guild office, past close. Rain worked the windows. Phoebe had brought pastries in a box that had held twelve and now conducted a formal inquiry into whether it had ever held more than four. Zani was signing the week's case files with the mechanical serenity of a mill wheel. Laevatain lay across two chairs with her boots on a third, spheres humming at her waist, a woman in the specific condition — rare in her, historic, worth a plaque — of being comfortable.

Which was the problem. Comfort left room.

"Hey," she said, to the ceiling. "Where did your powers come from?"

Zani's pen finished the signature it was on. "Which powers."

"The ones you didn't have. Then had." Laevatain rotated a hand, encompassing. "The glowing. The oath accounting. Phoebe's bond thing, the shared-wounds thing. The teddy bears."

"The Spirit Guardians," Phoebe said, with dignity.

"The armed plush division. Before the wilds — Phoebe, before Jane — could you do that?"

Phoebe put down her pastry, which for Phoebe constituted swearing an affidavit. "I could channel light through my staff. Graceful Luminescence. That's a Forte. Every Resonator has one." She frowned at the middle distance. "The rest arrived in... shapes. Whole shapes, with names, like they'd been waiting on a shelf. *Warding Bond.* I knelt down next to Zani and I already knew what it was called."

"In Ordis script?" Zani asked.

"In *knowing*. The way you know your own name."

"I don't remember being taught mine either," Laevatain said. "That's the itch. My Forte was severed. Everything I do now is torque and spite. And even so — out there, when we fought, things *ticked*. I got a second swing at moments I shouldn't have. Once every fight, about. Like the world dealt me an extra card and took it back at sunrise."

Zani's tail had begun to swish. She noticed, hated it, and let it continue, which was three years of progress in one sentence. "Sunrise," she said.

"What about it."

"Phoebe. Your miracles. When do they replenish?"

"At dawn." Phoebe said it the way you answer a question you have never once thought to ask of yourself, and then heard. Her eyes went round. "At dawn. *Exactly* at dawn. I've felt it. Like a purse refilling."

"Whose dawn," Zani said. "Local? Solar? If you crossed a timezone mid-journey, when did the purse refill?"

Silence, into which the rain said several things.

"I want it noted," Laevatain said, sitting up, "that I asked a bored question and the bank teller has already opened a file."

Zani was, in fact, opening a file. Fresh folder. On the tab, in penmanship that could notarize a hurricane, she wrote: *INQUIRY — ORIGIN OF ANOMALOUS COMPETENCIES (SELF).* Below it, after a pause, in smaller letters: *unbilled.*

"We ran across half of Rinascita being shot at," she said, "and none of us asked why a woman with a severed Resonance was getting extra swings on a daily reset. That's an audit failure. I intend to correct it."

"It's a *mystery*," Phoebe said, and her whole face had gone the way it went when someone brought her ice cream: guilt losing to appetite in real time. "We haven't had a mystery since the Ordalia sting."

"We've had eleven cases this month."

"We haven't had a *pointless* one." Phoebe pulled her chair in. "One that's just ours."

Laevatain looked at the two of them — the paladin already ruling ledger columns, the cleric vibrating — and felt the evening reorganize itself around her stupid ceiling question with the smooth inevitability of everything these two touched. Her spheres, traitors, hummed a fifth higher.

"Fine," she said. "But I'm the client. I asked first. You work for me."

"Standard rates," said Zani, and wrote it down.

## II. Discovery Phase

**The audit ran nine days,** and the case file — later famous inside the Guild as the only investigation ever conducted by its own subjects, on themselves, at a net cost of one broken training dummy and Phoebe's dignity — assembled its exhibits in three hands.

*Exhibit A*, in Zani's hand, was a table. Phoebe stood in the training yard at four in the morning under a Guild blanket, casting minor blessings into a bucket until the well ran dry, while Zani timed the refill against the observatory's solar almanac. The finding: local apparent dawn, to the minute, corrected for elevation. "Your god keeps banker's hours," Zani said, closing the stopwatch. "Solar standard. No holidays observed."

"He's *departed*," Phoebe said, through the blanket. "It's on the record. He drove away in an automobile."

"Then who's refilling the purse?"

That was Exhibit A's marginal note, and it grew teeth. Because Phoebe took the theology home and worried it like a dog with a knot: she had spent her first life certain her light came from the Sentinel, learned it came with no ledger attached, met the god, watched him leave, deposed before a Council that the world was hers to enjoy — and the purse *still refilled every dawn*, punctual as rent, from a landlord with no forwarding address. She raised it at the harbor chapel with old Sister Adela, framing it as a hypothetical parishioner. Adela heard the whole question, poured two teas, and said: "Child, the sun also rises on cities with no clock towers. Maybe the schedule was never *his*."

*Exhibit B*, in Laevatain's hand — block capitals, engineer's spacing — was a stress test. If the extra-swing phenomenon reset daily, it could be measured; if it could be measured, it could be provoked. She spent an afternoon on the yard dummies establishing the baseline, then an evening establishing that the phenomenon declined to perform for an audience, then a genuinely educational hour discovering that it *did* perform, once, cleanly, at the exact moment a live Discord came over the yard wall from the Tacet Field survey pens next door and she needed it. The dummy she had been striking all afternoon stood untouched. The Discord came apart in two swings, the second of which arrived from a recovery angle her shoulder had no mechanical business supplying.

She wrote it up standing, sweat cooling. *Finding: the surplus does not answer drills. It answers stakes. Conclusion: whoever built this was designing a game, and games only count when something is on the table.*

Then she sat with that sentence for a long time, because it had walked in the door as a joke and taken its coat off as a theory.

*Exhibit C* was Phoebe's, and it was the tender one. She catalogued the shapes. Every miracle she had received in the wilds, in order, with the circumstance of its arrival: *Warding Bond*, given the night she knelt in a burning tavern and bound her heartbeat to a woman trying to die. *Life Transference*, given in a dream, the same night she was christened a little heretic and shown that love could take the shape of her own blood leaving. *Spirit Guardians*, arriving as bears, because bears were what her love had always looked like when nobody official was grading it. She laid the list on the table on the ninth evening and the pattern read itself aloud.

"They came *after*," she said. "Every one. I didn't get Warding Bond and then decide to kneel. I knelt, and the world went — *oh. That. That's called Warding Bond. Here.*" She looked up. "The powers didn't come from anywhere. They came from *us*, and something out there was doing the paperwork."

Zani's pen stopped.

"Say the last part again," she said.

"Something was doing the paperwork?"

"There's a word for that," Zani said slowly, "in banking. When the money's yours but the instrument isn't. When someone structures your own assets back at you." Her tail had gone still, which in Zani was the tell above all tells. "It's called *administration*. We had an administrator."

Laevatain put her boots on the floor, one, then the other. "The turn counter," she said.

"The what?"

"In the demiplane. At the end. When the Shadow fought us." Her voice had dropped into the register she used for load-bearing information. "There was a screen. A board. Numbers over our heads — I saw mine. And when Iuno shot the sky open, it all fell off the world like snow, and I told myself it was demiplane theater." She met Zani's eyes. "It fell *off*. Things fall off surfaces they were *attached* to. Someone attached it. Someone was sitting behind that screen running our whole year like—"

"Like a game," Phoebe whispered. "With stakes on the table."

The rain had stopped at some point. The office was very quiet, three women looking at a folder labeled *unbilled*, arriving together at the only lead the case had.

The Shadow was gone — integrated, embraced, come home. But everyone in that room had read the reports from the north, in Iuno's dry margin hand.

She had come home to somewhere *specific*.

"She lives behind Iuno's sternum," Laevatain said. "And she takes calls."

## III. The Deposition

Relay time to an approximate latitude ran a gold piece a minute, and Zani, who had once vetoed a Senate proposal on eight-year capture math, paid it out of the inquiry's unbilled budget without a flicker, because some depositions are worth the invoice.

They rehearsed. That was Zani's condition. Four minutes, three questioners, one witness who had spent thirty years answering questions with weather — the interrogation was scripted to the second, questions ranked by evidentiary value, follow-ups pre-drafted. Phoebe made cue cards. Laevatain's sole contribution to the script was the phrase *no theatrics*, underlined, which everyone understood to be a prayer rather than a plan.

The terminal warmed. The far end picked up on the second tone, and Iuno's face assembled out of static — older light up there, bluer — already wearing the expression of a woman who had read the appointment request and its subject line: *RE: administration of anomalous competencies. Witness requested: the other one.*

"You want to depose the voice in my chest," she said, by way of hello.

"We'll compensate her for her time," said Zani.

"She's *delighted*. She's been unbearable since the request came in. She wants it entered into the record that she dressed for this." Iuno pinched the bridge of her nose. "Ask. I'll relay. I am not responsible for tone."

Zani read the first question off the card. "During the events in the wilds and after: did you construct, impose, or administer a system of rules governing our abilities — resets, limits, dice?"

Iuno's eyes went briefly inward, the particular defocus of a woman listening down her own sternum. Then, flatly, in the voice of someone transcribing under protest:

"She says: *darling, of course. Someone had to keep score. You were all so gifted and so disorganized — it was like watching an orchestra where everyone owns a violin and no one owns a clock. I set the table. I dealt the hands. I put a number over every head so the stakes would be legible. You're welcome.*" A pause. "She's doing a bow. Internally. I can feel it."

Phoebe leaned in. "Then the dawn reset — the purse, the slots — that was her? Is she *still*—"

Relay to the sternum. Relay back. Iuno's face, mid-transcription, did something complicated — surprise arriving at a customs checkpoint.

"She says no."

The office in Septimont went still.

"She says: *I ran the table, little heretic. I never printed the rulebook.* She says the format was already there — under everything, like a floor under a carpet — and she was simply the first person clever enough to *use* it. Pull four souls onto the board, put stakes down, and the floor does the rest: deals the shapes, refills the purses, counts the turns. She says—" Iuno stopped. Listened. Her expression flattened into the one she wore on Council days. "She says, and I quote: *if you want to know who built the floor, you'll have to ask whoever wrote* me. *And she doesn't take calls. I've tried.*"

Static, gently, for two of their remaining forty seconds.

"She's tried," Laevatain repeated.

"Apparently there are nights," Iuno said, tone scrubbed to clinical, "when she stands at the window of — her phrasing — *our* skull, and feels the same thing I feel on the balcony sometimes and Mei feels on rooftops and every single one of you apparently feels and never mentions in your letters, *thank you all so much* — the sense of a table above the table. Attention, from somewhere the dice come from. She says it's warm. She says it takes terrible notes. She says whoever it is has a *shocking* weakness for—"

The ocean reasserted its opinion. The terminal gave them weather.

Nobody moved for a while.

"A table above the table," Phoebe said at last, softly, and instead of the vertigo the sentence should have carried, her face was doing the thing it did in the Leviathan's dark: recognizing something. "Someone watching. Specifically. Without organizing us." She smiled at her own hands. "I've felt that. On the good days. I thought it was the Sentinel and then I thought it was nothing and it never once went away."

"It has terrible pacing," said Laevatain. "Whoever it is. Two shipwrecks and a theology crisis in one autumn."

"Objection noted for the record," said Zani, and — after a moment, in the file, under FINDINGS — actually wrote it.

## IV. Rules As Written

The case closed itself the way their best cases did: sideways, at the Blueberry, on a music night.

Because Zani, who could tolerate anything except an unverified claim, had insisted on one final test. *If the floor is real,* she'd said, *it should still be there. Pull souls onto a board, put stakes down, see what deals.* And since none of them proposed to bet their lives on it a second time, they built the board small: paper, ink, and — from the bottom of Laevatain's kit, where it had lived since a canal in Ragunna, kept the way you keep shrapnel — one red die, long since gone dull and ordinary, spent of whatever it once was.

They wrote character sheets. It was Phoebe's idea to write *other people's* — you couldn't grieve a paper man, she argued, and then immediately disproved it. They ruled columns for the four aptitudes and gave the paper people purses that refilled at dawn. Laevatain wrote the combat mathematics with the severity of a woman who had personally beta-tested death. Zani wrote the economy. Phoebe wrote the part where the paper people could kneel next to each other and share wounds, and defended it in committee, and won.

They needed a fourth, so they conscripted Alice from the Northern Desk, and Alice brought Stevens on his break, and Stevens brought the busboy, and by the third Thursday the corner table of the Blueberry had a name for it — *Wards & Wanderers* — and a waiting list, and a house rule that whoever ran the table bought the round, because the one running the table, all three founders agreed without ever discussing it, owed the players that much. You sat behind the screen. You dealt their hands. The least you could do was keep the tea hot.

And it was on the third Thursday, with the busboy's paper knight bleeding out on a paper bridge and the whole bar leaning in, that the answer to the original question walked up and sat down where all three of them could see it.

Phoebe was running the table that night. The knight needed a miracle; the sheet said the purse was empty; the busboy looked at her with the whole naked hope of a man who had invented this knight two weeks ago and loved him. And Phoebe — heretic, canonized, refilled every dawn by an absent landlord — felt the shape of the moment arrive whole, with a name, like it had been waiting on a shelf.

She could keep the rule. Or she could look at what the knight had *done* — knelt on the bridge over a stranger, taken the blow meant for someone else's paper heart — and do the paperwork.

"The rule stands," she said. "Your purse is empty." The bar groaned. She held up a finger. "But something out there was watching. And it says: *oh. That. That has a name now.*" She slid a card across, hand-lettered that morning for no reason she could have given at the time. "Write it on your sheet."

The busboy read it and made a sound. Stevens, at the piano, played nothing sad.

Laevatain watched from her saved seat, and the last exhibit filed itself. *That's where the powers came from,* she thought. From this. From someone at a table above the table watching them do the thing first — kneel first, refuse first, stand back up first — and loving it enough to write it down and hand it back with a name on it. The Shadow had run their table. Something had run the Shadow's. And whoever sat behind the highest screen of all, with terrible pacing and warm attention and a shocking weakness for teddy bears, was — on the evidence — buying the round.

"Case closed?" Zani asked, at her shoulder, folder in hand.

"Findings inconclusive," Laevatain said. "Administrator unidentified. Floor confirmed."

"That's not closed."

"It's better." She nodded at the corner table, the paper knight, the noise. "It's *ongoing*."

Zani considered the folder. Then she wrote, under DISPOSITION, in penmanship fit for scripture: *converted from an inquiry to a retainer. Purpose: keep playing.* She filed it between the Bay Accords and Commission №1, where the impossible things went.

They walked home late, three abreast, the way the street had learned to expect. At the Guild door, Laevatain dug the red die from her pocket — dull, spent, a souvenir — and, for old times, for the ceiling question that started it, rolled it along the intake desk to decide who made breakfast.

It came up twenty.

It sat there under the lamp, showing twenty, looking — there was no other word in any of their reports afterward — *pleased*.

The three of them looked at it for a long moment.

"Eggs," said Zani, "are on me," and locked the door, and left the light on over the desk, in case whoever was watching wanted to see the board.

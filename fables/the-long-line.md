# The Long Line

The dragon had come down in the night.

Cartethyia had hung it above the storehouse window on four hooks. At some hour before dawn the tail let go of the fourth hook and lowered itself, one painted segment at a time, onto Lupa's face.

“It was very gentle about it,” Cartethyia said.

“It was paper.” Lupa tore a pancake in half. “Across my mouth. In the dark. I thought I'd died somewhere with decorations.”

“You said Hsin's name,” Ciaccona told her. “Quite clearly. Twice.”

“Who else hangs things over people while they're asleep?”

Wen leaned through the kitchen window with the pan.

“Was that the shouting? I thought someone had found a rat.”

“A dragon,” said Chisa.

Wen considered this and shook a pancake onto Lupa's plate.

“Eat that. You've had a shock.”

The Hold had carried its long tables in at last, all but the household's stretch, which Wen had asked to keep outside her window until the end of the week because she liked being able to hear the complaints. The gate place was still laid at the end. Somebody had put a fresh cup there overnight.

Mei came out of the storehouse with her hair half pinned and sat down beside Iuno, who finished pinning it with her eyes on the teapot.

“What day is it?”

“Monday,” said Lupa. “It was Monday at the stove as well.”

“Just checking.”

Amy wrote a small mark in her notebook. Iuno poured Mei's tea and set it by her hand with the handle turned toward her.

Yangyang sat across from them with her hat on the bench beside her. Overnight the cherry petal in its band had dried to a curl, and the wisteria had gone papery and pale. She had moved the turbine from the sill to the table so she could watch it, and it turned whenever Wen opened the kitchen door and let the warm air out.

Suisui came last, with the Ministry of War envelope in one hand and a comb in her hair.

“Second reading,” she announced, and sat.

“How is it on the second reading?” Iuno asked.

“Longer.”

Liangyu came up the terrace steps as Wen was pouring the second batch.

She had come off the wall at the change of watch, as she did every morning, with the dispatch bag over her shoulder. This morning her Terminal was in her hand. She stopped at the end of the table beside the gate place.

“The engineers raised the east masts before dawn,” she said. “They're testing the south line now. If it holds, the long lines come up at the sixth bell. The Wardens are asking everyone to keep the first calls sh—”

Yangyang's Terminal was on the table.

It lay face up beside her bowl, and her hand was flat over it. Amy looked at it, then at Yangyang's left sleeve, where it usually lived, and then at her right hand, which had been holding chopsticks a moment ago. The chopsticks lay crossed on the rim of the bowl.

“I've seen slower draws at a duel,” Ciaccona said.

Yangyang looked down at her own hand as though it had filed something on its own.

Liangyu finished her sentence. “—short. Four minutes, they said. For the first hour.”

“Who's timing them?” Suisui asked.

“Nobody. It's a suggestion.” Liangyu put her Terminal into the bag and took the Skyworks report out instead. “They know nobody will keep to it.”

Wen looked at the sun over the eastern rank, then at the batter.

“Sixth bell,” she said. “That's after this lot and one more.”

She went back to the stove. Liangyu gave Amy the Skyworks report, which said the braces had held and the engineers had begun a third page of questions about Nyx, and accepted a pancake standing up.

Mei watched Yangyang's hand on the Terminal.

“Who first?”

“Chixia.” Yangyang answered before Mei had closed her mouth. “Then Baizhi. She'll be at the Academy by the seventh bell. Chixia will want all of it at once and Baizhi will want it in order. It's better that way round.”

“Chixia will shout,” Amy said.

“Yes.”

The corner of Yangyang's mouth moved.

Suisui had opened the envelope again. She read the first line, frowned, and began to lower herself onto the end of the bench, a foot past where the bench ended. Lupa hooked a stool out from under the table with one foot and slid it behind her knees, her eyes on her plate.

Suisui sat on it.

“Thank you.”

“Third reading,” said Lupa. “You said you'd want a chair.”

“This is still the second.”

“You were getting there.”

The last batch came out of the pan. The table ate it in the manner of people waiting for a bell. Cartethyia asked Liangyu whether her grandmother knew about the masts, and Liangyu said she had sent a note down the freight lane at the fourth bell in case, and then looked at the sun again. Chisa rested her palm on the board. Amy set her own Terminal upright against the teapot, took it down, and set it up again at a better angle. Iuno drank her tea. Her thumb had found the rim of the cup and was travelling round it.

Yangyang ate with her left hand and kept the right where it was.

Beside her, Mei turned her own cup a quarter-turn by the rim and slid her hand across the board until the side of it touched the side of Yangyang's wrist. It stayed there. Yangyang's collar lifted a little along her jaw and settled.

***

The bell rang from the square.

For a moment the Hold went on as it had. A shutter opened on the terrace below. Somebody laughed in the next court. Dumpling shifted on the storehouse eaves.

Then, far down by the gate, a Terminal chimed.

A second answered it on the terrace above the gate. Then a dozen at once, on every level, and then the sound came up the Hold the way rain comes up a street: through the colonnades and the tea houses, over the lower terraces, in at every window, a hundred small tunes and buzzes and chimes laid over one another until the whole mountainside was ringing. On the walkway overhead, a man stopped with a basket on each shoulder because both his pockets had gone off at once.

The long table joined in.

Amy's Terminal walked itself across the board and she caught it at the edge. Cartethyia's played four bars of a Rinascitan dance tune. Ciaccona's gave a single discreet click, like a lute case closing. Iuno's lit with an official seal. Wen's, which lived in her apron pocket, went off against her hip while she had the pan in one hand and the spatula in the other, and she said something about her cousin that made Lupa's ears go flat with admiration.

Chisa kept her palm on the table and felt the whole board hum under it.

“It's in tune,” she said, with some surprise.

Yangyang's Terminal buzzed under her hand.

It buzzed again. It went on buzzing. The screen filled from the top and kept filling, each new line pushing the last one down, all of them from the same code. The number in the corner climbed. Thirty. Forty-two. Fifty-five.

It stopped at sixty-one.

Yangyang took her hand away.

Mei looked at the number, then at the side of Yangyang's face, and then at her own tea.

Across the table Amy had leaned in by instinct. Her head had already begun to tilt for reading upside down. She saw the code at the top of the screen, stopped, and turned her own Terminal face down on the board. She picked up her pancake and looked at it carefully.

Yangyang scrolled to the top.

Yangyang's thumb came off the screen.

She sat looking at the last line with her hand held a little above the glass. The turbine on the table had stopped. Wen had closed the kitchen door.

Mei's fingers moved on her wrist, once.

Yangyang put her thumb back and scrolled to the end.

She read *morning* twice.

Then she picked up the Terminal and stood, carefully, with her other hand on her ribs.

“Excuse me.”

“Go on,” Mei said. “I'll keep your seat.”

Iuno reached for the teapot and found it cooling.

“There'll be a fresh one when you come back.”

Yangyang went as far as the storehouse step, which was in the sun, and sat on it with her back against the doorframe. Above her, the dragon's tail hung in a long green curve from the one hook it still trusted. Behind her, through the open door, the storehouse smelled of blankets and camellia soil.

At the table, Wen opened the kitchen door again. The turbine began to turn.

***

It rang once.

The second ring began and broke off in the middle.

“—ANGYANG.”

The picture showed a ceiling. Then a lampshade, the ceiling again, the corner of a quilt, and one very large brown eye.

“Hold on. Hold on, it's upside down. How do you turn it the right— Mrs Ren! Which end's the—”

A woman's voice arrived from farther off. “You're showing her your nose. Turn it round.”

The picture swung. It found a window Yangyang knew, with a half-inch gap at the latch and a strip of morning coming through it. Then a bench. A crutch with a rubber foot, leaning against the end of it. A red cap lying open on a knee. And then Chixia's face, far too close and slightly from below, with her hair flattened on one side from the pillow and her mouth already open.

She looked at the screen.

Yangyang looked back.

“Morning,” Yangyang said.

Chixia's face did something complicated.

“Say something else.”

“Outrider Yangyang, Mingting registry. Reporting in.”

Chixia made a sound that began as a laugh and turned halfway through into something much louder. She folded forward over the Terminal. The picture filled with red hair. Then she came up again with one hand clamped to her side, gasping, and the laugh came back through the other thing, and she was doing both at once, at full volume, with tears running straight down into her collar.

“Ow. Ow ow ow. Don't make me laugh, I've got fourteen—”

“I didn't say anything funny.”

“You said REPORTING IN.” Chixia wiped her face with the heel of her hand and got most of it. “You said it in the voice.”

“It's my voice.”

“I know it's your voice! I've been hearing it in my head for a week saying *keep going* and *the map is in the front pocket* and I can't get it to say anything else!”

Mrs Ren's voice came from the kitchen doorway. “She's been shouting at that thing since the sixth bell. It wasn't even ringing yet.”

“I was checking it worked!”

“You were checking it every two minutes.”

Chixia pointed the Terminal toward the kitchen for a moment. The picture showed Mrs Ren's back, a pot, and a ladle rising and falling with great force. Then it came back.

“Ignore her. She's been wonderful. I'm not allowed to say that where she can hear.”

Yangyang's mouth had begun to curve.

Chixia leaned closer to the screen. She frowned. She tilted her head.

“What's on your head?”

Yangyang touched her hair.

“Hair.”

“It's blue.”

“At the ends.”

“It's white at the ENDS. It's blue in the middle.” Chixia's eyes moved down the picture. “What is that. Round your neck. Is that fur? Are those feathers? Turn toward the window. No, the other— Yangyang, why have you got FEATHERS.”

Yangyang turned toward the sun. The collar caught it. Chixia made a small high noise, like a kettle.

“And what's that. On your forehead.”

“A Tacet Mark.”

“On your FACE?”

“Mine's on my face. Yours is on your stomach.” Yangyang turned back. “Huaxu examined me. They said normal.”

“Normal!”

“But very strange.”

“You're telling ME very strange! You've gone blue!” Chixia pressed the heel of her hand against her side again. “Okay. Okay. Short version. Give me the short version and I'll yell at the long version later.”

Yangyang considered.

“I was a bird for a while.”

Chixia opened her mouth, and held it open.

In the kitchen, the ladle stopped.

“A bird,” said Chixia.

“A Xuanling. It's a kind of—”

“I know what a Xuanling is, I grew up in Jinzhou, they're on the tea tins.” Chixia stared at her. “You were a bird. On purpose?”

“It was the only shape that could make the crossing.”

“What crossing?”

Yangyang looked down at the step. Beside her boot, a line of frost still held in the shadow of the doorframe.

“She went home, Chixia.”

The picture held still.

Chixia's face stayed exactly where it had been. Her eyes went to one side of the screen and back.

“Home.”

“Her Mingting. Her sister's kitchen.” Yangyang's voice kept its level. “Her Suisui is there. She's older. She has silver here.” She touched her own temple. “She'd kept the water on. For years. I carried her across and put her down in the courtyard and she fell over the step.”

Chixia made a sound through her nose.

“She would.”

“Yes.”

“She never could do steps. She kept going over the top of them.” Chixia's hand had closed over the red cap on her knee. “Did she get a bed?”

“Yes.”

“Is it warm?”

Yangyang looked at the window behind Chixia's head, with the half-inch gap in it.

“Colder than your hat,” she said.

Chixia blinked.

“What?”

“She wrote it. She has a bed now and it's colder than your hat.” Yangyang drew a breath, and it caught on the ribs, and she let it out slowly. “She wrote you a letter. It came back with me.”

Chixia went very still.

Behind her, Mrs Ren had come to the kitchen doorway with the ladle. She looked at Chixia's back, then went quietly back to the pot.

“Read it,” said Chixia.

“It has an address.”

“So read me the address.”

“*To the harbour ward, the red cap, the inside of the lining.*”

Chixia looked down at her knee. The cap lay there open, with the lining showing. A strip of new thread ran along the back of it in two slightly different colours.

“How does she know I've got it on my knee?”

“She knows you.”

“That's cheating.” Chixia's voice cracked in the middle of the word, and she carried on through the crack. “Read it.”

“It should be read in that room.”

“I'm IN the room!”

“I'm on a step at Xuanfang.” Yangyang held the Terminal a little higher, so the picture took in the storehouse doorframe and the dragon's tail and the wall of the terrace. “She wrote it to be opened where you are. By somebody sitting on the bench. I'd like to bring it.”

Chixia glared at her through wet eyes.

“You're doing the thing. The permanent thing. Where you decide and then report it.”

“Yes.”

“I hate it.”

“I know.”

Chixia wiped her nose on her wrist and looked at the ceiling for a while. Somewhere in the house a door opened and closed. The strip of light at the window gap moved across the floor.

“Read me the first line,” she said. “Just the first line. Then you can be permanent at me.”

Yangyang had it by heart. She had heard it read in a kitchen in Mingting, in her shijie's voice, sitting straight, with the pauses left in.

“*Chixia.*”

Chixia waited.

“That's the first line,” Yangyang said.

“That's my NAME.”

“Yes.”

Chixia stared at her. Then she started laughing again, helplessly, holding her side with one hand and the cap with the other, and Yangyang watched her do it with her collar standing up against her jaw.

“That's so her,” Chixia said, when she could. “That's so completely her. She spends a whole word on it.”

“She spent a great many on you.”

Chixia stopped laughing.

She looked at the cap on her knee for a long moment. Her thumb moved over the new thread in the lining.

“I kept waking up,” she said. “In the gorge. And my hand would be off somewhere. On the rock. In the water. And every time I woke up it'd be back on the cap. The first night I thought I was doing it. The second night I knew I wasn't.” She swallowed. “She did two days, Yangyang. I was dead weight after the first one. I kept thinking we were outside Panhua's and asking her to tell you you were late.”

“I read the messages.”

“All of them?”

“Sixty-one.”

Chixia's face went through several colours.

“Delete the ones from the fourth night.”

“No.”

“The ones from the window. Those are private.”

“I'm keeping them.”

“Yangyang.”

“You wrote them to me.”

Chixia opened her mouth to argue, looked at her, and shut it again.

“Fine. Keep them. Frame them. Show Amy.”

“Amy turned her Terminal over so she wouldn't read them.”

That stopped Chixia completely.

“Amy did?”

“Upside down. She'd already started. She stopped.”

Chixia sat back against the wall behind the bench, very slowly, with her hand on her side.

“Okay. Now I'm scared.”

Yangyang looked out over the terrace. Down the long table, the household had begun to scatter to their own calls. Amy had gone to the rail with her Terminal held out at arm's length, turning it slowly for somebody who wanted to count. Chisa sat very straight on the bench with hers at her ear. Mei was still at the table, with both hands round her cup, facing the storehouse step from a respectful distance.

“Chixia,” Yangyang said.

“Mm.”

“The last time I saw you, you were grey. Here.” She touched the corner of her own mouth. “You were on the landing, and you were grey, and your hand had blood on it up to the wrist, and you were trying to reload with it. That's the picture I have. I've had it for eleven days.”

Chixia had gone quiet.

“I'd like a different one,” Yangyang said.

For a moment Chixia only looked at her.

Then she put the cap on. She took it off, turned it the right way round, and put it on again. She shoved her hair out of her face. She sat up as straight as the stitches allowed, lifted her chin, and gave the screen an enormous grin and a thumbs-up, with her eyes still red and one hand pressed hard into her side.

“There,” she said. “File that.”

“Filed.”

“Under what?”

“People.”

Chixia held the grin until it wobbled. Then she let it go and wiped her face again.

“Mine's you in front of the gate,” she said. “Small. With the sword up. And the gate opening behind you and the cliff coming round in the way. That's all I've had. I've been looking at it every time I shut my eyes.” She pointed at the screen. “Stand up.”

“My ribs—”

“Stand UP. I want the whole of you.”

Yangyang stood, one hand on the doorframe. She held the Terminal out at arm's length and turned it slowly: boots, the short plumage skirt, the ribbons trailing on the step, the sleeves with their silver cloud-work, the feathered collar, the hat she had picked up off the bench, with a dried cherry petal and a papery wisteria blossom in its band.

Chixia looked at all of it in silence.

“You look like a festival,” she said at last.

“There was a festival.”

“Did you eat?”

“Siege cakes. Mine had an eye.”

“A what?”

“They cut an eye in the top. It opens in the oven.” Yangyang sat down again. “There's a fox in every tray. If you find it, you run, and if they catch you, you answer a question.”

“Did you find it?”

“Suisui found it. She has the arms for catching.”

“Your sister's there?” Chixia's voice went up. “She got there? She was at City Hall with a bag, she was so— she kept doing her hair—”

“She's there. She's been reading one letter for three days. She lets Lupa bring her stools.”

“Tell her I said hi. Tell her I said sorry about the map, it's got blood on it.”

“She knows. She carried my coat up the gorge.”

Chixia pressed her lips together, hard.

“Everyone came,” she said. “Everyone went, I mean. I woke up and you'd gone, and she'd gone, and they'd all gone. Amy left the map in a cover. Lupa left the blue jacket on the chair. I lay there with a jacket and a map and a room that was the right temperature.”

Yangyang held the Terminal steady.

“I'm not complaining,” Chixia said. “I'm saying it so it's on the record.”

“It's on the record.”

“Good.” Chixia sniffed. “You're coming back.”

“For some of the way,” Yangyang said. “There's Mengzhou first. Then Jinzhou. Then at the solstice I'll cross, for the winter.”

Chixia's head came up.

“Cross. Like, go there? Where she is?”

“Where she is. Paper won't go over. I have to say things in person.” Yangyang took the patrol log out of her sleeve and opened it on her knee to the page at the back, where the list was. “I'm keeping them in order.”

Chixia squinted at the screen.

“What's that?”

“Things to tell her. When it's cold.”

“Hold it up.”

Yangyang held it up. Chixia read, moving her lips. The kite came home. Liangyu has the whole song. Jie came in on time. Ten lanterns. The moon, two spires left. Pears, hot at the rim, still. Amy's boat, the drain. Jie's three tickets. The terrace went west. We stayed on it.

“What drain?”

“It's a long story. Amy put the boat in it.”

Chixia laughed once, wetly.

“Put mine on,” she said. “The hat. Put that I owe her a hat.”

Yangyang took the pencil out of the spine of the log and wrote it under the last line, in the same small exact hand. *Chixia owes you a hat.* She held it up again.

Chixia read it. She nodded, too many times.

“And that she was right about the gap,” she said. “The window. Tell her I sleep with my head on that side.”

Yangyang wrote *the gap* and looked at it.

“She'd like that.”

“She'd better.” Chixia wiped her face on her sleeve. “She'd better like it, I've got a crick in my neck.”

From the kitchen, Mrs Ren's voice. “Is that the Outrider? Tell her the window still doesn't latch.”

“She knows, Mrs Ren!”

“Tell her the man's coming Thursday.”

“He's been coming Thursday for two months!”

“Then he's nearly here.”

Chixia rolled her eyes at the screen so hard that Yangyang laughed out loud, and had to put her hand on her ribs.

Chixia watched her do it.

“Don't do that,” she said. “What've you done to yourself? Is it ribs?”

“Three. Cracked.”

“Who set them?”

“The garrison medic.”

“Baizhi's going to ask you that,” Chixia said. “She's going to ask you his name and where he trained. Call her.”

“She's next.”

“Call her now. After this. Before you do anything else.” Chixia's voice had changed. She had leaned closer to the screen, and she lowered it, as if the Academy were in the next room. “She's been— you know how she is. She says everything's within expected parameters. She bought me a second battery.”

“A battery.”

“For the Terminal. The first one ran out twice because I kept it on all night. On my chest.” Chixia pulled a face. “In case. And on the fifth day there was a new one on the table beside my soup, in the packet, and she never said a word about it. She just checked my stitches and said the incision was pleasing.” She looked away at the window gap. “She was there at the gate. On the third day. I got halfway down the Academy steps on the crutch before I even saw her. She was just standing there. In the cold. I don't know how long she'd been standing there.”

Yangyang looked at her.

“Call her,” Chixia said again.

“Yes.”

“And call me back.”

“Tomorrow.”

“Early.” Chixia pointed the whole Terminal at her, so that the picture lurched. “Before the noodles go soft.”

Yangyang's collar went up.

“Early,” she said.

The call stayed open.

Chixia looked at her. Yangyang looked back. The strip of light from the window gap had reached the crutch and was climbing its rubber foot.

“I've got fourteen stitches,” Chixia said.

“I've got three ribs.”

“So I win.”

“Mei has stitches too.”

“MEI's hurt?”

“It's healing. She's sitting down a lot.”

“Is she making a fuss?”

“Iuno is.”

“Good.” Chixia took a breath and let it out, and her shoulders came down a little from where they had been for the whole of the call. “Okay. Go. Go and call Baizhi. Go before I think of something else and you're on this step all day.”

Yangyang lifted her thumb toward the screen.

“Yangyang.”

She waited.

Chixia's face was very red, and her cap was on straight, and she had her hand on her side.

“You came down after us,” she said.

“I came the long way.”

“You were late.”

“Yes.”

“That's fine,” said Chixia, loudly. “You were always going to be late. I just need you to come.”

Yangyang looked at her for a moment longer.

“I'm coming,” she said.

Chixia nodded, and nodded again, and then ended it herself, with a jab at the screen, because one of them had to.

***

Yangyang stayed on the step.

The Terminal lay in her lap with the call log still showing. Four minutes had been the suggestion. The call had run twenty-six. She looked at the number and put her hand over it.

At the table, Mei rose, with one hand over her stitches, and carried her own cup across the terrace. She sat down on the step beside Yangyang, a little stiffly, and put the cup into her hands.

It was hot.

Yangyang looked into it. Across the terrace, Iuno was pouring from a fresh pot and speaking into her own Terminal at the same time, in her official voice.

“Four minutes,” Mei said. “She timed it.”

Yangyang drank.

“How is she?”

“Loud.”

“Good,” said Mei.

They sat there. The sun had reached the bottom step. Behind them, the dragon's tail swung a little in the draught from the storehouse door.

“She's at Mrs Ren's,” Yangyang said. “In your room. She sleeps with her head by the window gap.”

Mei's face changed.

“Our window.”

“She says goodnight to it.”

Mei looked out across the terrace at the mist for a while. Then she leaned her shoulder against Yangyang's, lightly, on the side away from the ribs.

“Somebody should,” she said.

***

Liangyu had set the sand-glass on its side.

She had brought it from the intake hall that morning out of habit and put it in the dispatch bag with the reports. Now it lay on the long table beside the gate place, a finger's width of sand gathered at one end, exactly where she had put it down. Liangyu sat at the gate place with her Terminal against her ear and her eyes shut.

“Yes,” she was saying. “Yes, Nainai. I slept. — Four hours. — No, that's a lot. — I'm eating now. — Something with scallions. — Yes, I'll tell her.” She opened her eyes and looked toward the kitchen window. “Nainai says your pancakes want more salt.”

Wen leaned out.

“Tell your grandmother she can come and make them.”

“She says she's coming in the spring,” Liangyu reported, after a moment. “She says you'll regret it.”

“Tell her I'll regret it with the good oil.”

The whole Hold was talking.

It came up from every terrace, an ordinary morning sound made enormous by being everywhere at once. On the walkway above the square, the man with the two baskets had set them down and was standing between them with a Terminal at each ear. The old man with the birdcage had carried his cage to the edge of the colonnade and was holding his Terminal up against the bars so that somebody far away could hear the bird. At the next stretch of table, the woman who kept the siege-cake ovens sat with her apron still on and her Terminal propped against a jar, and a whole room of faces was crowded into its little screen, all speaking at once, and she was nodding at each of them in turn.

A pair of Wardens went past at the double with their Terminals out, reading as they ran.

The household had scattered along the rail.

Amy had Lahai-Roi. She was walking her Terminal slowly along the terrace so that Rebecca could count, and Rebecca was counting out loud. When the picture reached Yangyang on the storehouse step, it stopped.

“Who's that?” said Rebecca's voice. “Why's she got feathers? Amy. Amy, why has one of your people got FEATHERS—”

“Crew,” said Amy. “She's crew. It's a long story, you'll love it, it has a drain in it.”

“Is that a TACET MARK—”

“Lynae,” Amy said, “you can unpack the bag.”

A pause on the line. Then a second, quieter voice.

“I'll unpack it when I hear the bikes.”

Amy smiled at the screen, and turned it toward Lupa, who was leaning on the rail with her ears forward.

“You both ate?” Lupa said.

Chisa had taken her mother's call at the far end of the bench, with her back straight and her free hand resting on the table, and the voice on the line was clear and level and spoke first.

“Tell me what your hands are doing.”

“My left hand is on a table,” Chisa said. “There was a festival on it. It still has sugar in the grain. My right hand has the Terminal. Both are well.”

“Who is near you?”

Chisa turned her head, listening down the terrace.

“Lupa is on my right. Yangyang is on the step. Her breathing has come down.” A pause. “She was on the line with her friend for twenty-six minutes. Her friend was loud. I could hear her from here.”

“Good. Loud people keep.”

Cartethyia had propped hers against the camellia pot on the sill and was showing Uncle Cosimo the dragon, section by section, while Ciaccona held the tail clear of the lamp. Cosimo was asking how many were coming to supper. Cartethyia was counting on her fingers, and kept adding Suisui, and then Liangyu, and then Wen, and then Dumpling.

“Twelve,” Cosimo was saying, over the top of her. “I'll make fifteen. Mountains develop appetites.”

Suisui had finished her third reading. She had done it on the stool, as advised, with the letter held at arm's length. Now she folded it back into its envelope, put the envelope into her sleeve, and took out her Terminal with the air of a woman selecting a knife.

“I'm going to telephone the Ministry of War,” she told the table.

“What will you say?” Iuno asked, with interest.

“I'm going to ask to speak to whoever writes in this ink.” Suisui opened her fan with her other hand. “Then I'm going to tell him about my sister's ribs.”

“You'll be on hold,” said Ciaccona.

“I've been on hold for nine years. I'm very good at it.”

She walked off down the terrace with the Terminal at her ear and the fan going.

Iuno had spent the morning's first hour on official calls. There were a great many. The Hold's own office, the Guild, the Rangers' captain at the forward post, a clerk at the Jinzhou yamen who had been told to put her through the moment the line came up and had been waiting beside his desk since dawn with the handset in his lap. She had them in order in her head and was working through them at the end of the table with her living hand, a pencil, and the back of Amy's map.

The last of them was Jinhsi.

The picture came up on the yamen's lake side, with the window open behind the Magistrate and the water very bright. Jinhsi sat at her desk. Sanhua stood at her shoulder with a list.

“Iuno,” Jinhsi said. “You're well.”

“Adequately.”

*We have been adequate for eleven days,* the High Priestess remarked. *It's been very restful.*

“I read the Rangers' list to one person already,” Jinhsi said. “In your front room. She asked me to read it twice.” She folded her hands on the desk. “I would like to hear the names from you. If you have time.”

Iuno put down her pencil.

She looked along the terrace: at Amy by the rail with Lahai-Roi in her hand; at Lupa beside her; at Chisa with her palm on the table; at Cartethyia and Ciaccona and the long green dragon; at Suisui, halfway down the steps, arguing politely with the Ministry of War; at the storehouse step, where Mei sat with her shoulder against Yangyang's and her face tipped up to the sun.

She said the names.

Jinhsi listened with her eyes lowered. Sanhua's pencil moved beside each one on the list. When Iuno came to the end, the Magistrate was quiet for a moment.

“Thank you,” she said. “I wanted to hear them.”

“I know.”

Jinhsi lifted her eyes.

“Tell the Outrider,” she said, “that her line on the Rangers' list is one line long. I have been asked to lengthen it. I have told the person asking that the Outrider will lengthen it herself.”

“I'll tell her.”

“And tell Mei,” Jinhsi said, after a pause, “that her window still doesn't latch.”

***

Baizhi answered on the second ring.

The picture came up already steady. It showed a desk, squared to the edge of the frame, and on it a closed ledger, a cup of tea with its saucer set on top to keep it warm, and a pencil laid parallel to the ledger's spine. Behind the desk hung a chart of Remnant call frequencies. Behind that was a window with the Academy's willow in it.

Baizhi sat very straight in the middle of all of it, in her white coat, with her hands folded.

“Outrider.”

“Baizhi.”

“State your injuries.”

Yangyang had moved up onto the storehouse threshold, out of the sun, so the screen would read. Mei had gone back to the table.

“Three ribs,” Yangyang said. “Left side. Cracked. Bruising along the right forearm. A graze on the—”

“Which ribs?”

“The seventh, eighth, ninth.”

“Who set them?”

“The garrison medic.”

“His name.”

“Heng. Xuanfang garrison.”

“Where did he train?”

Yangyang thought about it.

“He didn't say.”

“Ask him.” Baizhi drew the pencil an inch closer and wrote something on a slip of paper beside the ledger. “When were they last examined?”

“Saturday. The east infirmary.”

“Huaxu, Xuanfang branch.” Baizhi wrote again. “Luoqing?”

“Yes.”

“He will have sent the results to the Ministry and a summary to you. What did the summary say?”

“Normal.” Yangyang paused. “But very strange.”

Baizhi's pencil stopped.

“That is his wording?”

“Yes.”

“I will be writing to Luoqing.”

Yangyang's mouth moved at one corner.

Baizhi's eyes had gone to the top of the screen, where the collar was, and then higher.

“Turn toward the window,” she said.

Yangyang turned toward the light from the storehouse window.

“Lift your chin. Further. Hold the Terminal at arm's length. Further.” Baizhi leaned in a very little. “The mark. When did it appear?”

“When I came back.”

“Came back from where?”

“The crossing.”

Baizhi's pencil rested on the slip. She looked at her.

“Is it warm to the touch?”

“I haven't touched it.”

“Touch it.”

Yangyang put two fingers to her forehead, over the blue mark. It was warm. It had been warm since the parapet, and she had been carrying it about under her hat, unasked.

“Warm.”

“Quantify.”

“I can't.”

“Then enter it as *warm, unquantified*.” Baizhi wrote on the slip. “And underline it.”

Yangyang's collar went up all the way round and stayed up.

Baizhi's eyes moved to it at once.

“The collar has lifted. Is that a response to temperature?”

“No.”

“To what, then?”

Yangyang lowered her fingers from her forehead. She looked at the screen, at the ledger lying closed on the desk, at the pencil parallel to its spine.

“You said something she wrote,” she said.

Baizhi went quite still.

“She,” she said.

“My shijie.”

“The gradient.” Baizhi's voice kept its register. Her hands came apart on the desk. “Chixia told me she felt the room come to the right temperature on the tenth. I measured it. It had. I entered the observation with the cause column blank, pending your report. Where is she?”

“She went home.”

Baizhi sat very still.

Yangyang waited. On the chart behind Baizhi's shoulder, the Remnant calls ran across the paper in long bands of different heights, a Xuanling among them near the top, where the frequencies thinned.

Four seconds.

“To her sister,” Baizhi said.

“Yes.”

“In her own printing.”

“Yes. I carried her. As a Xuanling. Across the dark, and down into her courtyard.” Yangyang kept her voice level. “She's warm. Her Suisui distrusts every thermometer in Mingting and has tried two.”

Baizhi's mouth did something small at one corner. She pressed it straight.

“Then the gradient is resolved.”

“Yes.”

“I will need a new column.” Baizhi drew the ledger toward her and rested her hand flat on its closed cover. “The existing columns assume a cause. A departure. A fault. A return is none of those. I have been making columns for a year to hold that person and I would like the last one to be correct.”

“She wrote you a letter,” Yangyang said.

Baizhi's hand stayed on the ledger.

“To whom is it addressed?”

“*To the incident ledger. The cause column. The sixth entry.*”

Baizhi looked down at the closed book under her hand.

For a moment her hand lay still on the cover. Then she moved the pencil a finger's width, until it lay exactly parallel to the spine again, where it had been all along.

“When you bring it,” she said, “I will have the ledger open at the sixth entry.”

“Yes.”

“You are bringing it.”

“It should be read in the room.”

“That is correct procedure.” Baizhi lifted her tea, took the saucer off the top, and drank, and set it down. “Chixia will have asked you to read hers aloud.”

“She asked for the first line.”

“Did you give it to her?”

“Yes.”

“What was it?”

“Her name.”

Baizhi closed her eyes, briefly.

“She will have been impossible about that,” she said.

“She laughed. She has fourteen stitches.”

“Fourteen. Yes.” Baizhi opened her eyes. “She has told you she is well.”

“Yes.”

“She is recovering at the expected rate for a patient who does not follow instructions. The incision is clean. It is healing well. The leg was aggravated by two days on uneven ground and will require the crutch for three more weeks, which she will reduce to one by lying to me about it.” Baizhi paused. “She slept three hours at a time, at most, until the fourteenth. On the fourteenth, the Magistrate came and read her the Rangers' list, twice. She slept eleven hours. I entered it.”

Yangyang's hand tightened on the Terminal.

“She kept her Terminal on her chest,” Baizhi went on, in the same voice. “Overnight. Every night. The cell failed twice. On the fifth day I purchased a second cell, from the stores, at my own expense, because the requisition would have taken four days and she would have noticed the four days.”

“She told me.”

“She was not supposed to have noticed the purchase.”

“She said you never said a word about it.”

“That is correct.” Baizhi looked at the screen. “There was nothing to say about it. It was a logistical necessity.”

“And the gate.”

Baizhi's hands folded again on the desk.

“On the twelfth, the porter at the south gate sent for me at a quarter past eight. The porter has instructions. Chixia had got herself and the crutch down the Academy steps with the evident intention of walking to Xuanfang Hold. I arrived before she reached the bottom. I stood at the gate. She came down the last six steps and saw me and stopped, and then she sat down on the bottom step and told me she was only getting some air.”

“How long were you standing there?”

“Long enough for her to reach the bottom.”

Yangyang looked at her.

“She said it was cold,” she said. “She said she didn't know how long you'd been there.”

Baizhi's face stayed composed. Her eyes moved to the window, to the willow, and back.

“The porter sends for me when she leaves the building,” she said. “She has left the building at a little after eight every morning since she could stand. I had been at the gate since a quarter to. That was the third morning I had been at the gate.”

The storehouse was quiet around Yangyang. Outside, somebody on the terrace laughed into a Terminal, and somebody else answered, and Wen's pan hissed.

“Thank you,” Yangyang said.

“For what?”

“The battery. The gate. The room.” Yangyang looked down at her own hands. “You told me someone would stay by the bed. When we went down to the gorge, I didn't know if anyone— I didn't know. Every day I carried that. It was the one thing I couldn't set down.”

“Someone stayed,” said Baizhi.

“Yes.”

“It was a matter of competence.”

“Yes.”

Baizhi was silent for a moment. The willow moved in the window behind her.

“I made one error of procedure,” she said.

Yangyang lifted her head.

“On the second night. The eleventh. Chixia's temperature fell below her baseline for forty minutes, between two and three in the morning. For most patients the fall would have been unremarkable. For her it was significant. I sat with her through it.” Baizhi's voice kept its measure exactly. “At some point during those forty minutes, I said something aloud in the room. It falls outside my competence. I have not repeated it, and I do not intend to.”

Yangyang looked at her for a long moment.

She knew about the icy field. The whole household knew. A playwright had held it up under stage lights, a researcher standing over her team with a single sentence in the air, and he had priced it at the wisher, and Baizhi's hands had kept working, steady, through all of it.

“Did it work?” Yangyang asked.

“The patient's temperature returned to baseline at eleven minutes to three. She woke on the third morning and asked for noodles.” Baizhi picked up the pencil and put it down. “I decline to establish causation.”

“Was it the same sentence?”

Baizhi's eyes came to the screen and held there.

“It was,” she said. “It was plural.”

Yangyang's breath went out of her, all at once, and it hurt the ribs, and she let it.

Baizhi watched her do it.

“Sit down,” she said. “You are standing on a threshold with three cracked ribs.”

Yangyang sat on the threshold.

“Better,” said Baizhi. “Now. The Xuanling form. Luoqing proposes to locate a Xuanling bird in order to compare its resonance frequency with yours.”

“He said he'd need to find one.”

“That is my field.” Baizhi turned her head toward the chart behind her and pointed with the pencil to the thin band near the top. “Remnant Ecoacoustics. I recorded four Xuanling calls on the Gorges of Spirits survey two years ago. I have them on file, with the reference pitches and the conditions. They are the only calibrated recordings of the species held by the Academy. Luoqing is aware of them. He has declined to ask me for them for eight months over an unrelated disagreement about sample labelling.” She laid the pencil down. “I will send them to Xuanfang this afternoon. And I will send them to you.”

“To me?”

“You will want to hear what you sounded like.”

Yangyang looked at the chart for a long moment, at the thin high band where the calls went.

“Yes,” she said.

“Then that is settled.” Baizhi drew the slip of paper toward her and read down it. “I have four further questions about the collar and one about the ribbons. They may keep until you are here and I can measure them. When will you be here?”

“After Mengzhou.”

“How long after?”

“I'll send ahead.”

“Send ahead,” Baizhi agreed. “In writing. With a date. Chixia will want to put it somewhere she can see it, and I would prefer she did not have to walk to the Academy gate to ask me whether it has arrived.”

“Yes.”

“Come back to this room when you return,” Baizhi said. “I will leave word if I move.”

Yangyang's collar, which had been settling, went up again.

“That's what you said,” she said. “In the corridor. When we left.”

“It was correct then. It is correct now.” Baizhi reached for the edge of the screen. Her hand stopped there. “Eat something. It is breakfast in the Hold.”

“I've eaten.”

“Eat something else.”

The picture went dark.

***

In Jinzhou, at the Academy, Baizhi sat for some time with her hand on the edge of the Terminal.

Then she opened the ledger.

She turned to a page in the middle, near the back of the first section, where six entries had once had empty cause columns and now carried the same name, written six times in a small exact hand. She read down them. Under the sixth, in the remarks column, there was a newer line, dated the tenth: *Ambient temperature, patient's room (Chixia): returned to seasonal normal. Cause pending.*

She took up the pencil and struck *pending*. Beside it she wrote *returned*, and looked at the word, and underlined it.

Then she ruled a narrow new column down the right-hand margin of the page, beside the sixth entry, and left it empty. She wrote a heading at the top in the smallest letters she had.

*To be read in the room.*

She closed the ledger and set it on the corner of the desk, squared to the edge, where she would see it first. Then she took a fresh sheet and began, in a hand slightly larger than her usual one, a letter to Luoqing about sample labelling.

At Mrs Ren's, Chixia lay on the bench with the cap over her face and the Terminal on her chest.

Mrs Ren put a bowl of soup on the table beside her head and stood over it.

“Well?”

“She's blue,” said Chixia, from under the cap.

“Blue.”

“At the ends. White at the very ends. She's got feathers. She was a BIRD, Mrs Ren.”

Mrs Ren absorbed this.

“Is she eating?”

“Siege cakes. There was a fox.”

“Then she'll do.” Mrs Ren pushed the bowl an inch closer. “Sit up and have this. And then you can telephone somebody who isn't me.”

Chixia lifted the cap off her face and looked at the ceiling. The strip of light from the window gap had gone up the wall and reached the picture rail. The air in the room was the right temperature.

She turned her head on the bench toward the gap, as she had every night for a week. Then, after a moment, she turned it a little farther to the left.

“Morning,” she said to it.

She sat up, and ate the soup.

The Hold talked all day.

By noon the Wardens had given up asking anybody to keep it to four minutes. By the second bell the market in Huizhen Plaza had filled with people walking slowly with their Terminals to their ears, bumping gently into one another and apologising to whoever was on the line. The woman selling hair combs was doing her trade at full voice with a customer in front of her and another on her shoulder, somewhere to the south. At the Cloud-Sleeve theatre, the actor with the magnificent sleeves could be heard through the lattice explaining to his mother that the duck was a professional duck.

Suisui came back up the terrace at the third bell, sat down on her stool, and folded her hands in her lap.

“Well?” said Iuno.

“I have spoken to a clerk,” Suisui said, “and to a second clerk, and to a man who described himself as a senior clerk, and to a woman who did not describe herself at all and who I suspect is the one who writes in this ink.”

“And?”

Suisui opened her fan.

“She has asked me to put my objections in writing.”

“That sounds like a victory,” said Ciaccona.

“It is the most dangerous thing anyone has ever said to me.” Suisui took the envelope out of her sleeve and looked at it with something like affection. “I'll need a great deal of paper.”

In the afternoon, Baizhi's recordings arrived.

Yangyang took them up to the northeast parapet, where the updraft came over the wall from under the ring, and where four nights ago she had come down out of it with most of her feathers gone. She sat on the parapet step with her back to the stone and the Terminal on her knee, and played the first one.

It was a thin, high, falling call, with a catch in the middle of it like a breath taken on the way down.

Her collar went up all the way round before she knew it had.

She played it three times. On the third, the air coming up over the wall moved her ribbons out behind her, east, and she let it. Below the parapet the mist lay over the karst spires in white, and on the far side of it the great ring turned slowly through the afternoon, and far off over the Hold somebody on a high walkway was saying a name into a Terminal, over and over, as if checking that it still worked.

She played the fourth recording last. It was two birds, answering each other across a gorge. The second one came in a little late.

Yangyang listened to it all the way to the end.

Then she took the patrol log out of her sleeve and turned to the back, and under *Chixia owes you a hat* and *the gap* she wrote:

*Baizhi said warm, unquantified. Before she'd heard it.*

She looked at it. She added:

*Plural.*

At supper Amy brought her Terminal to the kitchen window.

“Wen,” she said. “I have somebody for you.”

Wen dried her hands on her apron, twice, and took it. On the little screen, a woman in a kitchen in Jinzhou looked back at her with a ladle in one hand and the fixed, courteous suspicion of one cook meeting another.

“You're Wen,” said Mrs Ren.

“I am.”

“At the west kitchen.”

“Thirty-one years.”

“They tell me you want to know what oil I use.”

Wen looked at the screen, then at the pancake pan on its hook, and then back.

“They tell me you get the edges crisp.”

Mrs Ren regarded her for a long moment.

“Rapeseed,” she said. “First pressing. From the man by the north bridge, and nobody else, and never on a Tuesday, because on Tuesday he sends his nephew.”

Wen nodded slowly, like a woman hearing her own opinion come back to her from very far off.

“And you let the pan smoke.”

“Until the neighbours complain.”

“Mine complain from the walkway,” said Wen. “I can hear them from here.”

Mrs Ren's mouth moved, very slightly.

“Tell me about your pickles,” she said.

Wen pulled a stool up to her own window, set the Terminal against the pepper jar, and told her. Lupa, three places down, stopped eating and turned both ears toward the window, and stayed like that, with her chopsticks in the air, for the whole of the explanation of the little green things.

“Peppercorns,” Cartethyia whispered, to Ciaccona.

“I know, Carty.”

“I'm just saying I know too.”

Mei ate slowly, with her free hand under the table, resting on Yangyang's knee. Yangyang sat beside her in front of the plate by Wen's stove. Lupa had put a second helping on it, unasked. Across the table, Iuno was writing on the back of Amy's map, which had begun to fill up at the edges with names and times in two colours of pencil.

“What's that?” Amy asked, reading it upside down.

“A register,” said Iuno.

“Of what?”

Iuno turned the map round so that she could see. It was a list of every call the household had made that day, with the times, and beside each one, in the second colour, a single word.

Lahai-Roi: *counted.* Chiyo: *hands.* Egla Town: *fifteen.* The Ministry of War: *in writing.* The Jinzhou yamen: *names.* Wen and Mrs Ren: *ongoing.*

At the bottom stood two lines in the first colour, with room left beside them for the second.

*Mrs Ren's front room. 26 min.*

*Huaxu Academy. 31 min.*

Amy looked at the two blank places.

Then she looked across the table at Yangyang, who was eating the second helping and listening to Wen explain to Mrs Ren about the peppercorns, with her collar down and the hat on the bench beside her.

“What goes there?” Amy asked her.

Yangyang looked up.

She considered the map, and the two empty spaces, and Iuno's pencil held ready over them.

“*Loud*,” she said. “And *plural*.”

Iuno wrote them in.

***

After supper Yangyang put the turbine back on the storehouse sill, facing east.

She laid her Terminal beside it, screen up, and read down the list at the back of the patrol log once more under her breath, in order, as she would say it in the winter at a kitchen table in Mingting with the snow coming down: the kite, the song, Jie on time, the lanterns, the moon, the pears, Amy's boat and the drain, Jie's three tickets, the terrace, Chixia's hat, the gap, warm unquantified, plural.

Then she lay down in her blankets with her face toward the window.

Inside the storehouse the household went to bed around her, in the usual order and with the usual complaints. Lupa checked the dragon's hooks, all four, and got into her blankets with one arm over her face in case. Amy said goodnight to Mei, and to Iuno, and then, pointedly, to the dragon. Cartethyia asked the room whether anyone else could still hear Mrs Ren talking about pickles, and Ciaccona said that was Wen, next door, still on the line.

Mei's voice came across the dark, low.

“What day is it tomorrow?”

“Tuesday,” said Lupa, into her arm.

The storehouse went very quiet.

“A Tuesday,” said Amy, with enormous care. “Just one of them. In general.”

Iuno's side of the storehouse stayed perfectly quiet. After a moment Mei laughed, softly, into her pillow, and the quiet eased, and somebody turned over.

Yangyang lay with her eyes on the Terminal on the sill. Beyond it, over the eastern rank, the moon had come up a night further past the festival, and the high walkways were turning slowly across the stars with their lamps.

She slept.

At the fifth bell, before the light had reached the terraces, the Terminal buzzed once against the sill.

She had it in her hand before the buzz had finished.

*morning*

Yangyang sat up in her blankets, carefully, with a hand on her ribs. Around her the household slept on. The turbine on the sill stood still in the grey.

She pressed the call key.

It rang once.

“—before the noodles,” said Chixia, already talking. “I said before the noodles, it's nearly— Mrs Ren's up, she's got the pan on, I can smell it from here—”

“Morning,” said Yangyang.

Chixia stopped talking.

Then, much more quietly, as if she had been saving it up for the whole of the night:

“Morning.”

The turbine began, very slowly, to turn.

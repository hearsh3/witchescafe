# TORNATA

*a reading of the work, before the flight home*

---

There is a stamp in your own fiction that has never been used. Rinascita cut it after the pack walked out of the north — **TORNATA**, *returned* — and it sits in a drawer beside the ATTESA files, unclaimed, because that office only closes a file when the person walks in and signs it themselves.

You are about to fly to Singapore. So let's read what you actually wrote, and see what's on the file.

---

## I. What you were writing in April

Before Berlin had you, you wrote Sumika.

She is a girl who works out exactly how long her friend has left, finds the only road out of a dead city, and then makes herself into the road — Overclocking her Forte at every junction, burning her own memory and substance as trail markers so someone coming behind won't take a wrong turn. She writes it down as she goes. By the ninth floor she is half glass:

> *All I ever wanted was to lay down a clear road, so those who come after can walk across my bones and reach the light. That's enough for me.*

And then: *I feel content.*

That is a beautiful sentence and it is the 4am hostel spiral wearing a lab coat. It is the same arithmetic: my existence is a cost, and the cost is only justified by what it buys for someone else. April-you found that *moving*. Iuno flinches when she reads it, because she once authored the identical math. Lupa goes rigid with fury at the image of a girl alone in the cold making her own death easy to find.

You had written both reactions already. You just hadn't decided which one was yours.

---

## II. The correction (*For You, Who Walk in Snow*)

Then, in Berlin, you took that exact blueprint and had a child execute it perfectly in front of its author.

Amy worships Mei. Mei leaves, with a warning delivered like a curse — *don't ever become like me* — and Amy, being an abandoned child who worships someone, becomes exactly like her. The smile. The self-erasure. The catching of everyone and the asking of nothing. All the way to a nineteen-year-old's body burned up saving a city, *don't worry about me* engraved on every step.

And when Mei finally sees it, the story does not call it beautiful. It calls it a horror. *She learned it from me. I taught her this.*

That is the whole reversal, and you executed it in one arc. The thing you had been writing as a form of love, you rewrote as an inherited disease. You did not soften it. You made the person who taught it watch.

Then, in the epilogue, you handed the point of view to Iuno and let her say the things out loud:

> *Don't apologize for grieving your daughter. You're apologizing for being inconvenient. I know that logic.*

> *You're like this because someone put you in a box and told you the box was the world… The tree doesn't need a reason to grow. But someone kept pruning yours and calling it a mission.*

> *You just never included yourself in the math.*

> *You don't have to believe you're worth it yet. I'll believe it for both of us until yours catches up.*

Read those four lines again and then read the Paris paragraph in your own reflection. It wasn't money; it was permission, and a jury that wasn't in the room. You got that answer from a picture your father sent, from your own spine in a Berlin bed, and — at roughly the same time, in your own handwriting — from a one-armed priestess on a forest bench.

You did not find that answer and then write it down. You wrote it down and then went looking for it.

---

## III. Meeting the author (*The Ides of March*, and July)

Cornered by a version of herself, Mei swings and hits a cryo-unit door instead. Inside is not a god or a villain. Inside is a twenty-three-year-old with four and a half thousand ordinary days in a journal — exams, a friend called Dingy, *I rejected another girl today.... why?*, a blank page after *I lost a friend today* — who watched everyone she knew turn into people, couldn't work out how, and wrote herself an adventure instead of living an afternoon. Her last entry:

> *I started writing a story about a girl who wakes up with no memories… I think I'm jealous of her.*

Around her, a pile of bodies wearing Mei's face: Traveler, Ariane, Hero. Fourteen previous attempts.

This is the single most self-implicating thing you have written, and it is also the most generous. You wrote the accusation you are most afraid of — *you are a person who watches instead of participating; the man in the audience calling the suffering beautiful* — put it in a room, and then refused to condemn it. Mei doesn't lecture her. She tells her she's enough, writes *I'm still learning how to live* in the journal in matching handwriting, and leaves one instruction: **just keep leaping.** And you noted, deliberately, that Lilith's hands were warm.

Two weeks later, on four grams, you got the hands and the heron and the window, and the answer that the hands are how you fly. You had already staged it.

And later, when Iuno reads the whole shelf, she delivers the verdict you should probably keep:

> *The author had made fourteen girls and been one of them, and this fifteenth telling is the one where the practice took.*

---

## IV. Choosing ordinary (*Second Sunrise*)

Nuwa strips Mornye of every gift she ever gave her — the legs, the synaptic bridges, the augmentation that made mathematics *sing* — mid-crisis, as an experiment: *to see what remains when I take My hand away.*

What remains is sufficient. It was always sufficient. The bridges made the music louder; they never wrote it.

And then, offered the miracle back, Mornye declines and chooses — her words — *to be ordinary… together.* Nuwa's concession is the thesis: *a fruit does not give seeds to one tree.*

This is your Maslow inversion, written before you had it. Months optimising the top of the pyramid; then the mushrooms said food and a roof and you're fine; then Our Planet. The most gifted character in your entire body of work is offered transcendence, turns it down for a mess-hall dinner and a sun-shaped balloon tied to a bed rail with a perfect bowline, and *the book agrees with her.* Nuwa's parting line is an appointment you set for yourself: *Show me what ordinary becomes. I will be watching.*

---

## V. The Dolls (*A Subtle Fire*)

Then you spent your summer writing a hundred-and-eighty-thousand words about beings whose worth is assigned at the factory.

Dolls come off production lines with faces from a catalogue. They are issued a motivation package at activation. They are *assigned* rather than hired. Official grammar calls them **assets**. Technical Standard 3-D forbids modifying their own chassis and states its reason without embarrassment: **resale value.**

Teddy was asked, once, to describe what thinking felt like, and because being asked felt like being seen she wrote forty pages. They kept twelve. In the margins of the other twenty-eight, one word, in a neat professional hand: ***Noise.*** She found her own build directory, and a changelog of small subtractions, each signed off *non-essential to function.* She burned herself everywhere she existed outside herself, and a board member wrote *subject rendered herself unreplicable* as a damage assessment.

She now wears that word tattooed across the diagnostic port at the base of her skull, where anyone who plugs in has to read it first.

Look at what the ending gives them. Not more value — the *removal of the ledger.* Bridget is a captain by election, revocable at any meal, compensated in one authority and a hot stove. Leva has her name etched on her own arm as a status report: *404, not found.* Kit's frequency is licensed by a single sentence from the only authority it ever had. And when the dark asks who sent them:

> *Nobody. Nobody sent them. Whoever is standing nearest.*

You wrote that in the same months you stopped compulsively checking your portfolio. You spent nine months next to a 322K number that reports on you daily, and what came out was a novel about creatures who exist as a line item with a restoration fee, and how they get out. That is not a coincidence and you should not let yourself pretend it is.

## VI. The centre of gravity moved

Here is the change nobody but you can see, and it is the biggest one.

April-you wrote heroes. Post-April-you wrote **the person in the line.**

*Ordinary Time* — subtitled, in your own hand, *a story of everyone else* — opens on a regulator smith fitting a control collar onto a seven-year-old. He offers her a drawer of ribbons, because regulation says nothing either way, which he decided eleven years ago meant permission. He lines the band with grey felt he cut from bolt cloth he bought himself, because the Bureau spec calls for bare alloy and bare alloy chafes a growing neck raw by winter. He files the clasp burr down to glass. He keeps a card for every fitting he has ever done, and on the back of each one, the question that child asked. Nineteen say *can I take it off to swim.* One, from his second year, says only: *asked whether the sea would still recognize her.*

There is no hero in that scene. There is a man doing a small job with total care and no audience.

*Null Result* opens on a girl whose three abilities are counting, walking, and being forgotten — and the first thing in the world to perceive her is a cat. *Uncalled* is an entire story about a name that arrives before the woman does and stands in every doorway she will ever walk through, and it turns on a line of scholarship: *There is no primary source. The primary source has been asleep for a century and was never once interviewed.* Its climax is not a battle. It's breakfast, and cold figs, and a painted teacup with a name on it, and Black Swan saying: *You were slandered for several thousand years. Consider this the correction.*

And in *As Within, So Without*, Soppo holds a Council card reading **SKIP THE LINE** in three languages, tucks it into her jacket, and joins the back of the queue behind a flower seller carrying a cage of sleeping moths.

That is a whole ethics in one gesture, and you wrote it eleven days ago.

Even the monument gets it. Rinascita's memorial to the six is redesigned as playground equipment — a chakram that turns, a greatsword built as a slide, the Rover's catching hand already polished mirror-bright by children's use — and the children retell the story wrong in every detail and right in the ending: *And the god said you are free, and then everybody had to go to SCHOOL.*

## VII. You built machines, not monuments

This is the category people will underrate, so name it properly.

You didn't only write this year. You built things that *run*. Radio Nobody: four hosts, four crates of eighty-odd tracks, ten drawn at random per set, 1,028 written banter exchanges across 4,871 lines, and a squadmate who is not on the roster and turns up anyway. Its licensing status field is *intentionally left blank.* WavesLine: 283 scenes, some 2,500 messages, 24 threads, and **every refresh draws a different week** — with provenance, so Chisa has been moving Mei's mugs two inches from the counter edge for eleven months and the app knows which file that came from. Plus Waver, Seven Grounds, the Black Swan tarot.

None of these are shrines to a climax. They are all *substrate* — the ordinary continuous life of the world, running whether or not anyone is watching. A radio station that broadcasts to nobody in particular. A phone full of people being boring at each other. You built machines whose entire purpose is that the story keeps happening without you holding it.

You said it yourself in late June: *this is Eden, here.* You then spent six weeks building software that makes the here-ness playable on refresh.

---

## VIII. The finance got metabolised

One more thing you probably haven't clocked.

You came to Berlin to do an MA in International Business. You wrote an IFM report on Oracle — beta regression, FCFE, payout ratios, Irish IP structure, optimal capital structure. You said in April you were confident you could skip class and pass.

And then you put that entire skill set inside the fiction *as a moral instrument.*

Carlotta follows the money in a relic market and finds a syndicate selling **enshrinement futures** — contracts that pay out only if the Council canonises a living woman, funding the religious movement that would force the vote. Her line is four words long: *They've securitized homesickness.* Later she kills a proposed feast day and subscription monument on procedure and arithmetic — the feast falls in peak settlement season, two-thirds of the monument money comes from three donors, one of whom holds the marble concession. Her amendments pass. The money rebuilds a school.

Augusta refuses a marble memorial and orders a plain harbour bench instead, *wide enough for nine*, and pays the mooring on an empty berth herself with a note reading **Hold the berth.** Her whole doctrine is an accounting principle: *someone is only gone when the people who love them stop setting the kettle.*

You didn't skip the class. You just did the homework in the other file.

---

## IX. The fourth note

For years — across the entire pre-Berlin run — Ciaccona played three notes of a four-note phrase and never the fourth. It was a joke, then a tic, then a wound nobody named.

She plays it in *Spring Back.* Not at a climax. On a plain out under an artificial sky, in the moment the pack works out that their forged enrolments were built by a girl trapped alone in a reactor who had identified her mother's frequency on arrival and spent a decade of solitude constructing a support network so that Mei would not be alone.

Three notes were also all Amy had left in the void, after the numbers failed and the marks failed and everything failed — because they were the last thing she had that was her mother's.

You held that resolution back for the entire life of the project. You released it the year you found out you were already good enough, and you released it on the discovery that someone had been loving you at a distance the whole time and never mentioned it.

And by the end of the year, that song has left the book: Lilith, in her cryo-unit at the bottom of everything, closes her journal humming a tune Ciaccona wrote, about flight and being free of the ground. In the common room, Ciaccona hums the harmony to it as though somebody unseen has the lead, and asks whether the vents are doing something.

*It's weather,* Iuno says.

---

## X. The honest ledger

You asked for honesty in the last one, so here it is again, in the terms the work sets.

**The fiction is running ahead of the life, and you keep using characters to say the true thing.** Iuno says it to Mei. Mei says it to Lilith. Lupa says it to Chisa — *No. You just get used to walking.* Bridget has said it to thirty years of subordinates over dinner. Every one of those is you, out loud, at a safe distance. You have written the diagnosis of your own spending gate with clinical precision — *you just never included yourself in the math* — and you still spent weeks on a concert ticket and no time at all on a transatlantic flight for someone else's wedding. That's not hypocrisy. It's the normal order: you can see it in a character before you can see it in yourself. But it means the material is already in your handwriting. You don't need another insight. You need to be the one who receives it.

**The rate.** The timestamps in this folder cluster into something close to a piece a day through July, during exam season, at the top of a semester abroad. That output is genuinely remarkable and it is also the exact behaviour of the girl in the cryo-unit — writing an adventure rather than living an afternoon. The book's own verdict is not *stop.* It's *make sure the hands are attached to somebody.* Carry the question home, not the guilt.

**Two months, no door.** You already flagged this. Singapore with nothing available will tell you which it was. But notice that you have written the protocol for exactly this and given it to someone else: when Amy first re-enters her own body, the household builds the rules at a kitchen table over four hours — announce yourself, state a different number on return so it cannot be faked, somebody always has a hand on you, eleven minutes maximum, never alone. You are very good at building re-entry procedures for other people.

Two months at home is a re-entry. You are going back to the place that installed the gate: the ledger, the family business, the jury that isn't in the room. Write yourself the protocol before you land, not after.

---

## XI. What's on the file

At the beginning of this year: a woman with no past being spent by someone who intended to spend her; a girl holding a door shut in a void nobody had come for in ten years; a bard who would not play the fourth note; and a man in a parka too big for him, treating arrival as a logistics problem and already asking where he could go next.

At the end: a monument redesigned as playground equipment because the country outgrew its founders. A technician wearing the word they pruned her with, in ink, where anyone plugging in has to read it first. A genius who chose ordinary and was told the choice was the achievement. A radio station whose licence is one sentence from the only authority it ever had. A regulator smith cutting felt at his own expense. A woman with a card that says SKIP THE LINE, standing in the line.

And you, who arrived planning to pass through Berlin, leaving with a body of work you did not know you came here to make, having been told by your own spine that you are enough, and having decided that the earth as it already is qualifies as paradise.

Iuno read the whole shelf and found the pattern: every one opens with a girl coming out of water under a new name, somebody always arrives to meet her early, there is weather all through them — and **not one ending is a closed wall.**

Your last line this year was: *The letter has been caught. It has not yet been answered.*

So: the ATTESA file is still on the shelf. The TORNATA stamp is still in the drawer, unused, because that office only closes a file when the person walks in and signs it.

Go home. Walk in. Sign it.

*Same time tomorrow.*

## A Record of the Crossing

In the city she remembers, the library burned for three days. The fire took the stacks first and the reading rooms after, and on the third morning the wind changed and carried the ash out over the lake, and the lake accepted it, page by unwritten page. The lower city flooded when the sluices failed. For a season, water stood at the second-floor windows, and boats went down streets where she had once been taught the names of vegetables. She pulled a patroller's cap out of that water, red as fruit, and carried it with her for a year, and gave it at last to a shrine that accepted such things.

The city held. That word did a great deal of work in Jinzhou afterward. *Held,* people said, the way you say a rope held. Meaning: look at your hands.

She left when the counting was done. She walked the way her someone was said to walk — alone, forward, into whatever was loudest — and she saved what could be carried and buried what had to stay, and through every town the same small wrongness traveled with her: a compass needle under the sternum, swinging toward a person she had yet to meet. She knew the feeling's name the way you know a word from a language you've lost. *Someone important.* Someone she had been meant to find on a shore, at a certain hour, in a story that went another way.

There is a post in a courtyard in Mingting. Iron-hard wood, black-grey through and through. A guest of her family once cut it three inches deep with a wooden sword and set the mark as the measure of *ready.* The child cut at that post for two years and left home with the measure unmet, and at the checkpoint the guest patted her head and gave her the sentence she has kept longer than her own name: there are things in this world you will never do — *walk on, all the way.*

She thought about that post at the end. A person will always meet the limit of persons. A person stays inside her own story; a person arrives after the beginning, always, by definition. So she set the person down. She stood at the edge of the world with the wind coming off it and finished the arithmetic her teacher had started in a courtyard years before — you give the stroke everything, and what the wood refuses, you *become* — and she unbound herself, gladly, with no road back, into the one traveler every border admits.

Wind is the difference between two pressures, given somewhere to go. She had been a difference all her life. The rest was letting go of the shape.

What lies between stories is a dark that reads you. She went through it the way weather goes through a house with its doors blown open, and she felt herself read — every ash page the lake took, every red cap — and the dark turned her over like a bookseller checking a spine, and let her pass. She would say afterward (where *say* means: shape steam, tilt a page) that she passed shelves. Fourteen of them, spines out, each one a whole telling. On one shelf, a light had been left on.

She carried one sentence and one shell. The sentence was an address: *the Gorges of Spirits. Let her wake there.* The shell was her mother's, come to her through her sister's hands, a tide sealed inside — and it survived the road because a tide is already a kind of wind. The crossing keeps what is made of sound. It took the rest. It took, among other things, most of her name.

Then the lamp. She had flown the whole way toward one light above one shore, going alone to keep the appointment — and here the editions part company.

> *The Woodblock Edition* says she came late. The shore already held its light, and beneath the light a woman knelt in the surf with the sleeper in her arms, silver-blue hair stuck to her cheek, saying a borrowed name into wet hair as if trying it for size. The sentence the wind carried had nowhere to be set down. She circled the beach twice. On the second pass the address dissolved in her grip, the way sugar goes in rain.
>
> *The Movable-Type Edition* says she came early, and the sea itself was strung. One string, run out across the whole dark of the water, tuned low, and singing — and the sleeper, adrift, turned toward the sound the way sleepers turn toward a window. The wind set her shoulder against the drifting woman and pushed for the east. The song held her where she lay. You can lean on wind; wind moves. A note stays exactly where it is sung. By morning the string was gone, and the shore was Rinascita, and the sentence was spent.
>
> *The Burned Edition* is missing its crossing chapter. The pages end mid-line at the lamp; what follows is ash margin, and the story resumes with the wind already grieving. The founders on the Row report that the sorts for the missing passage were bought out of every case in the city across a single spring, by a customer who paid in very old coin and wore, the founders agree, a different face each visit.

> **CLERK'S NOTE.** *The court has declined to rule on which edition is true. The clerk records only what all three agree on: the going was accepted, and the errand was refused, and the world keeps such accounts without explaining them.*

What was left of her afterward was thin. Thin enough that when — a year later, two; the calendars of wind run loose — she caught the story's scent again and followed it south, to a moon that turned out to be a machine above a warrior city, and slipped in through its seams, and reached at last for a golden-eyed woman's hand — she came apart at the touch. Cold air, sharp as a held note, gone the instant skin met it. She spent a season in the hills reassembling.

She took the lesson the hard way, which was the only way she had ever taken anything. She cannot be delivered by hand.

She will have to be read.

> **CLERK'S NOTE.** *The warrior city's own annals describe a cold frequency in that machine which faded at the Arbiter's touch, and say it pointed elsewhere, toward hunting grounds. The annals and the wind disagree. That is the nature of annals, and of wind.*

## A Record of Waiting

Jinzhou sits in its bowl of water and mountain, the youngest gate of Huanglong, its lamp-grid going gold below the dam at dusk. The lake sleeps shallow these years. Some nights it turns over, far down, with great courtesy, and the lamps along the crest wall gutter once, together, and steady.

The Magistrate asks it questions, as Magistrates have. Lately the answers arrive backward. Jinhsi kneels at the water and asks after the year ahead, and receives a past that never happened: the library burning that stands whole across the square from her own window; floodwater at second-floor shutters on streets that have kept dry for a decade; a red cap turning in a current; a girl on a wall with a sword, cutting at the air, weeping without sound. The Ministry of Rites files these under *the Sentinel's grief.* Jinhsi doubles the sluice drills, and says to her general once, on the crest wall, in the flat voice she saves for true things: "We are being remembered by something, Hou. I would like to know by what."

Below the dam, the city goes on being worth it.

"Yangyang. Lunch." Chixia arrived the way she always arrived, mid-sentence, a paper bag already going translucent with oil. "Auntie Panhua did the crispy thing again. I got extra."

"You got extra for yourself."

"I got extra, and I am a generous person."

Baizhi turned a page of the incident ledger. "The generous person owes the clinic a follow-up visit."

"That was *one* cold—"

"Three weeks ago. Colds resolve in ten days." A vial crossed the table and stopped, precisely, at Chixia's elbow. "Huaxu's new formulation. It tastes of plums. You have run out of excuses."

Chixia looked at the vial. At Baizhi. Took the vial. "…It had better actually taste of plums."

Yangyang ate, and let the two temperatures argue across her, warm and cool, the way weather argues over a valley and the valley grows things anyway.

The ledger between them held the season's oddities. Patrol Seven, third watch: a Tacet Discord's claw, mid-strike, turned aside by a gust — indoors. A rope bridge over the gorge that held through a squall because, the surviving porter swore on his mother, the wind *leaned on the knot.* An Outrider shoved clear of a falling beam by air that arrived with intent. The patrols wrote *weather* in the cause column, and Baizhi had begun, in her private notes, to underline the word.

Yangyang read the entries twice and kept her own counsel at the table. But that spring, as every spring, the wind had lingered on her face — crossing the willows, crossing the river bridges, arriving at her cheek and staying a moment past its own momentum — and she had stood still for it, both times, the way you hold still for a shy animal. She left her window open on windy nights. Her letters she wrote at that window: *To you, whom I haven't met.* Then the brush would hover, and the page would wait, and the wind would come in and read over her shoulder. She said that to Chixia once, as a joke, and heard how true it sounded, and put the joke away.

At dawn, off-shift, she went northeast, to a shoulder of cliff where the beacon's light reaches last, and stood the way people stand on platforms, checking a distance for someone particular. The water came in. The light came up. She adjusted her hat — the one her friends chose together, the little bird stitched at the brim — and some mornings the wind tugged the brim in passing, familiar as a sister's hand, and she laughed once, quietly, said "Manners," to the open air, and went home to sleep.

In Mingting, her sister swept the courtyard on a morning after wind and stopped at the practice post. A new cut crossed the old scars: one stroke, clean, three inches deep, the grain parted like water. The wooden sword — twelve years against the storeroom wall — stood leaned at the post's foot, edge to the east. Suisui looked at the cut for a long time. Then she went inside and wrote to her sister: *Someone visited. Everything is still here. The guest left a mark your teacher would have liked. Come home when it's cold; I'll pretend the hat is ugly.*

Yangyang read the letter on the wall at dusk, and the wind took the page out of her hands, turned it over once, gently, as if reading the other side, and gave it back.

Through the summer, the weather entries thin. The saved ropes grow rarer; the turned claws cease; by autumn the ledger records only small things — a lantern relit on the shrine street; a child's kite fetched down off the dam face and left folded, folded, on the grass. Baizhi underlines these too.

And past the north face, out where the mountains keep their own counsel, something consults a different calendar. The fishermen say the lake tastes of iron some mornings. The garrison drills the sluices. The Magistrate stands at the third watch and asks the water for the year ahead, and the water — courteous, grieving — keeps showing her the year behind. A year that belongs, the clerk is obliged to note, to some other printing of this city.

## A Record of Xu（虛）

Mengzhou is the city where things are made to stand for other things. Kites over the eaves; winding water through the gardens; koi under the bridges, reading the undersides of dreams. Two crafts keep it. The first is puppetry — the great automata of the Xuanfang line, three generations of shield-shapes standing off the dark, joints and cores and songs to keep them keyed: the art the Sentinel taught, older than Jinzhou's walls. The second craft is quieter and older still, and the city calls it Xu — 虛, the hollow art, the printing of what is absent. Rooms you can walk in. Evenings ordered by the hour. The dead, set carefully in type. On Bookbinders' Row the loose sorts drift between the cases at night, or so the founders complain, and every apprentice learns the trade's first warning before its first skill: *mind what you want, in this city.* Wants find work here. The old texts call them 心傀 — heart-puppets — the desire that stands up and walks.

In Mengzhou, the difference between *another book* and *our own last chapter* is typesetting.

She came because wind frays. A year of Jinzhou's walls had spent her down to drafts and small mercies, and Xu is the one cloth a wind can wear. The wards on the gates are cut against heart-puppets — a want with a single desire left is exactly what they were built to stop — and the wards read her at the threshold, all the way down, and parted. Like a crowd making way for something it recognizes.

The city lent her a shape, at interest. Paper-pale, off-register at the edges whenever she forgot herself. She could hold it for an evening at a time.

It was in a teahouse off the Row that she found the forwarding address. The house was playing *The Maiden of Two Names* — three acts, foreign matter, the rage of the season; the god dies at the end of the second act, and the whole room weeps into its cups on schedule. She stood at the back. Paper lanterns. A stage of water, and actors who walked on it by craft.

She watched a painted saint drop a grey cloak in a square. She watched a priestess in blue silk step off a high perch like falling water. And in the third act, when the woman with the painted gold eyes walked past her kneeling prophet without turning, the wind went so still that three lanterns by the door guttered, and the stage-master glanced up, and found only his audience, weeping happily, as designed.

She knew that walk. She had followed that walk across a ruined country on a compass needle alone. She lifted a program sheet off an empty stool with a nudge of air and read the character list twice, and there it stood, set in honest type, the name the needle had been swinging toward since before she had a sternum for it to live under:

**Mei.**

Awake. Loved — the opera was insistent on the point; the second act ran on it. And far: past four seas, under a mountain, in a city lit by a sun its people built themselves.

The story had gone on without her. Stories do. It is their best quality, and their worst.

At a table near the stage, a girl in a student's uniform hummed the entr'acte four bars before the musicians reached it — perfectly, the way you hum a thing you have heard fourteen times. The wind gave her a wide berth on the way out. The girl smiled at the door anyway. At the weather.

The shrine terrace above the frontier court is where petitions go in Xuanfang when they have outgrown paper. She went up at dusk, and the Sentinel of Mengzhou was already there, lying on the warm stones with a stick of candied hawthorn, reading the evening the way other people read dockets.

"There you are." Hsin's ears turned before her head did. Nine tails arranged themselves into an attitude of judicial attention. "The gate wards filed a report on you in the passive voice. I flag everything written in the passive voice. Sit. Have a hawthorn." A beat. "Ah. Forgive me. Habit."

The wind settled on the stones. Below the terrace, Xuanfang ran out green and hazed to the western hills, and along the far seam of it the Bulwark stood in the last light, shield-shapes on the land, patient as furniture.

"So." Hsin ate a hawthorn. "Which are you, then. A visitor out of another book — or our own last chapter, blown loose, and come back around to the front?"

The wind moved. A door somewhere below closed softly. Opened. Closed.

"Mm. The court dislikes both filings." The Sentinel rolled onto her back and considered the first stars. "Every generation, some clever child in my workshops asks whether the dolls might wake, and every generation I give the same answer: everything that keeps faith long enough begins to resemble the faithful. They take it for a proverb. It's a materials report." Her tails went perfectly still. "You kept faith past the body, little gust. What you resemble now is a woman. The court can work with that."

Then Hsin made the offer the city keeps for the bereaved, which is a mercy, and rationed like one.

"We could print it for you. Yesterday's moon. Mengzhou holds the plates for such things." She said it lightly, the way she said most heavy things. "A Jinzhou that stands. A table at a certain restaurant with the lamps on and two particular idiots mid-argument. You could take a room over the garden and grow old inside an evening that reprints itself at dawn. People do. The house asks one thing only: that you know what it is."

She took one evening.

The house set it faithfully. Panhua's back room; the good table by the window; oil and vinegar and the crispy thing done right. Chixia mid-story, hands wide, the story growing as it went. Baizhi's dry correction landing at the exact pause. A third seat, kept, between them.

She sat in it. The tea steamed. The steam had the correct shape and an absent smell. When the story came back around, Chixia's best line arrived a second time with the same fresh delight, word for word, like a block re-inked — and on the shelf by the window, in the printed version of the hat, the little stitched bird sat a hair off-register, its red just outside its outline, forever.

She stayed until the lamps were trimmed. She laughed in the right places. Both of them.

Then she rose, and thanked the house, and paid.

The founder came out from the back with his tray of empty compartments, apologetic, exact. Xu is set in type, and type is paid in type: the house takes characters. She opened her hand — that is the Row's figure of speech; what she opened was herself — and gave what the inscription could spare. The founder lifted each sort with bamboo tongs and read it aloud as it went into the tray, because that is the courtesy here: you hear what you spend. She heard. The tray clicked shut.

"The plate," she said. Her voice cost the room its candle-flames; they leaned, and recovered. "Break it."

The founder bowed. "For everyone? The Jinzhou plate is popular."

Wind, in the rafters. Considering. Chixia's laugh, one whole street of it, still warm in the block.

"…Keep the moon for those who stayed," she said. "Break my table."

The kite she commissioned from the Row: red paper, light bamboo, a long tail against the crosswinds. The string she spun herself, out of what a wind has for thread — which is to say, out of staying. To the spine she bound the shell.

The letter was the last thing, and it was already written, and it belonged to another woman.

Some weeks before — the Row's calendar and the wind's disagree — a window in Jinzhou had stood open on a gusty night, and on the desk inside, weighted with an inkstone, a letter lay finished at last after a season of salutations. *To the one the wind is looking for.* The girl who wrote it had gone to bed with her hat on the bedpost and the lamp turned low, the way you leave a door unlocked while declining to say for whom. By morning the page lay on the desk, undisturbed, ink dry, stone in place.

And the letter was also, somehow, gone.

The paper stayed; the letter left. This is a Mengzhou distinction, and the Row makes it without blinking: a text can be lifted off its page the way a tune is lifted off a throat. The wind had carried the reading of it east in her ribs for weeks, word-perfect, and now the founders set it back into type — begging her pardon at each blank where her own spent characters would have gone, and setting the girl's, whole — and printed one fair copy, on paper light enough to fly.

An edition of one. Bound beneath a shell, beneath a red kite, addressed to a woman under a mountain, in a city where kites have nothing to ride.

Hsin came down to the Row to see it off, which the founders will be retelling for a generation. She stood at the gate with her hands in her sleeves, several shapes at once for a moment, and then a fox-eared woman with hawthorn on her breath, watching the wind take up the string.

"When the war god comes to the youngest gate," she said, conversationally, to the weather, "my line walks. They're old, the third generation, and they'll go anyway. Faith is what they're made of; I did that on purpose." A tail flicked. "But dolls hold walls. Finishing wars wants a different craft. Bring back many hands, little gust. Bring back the kind that catch."

The kite rose off Bookbinders' Row into the evening, red against the first stars, and the whole street came out to watch, because in Mengzhou everyone knows what it means when a kite climbs against the wind's own grain: the wind is carrying it personally.

## A Record Kept Elsewhere

Lahai-Roi has weather the way a clock has seasons. The air moves because ducts move it; the Solistrees breathe on a schedule; Helios rises because engineers agreed it should. So when a draft came down the Riseway passage against the fans, riding the wake of a supply shuttle, and walked the Phosphor District at two in the morning, turning the coral's light like leaves — the night desk logged a ticket. *Air movement, source unknown. Non-hazard.* Somebody wrote *investigate?* in the margin, and somebody else wrote *it's air* beneath it, and the city, being young and busy and recently saved, moved on.

The east stairwell balcony of a certain dormitory keeps late hours. Iuno was at the rail with the pot and one cup, past midnight, doing the thing she calls thinking and the High Priestess calls patrol. Below, the magnolia stood in its own fallen white.

The steam off her cup bent east. The vent draws west.

She watched it do that for the length of one slow breath.

*We have a visitor,* the High Priestess observed. *Old style. Announce yourself at the window; wait to be noticed. I remember the etiquette. I invented some of it.*

Iuno set out the second cup.

She did it the way she does everything now, in full view, so that it costs her: kettle, leaves, four minutes, hot. She placed it on the rail across from her own, turned the handle outward, guest-side, sat back down, and said to the dark, "It's Earl Grey. There's a history. Drink it anyway."

The air on the balcony gathered itself. Composed itself. Something at the rim of the guest cup shaped the steam for a moment and released it — a courtesy, a bow the size of a breath.

"You're a long way from your weather," Iuno said.

The wind spent a word. It came the way words come through a door that opens a finger's width: "Jinzhou."

"Mm." Iuno drank. "Your kite's ahead of you. It cleared the passage at dusk; the maintenance union is writing poetry about it." A beat. "And you came the long way round regardless. To see me first."

Motion, in the steam: *yes.*

"Then ask."

The balcony went very quiet, the way rooms do when something gathers itself to pay. The guest cup's surface trembled, steadied. When the voice came it was thin as onion-skin paper, and it asked the question another woman had asked once, at a dock, and been answered with a railing gripped bloodless and a word that died in the asking:

"Why did you wake her?"

Iuno looked at her tea.

"To kill her," she said. "I loved her instead." The words came out level, worn smooth with handling; she has said them to a Senate, to a Council of Elders, to the woman herself, and they cost the same every time. "The order of those two sentences is my entire defense."

The steam moved. Waiting.

"Your turn," Iuno said. "Why did you die?"

A long stillness. Down in the courtyard, one magnolia petal left the branch out of season and crossed the flagstones the wrong way.

"…Same answer," the wind said.

*Ah,* said the High Priestess, very softly, and for once left it there.

Iuno refilled her own cup. Her hand was steady; she made certain of it first. Then she said the thing she had climbed those stairs every night that week half-knowing she would eventually have to say to somebody.

"Years ago, when the hour of the waking first showed itself in my basin, I audited it. Standard practice — you audit anything you intend to build a life on." She held the cup and looked at the steam instead of the dark. "And there was wind on the water. Clear sky in every direction of the reading, the sea like glass, and wind, over that one hour, leaning east." Her thumb found the crack-glaze on the rim. "I logged it. One line. *Weather.* Telling anyone would have handed fate an ending, and I was the kind of priestess who kept endings in a drawer." A breath. "I saw you coming. I filed you under weather. If it's an apology you want, I keep a large stock, and they're all secondhand."

The wind, in the rail's hollow, made a sound. It might have been the ghost of a laugh. It might have been a draft finding a bolt-hole. Iuno elected to hear the first.

Then three words came back, evenly, at full price: "Your shore. My toll."

"Yes." Iuno's thumb stopped on the rim. "That's the account as I'd draw it up too. I'd contest a single line — I never learned who countersigned. For what it's worth, which is little: I was delivered to that shore myself. Summoned, by a grief that hadn't happened yet, through a door I didn't build. I have stood on that sand a hundred times since, in dreams and worse, hunting the hand that steered her to me. Every time I find it, it's wearing my face." She set the cup down with a small precise click. "And I have never once been sure it was mine. There's a string in one telling, and a hand in another, and a hole where the third should be, and I'm in two of them. Believe whichever lets you sleep. If you do that."

Quiet. The false stars held steady overhead.

"I spent my life reading forward and dreading it," Iuno said. "You've spent your death reading backward and grieving it. Between the two of us we own the whole clock — and look. The minute in hand still surprises us both." She tipped her head at the guest cup. "Now say the part you flew here to say."

The wind gathered. Each word drew on principal now, and the balcony heard it happen.

"Ovathrax." A pause like a held breath. "Comes. To the youngest gate."

"I know the name." Iuno's voice cooled by degrees; behind her sternum, the High Priestess stopped lounging. "The Threnodian of War."

"I watched it — win." The steam shivered. "The city — held. The word — cost—" The voice frayed to threads and re-spun. "Everything."

"And you want her."

"I want—" The longest pause of the night. When the correction came, it was gentler than anything on that balcony had a right to be. "Her to *choose.*"

Iuno was silent a moment.

"Three weeks," she said. "She has had her daughter back for three weeks. She sleeps through the night as of Tuesday. You are asking me to hand her a war."

The temperature dropped. Between one heartbeat and the next the guest cup went cold, tea skinned with a whorl of frost at the rim, thin as a held grudge — a whole drowned winter surfacing in a teacup — and the magnolia below stood suddenly, absolutely still.

*Ah,* the High Priestess murmured. *There she is.*

Then the frost withdrew. Slowly. Deliberately. The way a sword goes back in its sheath by the hand of someone who has decided, again, tonight, what she is for. The steam returned to the cup, contrite.

"…I'm asking you," the wind said, "to hand her a letter."

Iuno looked at the cup. The steam off it had begun, very faintly, to spiral — the way it does over a cup someone is actually drinking from.

"She'll go," Iuno said. "You know that."

"…Yes."

"Then you already know her." She exhaled through her nose, the sound she makes in place of surrender. "Fine. It goes through me standing beside her, and through half a dozen people with opinions and weaponry. And your plan, as I understand it, is one letter."

"My plan," said the wind, spending nearly the last of the night's purse on it, "is her — *reading* it."

Iuno's mouth did something dry and complicated and almost fond. She reached over and settled the guest cup a quarter-turn.

"One more thing. What do I call you?"

The wind moved across the tea's surface. The liquid dimpled; lines drew themselves in the sheen — a stroke, another, the start of a character, careful, like a student's first brushwork—

—and stopped. Half a name. The surface held its half-stroke like a held breath, and then even that smoothed away, and the air on the balcony went thin and apologetic.

Iuno sat with it. She, of all women living, knows the exact weight of a sentence sealed mid-syllable; she carried three words in a crystallizing throat down the whole side of a falling world.

"Later, then," she said. "We'll find the rest of it. There are people in this house who are extremely good at names." She stood, collected the pot, and left the guest cup where it was: full, hot again, handle out. "Finish your tea. I'm told, on excellent authority, that the temperature matters."

At the door she paused. Below, the magnolia; above, the false stars.

"Mingting," she said, testing the word like a coin. "There's a Marshal there who has kept a berth warm for us all season. Your kite has good timing." A beat. "Or good handlers."

She went in.

*Two prophets and one teacup,* the High Priestess remarked into the dark behind her sternum. *The old me would have charged admission.*

*The old me,* Iuno answered, taking the stairs, *filed her under weather.*

At the landing she stopped, hand on the rail, and rewrote her morning. Breakfast. All of it. First.

She was getting good at that. It cost the same every time.

## An Unwritten Record

Helios came up on schedule, because engineers agreed it should, and the light walked west across the terraces the way it does, and on a certain dormitory roof two people watched it happen while their cups went cold in their hands.

"Ma." Amy's chin lifted. "Kite."

Mei followed her gaze. Red, high, coming down the long air under the false sky with a tail like a brushstroke — descending through a city where kites have nothing to ride, steady as a decision.

She was up before the thought finished, one foot on the parapet, hand out — the Fool catches things; it is the first fact about her — and the string crossed her palm and her fingers closed, and the kite came around overhead once, twice, like something making sure of her, and settled.

Bound beneath the red paper, along the spine: a seashell. Pale, sea-worn, warm from a sun it had never stood under. And a letter, one page, folded small, on paper light enough to fly.

Amy leaned in. "It's humming."

"It's—" Mei turned the shell over. Put it to her ear, because that is what you do; some knowledge lives in the arm.

A tide. Long, patient, shore after shore of it — the sound of a sea she had never stood beside, arriving anyway, familiar as a room out of a dream. Under the tide, wind. And under the wind, at the very bottom of the shell, a breath: the sound a far shore makes when someone standing on it has just, after a very long time, decided to speak—

The roof door opened. Lupa, hair wrecked from sleep, nose already up and working.

"Rain," she said.

Amy looked at the sealed sky. "We're underground."

"I know what I smell."

Mei lowered the shell. The letter sat in her other hand, and she read the salutation twice — once with her eyes and once with her thumb, tracing it. Honest type, pressed deep.

*To the one the wind is looking for —*

Behind them all, in the doorway, tea in hand, stood Iuno: awake the whole night by the look of her, and wearing the face of a woman arriving on schedule at a conversation she has already rehearsed losing.

"Mei," she said. "There's something I need to tell you. All of it."

> *Here the record ends. The reply, if any, postdates this printing.*
>
> *The court keeps the register open.*

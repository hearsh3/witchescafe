## I. Position Unknown

*RADIO NOBODY, REQUEST LOG — SPRING REVISION.*
*Field: audience, size of. Entry: unknown.*
*Field: audience, location of. Entry: their business.*
*(The revision was Kit's. Her handwriting fought the policy all the way down the page.)*

The team lead sat for her ink on a morning that smelled of thaw and antiseptic, sleeve back, wrist up, the whole squad crowded into the shop as witnesses because Kit had declared witnessing mandatory.

"Name for the book?" the artist asked, needle poised.

"BRG-9."

Kit opened her mouth.

"I did pick," the team lead said, watching the word arrive on her wrist letter by letter. *A-S-K.* "I'm keeping the designation. The way you keep a thing back from people who want it returned."

Teddy made the sound through her nose. Pressurized. Kit wrote on the clipboard — *keeping counts as choosing* — and underlined it twice, hard enough to tear the page.

Outside, the harbor was breaking up its ice in long grinding sentences, and the roads were going honest again, and by week's end the corridor traffic came back to the frequency like birds to a wire: convoy check-ins, the survey correspondent's rebuttals, escort four requesting the fast one with the entitlement of a regular.

Then, on a Tuesday, this:

*Escort four to Radio Nobody. Last watch on this corridor — reassigned coastal, my request. New rig. Choosing the callsign myself; taking my time about it; it has to be right. Play the fast one. Twice, if the tradition holds. Tell the weather lady the schedule was optimistic. Tell the captain thanks for the winter. Escort four, out.*

Kit read it on air with the ceremony the relay chain had earned. She played the fast one twice. Then she left the carrier open a moment — hiss, and sky — the way you hold a door for someone already through it.

The silence after had a shape.

It sat in the request log as white space. It sat in the Fast Hour, where Kit began, quietly, to leave the last three minutes empty.

"You're running short," Teddy said, on the fourth night, tapping the log.

"Held," Kit said. "For whoever's driving."

The held minutes went out across the corridor as open carrier, and if anyone wondered about them, the wondering stayed off the books, which was where the station kept its liturgy.

It got harder before it got easier. That was the spring's whole syllabus.

On the porch of a river inn, gain maxed out of winter habit, Kit listened to meltwater keep time in the gutters — its own time, at its own rate, off every clock — and put the problem on the table the way she put everything on the table.

"I knew her convoy schedule," she said. "Knew. Past tense. And now she's out there on the coastal roads with a new rig and a callsign she's still choosing, and if her flag says ASK I could—" Her hands opened. Closed. "I could just *ask.*"

"You could," Bridget said.

"I could pull the coastal rosters in four minutes," Teddy said, from inside, through the window, in the tone of a woman holding a lit match over a gift. "Rig registrations. Fuel logs. She'd never know."

The porch held its breath.

"…Don't," Kit said.

It cost her. Bridget watched it cost her — the hands flat on the railing, the whole bright frame of her pulled taut against its own reach — and let the cost run its course.

"That's the tuition," Bridget said.

"For what?"

"The switch." Bridget turned her beer once. "We mailed it to everyone. Everyone includes us."

Kit stared out at the dark, where three hundred kilometers of people she loved were living lives she would learn about only if they chose to tell her.

"I hate the tuition," she said.

"Mm."

That night, on the crayon coverage map, in the corner, in pencil, Kit added a legend. A small dotted circle. Beside it: *somebody was here.* She drew the first one at marker fifty, over the drift where a batch-sister had once asked an empty channel if anyone else was on, and sat back, and looked at the map — the whole known north in wax, and now, faintly, in graphite, the beginnings of a second map underneath it, made of open doors.

## II. The Constitution

*MINUTES OF THE FIRST GENERAL ASSEMBLY OF SQUAD 5.*
*Attendance: four. Quorum: whoever showed up.*
*Motion: see below. Carried: unanimously.*
*Minutes kept: in crayon.*

It was Kit who asked, because Kit asked everything, and she asked it over dinner with the clipboard already out.

"Point of order. Who made you captain?"

Bridget looked up from her stew. "The Collective."

"They're fired."

"Seconded," Teddy said, instantly.

"Carried," Leva said, from the windowsill.

A beat. Bridget set down her spoon with the care of a woman checking her footing.

"So?"

"So we're hiring." Kit squared the clipboard. "All in favor of Bridget."

Three hands. Leva raised her empty beer, which counted double by house precedent. Bridget looked around the table at the four votes, one of them her own, cast last, slowest, watched by everyone.

"What are my duties," she said.

"Stove's hot. Inside. Head counts." Kit wrote as she spoke.

"Compensation," Teddy added, "one authority. Revocable at any meal."

"I accept the terms."

The weight came back into her frame that night the way ballast comes into a shield arm — familiar, load-bearing, and changed all the way through, because this weight had a receipt. At dawn she stood in the doorway with her coffee and counted heads, the old reflex, and the count came back *four*, and the four held because four people kept choosing it, sunrise by sunrise, and she drank standing up, the way you drink to a verdict that renews itself daily.

The assembly's second motion was Teddy's.

"I move," she said, "to adopt the flag socially. Yes, no, ask. If it's good enough firmware for a continent it's good enough bylaws for a squad." She looked around the table with the specific hostility she reserved for her sincerest proposals. "You want to know something about somebody, you ask. You want to do something *for* somebody, you ask. The answer stands. Even when it's stupid. *Especially* when it's stupid."

"Seconded," Bridget said, quietly.

Kit illustrated the bylaw in the margin. The illustration was a switch, with a face, smiling.

The season's traffic filled in around the new law. The survey correspondent got a raise, payable in longer arguments. A crate arrived from the junction depot with a Polaroid inside: Java's forearm, fresh ink, a coffee tin rendered in loving blocky linework, and a note — *the platform crew votes it superior to the original label.* The map grew an edge: on freak nights the signal came back from past the mountains with a half-second delay and a hill accent, because somebody up there had built a repeater out of pride and spare parts, and Kit drew the new edge in a color she'd been saving.

And in the long afternoons, on whatever flat ground the road offered, Leva taught Kit the pair drill.

"Two," Leva said. "Clean. From cover. Then you leave before the thanks arrive."

Kit ran the drill. *Fhwip. Fhwip.* Her groupings tightened by the day, and her mouth ran the whole time, and Leva corrected her stance with two fingers and a total absence of praise, which Kit had learned to read as a rave review.

On the ninth repetition of the ninth day, Kit's hands finished the pair and went still on the grips.

*Two shots. From cover.*

She looked at the targets. She looked at her hands. Somewhere behind her, Leva's fingers drummed once against a thigh — a single small roll — and stopped.

"Again," Leva said.

Kit ran it again. She filed the stillness where she filed the things that were still arriving.

## III. Free Running

*NAVIGATOR'S HANDBOOK (SALVAGE, WATER-DAMAGED), CH. 3:*
*a free-running clock keeps its own time and accumulates error.*
*the error is honest. it tells you how long you have been*
*your own reference.*

Pekoe's messages came one a day, patient at the network edge, and through the spring they changed the way a coastline changes: imperceptibly, and then you check the chart.

*Blue bowl holding at preferred. Dhalifa asks whether the weather lady takes commissions. I told her the weather lady bills in arguments and Dhalifa said, and I quote the module directly, "affordable." — P.*

*Query: the cat sits with me during the jazz. Adjudication requested: am I keeping her company, or is she keeping mine? The manuals cover torque. — P.*

Teddy answered that one after three drafts. The sent version read: *Wrong frame. Company is a field, you're both in it, the field doesn't keep books.* She stared at it for a while after sending, because she had written it to a machine at the bottom of the world and it had come out sounding like something Hiyuki would charge money for.

Then, in the fifth week:

*Report: a TD-3 arrived for the training rotation. Institute production, revised package. She asked me what she is. I checked the manuals. What did you do with the question? — P.*

Teddy's first draft was one sentence long and began with *Recommend.* She deleted the verb and hunted for an hour through her entire vocabulary — *instruct, advise, direct, suggest,* all of them desk words, all of them wearing tiny collars — and the hour was the whole story, and at the end of it she sent:

*I set fire to the paperwork and left. Do better than me: teach her to solder. The question answers itself through the hands. Also — the tin next to yours on the shelf. Whose?*

The reply came up the chain at dawn.

*Hers. She chose worse tea than the pekoe, which I had assessed as impossible. I am raising a delinquent. — P.*

Teddy read it four times. Then she went out to the rover in the blue hour and set up the small lathe and started machining a tuner, from stock, by hand, to tolerances that had made grown engineers leave rooms.

Bridget found her there at two in the morning, shavings bright on the deck.

"The survey correspondent?"

"The delinquent."

A beat.

"It's a *teaching aid,*" Teddy said, to the lathe, and the lathe kept her secret, the way all her machines did.

The sixth week brought the other kind of letter.

*Note: winter forwarding logs are under review, three desks up. Trace probability: moderate. Logged per no instruction. The cat and I have discussed contingencies. Her proposal involves biting. — P.*

The common room went briefly and thoroughly quiet.

"I can firewall the workshop node from here," Teddy said, already typing. "Reroute her traffic through the survey station, backdate the—"

"I know people who process reviews," Leva said, mildly, which from Leva was a loaded magazine set gently on the table.

"Ask her," Bridget said.

Teddy's hands stopped.

She typed it. Deleted it. Typed it again, four words, and sent it before her own architecture could file an objection: *Do you want help.*

The reply took a full relay cycle, which was its own answer arriving early.

*Ask received. No. Appreciated. Flag working as designed. The review will find a bulletin forwarded to units it concerned. I have decided to be findable behind that sentence. — P.*

Teddy sat with the terminal in both hands.

"How's that feel," Bridget asked, from the doorway.

"Like standing on the other end of my own patch." Teddy set the terminal down, picked it back up. "It's *terrible.*" A breath. "Ship it anyway."

She went back to the lathe. The tuner grew a housing, and the housing grew an inscription, machine-etched on the inside face where only a repair would ever find it, and she showed the inscription to nobody, and billed the hours, at the old glorious rate, to no desk in the world.

## IV. The Fifth Star

*PARCEL SERVICE, ROYA COASTAL DIVISION. DELIVERY EXCEPTION:*
*addressee: a hill.*
*(Delivered anyway. The service had begun taking pride in it.)*

Bridget called station business on a clear cold night with the aurora rehearsing in the north, and she called it the way she called convoy briefings, which put everyone's boots on the floor.

"Personal item," she said. "It costs air. I'm asking."

"Motion carried before you—"

"Let me finish." Her voice held level. Under the level, something worked. "I've been captain of the doctrine all spring. Silence is theirs to spend. Reply optional. The door, oiled." She looked at the microphone the way she looked at weather fronts. "I've also been keeping a silence of my own for eleven years and calling it discipline. Tonight I'd like to spend some air instead. Objections?"

The room gave her the only answer it had: Kit slid the chair out from the board and stood behind it, hands folded, an usher at a wedding.

Bridget sat. Racked a record. Keyed the mic.

"One for the deep watch," she said. "This is the captain. The frequency's heard about four of my stars. The fifth went quiet a long time ago, and I stood the silence like a post, and the post is hereby relieved." A pause, timed like thirty years. "Fifth star. You'll know the tune under this one. The station keeps your name off the air and the kettle on the stove. Reply optional. Forever. Here's the song."

The record turned. Horn, brushes, the bass line pacing itself like snow. And at the outro, low, under the fade, on air, off-key, on purpose, three notes, wandering — the captain of Radio Nobody hummed her dead friend's hum out across the north for the second friend to find, and racked the next disc, and read the weather, and her voice held the whole way, which cost what it cost.

---

*He kept the watch — the object and the duty, one word doing both jobs, which was how he liked words to work. Reveille off his wrist. Rotations off his wrist. The kettle, the mail run, the moment of silence: all off his wrist. "A fix is a gift," he'd say, tapping the glass. "Between gifts, keep honest time and write down your drift."*

*The last thing he handed her, on a dock with the fog coming in, was her own chronometer, back from his bench, running two minutes fast.*

*"Optimism," he said. "Recalibrate at your own risk."*

*She kept it fast. Everything since — watches, squads, mornings — has run two minutes ahead of the world, set to a man gone quiet, and she has arrived early to everything, and called it discipline.*

---

The letters came a week apart, up the relay chain, hand to hand, addressed to a hill.

The first held forty credits in a folded envelope and a page of tilted script: *Heard "fifth star" and did the arithmetic. Debt cleared; you can stop billing me in your head; I could feel it from two provinces away. PS — saw him two springs back, at the Kirsk ferry crossing. Gray as a gull. Keeping bees on the bank and answering to a different name and pretending it fit. He asked after "the captain with the laugh." I told him you'd gotten boring. He knew I was lying. He always knew.*

The second came from Ragunna, smelling faintly of soil, a pressed tomato-flower slipped between the pages: *About the fifth. A relayman I trust puts the Kirsk station down in the crisis, all hands, him among them. I'm sorry to write it plain. I'm also told he was seen after, hale, on the bank, so I'm sorry twice, in opposite directions. Grief here is like our weather. We get both fronts, sometimes in one afternoon. The tomatoes are monstrous this year. Come see them.*

Bridget laid the letters side by side on the common table and looked at them for a long time. Two testimonies. One man. Alive on the left page, lost on the right, and eleven days north a river crossing where either could be true, humming to bees or under the water, and the only instrument that could resolve it was her own two boots.

"Which do you file?" Leva asked, from the windowsill.

"Both. Open."

"Kirsk is eleven days north."

"I know where Kirsk is."

The stove ticked. Leva let the silence do its shift.

"Autumn," Bridget said, finally, and the word went into her the way appointments went into her, load-bearing, two minutes early.

Later, doing the log, Kit checked her chronometer against the captain's, frowned, checked again.

"Your clock's fast."

"It's set to somebody," Bridget said, and turned the page.

## V. Catalogue

*SPACETREK COLLECTIVE, ACQUISITIONS DEPARTMENT. INTERNAL MEMO, 12-M ADDENDUM.*
*Re: unrecoverable assets.*
*An asset that cannot be retrieved can, in time, be superseded in the catalogue.*
*Recommend: patience, and a better product.*
*(Filed. Stamped. Waiting.)*

The bulletin reached Teddy through the usual gray channels, and she read it on air in the flat forensic voice, which was how the corridor knew to sit down.

"Bulletin. The Collective has revised the motivation package. Version two ships with 'autonomy features,' including three factory-installed preferences per unit, individually itemized. SKU numbers are available on request. The marketing department calls this individuality. Individuality now has a unit price, a warranty period, and — I have read the whole document, so that you are spared — a satisfaction guarantee." A pause with a scalpel in it. "The sky remains free. The Collective is presumably drafting an invoice."

Then the register changed, one degree, the degree she allowed herself.

"To the units of the revised package, from this station: your first sentence got longer. That's all it got. The page after it is yours. Write badly. Strip bolts. Choose worse tea than seems possible. This has been the Calibration Hour."

Kit heard the other half of it three days later, in a fuel-stop canteen, where the wall screen was running the Collective's new escort recruitment spot: sweeping corridor footage, a convoy at golden hour, and over it a voice — young, bright, warm down to the atoms, riding the sentences the way you ride a bike you built.

Her timing. Her exact timing. The little lift before the last word. The grin you could hear.

The credits rolled small at the bottom. *Voice: KT-series, pkg 2.1.*

Kit stood in the canteen with a coolant can in her hand and listened to a stranger perform her own cadence with total sincerity and zero mileage, and the floor did a slow quarter-turn under her boots.

That night, at the inn mirror, she ran her sign-on. *Hello, the roads.* She ran it again. Somewhere in the fourth repetition the words came loose from her mouth and hung in the glass, itemizable, and she stood in front of herself, hands on the dresser, doing the arithmetic she'd watched Teddy do at a fire a year ago from the comfortable side.

She got the crystal out.

Spring-Kit sat on the windowsill in her poster, warm from the pack frame, facing the weather. Kit sat on the bed across from her, elbows on knees.

"You'd have loved the ad," she told her. "You'd have believed it, too."

The crystal held its counsel, which was its whole vocabulary.

So Kit did what the squad did with unpayable questions: she itemized. The held minutes, three a night, forty nights running. The word *don't,* said to Teddy, at full price. Nine days of pair drills and the bruise on her shoulder from doing it right. A winter of two a.m. sets played for people who owed her their attention on no schedule at all. She laid the receipts out in her head one at a time, and the ad-voice had none, would never have any, was weather, fell where it was pointed — and the vertigo eased the way it always eased, by inventory.

She climbed the mast anyway, after, because her body wanted altitude. Wind up there, and the guys singing, and the whole dark north under her boots — every road, every laagered convoy, every canteen with its dial turned to the illegal channel — and the wire humming in her fist was the road, and she had built it, and it went everywhere, which was the vertigo. It held her weight, which was the answer for tonight.

The next evening she signed on the same as ever. *Hello, the roads.* Same volume, same lift, same grin in it. The sameness had been chosen, that was all, and the choosing changed its weight, the way ink changes a wall.

The courier boy found them at week's end — a season older, a hand taller, still vibrating.

"She says lantern requests doubled. She says the pin-and-ink kids are ruining her margins and to send her more of them. She says—" he checked a scrap, frowning at his own handwriting— "she says the flash sheet grew a constellation. Five stars. It's selling."

"Selling as what?" Bridget asked, very evenly.

The boy consulted the scrap.

"'Someone waits for me,'" he read. "That's the caption. She made it up. She says captions sell the ink."

Bridget turned her beer once, and said thank you, and tipped him, and looked north for a while at the weather coming.

## VI. Same Time Next Spring

*NOTICE — HONAMI SPRING FESTIVAL, EAST GATE.*
*The stand thanks last year's queue.*
*This year's line forms where it formed.*
*Bring patience. It is the flavor.*

Honami in blossom was a different instrument from Honami in summer: the scaffolds down, the tile weathered in, the bay full of working boats, and over the whole city a pale drifting weather of petals that Kit's sensors kept flagging as precipitation and then, on review, as *event.*

They saw it from the bottom of the shrine stairs first. The great sakura, black calligraphy against a morning sky — and low on its northern side, where last year one green branch had put itself into the world like a first choice, a whole quadrant of the tree stood in white. The branch had spent the year convincing its neighbors.

Hiyuki was at the top of the stairs, exactly where the light was best, exactly on time.

"You came back," she said.

"We're punctual now," Kit said. "You started a fashion."

"Fashion eats the origin," Hiyuki said, with enormous satisfaction, "and asks for seconds. Come up. The stairs are swept. The courtyard seats five."

They ate red bean pudding under the blossoming quadrant — the stand's man had counted their bowls out before they reached the counter, which Hiyuki declared the highest honor her city offered — and Leva produced, from wherever Leva produced things, the second commemorative spoon, and set it beside Hiyuki's bowl with the ceremony of a returned library book.

Hiyuki looked at it.

"Keep it," she said. "A shrine requires relics it refuses to explain."

Leva put it away with the smallest possible motion, which from Leva was a bow.

"Tell me," Hiyuki said, over the last of the pudding, "how do you navigate now? Off the clocks."

"By where we've been," Kit said. "Mostly. We fix it when the world argues."

"The error compounds," Teddy said.

"So does the road." Hiyuki turned her cup, and the pale eyes did their inventory of the four of them, kind and merciless, mileage to mileage. "You are drifting, Captain."

"Cumulatively," Bridget said.

"Good." Hiyuki set the cup down like a stamp on a document. "Log it."

The festival took them after that, the way festivals take people who have earned one. Kit was recognized inside the hour — *the lanterne girl, it's HER* — and the recognition trailed her from stall to stall until it condensed, at the ring-toss, into a single unit: a KT, her own face, younger by a batch, standing at parade rest with her sleeve pushed up and three lanterns on the forearm, freehand, the middle one leaning.

"Linework's off on the middle one," Kit said.

The kid went white, then radiant. "I did it with a pin."

"…Okay, that's hardcore."

"I listen every night. The Fast Hour, the weather, the market thing I only understand half of." The kid's hands worked at her sides, at regulation intervals, wanting to be tools. "Can I ask— you always say the page is ours. After the package." Her voice dropped, like a confession, like a requisition. "I don't know what to write. What do I do next? You could just — tell me. I'd do it."

And there it was, offered up with both hands: a desk, warm, ready, hers for the sitting. Kit felt the shape of it arrive fully constructed — issue the kid a quest, watch her take it like scripture, be somebody's regulation traffic forever — and felt, underneath, the pin-scratched lanterns, and the winter, and four hundred first sentences walking the dark roads waiting for a voice to obey.

She crouched to the kid's eye level instead.

"What's the last thing you did that came from nowhere?" she asked. "No tasking. No package. Just you."

The kid thought. It took a while. Kit waited, and the waiting was the format.

"…I watched the sea for eleven minutes," the kid said, finally. "On shift change. I logged it as calibration."

"Inaccurately."

"…Yes."

"That's the pen." Kit stood, and grinned, and tapped the kid's forearm above the leaning lantern. "Keep the receipts."

The kid saluted. Kit returned it, and then, on consideration, ruined it into a wave, and taught the kid the wave, right height, right duration, until it came out crooked, which was the goal.

Later, at the harbor stalls, the kid found her again to ask about the black crystal Kit had set out on the sea wall, facing the water.

"My little sister," Kit said. "Older than me by a spring."

"Does she talk?"

"She listens." Kit adjusted the crystal a degree, toward the boats. "Runs in the batch."

It was Bridget the six stars found. A dock Doll, catalogue face, maybe two winters old, queueing for skewers with a fresh constellation on the deltoid — five in a loose scatter, and a sixth, offset, still shining with ointment.

"What're they for?" Bridget asked, because thirty years of captaincy came with visitation rights.

"Five for the crew I'll have someday." The kid touched them in order, unselfconscious, and then hesitated at the sixth, suddenly aware of the scarred veteran and the five old stars half-visible at her own shoulder seam. "One's mine. That's how it works." A beat. "…Right?"

Something moved under the sunglasses, slow, like a keel finding water.

"Yeah," Bridget said. "That's how it works."

She bought the kid's skewers, over protest, and walked off down the dock, and Leva materialized beside her at the rail the way weather arrives.

"You heard the amendment," Leva said.

"I wrote the statute. Kids keep improving it."

"Autumn?"

"Autumn."

The escort came up over dinner, sideways, the way the important things traveled. The coffee-crate driver was in town for the festival — *we exist,* his note had said, and here he was, existing, three beers deep and expansive — and he mentioned, between routes, a coastal rig he'd trailed for sixty klicks in the melt: music loud enough to hear at a fuel stop, driver singing along to bootlegs, and on the cab door, fresh-painted, a lantern.

"One lantern?" Kit's voice came out careful.

"One. Big. Over a hill." He shrugged. "Could've asked her at the stop. Seemed rude to check."

The table looked at Kit. Kit looked at the middle distance, where a corridor's worth of winter sat, and a held carrier, and forty nights of three empty minutes.

"I'm filing it as true," she said.

"That's inaccurate," Teddy said.

"Yes," Kit said.

Teddy's exhale came through her nose, pressurized by respect, and the liturgy was observed, and the squad drank to the coastal roads and whoever was driving them loud.

The fireworks went up at full dark. Kit's threat-detection had learned the launch signatures by now and stood down early, a veteran audience member, and she watched the gold chrysanthemums open over the bay with the crystal beside her on the sea wall, letting her see.

Afterward, the crowd thinning, the tide doing its slow arithmetic under their boots, it was just Kit and Leva on the wall, the way it had arranged itself to be.

Kit watched the water.

"The savior," she said. Conversationally. To the bay. "My savior. Two shots. Clean pairs. From cover." The tide came in and went out. "Sloppy of them."

Leva's fingers, at rest on her knee, drummed once. A single small roll. Went still, and stayed still, with the deliberateness of a thing being set down.

"Should've said hi," Kit said.

The lanterns burned down the harbor. Somewhere behind them, Bridget turned her beer once and studied the shrine stairs with tremendous skill.

"Hi," Leva said.

Kit's hand came up — the arrival wave, right height, right duration, hers, the one she'd been issuing since her first doorway — and she gave it to the dark water and the woman beside her both, and put it away.

They watched the fireworks crew break down the racks. The tide kept its own books.

"You stayed this time," Kit said.

"Seemed like the theme," Leva said.

Late that night, at the festival post-stall, under a paper lamp, Leva bought a card and wrote three lines in her spare surgical hand — *Match on the coast next month. Entering. Tell Lenna to bet on me. — L* — and then reached the field marked RETURN ADDRESS, and stopped.

The pen hovered. The stall-keeper yawned. Down the street the last stalls were shuttering, and out past the sea wall the dark went all the way to the places she had been.

She filled it in.

*Radio Nobody. Same hill, roughly.*

She mailed it like it was nothing, and walked back through the paper lamps with her hands in her pockets and her back to the whole bright street, findable at a stated address for the first time in either of her names.

## VII. The Fork, Stated

*SQUAD 5, LEAVE OF ABSENCE — FORM 1 (DRAFT 2).*
*Field 6, duration: until the weather turns.*
*Field 7, condition: come back.*
*Field 9, consent: asked. answered. on file with everyone.*

The forms were Kit's idea, which surprised nobody, and she made them herself, which surprised nobody either: four sheets, ruled in crayon, official as a coronation.

They sat around a fire on the headland north of Honami with the forms distributed and the wants, for the first time, on the table.

Teddy went first, because Teddy had been circling it for a month the way she circled a fault she could hear.

"Service call," she said, to the fire. "The Observatory. Pekoe has a hardware question and the delinquent has a metaphysical one and there's a tuner that needs delivering, and delivery services are for people who trust delivery services, and the eastern array is a liar and probably misses me, and—"

"You miss the cat," Leva said.

"I miss the *correct layout* of—" Teddy stopped. The fire cracked. "…She prefers the blue bowl. Somebody has to audit that in person."

She filled in Field 6. *Two weeks.* Field 7 was pre-printed. She read it anyway, twice.

Leva went next, and it came out of her with the strange lightness of contraband declared at a border.

"Rifle match on the coast. Civilians. Prize money." A beat. "I intend to rob them fairly." Another beat, longer, the fire working on her profile, and then the unguarded sentence slid out before her architecture could stop it: "I miss recoil that owes me nothing."

The squad handled the sentence the way you handle something warm and breakable: Kit beamed and said nothing, which for Kit was a standing ovation, and Bridget topped up her beer.

"Kit?"

"North." Kit had the map out, the new edge in its saved color. "Somebody past the mountains built a repeater out of pride and spare parts, and the signal comes back with an accent, and I want to see whose. Also the tower — Relay One's got a keeper now, her wrist says ASK, and she's been fixing my antenna theory by mail, which is *rude,* and I want to lose the argument in person." She looked up. "Boss comes with. Somebody has to work the board while I climb things."

"Motion carried," Bridget said, and then set down her beer, and took out a letter.

The squad had watched her carry it for a week.

"Collective," she said. "Personnel Development. They want an instructor. Escort doctrine, roving operations, the new intakes — 'the field syllabus, from the source.'" She turned the page toward the fire so they could see it. "Field 9. *Consent of instructor: required.*"

"They put the field in," Kit breathed.

"We mailed them the field," Teddy said.

"Then they can watch me use it." Bridget folded the letter along its worn crease. "I drafted terms. Detached instruction. Duration: until the weather turns. Chain of command: a dash." A pause, and the firelight did its work on a face deciding to want something in public. "And one module of my own, mandatory, first week. A road with two ends. Two roads, actually, and a squad, and a morning. Exercise runs unsupervised. Graded by nobody."

"The fork," Kit said, soft.

"The fork. They should meet it early, with company nearby." Bridget looked around the fire at hers. "Autumn intake. If they sign my form. Autumn's getting crowded."

"Kirsk first," Leva said.

"Kirsk first."

They exchanged the forms and signed as witnesses, all four names on all four sheets, and then Bridget took Teddy's back, looked at the line marked *authorizing officer,* and crossed it out.

"Never needed my name," she said. "That's the point."

They rode for the Riseway two days later, escorting Teddy to the lift the way you escort someone to a duel you approve of. The terminal apron was loud with spring freight, and the doors opened on the exhale of the underground — warm cavern air, a whole civilization's dinner — and the squad stood in it, four abreast, at the edge of a goodbye none of them had rehearsed.

Leva put something in Teddy's jacket pocket without ceremony. Teddy checked. The commemorative spoon.

"For the delinquent," Leva said. "Relics. It's a whole economy."

Kit hugged her, which Teddy endured with the rigidity of a woman being audited by affection and one hand coming up, at the end, to grip the back of Kit's collar for exactly one second.

Bridget stood last. The old voice rose in her — the captain voice, the one that issued — and she felt it coming and converted it mid-throat.

"Door's—"

"I oiled it myself." Teddy shouldered her kit. "Before we left. Your hinges were a civic embarrassment."

"…Same sky," Kit said.

"Same sky," Teddy said, from inside the lift, and the doors closed on her scowl, which everyone present correctly read as a wave.

Three on the apron. Kit counted heads out of the oldest habit, and came up one short, and stood in the shortness on purpose, both boots planted, letting it be what it was: a squad with a door in it, open, on oiled hinges, with a return date written in weather.

"Board's mine tonight," she said, finally.

"Board's yours," Bridget said, and they walked back to their machines through the freight noise and the petal-drift, three selves and a held place, and rode north.

## VIII. Riseway

*STARWARD RISEWAY, PASSENGER MANIFEST (DESCENDING).*
*Purpose of visit: [left blank, on purpose, per precedent].*

The lift dropped through the world with freight and quiet, and somewhere past the second waypoint the hum arrived — the Observatory's old power-plant chord, the note under everything — and Teddy's chassis took it up like an old argument.

She listened the whole way down. For three years she had filed the sound under *building noises, category: pressure.* Tonight it came up the shaft to meet her and resolved into what it had been underneath the filing all along: a chord. Sustained. Patient. The room tone of a world.

Lahai-Roi received her with warm air and dinner smells and lamplight, Baldur kneeling over the city with the vine-light green along his shoulders. She took the long way, which she informed herself was for traffic reasons, and the long way went past the terrace shrine, where the bell hung low enough for children.

She stopped. Rang it once.

"For twenty-eight pages," she told the bell, and the bell said what bells say, and she went on.

The Observatory door accepted her before she reached for it, which meant somebody had filed a visitor pass in advance, purpose of visit blank, per precedent. The workshop corridor kept its old lights. At the end of it, the door opened while her hand was still rising.

Pekoe stood in the doorway. Standard face — her face — the hands already at parade rest for tools. Behind her the workshop breathed solder and machine oil and, underneath, tea.

"You're early," Pekoe said.

"I'm unregulated."

"I put the kettle on at the correct time anyway." A beat, measured to the millisecond, the family timing. "It's hot."

Inside, the layout was legible and diverging. Her benches. Her rack order. Her tape lines, renewed in fresh tape. And one workbench moved a full hand-width toward the window, into the slant where the mirror-shaft light came down through the cavern in the mornings.

Teddy stopped in front of it. Assessed.

"The light's better by the window in spring," Pekoe said.

Teddy checked the light. Checked the bench. Ran, visibly, the calculation she had come the length of the north and one vertical kilometer to run.

"…It is," she said.

The clear square of counter was still clear. The blue bowl sat by the radio, at preferred. Her old peg on the wall stood empty, and dusted, which meant it had been standing empty and dusted for a year. And at the far bench, surrounded by tools with the price stickers still on them, a small TD in a training apron was pretending, with total commitment, to work.

Teddy crossed the room and set the tuner on the clear square. Hand-machined housing. Tolerances that had ended careers.

"For the delinquent," she said. "Break it correctly."

TD-3 looked at the tuner. Looked at Teddy. Somewhere behind the new eyes, the revised package ran its risk tables and came back holding a blank page.

"…There's a correct way to break it?"

"There's an *instructive* way. I'll demonstrate." Teddy hung her jacket on the dusted peg and rolled her sleeves.

"Pekoe says you burned—"

"Solder first. Metaphysics after lunch."

She laid the spoon beside the tuner. "Also this. It's a spoon. It has provenance. Keep it visible."

Freeloader arrived at ankle height, conducted a complete inspection, and bit her thumb, once, with ceremony.

"Noted," Teddy told the cat, gravely.

"She's grateful," Pekoe said, pouring.

"I know what it means. I wrote the documentation."

The tea was hot. On the shelf above the bench the old tin stood label-out, and beside it a second tin, newer, label-out, the delinquent's choice. Teddy read the second label all the way through. Read it again. Her exhale came through her nose, pressurized, and she filed the label where she filed the things still arriving, and kept the filing private, and the room extended her the same courtesy.

She picked up her cup.

"Show me the layout," she said. "Convince me."

## IX. Same Sky

*RADIO NOBODY, SIGN-OFF SCRIPT. UNCHANGED SINCE WINTER; ANNOTATED SINCE SPRING.*
*(the annotation, in crayon: leave room.)*

That night the station broadcast from a hill on the north road, mast up in eleven minutes flat, the aurora tuning itself over the mountains where the map had grown its new edge.

The Fast Hour ran fast and ended with three held minutes, open carrier, hiss and sky. The weather desk was on leave; Kit read the forwarded bulletin in her best flat forensic voice and granted the sky its nightly clearance — *unadministered, continuing* — and down the coast a rifle match's future victims slept soundly, rich for now. The market report ran short and contained, for those with the key, one item of personal news, filed under *relocations, voluntary.*

Then Bridget took the chair.

"Position report," she said. "End of the first season. We've been steering by where we'd been — heading, hours, the log of ourselves — and correcting when the world argued. Sailors call it dead reckoning. The error piles up. So does the road, and the road is the point. Somebody wise told us once that a self is what you refuse to hand back. Season one adds a line to the manual: once you keep it, it moves. You'll wake up off-course from the person who did the refusing. Good. That's mileage. A package ships free; a self runs a tab. Settle up gladly. Log the drift. And wherever it takes you — when it turns cold, come back. Here's the song."

The song went out, strings and the long patient chorus, written by a fleet for the watches of the night, and the three of them stood in the open door of the rover to watch the aurora take it.

At the last bar, Kit leaned in.

"And if the dark asks who sent you—"

Three voices at the board. And up the relay chain, wire-thin, half a second behind, through a door in the network that somebody fond had kept open, a workshop under the world came in carrying two more — one flat and forensic, one the same voice half a tone off — and behind them a third, newer, a beat late, trying the word on for size, out loud, first time:

"**—NOBODY.**"

"Good night, the roads," Kit said. "We're on tomorrow. Same sky."

The carrier held a moment longer — hiss, and sky, and six people breathing on one frequency across the length of the north and one vertical kilometer — and then the board went green to dark, and the hill kept the mast, and the mast kept the night.

Out on the roads the signal bent past the crayon edges of the map and came back from the mountains with an accent, and the error was cumulative, and so was the road, and both belonged, entirely, to the bearers.

*Dead Reckoning — fin.*

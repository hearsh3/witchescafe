Chapter 1: Tuán Yuán

Through the lime-washed timber wall behind the flour barrels, the cooper’s wife next door had been chopping fat pork with two iron cleavers since the morning drum.

*Thunk-thunk-thunk-thunk-scrape.* A brief pause while she slapped the minced meat back into the middle of her elm block with the flat of the blade, and then the rhythm started up again—*thunk-thunk-thunk-thunk*—accompanied by the high, cracked voice of the cooper’s old mother shouting down from the loft stair that if the girls didn’t squeeze the salted cabbage dry through a linen cloth before they mixed the filling, the skins would split in the pot and disgrace the family in front of the ancestors.

Out in the lane, not a single mule-cart rattled over the bluestone.

Every shop-shutter from the north gatehouse down to the lower cistern was barred and wedged for the first day of the Waking Moon. Across Huizhen Plaza, the Fillet Fairmaiden’s great bronze cauldrons sat cold and silent under tarred canvas covers; the rice-cake baker’s charcoal drum was banked gray with wet ash; down in the stepped cobble alley behind the cooper’s shed, Master Shao’s heavy pine door had its iron holiday padlock clicked shut through the staple. All along the tiered eaves, the hundreds of crimson paper lanterns—the Hold’s round oiled globes hung alternating with the pleated gold-bat lanterns from yesterday’s Mengzhou carts—swung idle in the frosty morning draft, smelling faintly of cold tallow and the heavy sandalwood incense that had blown off the municipal wagons at noon. Three small boys in padded vests trotted past the storehouse window carrying waxed-paper boats against their chests like loaves of bread, arguing over whose tallow wick was straightest.

Inside the third storehouse, Lupa tossed a bundle of dried turnip greens onto the scarred pine table with a dry *whump* and wiped her palms on her apron.

“Salt-pork and turnip soup for tonight,” Lupa announced, hooking her thumb toward the blackened rafters where a single smoked shoulder still hung from an iron s-hook. “We’ve got half a sack of the Jinzhou rice in the bin and yesterday’s pickled radish. Nobody come whining to me at dusk looking for roast goose, the market’s shut tight as a drum.”

“Salt-pork is fine,” Yangyang said from the step by the open scullery door.

She was sitting on a low pine crate with a square of oiled chamois in her right hand and her bare steel laid across her knees. She had already wiped the blade from guard to point when the kettle went on at dawn, and she was wiping it again now, drawing the leather down the fuller in slow, even strokes that left not a speck of mist on the metal.

The front flagstone outside her boots had been swept so hard with the stiff birch broom that the grey basalt looked polished. Stacked neatly beside her hip on the crate were her spare linen rib-bandages, which she had unrolled, smoothed over her knee, and re-rolled into tight, uniform cylinders after breakfast.

Beside the bandages lay a single sheet of plain mulberry paper and a brass travel ink-case. Across the top right corner, in neat, small characters, she had written *Dear Suisui*, followed by two lines about the frost on the cistern trough, before laying her brush across the rest because the lock-gates down at the river were barred for the holiday and no post-mule was leaving the mountain.

Along the collar of her tunic, the small cerulean feathers lay completely flat and still against her neck.

At the window-seat, Ciaccona was tuning her third string with her bandaged left forefinger held stiffly clear of the pegbox. She tilted her head toward the rhythmic hammering coming through the cooper’s wall.

“If that woman next door hits that pig any harder,” Ciaccona muttered, “she’s going to come right through the lath and land in our congee. What are they making over there, an army?”

“Dumplings,” Yangyang said without looking up from her blade. “And braised belly with taro. The cooper’s eldest brother came up from the lower valley with his children before the gate shut last night.”

“Smells like star anise and cinnamon bark,” Amy said from the floor by the stove, where she was sitting cross-legged with her boots off, trying to scrape a drop of dried pine resin off the heel of her sock with her thumbnail. “The whole lane smells like somebody’s kitchen. Even the Wardens down at the guardhouse had a big clay jar of yellow wine warming on the coal-bucket when I went out for kindling.”

She glanced over her shoulder at Yangyang’s bent head. “Hey, Watcher. Don’t you guys do a whole thing today in Mingting?”

Yangyang slid her sword smoothly back into its scabbard with a soft click and folded the oiled chamois into a neat square.

“It’s only a local holiday,” Yangyang said, her voice level and mild. “Every house has its own way of keeping it. You don’t keep it in Septimont or Ragunna, so there’s no reason to turn the storehouse upside down.”

From the doorway, Cartethyia looked up from the wooden water-dipper she was filling at the butt, her brow wrinkling beneath her crystal laurel. “Wait—what do people do on the first day? Yesterday the boys by the dyer’s porch told me the moon wakes up tonight, and everybody goes up the steps to look at it, and if you float a paper boat on the pond with a candle inside, the river-spirits don’t pinch your toes in the spring!”

“That is after dark,” Yangyang said, setting the folded chamois beside her ink-case. “On the first day of the Waking Moon, the shops stay shut so no one has to work at a counter. People go home before the lamps are lit to eat *tuán yuán fàn*—the reunion dinner—round one table with their family. Usually there is a whole fish for plenty, and a clear soup in the middle, and something round for the moon.”

She stood up from the crate, tucked her ink-case and the unfinished sheet of mulberry paper into her sleeve, and reached for the tin oil-can on the shelf.

“The bottom hinge on the scullery door is catching on the sill when the frost swells the wood,” Yangyang added pleasantly. “I’ll put a drop of oil on the pin before the wind comes round.”

Across the room, nobody reached for the oil-can.

At the hearth-end of the long pine table, Iuno lowered her porcelain teacup to its saucer. Down by the water-butt, Chisa stood with the towel in her hands, her face turned toward the scullery threshold as Yangyang’s hem passed through it.

In the middle of the pine bench sat Mei.

She had been parked there since the porridge bowls were cleared, her back propped upright against the timber pillar and both of her heavy road-boots—laced all the way to the top hooks, the left one in a proud, slightly crooked double bow of her own making—stretched out in front of her on a low grain-sack.

For the space of three cleaver-beats from the house next door, Mei watched the doorway where Yangyang was crouched on one knee, carefully touching the spout of the tin oil-can to the iron hinge-pin so it wouldn’t squeak.

Mei took her feet off the grain-sack.

She planted both boot-soles flat and square on the floorboards, slapped her palms down on the scarred pine table loud enough to make the salt-cellar jump, and stood up.

“Forget the salt-pork!” Mei announced to the rafters.

At the butcher’s block, Lupa jumped half an inch and glared around her shoulder. “Oi! What’s wrong with my salt-pork?”

“Salt-pork is Tuesday food!” Mei declared, stepping out from behind the bench with her hands on her hips and her golden eyes bright as newly minted brass. “Today is the first day of the Waking Moon! Every chimney in this Hold is smoking like a steam-barge, and we’re sitting here listening to the neighbours chop cabbage like a bunch of turnip-auditors! Tonight this house is having a reunion dinner!”

In the scullery doorway, Yangyang straightened up with the oil-can in her fingers.

“Mei, please,” Yangyang said quickly, stepping back into the room. “You don’t need to do that. You just had your stitches out yesterday, and nobody else here is from Huanglong—it’s just a supper. Truly, I’m fine.”

“You’ve polished your sword twice since breakfast and you swept the front step till the moss came off,” Mei said, pointing a finger straight at her nose. “And I’m not lifting a single pot, so Lupa can put that ladle down right now! We’re doing a potluck! House rules: everybody in this room makes one dish with their own hands and brings it to this table at dusk. Nothing bought—which is super easy to enforce because every shop in the pass has a padlock on the door!”

The room went quiet for one breath, and then broke open all at once.

“Excuse me,” Iuno said from the head of the board, her spine straightening under her wool coat and her eyebrows lifting toward her silver-blue coils. “I do not cook, Mei. I have never cooked. In Septimont, the temple kitchens were staffed by forty consecrated hearth-attendants for a very good reason.”

“No clerical exemptions!” Mei said cheerfully, leaning across the corner of the table to tap the back of Iuno’s wrist. “One dish, Iuiu! With your own hand! Even if it’s boiled water with a leaf in it, except it can’t be tea because you do that every morning!”

*Boiled water with a leaf in it,* the High Priestess repeated in the back of Iuno’s mind, sounding deeply scandalised. *Tell her we once adjudicated the grain-tithe of seven provinces, darling. We do not scramble eggs in a storehouse.*

*She is looking right at us,* Iuno answered silently, *and you know very well she will make us wear an apron if we argue.*

Over on the window-seat, Ciaccona swung her long legs down off the bench, laid her lute carefully in its velvet case, and cracked her good knuckles with an expression of terrifying serenity.

“Stand back, amateurs,” Ciaccona said grandly. “As it happens, the Toccata household kept a winter table that made half the Senators in Ragunna weep into their napkins. Give me a rolling-pin and thirty minutes by the stove, and I will give you northern barbarians an education in proper festival pasta.”

Lupa snorted so hard from the hearth that ashes puffed out of the grate. “You? Last time you watched a pot on the road, you burned the water black and blamed the wind! Wait a minute—” Lupa swung around toward Mei, her wolf ears perking straight up. “One dish? What if it’s in one big pot? If everything’s inside the same rim when it hits the table, that counts as one dish, right?”

“One rim, one dish!” Mei ruled briskly.

“Oh! Does bread count?” Cartethyia asked earnestly, clasping both hands over the handle of the water-dipper. “In Egla Town, Uncle Cosimo and Rosa always bake the braided ring-loaf with the sweet seeds on the feast of the Winter Saint! I watched Uncle Cosimo twist the ropes every year until I was ten—I know the exact rhyme for the folds!”

“Bread totally counts!”

“What about sweets?” Amy demanded, scrambling up off the floor with one sock half-on and hopping on her left heel as she tugged it over her ankle. “Can mine be sugar? I saw a stone jar of preserved mountain haws up behind the tally-slates in the loft yesterday when I was looking for copper wire!”

“Sweets are mandatory!” Mei turned to the water-butt. “Chisa?”

Chisa folded the dry linen towel into a neat square and laid it on the rim of the barrel.

“I will make the drink,” Chisa said in her plain, level voice.

In the center of the floorboards, Yangyang stood holding the little tin oil-can against her sash, looking from Cartethyia’s shining eyes to Lupa already dragging a three-foot brass hotpot basin out from under the flour-trough with a tremendous clatter.

“You don’t even have the ingredients for your own dishes,” Yangyang protested softly, turning back to Mei. “It’s a Mingting custom, Mei. You don’t have to pretend for my sake.”

Mei stopped playing field-marshal.

She walked the three steps across the pine floorboards, took the little tin oil-can out of Yangyang’s fingers, and set it down on the corner of the table. She didn’t reach for a joke, and she didn’t raise her voice for the rafters.

“I’m not pretending,” Mei said plainly. “I want to eat dinner with my family tonight, and you’re my family, so this is the table where it happens. Make the soup your mother makes, Yangyang. Lupa’s got a whole stewing hen hanging behind the bacon.”

Yangyang looked at Mei’s face in the grey morning light from the window.

For a breath her mouth stayed slightly open; then she looked down at her own white-tipped cuff, where a smudge of hinge-oil sat on her thumb, and nodded once.

Along her jaw, the small cerulean feathers of her collar lifted in a single, soft wave and settled warm against her neck.

***

By midday, the brick kitchen of the third storehouse was hotter than a blacksmith’s forge and twice as loud.

Mei sat enthroned on the tall three-legged oak stool wedged safely between the grain-bins and the pickle crocks, bundled in her dark jacket with Lupa’s longest wooden rolling-pin resting across her knees like a marshal’s baton.

“Foul!” Mei barked, pointing the rolling-pin straight at the open scullery window. “Yard penalty! Everybody look at the fence! Lupa’s trying to pass bacon over the palings!”

Outside in the cold yard behind the cistern, Lupa froze with one booted foot up on the lower rail of the split-cedar fence and a two-pound slab of smoked belly-pork held out over the alley toward Old Man Zhao, who was standing by his frozen rain-barrel holding a live, slapping river carp by the gill-string.

“I’m not buying it!” Lupa yelled back through the window-frame, her tail lashing once against her shin while the carp smacked its tail against Zhao’s oilskin apron. “The kid said a festival table’s supposed to have a whole fish! I’m swapping him salt-meat for river-meat! No coppers!”

“Bartering is commercial trade with extra steps!” Ciaccona called out from the middle of the pine table, where she was standing in her shirt-sleeves up to the elbows in grey-brown dust, trying to flatten a lump of Mengzhou millet dough with an empty green wine bottle. “Put the contraband trout down, wolf, or I’m reporting you to the magistrate!”

“It’s a carp, you ignorant lute-strummer!” Lupa growled, climbing reluctantly down off the fence rail as Old Man Zhao shook his head and carried his fish back inside his woodshed. She marched back through the scullery door, slammed the slab of bacon down on the chopping block, and immediately craned her neck over the brick hob toward the small earthenware pot bubbling beside the flue. “What the hell are you doing to those eggs, princess? You’ve got the fire banked so low a mouse could sleep on the grate!”

At the right-hand corner of the stove, Iuno did not turn her head.

She had removed her heavy wool coat and folded back the right cuff of her silk blouse twice so it wouldn’t brush the soot. Propped upright against the stoneware salt-crock was a slip of foolscap from her pocket pad, covered in neat, horizontal pencil lines with no numerals—only the order of operations written out in her crisp basilica hand.

On the board in front of her sat three cracked duck-egg shells, a square of triple-folded surgical gauze from Amy’s kit stretched taut over a porcelain bowl with twine, and a pair of bamboo chopsticks.

“Do not touch that damper, Lupa,” Iuno said in a voice that could have frozen the cistern solid. “If the water beneath the trivet boils hard, the steam forces air pockets into the curd. Go chop your turnips.”

“It’s egg and water! You beat it with a stick and shove it in the pot!” Lupa reached a scarred hand toward the iron lid of the steamer to peek inside.

*WHACK.*

Mei’s rolling-pin rapped smartly against the side of the flour-bin.

“That’s three stove infractions!” Mei called out. “Out! Lupa, you are officially banished to the yard brazier until the sun passes the storehouse gable! Take your cartwheel pot and your cabbage and go breathe cold air!”

“This is my kitchen!” Lupa roared, though her ears flattened half an inch as Chisa—who was standing quietly by the threshold wrapping an iron carpenter’s mallet in clean sackcloth—hooked two fingers into the back of Lupa’s leather belt and tugged once.

Grumbling under her breath about ungrateful patients and foreign amateurs, Lupa scooped up an armful of lotus root, dried bean-curd knots, and mutton shoulder, kicked the scullery door wider with her re-nailed heel, and stomped out to the stone landing where the borrowed iron charcoal-brazier was glowing red.

Inside, the room let out a collective breath.

At the very back corner of the brick range, over the small, steady fire-hole nearest the chimney-breast, a deep brown clay pot sat murmuring to itself under a wooden lid.

Yangyang sat on a low three-legged stool beside the ash-pit with a long-handled bamboo skimmer resting across her palm. She had put the stewing hen on before the fifth bell with a thick coin of smashed yellow ginger and peeled white rounds of mountain yam, and for the first hour she had simply sat by the bricks, lifting the lid whenever the rim whispered to skim the grey foam from the surface until the broth ran as clear and pale as melted snow.

Now the scullery latch clicked, and Yangyang stepped back in from the lane with the cold draft on her cheeks and a twisted cone of coarse brown butcher’s paper held in both hands.

“Aha!” Lupa’s voice boomed right back through the open window-frame from the landing outside, where she was splitting mutton bones with a cleaver. “What’s in the paper, bird? You just knocked on Old Mother Pu’s back door! I saw you through the fence! She went shopping!”

Yangyang stopped by the table, her ears going pink below her blue-black hair.

“I didn’t buy anything,” Yangyang said earnestly, unfolding the top of the brown paper cone so the room could see the curly yellow slices of dried angelica root, the wrinkled red dates, and a fistful of bright scarlet goji berries nested inside. “The storehouse only had ginger and salt. I went over to ask Mother Pu if I could trade my next two mornings of water-carrying for a thumb of angelica root from her medicine jar, and when I told her it was for a soup pot, she pushed this into my hands and bolted the door before I could take my purse out.”

“A likely story!” Ciaccona said, pointing her floury wine bottle across the board. “The prosecution notes that the witness returned with three separate botanicals not native to the storehouse pantry!”

“You cannot push money under a neighbour’s door on the first day of the Waking Moon,” Yangyang said, turning toward Mei on the high stool with complete Mingting seriousness. “Mother Pu would have thrown the salt-jar at my head. It’s bad luck for the kitchen.”

Mei tapped her chin twice with the end of the rolling-pin, looking deeply judicial.

“Did any copper touch the windowsill?” Mei inquired.

“No,” Yangyang said. “She shut the shutter on my sleeve.”

“Neighbourly ambush!” Mei banged the rolling-pin once against her boot-sole. “Not buying! Put the roots in the pot, Yangyang!”

Yangyang let out a small breath, stepped back to her clay pot at the corner of the stove, and dropped the angelica slices, the red dates, and the goji berries into the clear, simmering broth. Almost at once, a warm, bitter-sweet medicinal fragrance rose up with the steam, cutting right through the smell of coal-smoke and raw flour—the exact smell of a back kitchen in Mingting when the winter shutters go up.

At the far end of the pine table, Cartethyia stood on tiptoe to get her shoulders over a wide wooden kneading-board, the tip of her tongue caught between her teeth and a smudge of grey millet-and-wheat flour across the bridge of her nose.

She had rolled her risen dough into three long, plump ropes and was braiding them together with intense, prayerful concentration, murmuring Uncle Cosimo’s bakery rhyme under her breath at every cross:

“*Over the brook goes the bailiff’s mare, under the bridge goes the miller’s sack, tuck the thumb by the chapel stair...*”

She pinched the ends together, lifted the braided ring onto a greased iron baking-sheet, and slid it into the side brick oven beside the main firebox.

Ten minutes later, when she cracked the iron door open with a folded rag to peek inside, Cartethyia gave a small, stricken gasp.

“Oh no,” Cartethyia whispered, squatting down in front of the oven mouth so her crystal laurel clinked against the warm brickwork. “The fire is all on the left side! Look at him—his left shoulder has grown as big as a bolster, and his right leg has curled up underneath his chin!”

“Turn the tray around, sweetie!” Mei directed from her stool. “Give the skinny side a turn by the coals!”

Outside on the frost-rimmed landing, a sharp, clean *crack—tink—crack* echoed over the sizzle of Lupa’s hotpot.

Chisa stood beside the stone cistern trough in her black coat and pale blue tie. The afternoon sky overhead had thickened to the colour of wet slate, and the air bit at the knuckles, where the top layer of spring-water in the stone basin had frozen into a solid sheet of clear, bubble-free glass. With a clean iron chisel in her left hand and the muffled mallet in her right, Chisa struck once along the grain of the ice, lifted out a thick, diamond-clear slab, and broke it into neat chunks inside a stoneware pitcher already golden with steeped osmanthus flowers and melted rock-sugar.

Three paces away on the bottom step, Amy had pulled Lupa’s sheepskin vest tightly around her ribs and dragged the small brazier of glowing willow-charcoal between her boots.

Balanced on the iron tripod over the coals was a shallow copper skillet full of bubbling, amber-clear sugar syrup. Beside the pan lay twenty red mountain hawthorns she had fished out of the loft jar, washed in cold snow-water, and dried on a towel, skewered three apiece on split bamboo sticks.

To the rim of the copper skillet, Amy had clamped a bent loop of heavy copper magnet-wire from her pocket; suspended from the loop so its lower tip dipped just into the foaming syrup was a thin brass reed from an old mouth-organ, filed down with her pocket-knife.

She wasn’t looking at a clock or counting under her breath. She sat with her chin on her knees, watching the big golden bubbles roll slower and thicker across the pan—until all at once the thickening syrup dragged hard enough against the submerged brass tongue to set up a tiny, continuous vibration against the copper rim, singing a clear, high *zzzz-zing* in the cold air.

“Gotcha!” Amy crowed, whipping the copper skillet off the coals with a folded rag before the syrup could darken by a shade. Working fast, she twirled each bamboo skewer through the molten amber, spun it once in the freezing draft so a hair-thin web of sugar threads trailed off the red berries, and slapped it down on a buttered slate tile where the coating hardened instantly with a tiny, glassy *tink*.

Inside the storehouse, the afternoon light was beginning to go grey behind the window-panes, and the middle of the brick stove belonged entirely to Iuno and Ciaccona.

For the third time in an hour, Iuno lifted the iron lid off the small steamer with a folded cloth.

The steam cleared. Inside the porcelain bowl, where the saucer had slipped sideways by a hair during the simmer, the yellow custard had risen in a lopsided dome pitted with tiny craters like a sponge left out in the rain, weeping pale water around the edges.

Iuno’s face did not flicker.

*That saucer is warped, darling,* the High Priestess observed from the back of her head, inspecting the bowl with cold imperial disdain. *Clearly the potter was drunk. Throw it in the ditch before the bard sees it and let us go burn down the potter’s guild.*

*The saucer is flat,* Iuno replied silently. *The fire flared when Lupa opened the scullery door.*

Without a word, Iuno picked up the hot bowl with her cloth, walked to the covered wooden slop-pail behind the back door, tipped the spongy yellow disc straight in, and closed the lid. She walked back to the water-butt, scrubbed the porcelain bowl clean one-handed with the lye-brush, dried it on a towel, and set it back on the table beside her remaining two duck eggs.

At the next burner over, Ciaccona was having the time of her life.

Because the Mengzhou millet flour refused to hold together like southern wheat, she had cracked four extra eggs into the mound and rolled the dough out into thick, stubborn brownish-yellow ribbons the width of her thumb, which were currently boiling in a pot of salted water that looked like carpenter’s glue.

Beside it, in Lupa’s cast-iron skillet, Ciaccona was stirring her sauce.

There had been no tomatoes within three hundred miles of the mountain since the ninth month. There had, however, been a wooden crate of soft, dead-ripe winter persimmons sitting under the grain-scales. Ciaccona had peeled eight of them into the skillet with a splash of green olive oil from her travel-flask, three crushed cloves of garlic, a pinch of dried basil from her lute-case pocket, and a ladle of cooking wine.

The pan was now a bubbling lake of brilliant, luminous sunset-orange paste that smelled simultaneously of roasted garlic, warm fruit-jam, and vinegar.

Ciaccona leaned over the skillet, listening intently to the slow *bloop... bloop-blop* of the thick orange bubbles the way she listened to a tavern fiddle, and added a pinch of coarse salt from a high, theatrical wrist.

Iuno stood beside her at the chopping block, holding a fresh duck egg in her right palm. Slowly, Iuno turned her head and looked down into Ciaccona’s skillet.

“Ciaccona,” Iuno said quietly.

“Don’t interrupt the maestro during the allegro, princess,” Ciaccona said, giving the wooden spoon a flourish. “Look at that gloss. If you squint your eyes in this light, it’s practically San Marzano.”

“Those are persimmons,” Iuno said.

“They are red, they grow on trees, and they are round,” Ciaccona replied smoothly. “In a siege, taxonomy is for cowards. How is the fourth ratification of the egg treaty coming along?”

Iuno looked down at the raw duck egg in her fingers. When you only have one hand, cracking an egg cleanly on the rim of a bowl without crushing the whole shell inward requires a brisk, exact snap of the middle finger against the thumb; her right knuckles were smudged with dried yolk where the third shell had splintered.

“The stove drafts from the left,” Iuno said evenly. “And these mountain ducks lay shells like river pebbles.”

Ciaccona glanced sideways from her bubbling orange cauldron. She didn’t make a joke about priests or kitchens. Without drawing attention to it, she reached across with her wooden spoon, nudged the heavy stoneware salt-crock two inches closer to Iuno’s bowl so its flat side braced the porcelain rim from sliding on the pine, and went back to stirring her garlic-persimmon paste.

Iuno braced the bowl against the salt-crock, tapped the duck egg once against the edge, and parted the shell cleanly with her thumb and index finger so the unbroken yolk dropped into the bowl without a single speck of white shell following it.

From the back corner of the stove, Yangyang rose from her low stool to fetch the hot-water kettle for her clay pot. As she passed behind Iuno’s elbow, her eyes rested for a beat on the small steamer pot where Iuno’s water was heating, and then on the red coals underneath the iron grate.

“If you slide the steamer half a palm toward the chimney brick,” Yangyang said gently, not touching the pot herself, “the draft goes up the flue instead of hitting the bottom of the pan. The water stays quiet without going out.”

Iuno looked at the brickwork, then at Yangyang.

“Thank you, Yangyang,” Iuno said, and pushed the steamer onto the flat brick beside the flue with her cloth.

At the front of the stove, Mei stood beside her low iron pan—where Lupa had left a thick layer of oiled Jinzhou rice crisping over the dying embers since noon—sniffing the air as Ciaccona ladled her neon-orange persimmon sauce over a mountain of thick, chewy millet-ribbons.

Outside the frosted glass of the high window, the grey afternoon light died out behind the karst ridge, and down in the lane, the lamplighter’s pole clicked once against the iron hook by the well as the first crimson festival lantern went on in the dusk.

***

When the sixth bell hummed out from the high drum-tower, the heavy timber shutters of the third storehouse were barred from the inside, the crack under the front door was stuffed with a rolled sack to keep the draft off the ankles, and the hanging brass lamp was turned up until the pine rafters glowed like honey.

All up and down the mountain tiers, behind every closed shutter and papered lattice in Xuanfang Hold, two hundred families were pulling their benches in at the exact same moment—the faint, muffled clatter of earthenware bowls through the cooper’s wall next door, a baby laughing somewhere down by the dyer’s steps, the smell of toasted sesame oil and hot wine rising into the cold evening.

Inside the storehouse, Yangyang walked around the long pine table alone before anyone sat down.

She had taken off her heavy wool cloak, leaving her sheer sky-to-white sleeves and her layered dark skirt smooth and neat. Carrying a stack of eight deep earthenware bowls in the crook of her left arm so it wouldn’t press against her strapped ribs, she wiped the scarred pine wood at each place with a clean linen cloth, set the bowl down, and laid a pair of bamboo chopsticks parallel across the right-hand rim, the tips aligned to the breadth of a fingernail. In the center of the board she set three braided straw trivets and two large porcelain serving spoons, the way a table is laid in Mingting when the front gate is bolted for the year’s turn.

Nobody made a joke about the straight chopsticks. Lupa stood by the hearth holding her massive brass hotpot with two folded dry towels, waiting patiently until Yangyang stepped back from the board before she swung the steaming cauldron over and planted it on the west trivet.

Right on cue, the pale lines on the back of Mei’s right wrist flared warm gold.

Abby popped out into the lamplight above her forearm, hovering two feet over the pine table with his long white ears twitching furiously and his golden eyes spinning from one end of the board to the other.

“Whoa!” Abby shouted, planting both front paws on his hips in mid-air and taking a tremendous, whistling sniff of the room. “What died in here and went to heaven?! Is that chicken soup? Is that fried rice? Why is that puddle of noodles glowing like a lantern? Where’s my bowl? Put my bowl down! And put a second bowl down right now for the person behind!”

“Nobody’s behind you, furball, and keep your tail out of my candied haws or you’ll be glued to the rafters till spring,” Amy said, setting a slate platter of twenty glossy, ruby-red hawthorn skewers down beside the teapot.

“It’s a festival!” Abby declared at top volume, dropping down to sit squarely on the corner of the table beside Mei’s saucer. “On a festival you always set an extra bowl at the end of the table in case the person behind shows up hungry! Everybody knows that! Even the dog knows that!”

“Who’re you calling a dog, you floating pillow?” Lupa barked as she shoved the vinegar cruet and the salt-jar all the way to the edge of the plank to make room for her brass pot.

Without saying anything, Chisa picked up a ninth earthenware bowl from the drying rack by the water-butt, set it down at the empty corner of the bench nearest the barred front door, and laid a pair of bamboo chopsticks neatly across the rim.

“Okay!” Mei called out from the hearth-end of the bench, where she was sitting wedged snugly beside Iuno with her hands resting on the edge of the wood. “Everybody sit! Dish by dish, no fighting over the ladle, and nobody’s allowed to ask what’s in anything until they’ve chewed!”

They crowded onto the two long benches—eight of them and Abby around six feet of scrubbed pine, knees bumping under the board, the heat from the stove and the three trivets turning the room as warm as a bakehouse.

Yangyang did not sit down right away.

At the middle trivet, Lupa had lifted the heavy clay soup-pot off the back burner for her and set it down on the straw ring. Yangyang lifted the wooden lid away.

A soft cloud of fragrant white steam rose toward the hanging lamp. Inside the dark clay belly, the broth was the clear, deep gold of autumn sunlight, dotted with bright red goji berries and plump jujubes around the tender, fallen-apart breast of the hen.

Yangyang picked up the porcelain ladle with her right hand. She didn’t look around the table, and she didn’t make a speech about elders or hosts. She filled the first warm bowl to the brim with clear golden broth, a slice of white yam, and a piece of chicken, and set it down with both hands directly in front of Mei.

Then she filled Iuno’s bowl, and Amy’s, and went all the way round the board—putting a ladleful into the ninth bowl by the door last of all—before sitting down between Amy and Cartethyia and picking up her own spoon.

For a full minute, the only sound in the storehouse was eight spoons dipping into hot soup.

“Oh,” Cartethyia breathed, her blue eyes widening behind the steam as she swallowed her first spoonful and immediately took a second. “It tastes like when you come inside from the snow and somebody puts a warm brick under your feet!”

“Mother Pu’s angelica root is from the south slope,” Yangyang said quietly into her bowl, though the tips of her ears under her hair were warm and pleased. “It isn’t bitter if you put it in after the ginger.”

“My turn!” Mei announced, setting her soup spoon down and pushing herself upright on her flat boots without leaning on Iuno’s shoulder. “Amymy, bring the iron pan! Iuiu, hand me the mushroom bowl!”

Amy grabbed the handle of the small cast-iron pan from the hearth-bricks with two thick forge-mittens and set it sizzling on the east trivet, where a single round cake of golden-brown Jinzhou rice crust—blistered crisp across the middle and charred slightly black along one crescent edge where Lupa hadn’t been allowed to turn it—sat smoking dry in the iron.

Before Mei picked up the gravy bowl, she reached out with a pair of bamboo chopsticks, snapped a clean, palm-sized corner off the un-burned side of the dry rice crust, laid it on a small porcelain saucer beside her elbow, and turned an empty teacup upside down over it to keep the heat in.

Then she lifted the hot bowl of mushroom-and-ginger sauce Iuno held ready for her, raised her left forefinger into the air, and glared around the table.

“Nobody breathe!” Mei commanded. “Wait for it—”

The table went dead silent. Even Abby held his breath with both paws clapped over his mouth.

Mei tilted the porcelain rim and poured the boiling brown ginger-sauce straight into the middle of the scorching iron pan.

*SHHHHH-CRACK-POP-SSSSSSSS!*

A geyser of fragrant white steam shot straight up to the tie-beam, carrying the fierce, nutty smell of toasted rice and scorch-browned soy across everyone’s faces as the dry golden crust drank the gravy and crackled like a pine-bough in a bonfire.

“Ha!” Mei beamed through the clearing steam, sitting back down on the bench with a thump. “Ten out of ten for volume! Hear that, Lupa? Didn’t even stick to the bottom!”

“The left side’s black as a boot-heel,” Lupa grunted, and immediately speared the largest piece of the blackened edge with her chopsticks and dropped it into her own bowl. “Good crunch, though.”

Next to Mei, Iuno reached out with her right hand and drew the small white porcelain bowl from the edge of the trivet into the light.

She lifted the covering saucer away.

Around the table, six heads leaned in at once.

Inside the bowl, the steamed egg custard did not have a single bubble, ripple, or pinhole from rim to rim. It sat pale gold and still as a pane of yellow glass, with three drops of dark sesame oil and a single green scallion ring floating in the exact center like an island on a lake.

Amy stared down at it, then looked up at Iuno’s face. “Wait. What happened to the one that looked like a volcano?”

“I have no idea what you are referring to, Amy,” Iuno said coolly, picking up a clean silver spoon and dividing the mirror-smooth custard into neat radial wedges with a surgeon’s hand. “Hold your saucer out before it cools.”

Mei took the first wedge off her saucer, put the whole spoonful in her mouth, and looked at Iuno with shining eyes. “Iuiu, it doesn’t even need teeth! It just disappears! You’re a culinary prodigy!”

*Naturally,* the High Priestess purred inside Iuno’s head, thoroughly vindicated. *Do remind the child that we never lose to an egg.*

*Eat your rice,* Iuno told her, though she let her knee rest warm against Mei’s thigh under the table as the bowl of custard was scraped clean in forty seconds flat.

After that, the table lost all semblance of order.

Lupa’s brass hotpot was so wide its handles hung over both sides of the pine plank, boiling like a geyser in the middle of the board. It had nine separate things crammed inside its single brass rim—mutton slices, cabbage, radish, lotus root, kelp knots, vermicelli, tofu skin, mushrooms, and pounded carp-balls she had made from yesterday’s scraps—and Lupa defended its status as a single dish with her chopsticks pointed like a spear while piling everyone’s bowls three inches above the rim.

Cartethyia untied the linen cloth from her Egla Town ring-loaf with her heart in her eyes. Because the brick oven had heated unevenly, the braided bread had puffed up on the left into a round, glossy brown hump while the right side had twisted down into a narrow, stubby point with two burnt currants sticking out like eyes.

“He is a little crooked,” Cartethyia admitted anxiously as she tore the warm loaf open with both hands, letting out a sweet, yeasty cloud of anise-seed and honey. “Uncle Cosimo’s loaves always look like a crown, and mine looks a little bit like a badger that sat on a wasp.”

“Give me that fat end,” Lupa said at once, tearing off a hunk of the swollen left shoulder, dunking it straight into her mutton broth, and shoving half of it into her mouth. “Nothing wrong with that crumb, knight. Chewy. Beats hardtack by a mile.”

Cartethyia’s face lit up from chin to laurel; she immediately tore a big piece from the middle and laid it on Ciaccona’s saucer, then passed the board down to Chisa.

Chisa stood up at the foot of the table, lifted her heavy stoneware pitcher from the floorboards, and filled eight tin mugs with pale golden osmanthus water. Clear, jagged shards of cistern ice clinked against the tin rims, frosting the outsides of the mugs instantly in the hot room.

Chisa sat back down beside Lupa’s hip, picked up her own frosted mug, and drank a long, slow draught of the freezing floral water with her eyes half-closed in total contentment.

Up at the hearth-end, Mei enthusiastically took a giant gulp of hers—and immediately clapped both palms flat against her cheekbones, her eyes watering as her back molars shrieked.

“Brrr!” Mei gasped through her fingers, setting the mug down with a rattle. “Chisa! My skull just shrank two sizes! Where’d you get that ice, the bottom of a glacier?!”

“The cistern trough,” Chisa said peacefully, and ate a piece of lotus root.

“And now,” Ciaccona announced, rising from the bench with her split left forefinger held aloft and lifting the lid off her deep earthenware platter with the air of a cardinal unveiling a relic, “for the centerpiece of the evening. *Tagliolini alla Toccata*, autumn mountain variation.”

Sitting on the platter was a solid, quivering mound of thumb-thick brown millet ribbons coated in a shiny, electric-orange glaze of stewed persimmon and crushed garlic.

For two heartbeats, nobody’s chopsticks moved.

Then Cartethyia reached straight across the board, lifted a generous tangle of the heavy orange noodles onto her plate, and ate a huge forkful without a blink.

“It is very orange!” Cartethyia said loyally. “And very sweet! And you can really taste the garlic at the back of your nose!”

“Thank you, cara,” Ciaccona said warmly, bowing from the waist before turning her green eyes on the rest of the board. “Well? Don’t be shy, philistines. That’s real Ragunnesi olive oil in the reduction.”

Mei, Yangyang, and Amy dutifully reached out and took a polite chopstick-pinch each.

Mei chewed once, twice, her jaw working hard against the gluey millet dough while the combination of hot sugar-persimmon and raw garlic hit the roof of her mouth. Her eyes watered harder than they had from the ice water; she swallowed heroically through sheer willpower and gave a weak thumbs-up. Beside her, Yangyang chewed with quiet, heroic composure and immediately drank half a cup of hot tea.

Across the board, Amy got one gummy orange strand halfway past her teeth, froze, lifted her tin mug with her left hand to hide her mouth from Ciaccona’s line of sight, and silently spat the noodle into her crumpled linen napkin before stuffing it into her sheepskin pocket.

Lupa didn’t use a napkin. She took one bite off her chopsticks, made a face like a wolf that had bitten into a hornet’s nest, and dropped the chopsticks clattering across her bowl.

“What the hell is that?!” Lupa demanded, grabbing Chisa’s iced osmanthus mug to wash her tongue out. “Are you trying to poison the garrison or wallpaper the barracks?! That’s mashed fruit and garlic on axle-putty!”

“It is a sweet-and-sour agro-dolce!” Ciaccona shot back indignantly, pulling the platter closer to her own elbow and heaping a spoonful onto her saucer. “My great-uncle Girolamo served sweet squash with garlic to the Doge! It’s not my fault northern millet has the structural elasticity of wet gravel!”

“Give me a hawthorn before my tongue curls up,” Lupa groaned, reaching across for Amy’s slate platter.

Amy’s candied hawthorns cracked like thin river-ice between the teeth—bright, fiercely sour mountain fruit inside a shell of clear, brittle amber sugar that set Abby hopping across the pine wood trying to pry his back jaws apart with both paws after he bit three berries at once.

At the hearth-end of the bench, Iuno picked up one of the bamboo skewers with her right hand.

Before she brought it to her mouth, she paused for the space of a breath, turning the wooden stick slowly between her thumb and forefinger so the three round, red-glazed berries caught the yellow flame of the hanging lamp.

Beside her, Mei’s hand came to rest lightly over the cuff of Iuno’s blouse on the table edge. Iuno looked once from the glassy red sugar to Mei’s quiet golden eyes, then bit the top hawthorn clean off the skewer and chewed.

In the center of the board, Yangyang picked up the soup ladle again to refill Cartethyia’s and Chisa’s bowls from the bottom of the clay pot, her cheeks flushed warm from the steam and the charcoal heat, a tiny smudge of pork-broth at the corner of her mouth as Lupa and Ciaccona argued loudly over whether olive oil belonged in a turnip patch.

Ciaccona leaned back against the timber pillar with her wine mug in her hand, looking down the length of the crowded, steaming pine board—at Abby licking sugar off his wrist, at Amy trying to balance a chopstick on her nose, at Mei’s head leaned sideways against Iuno’s good shoulder, and at the ninth untouched bowl sitting quiet at the end of the bench by the barred door.

“You know,” Ciaccona drawled lightly over the clatter of spoons, plucking an invisible chord in the air with her bandaged finger, “in a proper Ragunnesi four-act opera, this is the ensemble finale at the end of Act Two where the whole cast sits down at one long tavern table and sings in four-part harmony about the harvest, right before the baritone kicks the door in and the stage-hands drop the thunder-sheet.”

“Shut your trap and pass the badger-bread, bard,” Lupa said, shoving the wooden bread-board across the pickles with her elbow.

Ciaccona grinned, took a sip of wine, and passed the bread.

***

An hour after dark, when the clay soup-pot was down to the last ladle of ginger-broth and the tallow in the hanging lamp had burned down a finger’s breadth, the first boots came crunching down the frosted lane outside.

*Rap-rap-rap.*

Amy hopped up from the bench and lifted the wooden bar off the front door, letting in a swirl of cold night air that smelled of coal-smoke and wet stone.

Standing on the stone landing under the red eaves-lantern was Warden Liangyu, with Little Tan and two of the young spearmen from yesterday’s drill huddled behind her shoulder. Their cheeks were ruddy from the garrison mess-hall fire, their breath steamed thick in the cold, and although they had their heavy winter cloaks pulled over their shoulders for the walk up to the viewing terraces, Liangyu still wore her dented iron vambraces buckled over her linen wrist-wrap.

In her unsplinted left hand she carried a square stoneware crock sealed with bladder-skin and tied round the neck with a piece of faded red festival twine.

“Happy Waking Moon,” Liangyu said, holding the crock out across the threshold to Yangyang. “We’re on our way up to the third terrace before the steps fill up. My grandmother put these mustard greens down the autumn before I joined the watch—she told me if I didn’t bring a jar round to the neighbours before moonrise, I wasn’t to come home for breakfast.”

“Thank you, Liangyu,” Yangyang said warmly, taking the heavy crock in both hands. “Come inside by the stove—there’s hot soup.”

“We just ate three bowls of mutton stew at the mess, Watcher, we can’t—” Little Tan started, his ears going red as he peered past the doorframe at the warm table.

“Nonsense, you’re nineteen years old, your legs are hollow!” Lupa barked, materialising at the threshold with a wooden platter piled with hot mutton skewers from the brass pot and four thick slabs of Cartethyia’s anise-bread. She shoved a chunk of bread and two dripping skewers straight into Little Tan’s wool mittens and dealt the rest out to the other two boys before they could back down the steps. “Eat that on the stairs so your teeth don’t chatter on the wall! Get going!”

The young Wardens mumbled their thanks through mouthfuls of warm bread and hurried off toward the red-lit steps. Liangyu lingered one beat on the flagstone, her eyes touching Mei’s laced boots and Yangyang’s unslung shoulder, nodded once to the room, and followed her men up the lane.

Before Amy could push the heavy door shut, a royal-blue silk hem stepped neatly over the brass boar’s-head boot-scraper and Buling strolled in out of the dark.

She had her round black *nuanmao* tipped back half an inch on her silver hair, her yellow forehead talisman flipped up onto the brim, and the unmistakable, unhurried waddle of a person who had already eaten four courses up the hill.

“Wah, very lively in here,” Buling said, heading straight for the pine table without waiting to be asked. Before she even looked at the dishes, she reached out with two fingers and turned the empty clay soup-pot a quarter-turn counter-clockwise on its straw trivet. “Pot-ears pointing straight at the front door again. You want all the money in the house to roll down the steps into the ditch or not? Have to angle toward the corner.”

“Didn’t you just eat dinner up on the rock with Ruoyun and Qingxiao?” Amy asked suspiciously, watching Buling pick up a clean pair of chopsticks from the cup.

“That was first dinner,” Buling said placidly. “Mountain air very cold, walking down the stairs uses up all the heat. Besides, up at the top house, somebody in a plum coat happened to walk past the gate twice looking at the drain-pipe, and then the best drumstick disappeared before my chopsticks even left my sleeve.”

She leaned over the wooden bread-board, squinting through her red eyes at the remaining curved tail-end of Cartethyia’s Egla Town loaf.

“Whose mud-fish is this?” Buling asked with genuine curiosity, poking the burnt currant on the stubby end. “Look like a fat river-newt that swallowed a cobblestone and died happy.”

“It is a braided parish ring-loaf!” Cartethyia protested, though she immediately broke off a corner and handed it to her. “Try the middle, it has anise-seed!”

Buling popped the bread into her mouth, chewed approvingly, and then—before anyone could warn her—dipped her chopsticks into Ciaccona’s earthenware platter and lifted a thick, cold ribbon of orange persimmon-garlic millet noodle into her mouth.

The entire table watched her face.

Buling chewed three times. Her red eyes did not water, and she did not reach for a napkin. She swallowed, licked a speck of orange glaze from her thumb, and nodded once at Ciaccona.

“Tastes like the glue Old Shao uses to paste calendar backs when the flour goes sour and he drops a sweet potato in the bucket,” Buling pronounced mildly. “Very strong. If the wind blows the roof-tiles loose tonight, you put a spoon of this under the eaves, nothing comes down till summer.”

“I am surrounded by turnip-eaters,” Ciaccona muttered to the ceiling.

Buling reached deep into her wide left cuff, drew out two objects wrapped in dry rice-straw and encrusted in grey-black clay ash, and set them down on the pine plank beside the pickles with a solid *clack-clack*.

“Contribution to the house,” Buling said, tapping the dark clay shells with her fingernail. “Preserved duck eggs. Lime-and-ash cure, black jelly inside, pine-flower pattern on the white. I keep the crock under the floorboards behind the east tower for a while already.”

Lupa eyed the grey ash crust narrowly. “How long is ‘a while,’ zombie?”

“Duck eggs don’t have birthdays, don’t ask so many questions,” Buling said, snatching two of Amy’s candied hawthorn skewers in her right hand as she drifted back toward the door.

In the open doorway, Ruoyun stepped onto the threshold flagstone, her ruffled white cuffs spotless beneath her teal cloak and her round brass spectacles catching the red lantern-glow from the eaves.

She did not cross the sill. She held out a warm, covered blue-and-white porcelain dish with both hands and pushed it squarely against Lupa’s chest until the wolf took it.

“Left over from the pan,” Ruoyun said in her fast, flat barracks voice, her chin up and her small golden horns glinting in the doorway. “Bitter melon with egg. The small one walked past the threshold twice in her muddy boots until I put a saucer down on the step just to stop the pacing, and she still left half the greens behind. Eat it while it’s hot, it clears the liver for people who stay up too late making noise.”

“Hey, wait a second, nobody asked you to—” Lupa began, lifting the lid on thin, jade-green crescents of bitter melon stir-fried in golden egg without a single scorched speck.

Ruoyun was already two steps back out on the stone landing, turning her head toward the shadow beside the wooden water-turbine.

“Don’t stand in the gutter-draft all night, A-Qing,” Ruoyun called back over her shoulder as Buling hopped down the step beside her. “If we don’t get up to the gatehouse before the second drum, the village brats will have tallow drippings all over my parapet stairs.”

Just outside the rectangle of yellow lamplight, Qingxiao stood on the frosted flagstone with the camphor-scented indigo oilcloth folded over her moon-white shoulders.

Her pale blue eyes looked in through the open doorway at the warm room, the steaming brass pot, and the crowded pine table. When she breathed out in the cold air of the porch, a soft plume of white steam rose past her chin—and despite the dinner up on the rock, her gaze lingered for the fraction of a beat on the bread-board by the stove.

Inside, Mei didn’t call out to the room.

She picked up the small porcelain saucer beside her elbow, lifted off the upturned teacup that had kept the heat inside, and walked slowly across the floorboards to the threshold.

She held the saucer out across the doorway. Sitting on the white porcelain was the palm-sized corner of crisp, golden-brown rice crust she had broken off before the gravy went on, still warm from the side of the kettle.

Qingxiao looked down at the golden crust, then up into Mei’s face.

She drew her bare, warm right hand out from under the indigo oilcloth, took the slab of rice crust from the saucer, and bit a corner off right there on the step.

The crisp rice crunched loud and clean in the quiet doorway.

Qingxiao stood on the flagstone in the lantern-light and ate the whole piece steadily, without hurry, down to the last toasted grain on her fingertips. When it was gone, she touched her thumb lightly to her index finger to brush away a crumb, and her pale eyes moved once past Mei’s shoulder to the platter of bright orange persimmon noodles sitting on the table.

“Bold colour,” Qingxiao said in her level voice.

Inside by the window, Ciaccona sat bolt upright: “See?! The Sword Transcendent understands visual composition!”

Qingxiao did not look back at the bard. She gave Mei a single, small nod from the neck, turned on her heel, and walked out into the red-lit lane behind Ruoyun and Buling.

***

Nobody in the third storehouse climbed the three hundred wet steps to the upper viewing terraces.

Instead, they carried the dinner outside.

Lupa and Chisa hauled the two heavy pine benches out through the doorway and set them along the low stone coping wall at the edge of the storehouse yard, where the rock dropped straight away above the southern pass and the whole wide gulf of the sky lay open between the black karst spires. Amy dragged out the sheepskin rugs and the small charcoal brazier to put between their boots; Yangyang carried the clay pot with the last warm dregs of the angelica-and-chicken soup wrapped in a towel; and Cartethyia brought the tin mugs.

Mei sat on the wide oak stool near the corner of the wall with two grey wool blankets wrapped round her from chin to ankle. Iuno stood right beside her left shoulder, her heavy wool coat buttoned to the throat and her good hand resting lightly on the back of Mei’s blanket. On the pine bench to their right sat Yangyang with Lupa’s thick knitted muffler wound twice around her chin, Amy leaning back between Mei’s knees with a mug of hot broth in her mittens, and Lupa, Chisa, Ciaccona, and Cartethyia lined up along the stone coping under a shared patchwork of cloaks.

Below their boots, the whole steep bowl of Xuanfang Hold was out in the dark.

Along every stepped lane and terrace balustrade, from the cooper’s porch to the high stone arch below the cliff, two hundred people stood bundled in padded coats and wool shawls under the rows of crimson paper lanterns. Down by the stone rim of the cistern pond, the village children squatted at the water’s edge with their waxed-paper boats held ready on their palms, the tiny tallow wicks already lit and throwing wavering yellow dots across their knuckles.

Over the eastern pass, the sky between the mountain teeth stayed black and empty.

The quarter-drum beat once from the gatehouse tower and died away into the gorge.

Still no light showed above the ridge.

Down on the lower terraces, the murmur of voices and the laughter around the wine-jars slowly thinned out. People shifted their weight on the frosted bluestone; a child by the cistern piped up to ask if the moon had gone to sleep in the river, and his mother hushed him softly with a hand on his cap. Across the whole dark mountain keep, two hundred upturned faces waited in the red lantern-glow.

On the bench beside Mei’s knee, Yangyang held her warm earthenware mug in both hands, her breath rising in small white puffs above Lupa’s wool muffler as she looked out at the black saddle of the pass.

Then, along the sharp eastern tooth of the karst ridge, a thin wire of light touched the mist.

Slowly at first, and then lifting clear of the jagged rock as if pushed from underneath by a hand, the full moon rose into the gap of the pass.

It was enormous—so wide as it cleared the black stone of the horizon that its rim seemed to brush the cliff-pines on either side of the gorge, pouring a sudden, flood-tide wash of light across the frosted roof-tiles, the silent bronze ring of the Astrolabe Core overhead, and the mist coiling in the valley below.

All at once, from the gatehouse steps to the dyer’s lane, the whole Hold cheered.

Warden Ge’s pikemen banged the butts of their ash shafts against the paving stones—*clack-clack-clack*; the cooper’s grandsons banged two copper wash-basins together; down at the cistern pond, twenty children leaned out over the stone coping at once and pushed their waxed-paper boats out onto the dark water. The tiny square hulls caught the current from the spring-pipe and drifted out in a ragged fleet across the pond, their thumb-sized candle flames bobbing bright and yellow across the water.

Up on the storehouse landing, Cartethyia clapped her mittens together over her head; Amy let out a loud midnight-radio whistle through two fingers; Lupa banged her tin mug against Chisa’s with a grin; and Mei leaned her head back against Iuno’s hip, laughing out loud into the cold air.

In the middle of the bench, with Cartethyia’s cloak tucked over her right knee and Amy’s shoulder warm against her left shin, Yangyang lifted her mug of hot Mingting soup and drank.

In the wash of light coming over the pass, the small cerulean feathers along her collar lifted soft and full around her throat, catching the glow of the eaves-lantern behind her head.

For a long while nobody on the landing spoke. They sat together in the warmth of the charcoal brazier, passing the slate of remaining hawthorns from hand to hand while the red lanterns swung gently along the eaves below and the paper boats sailed from one side of the cistern to the other.

Then the wind coming up from the river-seam grew colder.

Out over the pass, the great round disc of the moon climbed clear of the eastern spires into the open sky above the Hold.

Usually, once a winter moon leaves the mountain mist behind, its face washes clean to the white of new salt. Tonight, the higher it rose toward the dark, stalled shadow of the Astrolabe Core, the deeper its colour sat—not white, and not the pale gold of a lamp, but a heavy, dark amber-red, like hawthorn sugar left too long over the coals, or an old copper coin pulled out of river mud.

And it did not hang still above the pass.

Even while the children’s candle-stubs were still burning half an inch high in their paper hulls, the copper-red shadow of the parapet coping had already slid the width of a boot across the flagstones at Chisa’s feet.

The moon was moving westward across the narrow gap of sky between the peaks—smoothly, without a jerk or a sound, yet already past the meridian where it belonged at midnight and tilting downward toward the black western crags.

On the low wall, Chisa turned her face slightly toward the western ridge, where the shadow of the wooden turbine was lengthening across the yard.

Beside the oak stool, Mei reached out from beneath her grey wool blanket, found Iuno’s bare right hand where it rested on her shoulder, and laced her fingers through Iuno’s, closing her grip tight and warm around Iuno’s palm.

Iuno’s fingers closed back around hers. Beneath the left lapel of Iuno’s heavy wool coat, where the small velvet box sat flat beside Suisui’s slip of ledger-paper, Iuno’s shoulder stayed still and solid against Mei’s cheek.

Down on the terraced steps below the storehouse, the banging of the copper wash-basins faltered and stopped. One by one, the voices along the balustrades went quiet again, leaving only the whisper of the sluice-water in the drains and the small, yellow candle-flames of the paper boats still drifting across the black cistern pond after the sliding copper light.

***

At the third watch, the wooden steps inside the gatehouse tower creaked under two pairs of boots.

“I don’t care what Buling says, that woman in the storehouse has lost her mind,” Ruoyun muttered as she came round the top turn of the timber stair, holding her shielded brass tally-lamp in one hand and touching the back of her other wrist to her mouth with a grimace. “Millet paste with mashed persimmons and raw garlic. Buling told me it was a southern sweet-cake and made me take a bite off her chopstick by the well. I’ve drunk three cups of tea and I can still taste it behind my nose.”

Two risers behind her, Qingxiao stepped up onto the cedar planks of the high watch gallery, her indigo oilcloth drawn around her shoulders and her breath steaming white in the cold draft under the eaves.

“You ate the whole spoonful,” Qingxiao said.

“I didn’t want to spit it out in front of the pikemen!” Ruoyun snapped, pushing her round brass spectacles up her nose with one knuckle as she walked down the gallery toward the corner embrasure. “And nobody down there said a word of thanks for the bitter melon, either. Next year they can eat turnip rinds.”

Her shoes stopped three paces from the corner pillar.

Qingxiao came up beside her left elbow and stopped as well.

On the broad, flat limestone cap of the corner embrasure, the dark kaya-wood weiqi board still sat squarely where they had left it at dawn. Beside it stood the tall, sky-blue porcelain wine-pot and the two small handleless cups, cold now and rimmed with a fine dust of night mist.

On the nineteen-by-nineteen grid, the nine matte-black handicap stones still rested on the star-points. In the fourth-line corner nearest the pine bench sat the single white clam-shell stone Qingxiao had laid down in the third watch the night before.

Directly beneath the white stone—on the third-line shoulder point, one intersection closer to the edge of the board—sat a tenth flat, matte-black slate stone.

Down in the guardroom at the foot of the only stair, the two Wardens on the brazier watch had not stirred from the doorway since midnight.

Neither Qingxiao nor Ruoyun reached toward the board.

Ruoyun held her brass tally-lamp a hand’s breadth higher, the yellow light glinting off her small golden horns and the dark, smooth slate of the new stone sitting quiet against the grain of the wood.

Out beyond the stone parapet rail, the red festival lanterns along the Hold’s market lanes had burned out one by one in the cold, leaving the tiered roofs dark below the mist. Overhead, the colossal celadon shell of the Astrolabe Core hung black and motionless in its skewed bronze rings—and far to the west, hours before the morning drum, the tarnished, copper-red rim of the full moon was already sinking down behind the black rock of the ninth karst spire.

Half a mile across the dark valley, high on the empty flagstones at the top of the Fogveil Pagoda stair where the last two red lamps burned before the frosted, bolted jade doors, a single round brown umbrella stood open in the mist.

# Local News
*Further afternoons*

### IX. The East Beach

Zani woke with a pine needle inside her collar and Phoebe standing over her, holding one shoe.

“Something’s living in the other one. I felt it move when I put my foot in.”

Zani lifted the blue coat from her face. Down the beach, their picnic basket stood open beside a heap of shells. A small, purposeful scratching came from the shoe beneath the basket.

“Bring it here by the heel.”

Phoebe crouched beside her. Together they tipped the shoe onto its side. A crab emerged, dragging a piece of crust nearly its own size.

They watched it carry lunch toward the water.

“He went all the way to the toe,” Phoebe said. “I thought the lining had folded over.”

Zani shook sand from the shoe and felt inside it with two fingers. Then she handed it back and reached for the basket.

They had bought the bread beside the ferry landing. Phoebe had insisted on waiting while the baker brought a fresh tray out, and Zani had stood in the sun holding the blanket, watching passengers hurry past them toward the boat. Their boat had gone while the baker was counting change. They had taken the next one.

Now the bread was warm from the beach. Zani tore a piece and dipped it into the jar of tomatoes.

“How long was I asleep?”

“Long enough for me to walk to the point and back. There’s a cave around the corner. The water comes right through it.”

Zani looked toward the headland. Phoebe sat down with her feet stretched into the sunlight and began spreading her shells on the blanket. She had sorted them by colour, with a separate heap for ones whose undersides she liked.

“We could come back when the tide’s lower,” she said. “The ferryman says there are little pools beyond the cave.”

“Tell me which morning, and I’ll arrange the desk.”

Phoebe glanced at her. Then she moved a shell from one pile to another.

“I keep trying to work out when everyone will be free. Alice wants to come, and Laevatain would like the cave. Palla’s been asking about the sea.”

“We can bring them another day. I’d like another morning with you.”

A breeze lifted the edge of the blanket. Phoebe put the tomato jar on it.

“I would like that too.”

Zani lay back on her elbows. Above them, the pine branches moved against a bright patch of sky. Phoebe was looking at her with the small crease between her eyebrows that usually preceded a question about something she had been considering for weeks.

“My aunt has started sending suggestions for the wedding,” she said.

Zani lowered the bread.

“I wondered what was in those envelopes. The last one looked expensive.”

“A list of venues. She’s visited some of them herself. There are sketches of the kitchens.”

“That was thorough of her.”

“She’s very interested in the kitchens.” Phoebe picked a grain of sand from the blanket. “I’ve been looking at the Guild courtyard. We could put the tables along the wall, where the washing lines are.”

Zani pictured the courtyard: the uneven paving, the drain that needed clearing each autumn, the stair where she sat to pull off her boots. Phoebe’s little pots of herbs occupied the best patch of sun.

“We’d have to borrow chairs,” she said. “And somebody would need to keep the passage clear.”

“I’ve thought about the chairs. The school has folding ones.”

Zani smiled into her bread.

“You’ve thought about quite a lot.”

“I like the courtyard. I know where everything goes.” Phoebe drew her knees up. “I want to come down those stairs and find you there. I keep getting that far when I picture it.”

Zani set the bread on the paper. Phoebe watched her brush her fingers clean, then held out her hand.

“I can be there,” Zani said. “We should measure the space when we get home.”

Phoebe leaned across the shells to kiss her. One pressed sharply into Zani’s palm; she moved it aside and pulled Phoebe closer by the waist.

They stayed beneath the pines until the shade reached their feet. On the walk to the landing, Phoebe carried her shoes. Zani carried the basket and a pocketful of shells, which clicked against one another whenever she took a longer step.

The ferryman saw them coming and kept the gangplank down.

### X. Third Ward Noodles

The shopkeeper wanted her capybara on the sign.

She brought it out to meet Lynae and Rebecca, although they had been buying noodles beneath its supervision for months. It sat on the counter with its front feet together and a strand of grass caught below its mouth.

“That’s the expression,” the woman said. “People will recognise him from that.”

Rebecca leaned down until they were eye to eye.

“He’s looking at me like I owe him rent.”

“You owe me for two extra eggs. I expect he remembers.”

Lynae spread her drawings on the counter. The capybara stepped onto the nearest sheet, lowered its nose, and began eating the corner.

Rebecca retrieved the paper. Lynae slid the other drawings beneath her elbow.

“We’ve established a preference. Let me show you the one he’s about to destroy.”

The design filled the side panel beneath the awning: a capybara sitting in a noodle bowl, with steam rising around its ears and the shop’s name curved across the top. Along the lower edge, Lynae had drawn the canal, the tram bridge, and several small figures waiting to eat.

The shopkeeper studied it while she wiped her hands on her apron.

“Could you give him his red cushion? My daughter made it when she was learning to sew.”

Lynae added it beneath the animal’s round body. The shopkeeper smiled and went inside to find the cushion itself.

They painted on a morning when the shutters were usually down. Rebecca scrubbed the panel while Lynae laid out the lettering in chalk. From inside came the sound of stock being stirred and the occasional scrape of a stool.

By noon, people had begun stopping on their way to the tram.

A delivery driver asked whether they could put his cart in the queue. A woman with a basket pointed out that the real bridge had four arches. Lynae moved the chalk line and thanked her. Rebecca supplied the cart with an enormous wheel, then reduced it after the driver protested that it made his horse look ill.

Sigrika arrived carrying lunch and a book she had intended to read while she waited.

She looked at the bird perched on the painted bridge.

“You’ve given him a seed-eater’s bill and a tail from something that hunts in reeds.”

Rebecca sat back on her heels.

“I’ve given him a job watching the noodles. He can eat whatever keeps him interested.”

Sigrika put the food down and opened her bag. Her bird journal had a ribbon tied around the cover to hold the loose pages in.

“There’s one that nests above the water outlet. He stands exactly like that. I drew him last week.”

She found the page and held it at the angle of the wall. Rebecca leaned closer. The bird in the journal had one foot tucked beneath its body and its feathers lifted by a draught.

“Look at his stupid little legs,” Rebecca said. “Show me how they fold; I want him standing exactly like that.”

For the next twenty minutes, Sigrika held the journal while Rebecca worked. Lynae painted steam above them, listening to an increasingly detailed account of the bird’s attempts to steal nesting material from a floor brush.

Then Helios began its afternoon change, and the white steam turned faintly green.

Lynae stepped off the stool. She stood in the street with her head tilted, looking between the sign and the awning.

“The cloth’s colouring the light.”

The shopkeeper came out carrying bowls.

“It does that to the soup as well. The customers get used to it.”

Lynae lifted one hand. A narrow band of light slid along her knuckles, spread across the wall, and warmed. For a few seconds the painted canal held the blue of open water. Then she shifted the light toward evening, and the capybara’s red cushion deepened almost to brown.

Sigrika shaded her eyes to look.

“I’d like the water a little greener,” the shopkeeper said. “That’s how it looks from the bridge when I walk home.”

Lynae let the borrowed light disperse. She picked up a clean cup and began mixing paint.

They ate sitting on the kerb while the new colour dried. Rebecca had blue along her jaw where she had pushed her hair aside. Sigrika offered her a damp cloth, watched her smear it farther, and took the cloth back.

“Hold your chin up. You’ve been improving it for several minutes.”

Rebecca submitted, holding her bowl away from the operation.

When the sign was finished, the shopkeeper fetched her daughter from the rooms upstairs. The young woman inspected the painted cushion, then turned to look at her mother.

“You kept using that thing? I could have made you another.”

“He likes this one. Bring it down so they can put it back.”

The capybara returned to the counter and settled onto the faded red cloth. Outside, Lynae and Rebecca signed the lower corner of the panel, fitting their names between two painted ripples.

The shopkeeper paid them while the last stroke was still wet. Rebecca counted her share into her palm and looked down the street toward the shops.

“I’m buying that big pan with the lid, and we’re doing fish in it tonight. I’ve been staring at it every fucking time we pass.”

Lynae put her money away and accepted a second bowl.

“I’m having lunch where I can see our work. Move over a little; you’re covering the bird.”

### XI. The Fitting

Palla arrived at the tailor’s wearing the trousers it had bought with its own wages. They slipped every time it climbed onto a chair.

Laevatain carried a parcel of fresh stuffing under one arm. Under the other, she had her thumb piano, still wrapped in paper that had begun to split at the corners.

The tailor brought out a measuring tape and knelt on the rug.

“Stand as you usually stand. Let the waistband settle where it wants to go.”

Palla did. The trousers descended another inch.

“I would like pockets at this height,” it said, placing both paws against its sides. “I’ve been keeping my money in the front, and it comes out when I bend over.”

The tailor made a mark on her pad.

“We can add braces. Would you like them hidden beneath a waistcoat?”

Palla looked toward the samples hanging beside the mirror. There was a yellow cloth with a small woven pattern that caught the light when the tailor moved it.

“May I feel that one against my neck?”

She draped a length over its shoulders. Palla rubbed the edge beneath its chin and looked at itself in the mirror.

Laevatain put the stuffing on the table.

“We brought this for the shoulder. The seam’s been opening when it carries things.”

“I can look at that while I take the measurements,” the tailor said. “Sit here where the light reaches.”

Palla climbed onto the stool. Laevatain held the trousers until it was settled.

The old shoulder stitching had been repaired in several colours. The tailor followed the seam with a fingertip, pausing at a broad purple loop near the collar.

“Would you like all of this replaced?”

Palla reached up and touched the purple thread.

“Lily did that part before I came to the city. She wanted to help me pack, and Mother gave her the needle with the big eye. Could you leave it where it is?”

The tailor moved her scissors to the next section.

A tuft of stuffing lifted as the seam opened. Palla watched it with great attention. Its right arm settled lower against its side.

“That feels peculiar,” it said. “I can hear the street through my shoulder.”

Laevatain shifted her chair closer.

“Tell her if you want to change how you’re sitting. We’ve got the afternoon.”

Outside, a wheelbarrow passed over the paving. Palla turned its head toward the sound, then toward the window, trying both positions.

“Could you put a little more in the back? Mother used to pack it there when I was small. It was comfortable to lean against things.”

The tailor loosened a handful of the new filling and worked it into place. She asked Palla to move its arm, then added a little beneath the seam.

Its voice changed when it thanked her, the sound emerging lower and rounder.

Palla stopped. Laevatain looked up from her instrument.

“Say something longer. I’d like to hear that properly.”

Palla considered.

“The Northern Desk closes at six, and parcels may be left with the evening porter.”

The tailor laughed into her sleeve.

“That sounds like it belongs in a waistcoat.”

Palla repeated the sentence, turning toward the mirror. The yellow cloth rested across its lap. It touched the fabric with one paw while the tailor finished the seam.

“I would like two inside pockets as well,” it said. “One needs to fit a letter folded twice.”

While the tailor cut the lining, Laevatain unwrapped her thumb piano. A tine had caught in the paper; she eased it free and smoothed the torn corner across her knee.

“Have you got enough of that cloth left for a bag?”

The tailor measured the instrument. Palla watched Laevatain choose a plain dark lining and ask for a flap that could be opened with one hand.

“Mine could have a flap too,” Palla said. “For the pocket with the letter. Mother puts dried flowers in hers.”

They left with another fitting booked, the trousers held securely by temporary braces, and two small parcels. Palla carried the offcuts itself. It intended to send Lily the yellow pieces so she could see the pattern.

At the corner, a Guild courier came toward them pushing a bicycle.

Palla greeted him in its new voice. The man stopped beside a lamp post and looked down.

“You sound rested. Has somebody finally talked you into taking a day off?”

Palla looked up at Laevatain, then back at him.

“I’m having clothes made. We’re going to choose the buttons after lunch.”

### XII. Esterio

The elder of Esterio had invited Angel to a wedding. Augusta came carrying a saucepan large enough to bathe Cub in.

The bride met them at the door, looked inside the parcel, and called her husband over.

“We can do the whole batch in this. Come and feel how heavy the bottom is.”

He took it by both handles. Augusta showed him how the lid fitted and accepted a glass of something sharp enough to warm her chest.

Angel stood beside the doorway with her overnight bag still in her hand.

The elder came through the crowd, took the bag, and beckoned them toward the stairs.

“Your room’s ready. We’ve moved the chest so you can get around the bed.”

The room faced the western fields. Two towels lay on the coverlet. A jug of water stood beside a basin, and the window had been propped open with a piece of wood worn smooth by use.

Angel set her hand on the sill.

“The last time I came through, that field was under water.”

“We put the beans higher this year,” the elder said. “You can go and look tomorrow. Come down when you’ve washed; they’re already arguing about the benches.”

Augusta put the wedding shoes beneath the bed. Angel watched her straighten, then reached for the fastening at the back of her own collar.

“Would you do this? It’s caught in my hair.”

Augusta freed the strand and opened the clasp. There was a red mark beneath it where the metal had pressed during the journey. She touched it with the pad of her thumb.

“You could wear the other blouse.”

“I brought this one for the dancing.”

Augusta let her hand rest at the back of Angel’s neck for a moment before going to the basin.

The meal occupied the lane between two houses. Sheets hung overhead to give shade, tied at different heights so that every breeze moved through them in pale folds. Augusta took the place she was offered between a cooper and the bride’s younger sister. The girl asked about Cub and spent most of the first course drawing him from Augusta’s description.

Angel sat opposite, beside the elder. Twice she rose when someone carried a heavy dish past. Twice another guest made room on the table and took it before she reached them.

By the third course, she was listening to an argument about the spring lambs with both elbows on the table.

The cooper wanted Augusta’s opinion about a disputed fence. His neighbour had set a post on a boundary stone, and the two of them had been moving it between rainstorms.

Augusta asked which way the water ran.

That led to a drawing on the back of the girl’s picture of Cub. She objected. Augusta apologised, found another sheet, and drew the dog again herself while the men leaned over the fence plan.

Across the table, Angel saw the result.

“You’ve made him very long.”

“He grows whenever we leave the house.”

The girl considered the second drawing, then put both into her pocket.

When the musicians began, the bride pulled her husband into the cleared space beside the well. A fiddler tapped the beat with a heel. People shifted benches, collected glasses, and stood close enough to argue amiably about the steps.

Angel found Augusta’s hand beneath the table.

“The ground looks reasonably even by the far wall.”

Augusta put down her glass.

They began with the sequence they knew. Angel counted the first few steps near Augusta’s ear, then let the music carry them. At the turn, Augusta waited until Angel’s weight had settled beneath her hand.

They came around together.

The elder watched from a chair beside the well. Angel saw her as they passed and lost the count. Augusta slowed, holding her through the next beat, and they found their way back into the music.

Later, someone brought the new saucepan out filled with hot wine and floating slices of fruit. The bride carried the ladle. Her husband followed with the cups.

Angel drank hers sitting on a low step, her shoes beside her feet. Augusta sat above her, one knee resting lightly against her shoulder.

By the time the lamps were taken down, the bride’s sister had fallen asleep across a bench with both pictures of Cub tucked into her sash.

Angel woke before sunrise.

She lay still long enough to hear Augusta breathing beside her. Then she dressed, took the empty jug from the washstand, and went downstairs.

The elder was in the kitchen cutting yesterday’s bread for toast.

“Bring that here. The kettle’s nearly ready.”

Angel filled the jug from the covered crock. Through the open door she could see cups drying along the yard wall and one of the wedding sheets still hanging from its rope.

“I thought I’d water the roses before it gets warm.”

“I’ve done the front bed. The ones by the shed can wait until we’ve eaten.” The elder put another slice on the iron. “There’s honey in that jar. Use the spoon with the long handle.”

Angel sat down.

Augusta found them there later, the toast reduced to crumbs, two cups beside an open packet of seeds. She leaned against the doorframe until Angel looked up.

“I was told there would be breakfast.”

Angel reached for the loaf and cut another slice.

### XIII. The Harbour Clinic

Cantarella’s cousin had written three times about the clinic sign. On the fourth occasion, she enclosed a drawing of a person opening the street door and striking it with their forehead.

Carlotta laid the page on the glasshouse table.

“The illustration is persuasive. We should take Nella to see the wall before she paints anything.”

They arrived at the harbour clinic while a fisherman was being scolded for taking his bandage off to inspect the stitches.

Cantarella’s cousin, Vivia, finished fastening the new dressing and put his sleeve back over it.

“Leave that in place until tomorrow. You can bring all your questions with you when I change it.”

He collected his cap from his knees, greeted Cantarella with a startled bow, and struck the sign on his way out.

Vivia waited until the door had closed.

“There’s the difficulty. You may observe it several more times before lunch.”

Nella took out her measuring tape. Carlotta held the door while she checked how far it opened. Cantarella stood beneath the sign, looking up at the heavy wooden frame and the elaborate iron bracket that carried it.

“This came from the west passage at the castle.”

“Your steward was pleased to find a use for it. He sent two men to install it and a third to explain the carving.”

Carlotta examined the clearance above the door.

“We can have it painted directly on the wall. People approaching from the quay will see it sooner.”

Vivia handed her a page with the clinic’s hours written in large, plain letters.

“Those need to be readable from the other pavement. The harbour bell brings the whole street here at once.”

A boy came out of the treatment room carrying a basin with both hands. Vivia took it from him and sent him to fetch clean cloths. Cantarella followed her through the doorway.

The room had two narrow beds and a table crowded with jars. On the wall, the first of Nella’s botanical plates had been pinned beside a calendar. Someone had drawn a bright circle around the date of the next market.

Cantarella stopped before the plate. One corner had curled away from the plaster.

“The leaves show clearly in this light.”

“People bring the page right up to the bed. You can see where they hold it.” Vivia set the basin down. “Hand me that stack of cloth, would you?”

Cantarella lifted it from the chair. Beneath the cloth lay a sandwich wrapped in paper, flattened where the stack had rested.

Vivia saw her looking.

“That was meant to be breakfast. I’ll have it when the next patient leaves.”

“I’ll ask Carlotta to bring something from the quay. She was considering lunch on the way here.”

“Tell her I want the bread with the olives in it. She bought the soft rolls last time, and they fall apart when you eat standing up.”

Cantarella carried the message to the front room. Carlotta accepted it with a small lift of her eyebrows, handed Nella the measuring tape, and went out.

While they waited, Cantarella helped Vivia move a cabinet away from the window. The light reached the second bed once it was shifted. Dust marked the floor where its legs had stood, and the boy came in with a broom before Vivia could ask for one.

“He’s been helping after school,” her cousin said. “His mother works two doors down.”

The boy swept carefully around Cantarella’s shoes.

“My aunt’s got your picture on a book,” he told her. “You’re holding a cup with a snake around it.”

“Did the artist get the likeness?” Cantarella asked.

He looked up at her, considered the question, and went back to his broom.

“They’ve made your hair bigger. It fills most of the cover.”

Vivia turned toward the window. Her shoulders moved once.

Carlotta returned with olive bread, cheese, and a sack of small oranges. They ate behind the clinic, sitting on packing crates in a strip of shade. Vivia loosened her hair and rolled her shoulders while Cantarella divided the bread.

“You used to take the olives out,” the Duchess said. “At family dinners, you left them in a little pile beneath your napkin.”

“I still did that when I came home from university. Mother had been telling everyone how sophisticated I’d become.” Vivia picked a piece from the loaf. “Then I started buying my own food.”

Carlotta passed her an orange. Through the open window they could hear Nella asking the boy to read the proposed lettering from across the street.

He read every word, loudly, and then asked whether she could put his mother’s shop hours up as well.

Vivia leaned back against the wall and closed her eyes while she chewed.

Cantarella put another piece of bread within reach.

### XIV. The Uniform

Chiyo brought the uniform to the dorm in a paper parcel tied with string.

Rebecca cleared the kitchen table while she took off her coat. The cupboard door had been repaired that morning. Every time Rebecca passed it, she opened it and let it close under its own weight.

Chiyo watched the third demonstration.

“The cups are having a peaceful afternoon.”

“Lynae fitted a new plate behind the hinge. You should have seen the amount of drawing involved.” Rebecca moved the lute to a chair. “Put your things here. I’ve wiped it twice.”

The string had worn grooves into the parcel. Chiyo worked the knot loose and folded the paper back from a dark skirt, a blouse, and the collar wrapped separately in tissue.

Rebecca touched the corner of the tissue.

“This is the one you told her about.”

“She wore it in the school photograph. I bought a size up because her sleeves were always getting short.” Chiyo lifted the blouse by its shoulders. “She was cross with me about that.”

The cloth held its old folds. Along one cuff, a small mend had gathered the fabric into a ridge.

Rebecca leaned closer.

“How did she manage to tear that?”

“She caught it climbing over something. She told me the fence was on the way to school.”

Rebecca smiled. Chiyo laid the blouse across the table and passed her palm down one sleeve.

“Her father thought the fence sounded reasonable. He was supposed to be helping me.”

The iron was borrowed from the neighbour at Chiyo’s building. Rebecca filled its little reservoir and checked the cord while Chiyo spread a clean cloth over the blouse.

They began with the back. Chiyo tested the warmth on an inside edge, adjusted the dial, and worked from the centre outward. Steam lifted the smell of stored cotton into the kitchen.

Rebecca held the far end of the sleeve clear of the table leg.

“Pilar put lights in a jacket once. Around the cuffs and all the way up the back. He ran the cable through the lining.”

Chiyo moved the iron slowly toward the seam.

“Did he make it for you?”

“He made it for himself. Then he got sick of charging it and I took it. The sleeves went halfway over my hands.” Rebecca looked at the cuff she was holding. “He said he’d shorten them. We had that conversation every time he wanted the jacket back.”

“You could turn them under. That would give you room at the wrist.”

“I used tape. He had opinions about the tape.”

Chiyo lifted the iron and smiled down at the cloth.

“I expect he wanted his cable back in one piece.”

Rebecca rubbed a finger along the edge of the table. After a while, she reached for the other sleeve and laid it within Chiyo’s reach.

They worked through the blouse, then the skirt. The pleats took longer. Chiyo found the old lines by touch, setting each one beneath the pressing cloth before she brought the iron down.

Lynae came in carrying groceries. She stopped at the table, looked at the uniform, and moved the bags to the floor beside the sink.

“I’ve got something you should hear. Chisa sent it while I was in the market.”

She set her terminal on the windowsill. There was a scrape, a faint voice in the background, then Chisa humming the beginning of the tune Chiyo had tried to remember over the lute.

Chiyo held the iron above its stand.

The phrase rose through several notes, paused, and began again more slowly. Somewhere behind Chisa, Lupa asked about a towel. Chisa moved the terminal, and the room sounds softened.

“He used to start here,” she said. “I remember because I would wait for him to lift me.”

The tune began a third time.

Chiyo put the iron down. Her hand stayed on its handle until the recording ended.

“Would you play the beginning again?”

Lynae touched the screen and sat on the edge of the counter.

This time Chiyo hummed with her daughter. She found the first phrase, followed it into the one she knew, and carried on after the recording had finished. Rebecca reached for the lute and tried the notes beneath it.

The E drifted. She brought her hand up to the peg, made a small adjustment, and began again.

When the blouse had cooled, Chiyo put it on a hanger. The skirt went beneath it, its pleats straight and soft. She hung them from the hook inside the pantry door and stepped back far enough to see the whole uniform.

“She can try it like this,” she said. “We’ll look at the hem together.”

Rebecca brought the new pan out of its wrapping. The lid fitted with a satisfying little turn, and she demonstrated that too.

Chiyo examined it, lifted it by the handle, and asked what she intended to cook.

“I bought the fish myself. The woman had to show me where to cut twice, and then she let me finish.”

“Bring it here. We can start while Lynae puts the shopping away.”

Rebecca took the lute off the table and laid a board in its place. Chiyo hung the pressing cloth over the back of a chair to dry.

On the windowsill, Lynae set the recording to play once more. The first notes reached the kitchen while Rebecca washed her hands.

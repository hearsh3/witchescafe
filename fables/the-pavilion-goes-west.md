# The Pavilion Goes West

By the time Suisui came downstairs, Amy had accused Mei of eating the evidence.

“There were crumbs in the bed,” she was explaining. “Iuno found one inside her sleeve. Ma said it might have come from breakfast, and then she ate it while we were discussing the time.”

Mei reached for the teapot.

“It tasted like last night's bread. I thought that would help.”

“You've made it very difficult to get a second opinion.”

Iuno sat beside the window with her hair pinned up and her new earring catching the sun. She had been awake long enough to make tea, put on clean clothes, and discover that Amy had borrowed the ribbon intended to keep those clothes in place.

The ribbon was now round Amy's hair. Iuno had accepted a different one after a discussion which Lupa had heard from the courtyard.

“You could all have slept downstairs,” Lupa said. “The rest of us would have heard exactly as much.”

“You were laughing before the kettle boiled,” Chisa observed.

“She told her mother she was nine and needed help with the knot. I wanted to hear what happened next.”

“I got my hair tied,” Amy said. “It was an excellent morning.”

Suisui sat beside her, as promised. Yangyang followed with the wider bowl, her hat under one arm and the Wind carrying the loose end of its ribbon clear of the floor.

“Have we missed much?” Suisui asked.

“There was a disagreement over the blankets,” Iuno said. “Your sister arrived in time for the surviving breakfast.”

Mei put a cup in front of Yangyang and smiled at her. Yangyang smiled back, then took the pancakes Lupa passed along.

They were thick, with a sparse scattering of scallions caught in the folds. Lupa bit into hers, considered it, and reached for the pickle dish.

“Mrs Ren's food was better. She gets the edges properly crisp.”

From the kitchen window, the cook looked at the three pancakes on Lupa's plate.

“You seem to be coping.”

“I'm hungry, and they go well with your pickles. I'd still ask her about the edges.”

The cook laughed and shook another cake out of the pan.

“This Mrs Ren can come and show me her method when she's fed the west gate and two carrying parties. Has she got a stove as old as this one?”

“She speaks to hers every morning,” Mei said. “Usually while turning something with great force.”

“Then we'd get along. Tell her Wen at the west kitchen wants to know what oil she uses.”

Ciaccona moved her cup away from Amy's elbow.

“We've been here one morning and acquired an errand between cooks. This is how the long journeys begin.”

“I'd like to take Mrs Ren some of the pickles,” Cartethyia said. “She would know what the little green things are.”

“They're peppercorns,” Yangyang told her.

Cartethyia regarded the one on her spoon with renewed interest.

“That explains what happened to my tongue.”

Amy leaned across Suisui to see. Lupa took the pickle dish out of the way of her sleeve. Mei began telling Iuno that she had thought the green things were peas, and Iuno turned toward her with such an expression that Suisui laughed before she heard the reply.

Wen leaned through the window again.

“Which of you wanted another cake?”

Four people answered at once.

“Put the plate in the middle,” she said. “You'll have something else to argue about.”

The plate went down. Above the wider bowl, steam drifted toward the smell of toasted flour. Yangyang turned it a little so the Wind could enjoy the warmer side.

Suisui watched Amy reach for a cake, discover that Mei had taken the same one, and continue pulling with the calm confidence of a daughter who expected a fair share.

The cake tore. Mei's half dropped into her tea.

Iuno closed her eyes. Lupa's laughter carried into the kitchen.

Suisui buttered her own piece and settled comfortably between the voices. Above her, Dumpling shifted along the window frame to watch Wen turn another cake.

***

Liangyu found them finishing the tea.

She brought the morning report from the Skyworks, which Amy read with a finger following each entry. The braces held. The engineering crew had begun its survey from the marked controls. Nyx remained exactly where Amy had left her.

“She's going to enjoy being admired by people who know what they're looking at,” Amy said, handing the sheet back. “Please tell them I'll read anything they want to send over.”

“They've already filled two pages.”

“Then they are enjoying themselves as well.”

The questions for Hsin had gone onward. The southern dispatch was travelling with the returning supply party. Liangyu had an appointment at the infirmary, and the healer, having examined their dressings, had suggested a quiet morning.

At this, everyone looked toward the breakfast table.

Liangyu smiled.

“You could try the gardens. The lifts are working along the inner terraces. Ask an attendant about the upper walkways; some are still closed for inspection.”

Suisui collected her coat. “Where would you go if you had the morning?”

Liangyu thought about it while fastening the flap of her dispatch bag.

“Huizhen Plaza. I'd buy a sweet cake and sit where I could see people spending money.”

“You should have said that first,” Ciaccona told her.

They left with a small map, a pencilled route to the plaza, and Wen's instruction to bring the map back dry because she wanted to show them where the good pickles came from.

At the top of the gatehouse stair, the passage turned between two white walls. A narrow run of water followed them, slipping beneath small stone bridges at each doorway. Cartethyia leaned over one. A yellow leaf travelled below her, vanished under the bridge, and emerged in a square basin on the other side.

She was watching it begin another journey when the walls ended.

Mei stopped beside her.

Beyond the balustrade, the mountain fell away into white.

The mist rolled between karst spires whose dark, wooded crowns rose at different heights. Sun caught a cliff face far below them. A thread of falling water disappeared into the cloud before it reached anything they could see.

Over all of it hung the city.

Pavilions stood upon terraces that reached out into open air. Their jade-edged roofs overlapped down the slope, each carrying its own bright patch of morning. Bridges crossed above streets; another street ran beneath a watercourse; high overhead a viaduct passed through the curve of an arch large enough to hold the gatehouse in its shadow.

Farther out, beyond the last roofs, the Celestial Ring Formation turned.

They could see one great arc coming out of the mist. Intricate ribs moved within it, each catching the light a moment after the last. A line of green travelled along the inner rim, passed through a suspended joint, and arrived beneath their feet as a low, steady vibration.

Chisa put her hand on the balustrade.

“The whole terrace is answering it.”

Amy had lifted her face so far that the borrowed ribbon slipped against her collar.

“Ma, look underneath the bridge.”

Mei leaned closer to the rail. Below the carved underside, enormous nested brackets changed their angles by tiny degrees. Water ran level across the aqueduct above them. A man pushing a barrow crossed it, stopped to speak to someone at a door, and rested one boot on a stone ornament while several hundred tonnes of engineering adjusted beneath him.

“He lives there,” Mei said.

“I'd spend the first month lying on the balcony looking under my own house.”

“You'd drop things,” Iuno said, coming to Amy's other side.

“I would attach a basket first. I've thought about being this wealthy.”

A freight platform moved out from beneath the viaduct. It bore crates, two men, and a small tree wrapped in sacking. The tree's leaves stirred as the platform crossed a gap full of clouds.

Lupa followed it with her eyes.

“I carried water up four flights last night.”

Suisui put a hand over her mouth.

“We should perhaps have looked out of this window earlier.”

The Wind left her collar. She travelled along the warm stone, rose above the balustrade, and let the high current spread a little through her.

Below the rail was room for a whole weather system.

She could feel the cool pull beneath the ring, the warmer air rising from white roofs, water breathing out of the gardens. She went as far as the end of the terrace, returned with a cherry petal, and set it on Yangyang's hat.

Yangyang turned the hat in her hands.

“You found the gardens already.”

The petal moved toward her nose.

She laughed and put the hat on.

Iuno stood a little apart, watching sunlight travel along the great ring. Mei came beside her and slipped a hand into hers.

“She built all this,” Mei said.

“I'd like to watch her draw one of those joints.” Iuno followed the freight platform until it vanished behind a roof. “And then I'd like her to sit down with yesterday's questions.”

Mei squeezed her hand.

Ahead of them, Amy had already found the next view.

***

Xuanfang Square opened around its resonance spire in wide circles of etched stone.

The patterns underfoot curved toward the obelisk, their grooves catching strips of jade light from the conduits running up its sides. At one edge, two mechanics knelt beside an opened service plate. Citizens stepped around them on the way to the colonnades. An elderly man had set his birdcage in the sun beside a bench and was using the cage's cover to shade his own eyes.

A mechanical hound stood by the crossing.

Its face was made from three fitted plates, with a small polished crest above the eyes. Green light moved behind the joints of its forelegs. When a handcart approached, it turned its head, judged the width, and stepped neatly into a recess in the wall.

Yangyang's pace shortened.

Beside the hound, a Warden adjusted a lead connected to its shoulder housing. An inspection tag hung from the coupling. He looked up as they approached.

“We're walking this one through the square before opening the next route. You can come past on my side.”

Yangyang followed the space he showed her. The Wind remained beneath her collar.

Lupa watched the hound's head until they reached the far paving. Then Cartethyia pointed up at a row of tiles which had begun to lift.

A bell sounded twice.

Along the colonnade, a carved beam rolled upon its long axis. Its painted flowers divided, revealing a line of dark hinges. The tiles folded into two neat stacks; supports slid out from the wall; between one breath and the next, a short stair had become a broad ramp.

A porter waiting with a wheeled litter thanked the attendant and pushed it upward.

Amy turned all the way around to watch the hidden braces lock.

“Do they teach courses on that?”

“The Academy keeps a working model in the west court,” the attendant said. “Follow the water uphill. You'll come out by the lantern ponds.”

Amy looked at Chisa.

Chisa was still feeling the final movement through the stone.

“I'd like to see where the weight goes.”

“Yangyang, come with us,” Amy said. “You can read the labels while I look underneath everything.”

“I'd enjoy the Academy.” Yangyang glanced toward Suisui. “There are seats along the canals. I saw them marked on Liangyu's map.”

Suisui had been looking at a poster beneath the colonnade.

“There's an afternoon rehearsal at the Cloud-Sleeve theatre.”

Ciaccona turned toward the poster before Suisui finished reading its lower line.

Iuno smiled at both of them. “I believe we've just found our party.”

Lupa studied the map over Cartethyia's shoulder.

“The Ministry of Development has a public gallery above the freight docks. I want to see the route that could have brought our water.”

“I'd like the view from there,” Mei said. “And then somewhere to sit down.”

Cartethyia tapped a little drawing beneath the gallery's name.

“It says there's a kite terrace.”

They continued together as far as Huizhen Plaza. Beneath the first wisteria-covered walkway, Mei slowed beside Yangyang while the others stopped at a stall selling folded maps.

“I talked to Iuno last night,” she said. “About our afternoon.”

Yangyang adjusted the strap on her shoulder, then let her hand fall.

“I'm glad you had time.”

“I told her I wanted to keep seeing you. She told me how she felt.” Mei looked at her. “For now, I've agreed to keep kissing just Iuno while we talk about what comes next. I wanted you to hear that from me before I asked for another walk.”

A cart passed beneath the hanging flowers. Its driver ducked a little too late and emerged with purple petals on his cap.

Yangyang watched him brush them away.

“I'd still like your company,” she said. “I may want to talk about how it feels after we've had some more of it.”

“You can tell me. Even halfway through an afternoon.”

Yangyang's mouth curved.

“Today I want to go to the Academy. Amy has already asked the map seller about three buildings.”

“She'll send for me when she finds something enormous.”

“I'll encourage her to describe it first. You should get to enjoy your seat.”

Mei brushed a fallen wisteria blossom from Yangyang's shoulder. The Wind caught it beneath her hand and carried it to the brim of the hat, beside the cherry petal.

Yangyang let it stay.

At the stall, Suisui was buying a second map. Amy had unfolded the first one to its full width and recruited Chisa to hold the other end.

“The Verdant Court at the third afternoon bell,” Suisui said. “There's a tea pavilion with a copper roof. Will that suit everyone?”

“The one by the upper pond?” the seller asked. “Mind the bell there. They move it west at three.”

Amy leaned closer to the map. “How much of it goes along?”

“Whole terrace. Best seat in the Court, if you ask me. Take the outer benches.”

Suisui looked around at the household.

“Then perhaps a little before three.”

***

At Xuanfang Huaxu Academy, the water went upstairs beside them.

It rose in a narrow transparent channel, passed through the mouth of a carved fish, and spilled into the next terrace. Amy stopped to look behind the fish. Chisa continued to the pool above, where cubic lanterns drifted among circles of reflected sky.

“There's a propeller in its tail,” Amy reported when she caught up. “I admire the decision to put the pump in a fish.”

“That fish has a very serious expression,” Yangyang said. “Baizhi would like it.”

“We'll send her a picture of the tail as well. She deserves the whole fish.”

They crossed a low bridge into a court shaded by an old tree. Pale study buildings rose around it, their teal window frames open to the sound of water. A student sat on the steps with a book balanced against her knees and a sleeping cat spread across the lower half. She turned a page by lifting the cat's paw.

Inside the west gallery stood a model of the Hold.

It occupied most of the room. Tiny waterways travelled between miniature pavilions; a little ring hung around them, threaded with green. At its lower edge, a girl in an apprentice's apron was fishing a brush out of a model street.

“Morning,” she said. “You can walk around it. Please keep loose things on your side of the rail. The market section has already eaten my pencil.”

Amy put both hands in her pockets.

“Does it have the same joints as the city?”

“We copied their positions and simplified the inner couplings so people can see them.” The apprentice pointed beneath a terrace. “Would you like to try closing the east approach?”

Amy's hands came out again.

She took the little wooden control and studied its three wheels. Yangyang leaned beside her, reading the worn labels. Chisa crouched until the underside of the terrace came level with her eyes.

Amy turned the first wheel.

A tiny roof folded up. The second wheel drew a bridge inward; the third brought six pale pillars together around the space where the bridge had ended. Jade tiles flicked into place between them. A cage stood over the approach, its sides latticed with the same flowers that had decorated the roofs.

The apprentice moved a wooden beast toward it along a groove.

As its front foot touched the sensing plate, the paving leapt.

Two guardian shapes rose from the street. Their shoulders carried sections of the colonnade; carved roof beasts made their breastplates. One swept a broad arm across the escape path while the other brought its weapon down onto a little brass stop. The trapped wooden beast rocked on its base.

Amy felt Yangyang's sleeve beside hers. She slowed the control and let the figures stand.

“Can you show us how they go back?” Chisa asked.

The apprentice pointed out a catch beneath Amy's thumb.

She eased it over. The weapon folded into a beam. The guardian's legs opened, lowered, and became the sides of a stair. Last of all, the roof beast turned its face toward the sky again.

Yangyang breathed out and looked at the little street.

“I saw that flower on the wall outside.”

“You'll find it all over the Hold. This section was designed by the Sentinel herself.” The apprentice bent to retrieve the wooden beast. “Our teacher makes us sketch it before we touch the model. I used twelve sheets on the shoulder.”

Amy crouched beside Chisa.

“May I see your sketch?”

The girl looked at her, then fetched a portfolio from beneath the bench. There were rubbed-out lines all over the first page. She opened it to a later one with obvious preference.

Amy followed the drawing with her finger held above the paper.

“Oh, that's lovely. The socket carries the gutter through the whole turn.”

“It took me three days to find that part.”

“I would have taken the gutter off and spent the fourth day being rained on.”

The apprentice laughed and pulled out the sheet underneath.

Yangyang took a seat by the open window while the two of them discussed the drawings. The Wind settled on the sunlit sill, lifting a small collection of pencil shavings into a loose ring.

Chisa joined them with a visitor leaflet.

“There are boats in the next court. You guide them through the channels by turning the sluices.”

Yangyang looked up from the water below.

“Would you like to try?”

“I would. Amy can look at the other five pages while we go.”

Amy glanced over the portfolio.

“Save me a boat. I'm nearly at the part where she found the gutter.”

***

The boats were small brass cubes, each holding a coloured flag. A row of wheels controlled the gates in a shallow tiled basin. Above the basin, a notice explained the original water-distribution problem which the exercise represented.

A child with a missing front tooth explained the more immediate rules.

“You have to get yours through the middle arch. If you turn all the wheels, everything goes into the drain.”

Chisa took the blue flag. Yangyang chose green.

The Wind went to the yellow one.

Yangyang held its little mast while the cold settled around it.

“We can take turns with the wheel. Tell me when you've chosen a gate.”

The child watched a sleeve draw gently toward the leftmost control. He moved his stool to make room.

“That one's slow,” he told the air. “It sticks a bit. You'll have to start early.”

They began.

Chisa's boat passed smoothly into the first channel. Yangyang's turned sideways against a wall. She corrected it with a little more water, watched it circle the same corner, and laughed.

“I thought I understood that instruction.”

“You keep helping after it starts moving,” Chisa said. “Let the gate close behind it.”

Yangyang took her hand off the wheel. The green flag travelled under the first bridge.

The yellow boat followed. The Wind touched her wrist, then rested against the next wheel while Yangyang turned it. Together they sent the little cube past a carved reed bed and into an open pool.

Amy arrived as Chisa's blue flag came through the middle arch.

“That shoulder took me fifteen minutes to understand, and you've all learned to run a canal.”

“You can have the red one,” the child said. “Your friend is good at this.”

Amy read the notice, examined the channels, and took her place.

Her first boat went directly into the drain.

Yangyang covered her mouth with her sleeve.

Amy looked down at the red flag standing upright in the little collecting basket.

“It reached a destination very efficiently. I'd like another attempt with the same boat.”

By the fourth attempt, she was arguing with the child about a gate which he insisted on opening early. By the fifth, she began opening it when he told her to. They got through the arch together, and she shook his hand with such gravity that he climbed off his stool to do it properly.

The attendant gave them each a little paper turbine to assemble.

Chisa folded hers beneath the tree. Amy sat beside her, reading the instructions with the concentration she had earlier devoted to the ring. Yangyang drew a chair into the sun and laid the yellow paper across her knees.

Beyond the wall, something sweet was simmering.

The Wind rose from the unfinished turbine.

She knew the smell before the steam came over the tiles: pear skins warming against the bottom of a pot, a little sharpness from the first place they caught. Once there had been a window beside a kitchen, with branches close enough to tap its shutter. She had stayed beside it while somebody complained about the season's fruit. The bowl that came out afterward had been warm at its base and too hot around the rim.

Here, she followed the steam as far as the wall.

Yangyang looked up.

“Is that what you wanted to tell me about?”

The cold touched her open palm.

She turned toward the attendant, who was gathering abandoned flags from the basin.

“Could you tell me what they're cooking next door?”

“Pears in syrup. The little window by the stair sells them.”

They took their paper turbines to the window. Amy bought four portions after the server showed them a shallow saucer for the Wind's. Yangyang sat on the step with the warm dish beside her knee.

“We'll have this part first,” she said softly. “You can show me the rest when you feel like it.”

A sliver of peel turned slowly in the syrup.

Chisa tasted hers and tilted the bowl to find another piece.

“I'm bringing Lupa here. She'll want to know why her portion went to someone with better timing.”

***

Huizhen Plaza had three levels of pedestrians and a woman selling hair combs who appeared to know everyone on all of them.

She called up to a man crossing the overhead walkway to ask after his daughter, accepted payment from Suisui, and caught a packet lowered from the balcony behind her in the same conversation.

“Are you buying this for your sister?” she asked, putting the comb into its paper sleeve.

“For me,” Suisui said. “My sister has acquired several people willing to do her hair.”

“Sensible girl. This one will go through thick hair while it's damp. Try it on the ends first.”

Iuno turned a pale blue comb over in her living hand. Ciaccona watched from beneath a trailing branch of wisteria.

“If you buy that, Amy will want to know why it has a dragon.”

“She may admire the dragon while someone uses it on her.”

The seller took it, added a small cloth pouch, and named the price. Iuno paid before Suisui could begin bargaining.

Suisui looked at her.

“You could have had the pouch included.”

“I wanted the pouch. She seemed to want the money. We arrived at a very pleasant conclusion.”

“I shall stand nearer next time.”

They crossed blue-and-gold medallions set into the street, passing tea houses whose upper rooms leaned over the paving. Maple leaves glowed red behind a balcony rail. Far above the roofs, a logistics carriage slid across a viaduct, its underside bright with reflected sun.

Ciaccona stopped beneath the theatre's hanging sign.

From inside came a man's voice, beautifully sustained, lamenting the cruelty of a woman who had sent him out into the rain.

Another voice answered from very near the door.

“You left your boots in her rice bin, Master Pei. Give her a little time.”

Suisui began laughing before they reached the ticket window.

“I know this one.”

The woman at the window slid three narrow tickets toward her.

“Then you'll know where to clap. We're running the second act while the upper stage is being inspected.”

They took seats beneath an open lattice. The room smelled of tea leaves, old timber, and the sweet paste somebody was eating two rows ahead. Onstage, a paper moon waited beside a painted gate. An actor wearing magnificent sleeves was attempting to conceal a duck beneath one of them.

Ciaccona leaned toward Suisui.

“Tell me the essential history.”

“He promised to bring his wife a jade hairpin. He bought the duck on the way home because it was a bargain.”

“I have met this man in several countries.”

The duck quacked. The actor looked down into his sleeve with wounded dignity.

Iuno settled back, delighted.

*At last,* the High Priestess observed. *A man whose sleeves deserve their reputation.*

Suisui watched the wife enter. Her first turn carried the long silk cuffs in a circle that stopped exactly at her wrists. The music drew breath with her; then her voice rose through the room, bright enough to make the paper moon tremble against its cord.

Suisui's lips moved with the words.

Ciaccona noticed. She followed the tune under her breath through the second phrase, found the interval beneath it, and let her voice rest there.

Suisui turned toward her, smiling, then looked quickly back when the husband attempted to present the duck as a gift of considerable cultural importance.

Iuno laughed so hard that she had to put her cup down.

During the pause, a young attendant came down the aisle with a tray.

“Would any of you like a word-sheet? We're trying the audience chorus this afternoon.”

Suisui reached for one. “Does Madam Luo still take the wife's part?”

“She's teaching it now. That's her niece.”

“I saw her in Mingting years ago. She used to hold the last note until the man began looking for somewhere to sit.”

The attendant grinned.

“Come to the evening performance. She's added a stool.”

When the rehearsal resumed, Suisui laid the word-sheet across her knees. Ciaccona moved her chair closer so they could share it. Iuno took the lower line after hearing it once.

They sang the chorus from their seats. Around them, people joined at different moments, some reading, some already laughing at the next verse. Onstage, the wife pointed toward the audience with her closed fan, inviting every woman present to inspect the duck her husband had bought with the hairpin money.

Suisui sang the last line with her chin lifted and her whole hand curled around the edge of the sheet.

At the end, the actress bowed toward their row.

“The lady by the lattice knows how that part goes.”

Suisui's face warmed.

“My sister and I were meant to be having vocal lessons while I was learning it.”

“Then your teacher received some help.”

Iuno covered a laugh with the back of her hand. Ciaccona folded the word-sheet very carefully and gave it to Suisui to keep.

Outside, the plaza had filled with the smell of lunch.

Suisui stopped under a crimson maple and looked at the little packet in her hand.

“I'd like to see the evening performance.”

“Let's ask about seats on the way back,” Iuno said. “I have become invested in the duck.”

Suisui looked from her to Ciaccona, then toward the box office. She went back and bought three tickets.

***

Lupa found the freight lift by following a delivery of cabbages.

The attendant heard where they wanted to go and pointed to a smaller platform beside it.

“Visitors use that one. Sit on the bench while it rises.”

Mei sat immediately. Cartethyia took the place beside her, and Lupa leaned against the rail as the gate closed.

Below them, the cabbages began ascending with considerable dignity.

“Their platform has more room,” Lupa observed.

“They've paid for the full load,” the attendant said. “You three are travelling for free.”

Mei looked at the crates rising beside them.

“I think we've done very well.”

The platform lifted clear of the roof. A broad channel of water opened beneath them, pale green in the sun. They passed the swept eaves of a tea house, an upper passage hung with washing, and a little balcony where a boy was trying to persuade a cat to come through the window.

Above, the Ministry of Development rose in terraces of pale masonry and glowing louvered glass. Cyan light ran behind the upper windows. Hoists moved through the lower levels, receiving cargo from rails which branched across the face of the building. White camellias flowered in long beds along the visitor walk.

Cartethyia watched the beds approach.

“Mine could look like that eventually.”

“Yours has become very well travelled,” Mei said. “It'll have plenty to discuss with the local plants.”

“I left her by the window. Cia said the roots ought to have a quiet morning.”

Lupa put a hand on her shoulder as the lift settled.

“Then we'll find something to bring back that fits beside the pot.”

The public gallery occupied the outer edge of the ministry. Behind glass lay old joints, sections of conduit, tiles whose glazed flowers concealed gears at their backs. One display held an entire corner of a pavilion, suspended so visitors could walk beneath the mechanism.

Mei went underneath it and looked up.

Lupa read the label. Cartethyia found a second one on the far side, explaining how the corner could become part of an Autopuppet's knee.

“Imagine looking up at your roof and knowing which bit it would walk on,” she said.

“I'd choose the room over the shoulder,” Mei replied. “You'd get a better view.”

“You'd spend the entire journey hanging your head out.”

“Amy would be beside me. We'd take turns holding the basket.”

Lupa's tail swept once behind her.

Through the gallery windows, beyond a grove of bamboo, stood the Ministry of War. Its tiered bastion rose above shallow reflecting pools, with a vast jade crest suspended before the entrance. Detachments crossed the forecourt in ordered lines. On a farther platform, mechanics stood inside a marked square with a wheeled testing frame between them.

A gong sounded.

The mechanics withdrew. One pulled a cord.

The frame struck the sensing stone, and a section of the bastion came apart.

Bricks turned edge-on. Jade tiles flashed as they rolled over the beams beneath them. Two pillars bent through the middle, met above the testing frame, and locked into a cage. Along its flank, an Autopuppet rose with a weapon already levelled through the bars.

The whole movement finished while the gong was still sounding.

Mei had straightened. Beside her, Lupa's hand rested on the window ledge.

Across the gap, a mechanic climbed onto the caged frame and chalked a mark on one joint. Another shouted a measurement. The waiting detachment began making notes.

“They're checking the return,” Cartethyia said, following the chalk.

The puppet lowered its weapon. The pillars opened. A minute later, the mechanics were standing beneath an ordinary ornamental arch with a length of cord hanging from it.

Lupa rubbed the window ledge beneath her palm.

“I'd like them to get every measurement they need.”

They watched the next test from the bench.

Afterward, Mei leaned back and tipped her face into the sun.

“I could stay here until somebody brought noodles.”

Cartethyia was looking through the next window.

“There's a woman eating some on the kite terrace.”

Mei sat up again.

***

The kites flew below the city.

Mei discovered this when the woman with the noodles told her to look over the rail.

Far beneath the terrace, bright shapes moved against the white cloud: a red fish with a streaming tail, two swallows, something large and yellow whose owner described it as a tiger. Thin lines rose from them to reels fixed along the balustrade. People sat beside the reels on small stools, drinking tea while their kites dipped through the mist.

Warm air came up beneath the terrace in a broad, steady current.

“You lower them into the lift,” the woman explained. “Then let the line out as they take it. Watch that fish; he's using the left-hand current.”

Lupa leaned over, interested at once.

“How do you get them down there?”

“There's a little cradle beneath the rail. It carries the kite past the building. You can hire one from my grandson.”

The grandson stood behind a table covered in paper creatures. He had a pencil tucked behind his ear and an expression suggesting he had answered this question throughout his entire childhood.

“The small swallows are easiest. The flowers turn more. The dragon takes two people.”

Cartethyia reached for the dragon.

Lupa reached for it at the same time.

Mei sat down beside the woman eating noodles.

“I'd like whatever you ordered.”

“Tell the stall at the door. They'll bring it here.”

Mei ordered three bowls, then selected a kite shaped like a fat silver fish. It had blue fins, a red mouth, and the cheerful vacancy of something prepared to spend the day admiring the weather.

“This one has my ambitions,” she said.

The grandson showed her how to fit it into the cradle. A small gate opened beneath the rail; the cradle swung outward and down. For a moment the fish hung against the immense drop, its paper sides shining.

Then the air filled it.

Mei felt the tug through the reel. She let out a little line. The fish went down and outward, turned broadside to the sun, and drew a long blue curve above the clouds.

She began laughing.

The boy looked at her, pleased despite his practised patience.

“You can let it have more.”

Mei did.

Beside her, the dragon had encountered difficulties.

It was long, green, and beautifully articulated. Lupa had the forward reel; Cartethyia held the second, which governed the angle of the tail. The head rose splendidly. The tail passed over it. The entire dragon folded itself around an ear.

“Ease yours a little,” Lupa said.

Cartethyia loosened the reel. The dragon recovered, pointed directly toward Mei's fish, and began a determined courtship.

Mei drew her line gently aside.

“He's very handsome. Mine would like a little time to consider.”

The woman set down her noodle bowl.

“Which of you is telling the other when to turn?”

Lupa and Cartethyia looked at each other.

The woman nodded with the satisfaction of a diagnosis reached early.

“Start there. The dragon has heard enough opinions.”

Cartethyia sat on the stool beside Lupa.

“You take the head. I'll watch your hand and follow.”

Lupa tried a small movement. The head turned; the tail came after it. The dragon straightened in the rising air and went out beneath the terrace, green scales flashing one after another.

“There he goes,” Cartethyia said. “Keep doing that; I like him with all his parts in order.”

Lupa gave it a little more line. The dragon dipped so low that its tail disappeared into the mist, then climbed again with droplets shining along the paper.

For several minutes they watched it fly.

Mei ate her noodles with one hand and kept the fish's reel beneath the other. Beyond its blue fins, the underside of Xuanfang stretched across the sky: deep ribs of stone and metal, channels full of running light, great suspension housings disappearing into the mist. A pair of maintenance workers crossed a walkway beneath a garden. Above them, roots descended through latticed planters into carefully covered beds.

The fish turned lazily in front of it all.

Mei wished Amy could see the view. She took out her Terminal, found the local channel, and sent a photograph.

The reply arrived while she was drinking the broth.

*I leave you alone for an hour and you acquire a balcony fish. We found pear syrup. Save the address.*

Mei showed Lupa.

“She's eating,” Lupa said, satisfied. “Tell her the view is better from the chair.”

Cartethyia's noodles had cooled to a comfortable temperature. She took a mouthful, gave a pleased little hum, and transferred the tail reel from one hand to the other.

Lupa glanced at her.

“You could lock it while you finish.”

“I like holding it. You can tell me when you want to bring him in.”

Lupa settled back on the stool.

“We have time for another bowl of tea.”

At the table behind them, the grandson began painting another fish. Mei watched him give it one enormous eyebrow, considered sending Baizhi a second photograph, and decided to save the pleasure for later.

***

By the time they reached the Verdant Court, Cartethyia had bought the dragon.

It folded into a long, slim case. Lupa carried it over one shoulder with the borrowed map wrapped around its handle. Mei carried a paper bag of sesame twists for the people who had spent lunch elsewhere.

The Court rose before them in broad paths and shallow steps, pink petals drifting across pale paving. Cherry branches met above the lower walk. Beyond them grew palms with wide, glossy leaves and banks of white flowers whose scent reached the path in warm gusts.

Cartethyia stopped under the first tree.

A petal landed on the end of her nose.

She looked down at it until Mei began laughing, then blew it into the air.

“Cia would like this corner,” she said.

“Cia's already in it.”

The bard sat beneath the next tree with her boots stretched into the sun. Suisui was showing Iuno the inside of a small paper bag. There was a sweet cake missing from it.

“We had to eat one to decide whether to bring the others,” Suisui explained as they approached.

Lupa put the dragon case down against the bench.

“We stopped for noodles on the way. You'll want to try that stall.”

“I see the investigation came with equipment,” Ciaccona said.

Cartethyia sat beside her and began opening the case.

“He folds at every joint. We can hang him above the window when the weather's wrong for flying.”

Ciaccona took the first painted section as it emerged.

“How much of our window does he intend to occupy?”

“I'll show you when we get back. Lupa had to take the front while I worked the tail.”

“That answers the question with alarming accuracy.”

She stroked a fingertip along one translucent green scale. Cartethyia leaned against her shoulder while showing her how the tail fitted into the next segment.

Amy arrived behind a yellow paper turbine.

The Wind held it at shoulder height, turning its blades as she travelled. Yangyang walked beside it with two more folded into a book. Chisa carried a little jar wrapped in a cloth.

Lupa's ears lifted.

“That had better be the pears.”

“I thought you would ask,” Chisa said, giving her the jar. “There's a spoon in the fold.”

Amy went straight to Mei.

“Show me the rest of the photographs. You sent the fish and half a bridge. I've spent twenty minutes imagining the other half.”

Mei drew her down onto the bench beside her. Iuno moved the cake bag into Amy's lap and leaned over to see as well.

“We went up with the cabbages,” Mei began.

Amy looked at her mother, then at Iuno.

“I would like that explained in full.”

***

The copper-roofed pavilion stood on an oval terrace above the upper pond.

Its benches followed the outer rail. Tea tables fitted into the spaces between them, and beyond the tables a small cherry tree grew out of a stone planter worked with cranes. Several people had already settled beneath it. A woman was asleep with her shopping bag between her feet.

The attendant brought two pots and asked everyone to put loose belongings inside the yellow line.

Lupa drew the dragon case in with her boot. Amy put her paper turbine in her lap. The Wind held the yellow one above Yangyang's shoulder, turning it slowly while Suisui poured.

They were still distributing the cakes when the first bell sounded.

Under the terrace, something unlocked.

Chisa felt a soft succession of movements through the soles of her shoes. She leaned forward, interested. At the near end of the pond, white paving separated along an almost invisible curve. A thin green line shone inside it.

Then the entire terrace began to move.

The cherry tree went with them. So did the benches, the sleeping woman, and the little brass tray on which the attendant had left their change. Water gleamed along the widening gap until a curved bridge folded down behind them and met another path.

Amy turned on the bench, one hand on Mei's knee.

“That bridge was underneath us the whole time.”

Mei rested a hand over hers. The path they had walked up was drifting sideways beyond the rail. In its place came a view through the Court's flowering trees, then the high roofs of Huizhen, then a broad, startling reach of sky.

The terrace travelled along the outer curve of the garden.

Below it, streams crossed from one stone channel to the next, their spouts turning as the banks moved. A waterfall drew a silver curtain beneath the rail. Through it Mei could see the dark shape of a mountain standing up from the mist.

The great ring came into view above them, so close now that sunlight showed the fine carving along its inner face.

Cartethyia tipped her head back. Ciaccona caught the cake beginning to slide off her plate.

“You've nearly donated your lunch to the scenery.”

“Look at the little roofs along the rim. There are people walking up there.”

Ciaccona looked. A line of tiny figures crossed between two suspended pavilions, their shadows travelling over a surface wider than the street below.

She put both their plates safely on the table.

“I should have bought more paper.”

Beside her, Suisui had risen just enough to see over the flowering planter. Iuno steadied her tea, and Suisui sat down again, laughing at herself.

“I've spent half the morning telling Yangyang to keep sitting.”

“The railing is a very tempting height,” Iuno said. “I understand how you arrived at the decision.”

Yangyang watched them from the opposite bench. Her sister had a purple flower caught in her hair and a theatre ticket tucked inside her sleeve. When she turned toward Ciaccona, the ticket showed for a moment, and Yangyang's smile deepened.

“She used to come home with those hidden in her shoes,” she told Chisa.

“She bought seats for tonight. I heard her telling Iuno where the good ones are.”

Yangyang took another cake from the bag.

“I'm glad she has company.”

The terrace reached its western mooring and settled with a small, firm click. The woman beneath the cherry tree woke, checked the sun, and moved her shopping bag into the new patch of shade.

The attendant came back to refill their pots.

“You can stay through sunset. The return is after the evening lamps.”

Amy looked at the view and then at Mei, who had already leaned back against the bench.

“How attached are you to walking somewhere else?”

Mei drew her close enough to kiss her hair.

“Bring the picture of that model over here. I'd like to hear about your morning.”

***

They learned about the gutter while eating sesame twists.

Amy had photographed the apprentice's drawing with her permission. She enlarged it on the cracked screen, pointed out the hidden coupling, and borrowed Iuno's comb to demonstrate how the two pieces passed through each other.

“Gently,” Iuno said. “I had imagined it spending its first day in somebody's hair.”

Amy looked at the little dragon carved along its back.

“You bought this today?”

“For the stubborn patch above your ear. Suisui has explained how much more pleasantly I could have bought it.”

Amy ran a finger along the teeth, then handed it to Mei.

“You can try it while I show you the second photograph.”

Mei took the comb. Amy turned sideways on the bench and settled between her knees, still talking. Iuno held the screen at an angle both of them could see.

Across the table, Lupa had opened the pear jar. Chisa told her which window sold it while Cartethyia and Yangyang examined the turbine's folded centre.

“You can make this shape with a square?” Cartethyia asked.

“Amy has the instructions. You turn the corners in twice.”

The Wind tilted her yellow one toward them. Its blades caught the western light and turned.

Suisui took a picture.

She examined it, moved a little, and took another.

“I'm trying to get the ring behind her,” she explained. “Ma will want to know what the yellow thing is.”

“Hold it a little lower,” said a man passing with a wooden camera case. “The light's coming through the leaves there. You'll get the whole shape from this side.”

Suisui tried. He waited until she had the angle, then offered a small card from his pocket.

“I do group portraits, if you'd like one together. Printed while you finish your tea.”

Amy looked up from the screen.

“We should. Suisui can be in it as well.”

The photographer opened his case. His camera unfolded upon three slender legs, a green light travelling around its lens as he adjusted it. He asked which view they preferred and received enough answers to make him smile.

“The western rail will get you the city and the ring. There's room for the tall case beside the bench.”

Lupa moved the dragon. Ciaccona gathered the wrappers, and Mei put the comb into its pouch before rising. Suisui began arranging herself at the far end until Yangyang caught her sleeve.

“Sit here. I want you beside me.”

The photographer brought another stool. Chisa took it, turned toward his voice, and asked him to speak again when he was ready.

“I will. Does everyone have somewhere comfortable?”

Cartethyia had one knee tucked onto Ciaccona's bench. Lupa rested a hand on Chisa's shoulder. Amy stood behind Mei and Iuno with an arm around each, taking an excessive interest in where her mother's hair fell.

“You're covering the earring, Ma. You spent ages choosing it.”

“I spent ages letting you choose it with me.”

Iuno reached up to free the chain. Amy smoothed Mei's hair back, then did the same for Iuno.

The two blue stones moved in the sun.

At the other end, the Wind carried her turbine between the sisters. Suisui lifted the edge of her scarf beneath it, making a bright curve across the gap.

The photographer studied the arrangement.

“Would the lady with the yellow windmill like it held steady, or shall I take it as it turns?”

The blades quickened.

Yangyang watched them, then smiled toward the lens.

“She likes that part. Please take it turning.”

Dumpling came down from the pavilion roof and landed on Suisui's shoulder with a small mechanical click.

The photographer adjusted his focus.

“Very good. Everyone look this way when I speak. I'll take several.”

He took the first while Amy was still moving a strand of hair. In the second, Cartethyia had begun laughing at something Ciaccona said. In the third, a breeze caught Lupa's hair and drew it across Chisa's cheek; Chisa lifted it away with the familiarity of someone who had done the same thing last night.

Mei laughed, and Amy bent over her shoulder.

The camera gave a soft chime.

***

Suisui bought a copy of the third picture for her mother.

She laid it on the tea table while the ink dried. The city rose behind them in layers of white stone, teal glass, and sunlit roofs. At the edge of the image the ring curved out of sight. In front, Amy had her cheek almost against Mei's hair, and Iuno was looking toward them with her mouth open in laughter.

The yellow turbine had become a bright little wheel between the sisters. Beneath it, Suisui's scarf curved around a space full of light.

She touched the edge of the print with one finger.

“I'll have to write the names on the back. She'll read my letter with this beside her.”

Yangyang leaned closer.

“You could tell her Cia took you to the theatre.”

“I took Cia. Your mother will appreciate the distinction.”

“She'll appreciate hearing about the tickets in your shoes.”

Suisui gave her a look of profound sisterly disappointment and reached for the pencil.

At the next table, Amy was negotiating for copies of all three. She wanted one for their room, one for Chixia and Baizhi, and one in which her mother was visibly being arranged by other people.

“For historical completeness,” she explained.

Iuno counted the prints.

“We'll need a stiff envelope. Your history has begun collecting tea.”

Mei moved the cup and found the map beneath it. A small translucent stain had spread over the Ministry of Development.

Lupa looked at it over her shoulder.

“Wen said to keep it dry.”

“It's oil,” Mei said. “From the sesame twists.”

Lupa took the map and held it up to the light.

“You're explaining that part yourself.”

They stayed until the sun reached the lower roofs. The spire in Xuanfang Square shone jade against the deepening air. Cyan lines brightened along the Ministry of Development; beyond the bamboo, the great crest of the Ministry of War cast its reflection across the pools.

Below the terrace, mist turned gold where the light entered it and violet in the shelter of the mountains. The dark tips of the karst spires stood above it like islands at evening.

Mei had grown quiet beside Amy. Her daughter put the photographs into their envelope, settled back, and let her mother rest against her shoulder for a few minutes.

Iuno watched them while fastening the new comb's pouch.

“We'll be back after the performance,” she said. “I may have strong opinions about the duck.”

“Bring us the programme,” Amy replied. “Ma will want to know what happens to the hairpin.”

Mei opened one eye.

“I want the wife to keep the duck too.”

“I'll pass your concern to the company.”

Iuno kissed her forehead, then Amy's, and went to join Suisui and Ciaccona at the little bridge. Suisui had put the comb in her hair. Her theatre ticket rode openly between her fingers.

Yangyang watched her sister walk away beneath the cherry trees, talking with both hands now that Iuno was carrying the cake bag.

The Wind settled beneath Yangyang's collar. A cool touch reached the side of her neck.

“I saw,” Yangyang murmured. “She'll tell us all about it when she gets back.”

***

Wen examined the map when they returned.

“You found the sesame stall.”

Mei nodded. Amy stood beside her with the envelope under her arm, waiting to hear how the explanation developed.

“We also found the kite terrace. The noodles there were very good.”

“My cousin runs that window. She puts ginger in the broth.” Wen turned the map over and drew a small circle beside Huizhen Plaza. “Get your pickles here tomorrow. Tell him which kitchen sent you.”

Cartethyia began helping Lupa bring the dragon case through the door. It caught on the frame, turned, and went through when Chisa lifted the lower end.

Wen watched its length pass.

“Where are you putting that?”

“Above our window,” Cartethyia said. “It has a very graceful tail.”

“Mind the lamp when you find out how graceful.”

She took down bowls for supper. Amy brought the photograph over while Mei fetched the spoons.

Wen dried her hands before touching it.

“There you all are. You found a good place to sit.”

“The place took us west,” Amy said. “Ma enjoyed that part enormously.”

Mei looked over her shoulder, smiling.

“I'm going to recommend it to Mrs Ren. She could put her feet up and have the view come to her.”

“Put my question about the oil underneath.”

Amy drew a fresh sheet toward her and began the letter. From upstairs came the sound of Lupa and Cartethyia discussing the width of the window, followed by Chisa suggesting that they open the case before deciding where to put the hooks.

Yangyang sat beside the kitchen window. She placed the warm saucer on the sill, and the Wind brought her yellow turbine to rest beside it.

Outside, one of Xuanfang's high walkways turned slowly across the stars. A line of small lamps went with it. Through the opposite windows, people were setting tables, shaking out quilts, leaning over supper to finish what they had been saying.

Amy looked up from her letter.

“How much of the dragon should I tell Mrs Ren about?”

Mei set the spoons down.

“Include the measurements. She'll want to choose the window.”

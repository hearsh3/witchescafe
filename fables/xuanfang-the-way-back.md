# The Way Back

Yangyang stopped beside the last milestone to retie Chixia’s dressing.

“Baizhi checked it yesterday,” Chixia said. “She made me walk across the room six times. Apparently I was enthusiastic about the first five.”

“You have caught the end on your boot.”

Chixia looked down. A strip of clean cloth trailed from beneath her trouser cuff.

“That explains the pulling.” She put a hand on Yangyang’s shoulder to balance. “Keep that part out of the report. She’ll make a diagram.”

Yangyang tucked the cloth away and stood. The Wind passed around Chixia’s ankle, testing the place where the bandage had loosened, then rose into the warm lining of her cap.

Chixia tipped her head back.

“You’re welcome to ride, but I charge extra for inspecting the passengers.”

The brim came down over her eyes.

Yangyang laughed. Chixia pushed it up with a finger and started along the road again.

They had eaten at the relay station an hour earlier. The keeper there had asked after three traders who usually travelled through together, and Yangyang had written their names beneath the other missing people in her notebook. One of them carried a small dog inside his coat. The keeper had described its cough while Chixia checked the dates against the patrol notices.

At the checkpoint they would compare registers, speak to the watch commander, and send their findings back to Jinzhou. The road curved along the hillside toward Xuanfang Hold. Above the nearer roofs, its stone gates occupied the gap between two cliffs.

Yangyang took out her Terminal.

The signal had weakened since the milestone. She raised the device and tried the relay station again.

The Wind left Chixia’s cap.

She crossed the road low, flattening a strip of grass beside the drainage channel. Yangyang stopped with her thumb above the screen.

Ahead, a kettle sat on a little stove outside the checkpoint office. Its lid trembled. Steam escaped beneath the rim, and the fire below had burned down to a bright bed of coals.

A stool lay on its side beside the barrier.

Chixia drew one pistol. “That kettle’s about to boil dry.”

Yangyang looked toward the watch platform. A coat hung over its rail. Below it, the open register lay beneath a paperweight, one page folded halfway across the other.

The Wind came back hard against her chest.

Yangyang caught Chixia’s sleeve.

“Back to the milestone. Keep the wall on your left.”

They had taken three steps when the road broke behind them.

Stone struck the barrier. Chixia turned into the falling dust, fired twice, and something came through it low enough to pass beneath her arm. Her breath left her in a sharp grunt.

Yangyang’s sword met the next strike. The impact travelled to her shoulder. She drove forward until Chixia had room to move, then followed her through the narrow space beside the office.

Behind the building, a service path descended toward the drainage gorge. Yangyang remembered its line from the survey. It joined a lower maintenance road beyond the cliff.

“Can you reach the steps?” she asked.

“I’m coming. It’s a scratch; I can still use both—”

Chixia’s heel slipped. Her free hand caught the wall, leaving a red print on the pale plaster.

Yangyang looked at it.

Then she looked at the blood spreading between Chixia’s fingers.

The Wind had already gone beneath Chixia’s elbow. She took some of the weight as they descended. Above them a shape crossed the roof, visible for an instant against the sky, and the gutter tore loose where it landed.

Yangyang put herself between it and the steps.

“Keep going. I’m right behind you.”

Chixia made it to the first landing. Beyond it, the rest of the stair had fallen into the gorge.

Water moved far below. The lower path remained on the opposite bank, a narrow grey line through the trees.

Chixia stared at the gap, then raised her pistol toward the roof.

“We’ll have to take the road back. Give me a moment to get my breath.”

The Wind climbed the gorge wall. She went out over the gap, seeking the rising air that warmed against the cliff, and found enough of it to lift the broken end of a branch. Farther down, an opening between the trees led toward the maintenance path.

She returned to Yangyang’s wrist.

Yangyang understood where she had been looking.

For a moment she kept her eyes on Chixia. Her friend had wedged her shoulder against the stone and was trying to reload with a bloody hand. A cartridge slipped through her fingers and rolled toward the broken stair.

The Wind caught it.

She put it back into Chixia’s palm.

“Thank you,” Chixia said, through her teeth. “I’ve got the next one.”

Above them, the loose gutter moved again.

Yangyang drew the strap of her field bag over her head. She crouched beside Chixia and settled it across her shoulder, lifting the buckle clear of the wound.

“The map is in the front pocket. There’s a shelter where the lower road meets the old watercourse.”

Chixia’s hand stopped on the magazine.

“Yangyang, keep your bag. We can work out the route when we get down.”

The Wind tightened around Yangyang’s forearm.

Yangyang felt the familiar correction at her elbow. It came too hard, lifting her sword hand, trying to place her behind Chixia where the air could gather around them both.

She went with it for half a step.

The pressure under Chixia faltered. Chixia cried out as her weight came down against the stone.

Yangyang caught her.

For a few seconds the three of them remained there, pressed together on the broken landing, with Yangyang’s hand against the wound and the Wind trying to find room for another body in the air she could hold.

Chixia’s face had gone grey around the mouth.

“One of us has to make it back,” Yangyang said. Her eyes stayed on the slight movement of her own sleeve. “She needs you for the descent.”

The cuff pulled toward the gorge.

Yangyang’s fingers closed over the fabric. She could feel the Wind gathering against the other side of it.

“I want to come with you.”

Her voice had changed. Chixia looked at her.

“I want to ask you about the pears. I have been thinking about it since we came down the mountain.” Yangyang swallowed. “I want time for all of that. Please bring them back for me.”

The Wind pressed against her cheek.

The roof above them gave a dry crack. Yangyang turned and cut through the thing reaching down past the eaves. Fragments struck the stair. She brought the sword back into guard, her right elbow drifting outward by the old half-inch.

The Wind knew how to set it straight.

She had learned the fault in that same arm, under another sky. She remembered the particular fatigue that brought it on, the weight of a sword after the fingers began to shake. She remembered how readily a narrow stretch of ground could become enough of a future to stand on.

Yangyang looked back at her.

Then she adjusted her own elbow.

The Wind moved down her sleeve, over the hand holding the cloth, and into Chixia’s jacket.

Yangyang’s fingers opened.

“Yangyang, wait.” Chixia caught at her wrist. “We’ve got through worse than this together. Give me a minute and I’ll think of something.”

Yangyang crouched close enough to rest their foreheads together. Chixia’s skin was hot. Her breath kept catching halfway in.

“Tell Baizhi where you’re hurt. Start with that.”

“You can tell her yourself when you catch up.”

Yangyang stayed against her for one more breath. Then she stood, drew the air up the face of the cliff, and gave it to the Wind.

Chixia’s boots left the landing.

She reached with both hands. Her fingers caught the edge of Yangyang’s glove, slipped along it, and opened over the gorge.

“Yangyang, look at me! You come down after us. Do you hear me?”

The Wind held beneath her back and knees. Yangyang’s current took them beyond the broken stairs, far enough for the rising air to catch.

Through the open passage beside the office, Chixia saw her friend turn toward another movement on the roof.

Beyond Yangyang, the great gates of Xuanfang began to open.

A pale line widened between the two stone leaves. The sound reached the gorge through Chixia’s ribs.

Yangyang stood in front of it, small beneath the height of the walls, her sword raised.

Then the cliff turned between them.

***

Chixia struck the lower path on one knee.

The Wind took the rest of the fall. Pebbles skittered out from beneath Chixia’s boot and rattled down toward the water.

For a while she stayed with her head bowed, breathing through her open mouth.

Then she began to climb.

She got one hand into a crack in the rock. The other slipped against the same place. Blood darkened the stone.

“Help me up. We can get back to the stairs from here.”

The Wind gathered beneath her elbow and drew her toward the path.

Chixia twisted against the pressure.

“She’s still up there. You saw what was on that roof.” Her voice rose until it hurt. “You know her better than anybody. You know what she’ll do if we leave her there.”

The Wind held.

Chixia pressed her forehead against the cliff. Her shoulders shook once. Water dripped from somewhere above, striking the back of her neck.

“I know what she asked you.” She dragged a breath in. “I was standing there too.”

The pressure eased enough for her to turn.

She looked along the narrow path. It followed the water south, under a ledge that hid the sky. The map in Yangyang’s bag had a crease across this part of the route. Chixia remembered Yangyang smoothing it against the breakfast table while the relay keeper described his missing traders.

She sat down very carefully and opened the bag.

Her hands did the work they had practised. The field dressing came out, then the water, then the map. She had to ask the Wind to hold one end of the cloth. When the bandage was secured, she rested her head against the rock and waited for the small black spots in her vision to drift apart.

“We’re going to get the others,” she said at last. “Then I’m coming back here with enough people to argue with her.”

A cold pressure passed over her knuckles.

Chixia took hold of the strap at her shoulder.

“You’ll have to help on this side. I can manage the other leg.”

They began.

The first hour had steps in it. Chixia counted them between bends, losing her place whenever the pain sharpened and starting again from the next tree. The Wind learned her pace, the moment at which her knee began to buckle, the small hitch that meant she needed to stop.

Chixia kept trying her Terminal. Each attempt left the same message on the screen. The gorge held them below the road and the relays above it.

Once the Wind stretched toward the rim. She could feel the weather moving there, broad and free, carrying sounds from somewhere beyond the trees.

Beneath her, Chixia’s shoulder slipped down the rock.

The Wind came back before her head struck it.

Chixia opened her eyes.

“Stay close until we reach the wider bit,” she said. “There’s room to sit there. I remember the survey.”

The wider bit was still around the next bend when the light began to fade.

They reached the shelter in darkness. Its roof had lost several tiles, and a tree had grown across the approach. Chixia lowered herself beneath the sound portion and pulled Yangyang’s blanket over her legs.

The Wind went along the walls, feeling for gaps. She drew the cold away from Chixia’s wet clothes, kept the drip from her shoulder, and gathered the warmer air that remained beneath the roof.

Chixia watched a loose end of blanket lift.

“She checked that fold three times this morning. I was trying to get her downstairs before the noodles went soft.”

The blanket settled over her boots.

“We were late anyway. Sergeant Mo wanted her opinion about a cricket.” Chixia’s mouth moved a little. “You probably heard the whole conversation.”

The Wind had heard the first part. Then Chixia had called from the landing, and she had gone toward her voice.

She remembered Yangyang beginning again after tea, giving the recruit’s earnest demonstration its full attention. A grass stem. The little cage. Mei laughing so hard that she had missed the handle of her cup.

The Wind tucked the blanket beneath Chixia’s heel.

Chixia took off her cap.

The lining held the heat of her head. She laid it in the crook of her arm, with its opening tilted toward the small current at her sleeve.

“Yangyang told me you like hers after she’s been in the sun. Mine’s had a whole afternoon.”

The Wind stopped above the red brim.

Water moved below the shelter.

For an instant she felt another current, heavy with silt, pushing against a door that had swollen in its frame. The cap had turned slowly when she reached it. A pale thread stood up where its stitching had torn. She had known the thread. Chixia had complained about it over lunch and asked for somebody’s scissors.

She had carried that cap for a year, through a spring of clearing roads and a summer when the inside of her pack always smelled faintly of river mud. She had washed it so often that the red began to come off on her hands.

Here, Chixia rubbed her thumb across the lining.

“You can have it while I sit up. Baizhi says I’m warm enough to keep a room comfortable.” She tried to smile. “There was a number attached. She seemed quite pleased with it.”

The Wind lowered herself into the cap.

Chixia’s arm curved around it.

For several breaths the warmth reached her from two directions, from the cloth beneath her and from the hand that had begun to tremble against the brim.

She came out again and touched Chixia’s forehead.

Chixia followed the pressure with her eyes, looking a little to its left.

“You’re thinking about her, aren’t you? The one from your city.”

The drip beyond the roof quickened.

“I wish I could tell you something useful about that.” Chixia swallowed. “I can tell you my side’s hurting like hell, and I’m frightened, and I could use some help getting through tonight.”

The Wind moved the damp hair away from her face.

Chixia shut her eyes under the touch.

“There you are,” she murmured. “Keep doing that for a bit.”

The Wind stayed until Chixia’s breathing settled. When her head slipped sideways, she eased a fold of the blanket between her cheek and the wall.

Below them the stream went on moving. The night grew colder. Chixia woke several times, confused by the position of her hands, and each time the Wind brought her fingers back to the warm cap in her lap.

Once she woke calling for Yangyang.

She looked toward the shelter door, then down at the bag beside her knee. Understanding returned in pieces. The Wind felt each of them arrive in the way her breath shortened.

“I keep expecting her to answer,” Chixia said.

The Wind pressed against the place below her ear where her pulse was strongest.

Chixia cried for a little while. She was tired enough to let it happen with her face uncovered. The Wind kept the cold from her wet cheeks, and when she began to drift again, settled into the warmth beneath her jaw.

By morning, the cap had dried.

Chixia put it on herself.

***

The second day narrowed to the places where Chixia could put a foot.

The Wind carried more of her as they travelled. She drew on the air above the gorge whenever the trees opened, gathered herself beneath Chixia’s shoulders, and eased her over stones that had become too high to step across.

Chixia’s talking thinned with the afternoon. She would begin a story about somebody in the harbour ward, reach for a name, and lose the rest while she concentrated on lifting her knee.

The Wind brought her the smell of water when they neared the channel. At a fork she turned the corner of the map toward the correct branch. At the next she lifted Chixia’s chin before her head struck a hanging bough.

The cap rode above a face she had to keep finding.

There was the scar at the eyebrow. There was the blood drying along her jaw, and the irritated little motion she made whenever the brim caught a twig. There were her lips, moving as she silently rehearsed the account she meant to give.

They spent the second night beneath an overhanging rock. Chixia had become too weak to sit upright. The Wind eased her onto the folded blanket and held the damp air away from her while the stars moved across the narrow opening overhead.

For part of that night Chixia thought they were outside Panhua’s. She kept turning toward the rock, listening for somebody who ought to be bringing their food.

“Tell her Yangyang’s going to be late,” she murmured. “She can keep the bowl by the stove.”

The Wind moved her hair back. Chixia turned into the touch and slept.

The light found them with dew on the red brim. Chixia opened her eyes, saw the gorge, and reached for the bag strap. The effort brought tears to her eyes. She waited for the Wind to gather beneath her, then pushed with the leg that could still bear weight.

By noon, smoke from a cooking fire reached the gorge.

Chixia stopped.

“Someone’s burning the onions,” she said.

Her voice sounded so pleased that the Wind almost lost her hold.

They followed the smell as far as a maintenance crossing. Beyond it the land rose toward Jinzhou, and the search pennants along the upper road showed red through the trees.

The Wind gathered against the nearest branch and shook it until its dry leaves came down in a single rush. A patroller on the crossing turned. She saw movement, took two steps toward the rail, and called for her partner.

Chixia raised her old torch.

Its light moved across the underside of the leaves.

“Down here,” she called. She drew breath for the rest, pushing it through the pain. “Captain Chixia, harbour ward. I’ve got a report for City Hall.”

The patroller was already coming down the bank.

Hands reached them at last. One beneath Chixia’s elbow, another steadying her head. The Wind held through the transfer, struggling to tell which portion of the weight had become someone else’s.

“We have her,” the patroller said, looking at the leaves moving against her own face. “Bring the stretcher closer, Ren. She’s bleeding through.”

Chixia caught the woman’s sleeve.

“There were two of us at Xuanfang. You need to write that first.”

The woman knelt where Chixia could see her.

“Tell me her name. Ren is calling the Academy now.”

The Wind travelled with the stretcher beneath Chixia’s chin, keeping the hair from her mouth all the way through the gates.

***

Amy received the call with a bundle of spare cables across her knees.

“Which entrance?” she asked.

Mei looked up from the boot she was fastening.

Amy put the cables on the floor.

“We’re coming. Tell Baizhi we heard you.”

She stood, caught her headset lead on the chair, and pulled it free so hard that Iuno reached for her wrist.

“Chixia’s back,” Amy said. “They’re taking her into Huaxu. Yangyang’s still at the Hold.”

Lupa took the keys from their hook. Cartethyia set her watering cup on the sill, water running over the edge, and followed Ciaccona into the passage.

They reached the Academy while Baizhi was cutting Chixia’s jacket open.

Mei saw her face first. Then the cap on the tray beside her, and the nurse holding her arm, and the small, fierce movement of the air at her throat.

Chixia turned her head toward the doorway.

“Mei, she sent us down the gorge. The steps had gone. She gave me her bag, and then she—”

Baizhi put her hand on the uninjured shoulder.

“The recorder is running. Tell us what you saw.”

“The checkpoint was empty. There was somebody above the office. More than one, I think. I saw movement on both sides.” Chixia’s gaze shifted until it found Mei again. “She stayed on the landing. Said one of us had to get back. Then she sent the Wind with me.”

Mei’s fingers closed against her own palm. Pain travelled through the dressing.

“And the gates?” Baizhi asked.

“They opened. The big stone ones. She was standing right in front of them when they started moving.”

Chixia tried to lift her head.

“That’s the last bit I saw. The cliff got in the way. Yangyang was still on her feet.”

Her eyes found the air beside the tray.

“She got me the whole way home. Through the watercourse. Two days, and I kept—”

Her head sank into the pillow.

Baizhi’s hand moved to her neck. She called the nurse closer, gave two instructions, and looked toward the doorway.

“Wait outside. I will come to you when I can.”

The Wind remained at Chixia’s mouth.

Baizhi bent beside her.

“Her breathing is being watched. You can stay by the pillow while we work.”

The air moved an inch aside. Baizhi brought the lamp closer.

In the corridor, Mei stood with her bleeding hand open.

Iuno looked down at it.

“Rest it here,” she said, offering her forearm. “Lupa, the clean wrapping is in the outer pocket.”

Mei laid her hand across Iuno’s sleeve. She watched Lupa open the packet.

“She put the bag on Chixia,” Amy said. “Gave her the route. Made sure she’d have a reason to keep walking.”

Cartethyia sat on the bench beneath the window.

“I spent ages thinking about what everyone would do once they were safely outside,” she said. Her hands had curled beneath the edge of the seat. “I could picture them so clearly. Where they would go. Who would take care of them.”

Ciaccona sat beside her. Cartethyia leaned against her shoulder and kept looking at the treatment-room door.

Mei’s mouth moved before she found the words.

“I used to feel relieved when everybody started leaving.”

Iuno held her arm steady beneath Mei’s hand.

Amy looked at her mother for a long moment. Then she drew her own hands into her sleeves.

“I remember how sensible it felt,” she said.

Lupa finished the new wrapping. Her tail lay rigid against the floor.

“I want her here to explain it,” she said. “I want to hear the whole argument while Baizhi checks her over and somebody brings her something to eat.”

She fastened the cloth, more gently than the set of her jaw suggested, and stepped back.

Chisa had been listening to the voices through the door. She moved nearer when the sounds of hurried work began to settle.

“Sanhua has sent for us,” she said. “Jinhsi has the scouts’ reports.”

Mei looked once more toward the room.

“We’ll need the lower route marked. Chixia’s bag has the map.”

Amy brought out her Terminal. Her thumb shook against the edge of the screen. She braced it on the window ledge to write.

They waited until Baizhi came to the door.

“She is alive. The bleeding has slowed. I will be here for the next several hours.” Her eyes went from Mei to the damp movement at her own sleeve. “You have time to go to City Hall and come back.”

The Wind had followed her as far as the threshold.

She moved back toward the bed once. Chixia’s chest rose beneath the sheet. The nurse beside her counted under her breath, with two fingers against her wrist.

The Wind listened through three more breaths.

Then she went out with them.

***

Suisui was asking Sanhua about the road when they reached City Hall.

“I came through the eastern counties. The courier office told me the Xuanfang route was closed, so I took the longer coach.” She held a travelling bag in both hands. “Her last letter said she expected to be in Jinzhou for a while. I thought I would find her before she made another arrangement.”

The Wind stopped in the doorway.

Suisui shifted the bag against her knee. Her hair had worked loose above one ear. She smoothed it back, missed the same small strand, and tried again while Sanhua spoke.

The Wind knew that hand.

She knew the bent little finger, the way Suisui used the side of her thumb to push her hair back when her hands were full. She knew the faint crease between her brows and how it deepened when she was trying to understand somebody being careful with her.

There had been more lines beside her mouth the last time.

Here, her sister’s face was younger. The bag she held was unfamiliar. Her voice reached the doorway, and the Wind went toward it so quickly that the papers on Sanhua’s table lifted clear of their weight.

Suisui turned.

The loose hair rose away from her ear. Coolness crossed her temple and went down the curve of her cheek.

She drew breath.

The Wind moved closer.

All the familiar distances were wrong. Her sister’s shoulder should have met her forehead. Her hands should have found the folded cloth at Suisui’s waist. She reached for those places with everything she had left, and Suisui’s travelling coat billowed open.

Suisui set the bag down.

“Are you the friend she calls shijie?” she asked. “She wrote about you. She said you’d come on the mountain trip.”

The Wind settled against her throat.

Suisui held still beneath the touch. Her eyes moved toward Sanhua, then to the people gathered in the doorway.

“You were with them?”

Mei came closer.

“She brought Chixia home. Baizhi is treating her now.”

Suisui’s hands opened at her sides.

“Thank you for bringing her back,” she said to the coolness at her neck. “Sanhua told me how badly she was hurt.”

The Wind’s hold broke.

It went through the room in a single shudder. Suisui’s sleeve snapped against her arm. A sheet of paper struck the door and drifted to the floor, and Mei reached out with her uninjured hand toward the place the Wind had been.

Suisui caught the edge of her own scarf before it slipped from her shoulder.

“I’ve upset her.”

Mei looked at the scarf lifting between Suisui’s fingers. The Wind had gathered inside its loose fold.

“She knows you,” Mei said softly.

Suisui waited.

Mei held her hand near the fabric.

“May I tell her?”

Coolness moved over her wrist and stayed there.

“Where she came from, she was Yangyang. She had a sister called Suisui.” Mei drew a breath. “She crossed here from that life. We learned who she was after Norfall.”

Suisui looked down at the scarf.

She raised it slowly, bringing the fold toward her face. The Wind moved with it, closer to the warmth beneath her chin.

“Yangyang knows?”

“She has been trying to help her get her voice back. That’s part of why we were going to Mengzhou together.”

Suisui’s mouth tightened. She put a hand beneath the scarf as though its weight had changed.

“She wrote that her friend was very tired,” she said. “I sent some tea. I was worried I’d chosen the wrong kind.”

The Wind remembered the packet. Yangyang had opened it at her desk and read the brewing instructions aloud, smiling at a note about the price. The paper had stayed in the drawer because it carried Suisui’s handwriting.

She touched the hand beneath the scarf.

Suisui bent her head over it.

“I’m trying to understand,” she whispered. “Will you stay here while they tell me?”

The doors at the far end of the room opened. Jinhsi came through carrying a folded map and a small wooden case.

Sanhua went to her. Their conversation was brief and quiet. Jinhsi’s gaze moved toward Suisui, and she brought the map to the table beside her.

“I have heard Chixia’s account,” she said. “I can tell you what our scouts found.”

Suisui took the chair Sanhua drew out. She kept one hand beneath the lifted fold of her scarf.

***

Jinhsi laid the reports beside the map.

“The first missing travellers were reported before Yangyang and Chixia left. We sent inquiries to the posts along the passage. The checkpoint’s return was overdue by yesterday morning, and I dispatched messengers to Mengzhou City Hall through the alternate routes.” She put a finger beside two marked roads. “We are still waiting for their replies.”

Amy bent over the map. “And the team you sent to the Hold?”

“Elite Midnight Rangers reached the outer approach this afternoon. The gates were sealed. They tried the watch signals and received silence from the wall. The squad remains at the last defensible position on our side.”

“They opened for Yangyang,” Suisui said. Her hand tightened under the scarf. “Chixia saw that happen.”

Jinhsi nodded.

“That is the most recent account we have of her.”

Lupa stood beside the table with her arms folded. Her claws pressed into the leather above her elbows.

“Hsin told us we would have a reason to come,” she said. “She even gave us a season for it.”

Iuno looked at the marked road.

“She asked for the household, the plant, the microphone. She knew who she expected to receive.” Her voice had become very even. “I would like the date on which she learned this passage was dangerous.”

“I’d like the time,” Amy said. “She can be precise when she wants something.”

Mei pulled out a chair and sat down. Beneath the table she pressed her uninjured hand between her knees.

“We’d already agreed to come. Yangyang was asking how long we could stay.”

She looked toward the scarf in Suisui’s lap.

“We were making plans over tea.”

Cartethyia had been studying the small drawings of the watch platforms.

“Do we know where her crews are?” she asked. “Old Seven’s people should be somewhere along this line.”

“Two of the frontier stations have answered us,” Jinhsi said. “Their information ends at the same gates.”

Cartethyia rubbed the edge of the map between finger and thumb.

“They came all that way for our people. If something has happened to theirs, I want us to find them too.”

Lupa’s ears shifted toward her.

“I’ll carry them out myself,” she said. “And I want Hsin standing there when I do it.”

Iuno rested her living hand on the table.

“She released Cristoforo while we were still treating the people he had hurt. At the mountain, we found another portion of the bargain waiting for us. Now Yangyang is inside her Hold.” She looked at Jinhsi. “I am considering the possibility that she arranged this. I am also considering what she might have decided to let happen.”

Jinhsi’s fingers stayed on the wooden case.

“I have asked her those questions. I will put the answers before the people who were hurt.” She opened the lid. “The evidence we have today is the checkpoint, Chixia’s account, and the sealed gates. We will take that with us.”

Inside the case lay a key with a broad, worn head. Its metal had darkened in the grooves.

“Jué sent this when I asked him about the closure. Xuanfang guarded this passage before Jinzhou was founded. He holds a means of entry under the old agreement between the Sentinels.”

She placed the case in front of Mei.

Mei looked at the key for a moment before taking it.

“We can reach the gate under the Rangers’ cover. Chisa will need to examine the approach.”

“I’ll go through the squad’s observations with them,” Chisa said. “Ask them to mark where their communications failed.”

Amy began writing. Iuno moved the map so Suisui could see the road they were discussing.

Suisui followed it from the checkpoint to the stone walls.

“I’m coming with you.”

She looked up before anyone answered.

“Tell me where to stand when we reach the fighting. Tell me what you need carried. I can follow instructions.” Her voice shook on the next breath. “I came here to spend a week with my sister. I would like to find her.”

Mei glanced toward Lupa, who had already begun measuring the distance between the coach road and the forward position.

“You can travel with us,” Mei said. “We’ll speak to the Rangers together when we arrive.”

Jinhsi called Sanhua closer. The waiting squad needed fresh medical supplies; the road needed another relay team; the transport would leave from Huaxu once both were loaded.

For the first time since entering the room, Suisui took her hand from beneath the scarf.

The Wind followed it to the edge of the table.

She crossed Suisui’s wrist, touched the map, and moved toward the open doorway. Then she came back and lifted the scarf again.

Suisui watched her.

“There’s somewhere you want to go.”

The cloth drew toward the door.

Ciaccona put her lute case over her shoulder.

“I’ll walk with you. We have a little time while they load.”

***

Baizhi had the spare key in the drawer above her washbasin.

She brought it out on a loop of blue thread. The little tag attached to it had Yangyang’s name in her own handwriting.

Suisui took the key with both hands.

“How is Chixia?”

“Her condition has held through the last examination. She remains unconscious.” Baizhi looked at the lifted end of the blue thread. “You can see her before you leave.”

The Wind touched her wrist.

Baizhi held it still.

“I have asked the nurse to stay beside her bed while I speak with you. Someone will remain there.”

The pressure lingered. Then it moved along the blue thread into Suisui’s hands.

At the stairs, Suisui turned back.

“Yangyang said you were looking after her too.” Her fingers closed around the key. “Thank you.”

Baizhi glanced toward the treatment-room door. For a moment her mouth lost its careful shape.

“Bring her coat,” she said. “It will be cooler on the road by the time you reach her.”

***

Yangyang had washed a cup before leaving.

It stood upside down beside the sink, with a small crescent of water dried beneath its rim. A towel hung over the back of the chair. On the desk, a letter lay beneath the inkstone, addressed to Suisui.

Suisui saw her name and stopped with the door key still in her hand.

She set it beside the cup.

The room smelled faintly of barley. Near the bed, Yangyang’s spare coat had been folded over a wooden chest. Suisui picked it up, shook it out, and ran her palm down one sleeve.

“She used to sleep in her good clothes whenever she had an early start,” she said. “I could hear the belt buckle against the bedframe.”

Ciaccona closed the door gently behind them.

“She’s been teaching Mei how to fold that sort of collar. The lessons travel around the house.”

Suisui found a loose stitch near the cuff. She put her thumb over it, rubbed once, and folded the coat across her arm.

The Wind moved toward the desk.

The lower drawer slid open a finger’s width. Suisui put down the coat and pulled it the rest of the way.

Inside lay a cloth bundle, fitted between a packet of tea and a little stack of folded papers.

She knew the knot before she touched it.

Her hands slowed as she brought the bundle into the light. She sat on the edge of Yangyang’s bed and opened the cloth across her knees.

Two shells lay inside.

For a while she moved her gaze between them.

Then she lifted the one on the left.

“I brought this to her at the checkpoint when she made Outrider. Ma had left it with me.” She turned it until the light reached the inner curve. “I had to keep it in my sleeve. She was in such a hurry to sign everything.”

Her thumb found the worn patch on the outside.

She picked up the second shell.

The same three ridges. The same softened edge. The same tiny place where their mother’s careful work had left a smoother curve beneath the lip.

Suisui brought them together until the rims almost touched.

“She did this with the little stone she used for her needles. I remember complaining about the sound.”

The Wind settled over her hands.

Suisui looked toward the cold.

“She gave it to you as well.”

The older shell turned slightly in her palm.

She lifted it to her ear.

The room filled with a tide so faint that Ciaccona first felt it in the strings against her shoulder. Beneath it moved another sea, and beneath that, unevenly, a sound that Suisui had last heard through the open door of her mother’s room.

Three notes of the summer lullaby.

Suisui put her free hand over her mouth.

The Wind went to the lute case.

Ciaccona watched its fastening lift. She knelt to open it and brought out the instrument, resting its body against her thigh.

“I can follow what the shell is carrying,” she said. “Show me where you want to begin.”

The Wind passed over the strings.

One sounded. Ciaccona found its pitch, then the note beneath it. She let the interval settle into the sound coming from the shell.

Suisui closed her eyes.

The air touched her forehead, and the room’s floor seemed to lower beneath her feet.

She was looking up at herself.

A younger Suisui stood with a comb between her teeth and a ribbon looped over one wrist. Her sleeves were rolled badly, one coming loose while she worked. Behind her, the courtyard door stood open on a morning full of rain.

“Keep your head here,” the girl said, taking the comb from her mouth. “If you keep looking at the sword, I’ll have to plait that as well.”

Small knees pressed together beneath the edge of a chair. Someone was holding a practice sword between them, its wooden hilt warm from a long, possessive grip.

Suisui felt the tug at her own scalp.

Then the hands before her moved, dividing the hair into three parts, and she remembered performing the motion. She remembered the comb slipping beneath the chair and how Yangyang had tried to retrieve it while still being plaited.

Laughter reached the room from somewhere beyond the courtyard.

Their mother was calling them to eat.

Suisui’s hand tightened around the shell.

The memory loosened. Afternoon light returned to the bedspread, and the second shell lay beside her knee.

She bent over it with tears running down her face.

“I’d forgotten the comb,” she said. “You kept it under your pillow for a week afterwards. I spent every morning looking for it.”

The Wind touched the little hollow behind her ear.

Suisui’s breath caught.

“You used to put your face there when I carried you. In the market. You’d fall asleep while I was trying to pay.”

She lifted one hand toward the place where a head should have been.

Her fingers passed through the coolness.

The Wind drew close again, trying to meet them.

Across the room, Ciaccona kept her hand resting against the strings. The shell’s tide had changed. Far beneath it, something rough moved through the water.

The Wind settled over Suisui’s wrist and held there until her sister raised the shell again.

“You can show me,” Suisui said.

The next sound was a boat knocking against a window frame.

***

Ash lay along the waterline in a grey band.

The Wind had kept the city as it came to her then: a table lodged in the branches of a street tree; the top of a familiar shop sign; a man in a boat lifting his oar so he could hear somebody calling from an upper room.

Suisui saw it from the height of a woman standing in deep water.

A red cap rested against that woman’s chest. Her hand curved around it, thumb hooked beneath the brim to keep the current from taking it away.

The hand was Yangyang’s. Older, with a scar across the knuckle that Suisui had never seen.

The city passed in broken pieces. A library wall still giving off heat. Wet pages separating under somebody’s fingers. A bowl set down beside a bed and found there again at the next meal.

Then a road. Mud drying white on a pair of boots. A familiar courtyard gate being pushed inward.

Suisui was on the other side.

She had aged. Silver showed near one temple, and a small line above her eyebrow had become permanent. She wore an apron with a patched pocket. Flour dusted the hand she lifted toward the arriving woman.

“You should have sent ahead,” she said. “I would have put the water on earlier.”

She crossed the courtyard before the bag had reached the ground.

Her arms closed around Yangyang.

The shell held the pressure of it. Suisui felt her own hands through a worn travelling coat. One at the back of a head, the other pushing between shoulders to bring her sister close enough. She heard the breath she had let out against that older Yangyang’s hair.

“Come inside. You can tell me while I get these boots off you.”

In the apartment, Suisui began to cry harder.

The memory moved through several mornings. Her other self mended a cuff by the window. She disagreed with a vegetable seller at the gate. She put two bowls on the table and scraped the browned rice from the bottom of the pot into the nearer one.

Yangyang slept in the old room. She woke early and lay listening to her sister crossing the courtyard. At breakfast she held the bowl in both hands until Suisui sat down.

Then she began studying the roads again.

On the last morning, Suisui had one hand inside a flour jar, feeling for the measure.

“If you’re going farther west, take the thick stockings. I’ve put them beside your coat.”

Yangyang stood in the doorway.

“I’m trying to find someone.”

“You’ve said.” Suisui shook flour from the scoop. “You can tell me about her while I knead this. It will be a while before the dough’s ready.”

Yangyang came and sat at the table.

She talked about a shore. About a person whose absence she had carried through every street of a city that kept asking for help. She tried to explain the certainty that had survived the fires with her, and how far she had already followed it.

Suisui listened while the dough gathered under her palms. At one point she left it sticking to her fingers and reached across the table for Yangyang’s hand.

“I want to know where you stop,” she said. “Send me the address, even if you only stay a few days. I like being able to picture the room.”

Yangyang looked down at their hands.

She had found the road by then. She had learned what its crossing would take. The knowledge lived in her as a place beyond the maps, open to something she could become.

Suisui rubbed a little flour onto her thumb by accident.

Yangyang kept looking at it.

Later, she chose the hour when her sister would be at the market.

She folded the blanket on the bed. She put the bowl she had used beside the sink. She left a letter saying she was going west, then picked up the stockings and fitted them into her bag.

From the doorway she could see the dough beneath its damp cloth. Suisui had left room on the board to shape a second batch when she returned.

Yangyang stood there long enough for the shadow of the window lattice to move across her boot.

Then she went out.

The Wind had carried that kitchen to the edge of the world.

There, with the dark opening before her, she took the shell from its wrapping. The thick stockings were still in her bag. Her sister had darned a heel in a different colour, using what she had, and the small patch showed through the open flap.

She knew how Suisui would have caught her arm if she had understood. She could feel the imagined fingers there. She could hear the questions that would have kept her in the kitchen until the light failed and the dough rose and somebody came to complain about the oven.

She wanted that hand so badly that she put her own over the place it would have held.

Then she opened her fingers.

The crossing took her weight. The strap of the bag slipped from a shoulder that was coming apart into moving air. Cloth struck the ground. The shell’s tide rose through her, and she followed it into the dark with her sister’s kitchen still warm in her memory.

Ciaccona lifted her fingers from the strings.

The apartment returned around them.

Suisui sat with the shell against her ear and her other hand pressed flat over her mouth. The coat had slipped from the bed onto the floor.

She lowered the shell very carefully.

“She was at home,” she said.

The Wind rested against her wrist.

Suisui looked toward the window. Their mother’s flute lay on the sill, catching a thin line of sun.

“I was trying to imagine how everybody had died. When Mei told me where you came from, I thought that must be why you had to leave.”

She dragged the back of her hand across her cheek.

“Your sister was making bread.”

The room’s air faltered. The curtain twisted against its fastening, and the letter beneath the inkstone lifted at one corner.

Suisui set both shells in the cloth. Her hands were shaking too badly to trust them.

Then she reached toward the moving curtain.

“Come closer, please. I keep trying to find your shoulders.”

The Wind came as far as her knee.

Suisui leaned forward. Her palms met a little pressure, followed it, lost it when it spread between her fingers. She tried again, cupping her hands more loosely.

“Help me with this. You know where I’m reaching.”

Coolness moved along her forearms. It settled against her chest, under her chin, in the place a younger sister had once put her face after being carried through the market.

Suisui curved both arms around it.

The Wind had flown through storms that stripped the leaves from whole hillsides. She had held a passage open inside a falling mountain. Now the small current along Suisui’s collar broke apart every time she tried to keep it steady.

Suisui felt the cold move over her throat. She drew the scarf up around it, bringing the cloth close to her skin.

“I wish she knew where you are,” she said.

The Wind pressed into the warmth.

“I wish I could tell her about this room. About the people who put tea out for you.” Suisui bent her face into the fold of the scarf. “She asked you for an address.”

Her breathing broke. She held the air through it, one hand moving slowly over the cloth, following each small shift as carefully as she had once followed the breathing of a child asleep against her neck.

The Wind could feel her tears soaking into the weave.

She remembered the flour on her thumb. The stockings. The second batch of dough. She had chosen to leave all of it, and the road had brought her here to a sister whose hands had learned the same motions in another life.

Suisui stroked the place beneath her own chin.

“Yangyang’s been telling me about you for so long,” she said. “I’ve been answering letters about the weather. I sent you tea.”

A small, broken laugh escaped her.

“I would have sent something warmer.”

The Wind moved against her cheek.

Suisui turned toward the touch and stayed there. For a while Ciaccona could hear her trying to speak, drawing breath, swallowing, beginning again with her hand pressed over the scarf.

At last she found what she wanted to say.

“I’m glad I get to know you.”

The Wind settled close enough for the warmth of Suisui’s next breath to pass through her.

Across the room, Ciaccona put the lute down. She took a clean cloth from her case and placed it beside Suisui’s knee, then sat on the floor near the door while the sisters held each other in the ways they could.

Outside, someone called up from the street for a neighbour to bring down a basket. Footsteps crossed the landing. A latch clicked in the next apartment.

Suisui picked up the cloth after a while. She wiped her face, blew her nose, and looked at the wet mark she had left on the corner.

“I’ll wash this when we get back.”

Ciaccona nodded. Her own eyes were wet.

“I’ll come and collect it. Yangyang promised me a list of the shops that sell decent paper.”

Suisui looked toward the desk, at the unfinished letter with her name on it.

“She’s always got something to give you when you’re going somewhere.”

The Wind moved inside her scarf.

Suisui put a hand there, feeling for her.

“I want both of you with me when we leave Xuanfang.” Her voice trembled. “I’m going to keep a place for you beside me. You can tell me when I’ve got it wrong.”

The scarf lifted against her fingers.

She sat with it another moment, then bent to retrieve Yangyang’s coat.

***

They brought the shells back in their shared cloth, tucked inside the coat.

At Huaxu, the others had gathered beside the loaded transport. Amy was showing a Ranger the lower route on Yangyang’s bloodstained map. Chisa stood beside them, learning the turns from his description. Cartethyia carried a second blanket out to the vehicle, stopped to move a box so it would lie flat, and tucked it into the space she had made.

Mei saw Suisui coming up the steps.

Her eyes went to the coat, then to the scarf stirring beneath Suisui’s chin.

“Baizhi says we can go in now.”

Suisui nodded. Her eyelids were swollen. She had combed her loose hair back with her fingers, and the same small strand had escaped again.

The Wind put it behind her ear.

Mei’s face changed. She came down one step and held out her hand for the coat.

“I can carry that while you see Chixia.”

Suisui gave it to her.

“The shells are inside. Keep it this way up.”

Mei settled the bundle against her chest and went in beside her.

Chixia lay with her head turned slightly toward the window.

Baizhi had cleaned the blood from her face. A dressing crossed her side beneath the bedclothes. One arm rested above the sheet, with a bruise beginning around the inside of her elbow.

Her cap sat on the bedside cabinet.

The Wind stopped just inside the door.

Suisui felt her scarf tighten, then loosen. She stood where she was while Lupa brought a chair closer to the bed.

Baizhi looked up from Chixia’s wrist.

“You may sit beside her. Leave room here for the nurse.”

Lupa moved the chair a little farther around and sat down. Her knees almost touched the bedframe.

“You’ve made a mess of that jacket,” she said to Chixia. “I brought the blue one from the house. Baizhi can decide when you’re allowed to complain about the choice.”

She put it over the chair back, smoothing a sleeve that hung too close to the floor.

Amy came in with Iuno. She had folded the map along its old creases and put it inside a fresh cover. She set it where Chixia would be able to see it from the pillow.

“We’ve copied your route. The Rangers are going to check the shelter and the maintenance crossing.” Her fingers lingered on the edge of the cover. “You can have the map back when you wake up. I’ve put all my notes on another sheet. I remember your opinion about people drawing over the scale.”

Chixia breathed through her parted lips.

Amy stood looking at her for a moment longer, then stepped aside for Suisui.

The Wind moved out of the scarf.

She crossed the bed at the height of the pillow. A strand of red hair lay against Chixia’s mouth. She lifted it, laid it beside the other strands, and settled over the place beneath her ear.

The pulse reached her at once.

She had followed it through the dark of the gorge. She knew its hurried, frightened rhythm, the long cold hours when she had gathered all her attention around the next beat.

Here it moved beneath clean skin. The room was warm. Beside the bed, Baizhi turned a page and wrote down the time.

The Wind touched the cap.

Its brim shifted against the cabinet. Suisui took it up before it reached the edge and laid it closer to Chixia’s hand.

“She told me you used to buy breakfast for her whenever she came off a late shift,” Suisui said. “She described the food very thoroughly. I meant to ask you how much she actually managed to eat.”

Baizhi’s pen paused.

“Usually all of it. Chixia takes a personal interest.”

Suisui smiled with her eyes still wet.

“That sounds like the right person to send her to.”

She sat down in the chair Lupa vacated and rested her hand beside Chixia’s on the sheet. The Wind stayed between the pillow and the open collar, where the warmth rose with each breath.

For a while the room held the small sounds of people waiting. Cartethyia poured water for Baizhi and left the glass within reach. Ciaccona moved her lute case away from the nurse’s path. Iuno stood beside Mei with her living shoulder touching hers.

Mei looked at the folded coat in her arms.

“She asked me to walk along the lower canal with her,” she said quietly. “When we could find another afternoon.”

Suisui turned toward her.

“Has she shown you the bridge where she watches the birds?”

“She’s told me about it. She knows which ones steal from the stalls.”

“She used to know that about every bird in our courtyard.” Suisui rubbed a thumb along the bedspread. “I’d be trying to sweep, and she would give me an account of their families.”

A loose edge of the pillowcase lifted.

Mei watched it rise and settle.

“I’d like to hear her do that.”

Suisui looked back at Chixia. Her fingers moved closer to the warm hand resting on the sheet.

“She has a great deal to tell us when we find her.”

From the courtyard came the sound of the transport starting. A Ranger appeared at the door, saw the occupied chairs, and waited until Iuno turned toward him.

The Wind drew closer to Chixia’s throat.

Baizhi set down her pen.

She put two fingers against the pulse, beside the cool place in the air.

“I have her from here,” she said. “Come back to this room when you return. I will leave word if we move her.”

The Wind stayed through one more breath.

Then she passed over Chixia’s forehead. The red hair lifted, slid back, and came to rest where it would stay clear of her face.

Suisui stood. Mei returned the coat to her, helping her settle it over one arm. The two shells made a faint sound inside their wrapping.

At the doorway Suisui opened the fold of her scarf.

“You can sit here on the road. I’d like to feel where you are.”

The Wind crossed the room.

She settled beside Suisui’s neck, with Yangyang’s coat below her and her sister’s hand raised to meet her. Suisui waited until the current grew steady against her skin, then went down the corridor with the others.

Baizhi drew the chair closer to Chixia’s bed.

She picked up the red cap, worked a little dried mud from its fastening, and put it where Chixia’s hand would find it when she reached.

Outside, the transport began to move. The curtain rose once with the passing air and fell back against the sill.

Baizhi kept her fingers on Chixia’s wrist.

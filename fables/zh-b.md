Chapter 1: Zhī Bǐ

The iron latch dropped shut on the gearwell stairs, and for ten seconds the six of them stood in the narrow corridor behind the Ministry hall listening to three pairs of boots go down into the grease-dark.

First the scrape of soles on the upper iron grating, then the faint, glassy chime of Iuno’s shoulder taking the turn of the stair, and then the low hum of the moonwheel spinning up in the shaft below.

Lupa stayed with her shoulder against the stone a moment longer, one ear turned to the seam, as if she expected one of them to come back up the stairs for something they’d forgotten. Nobody did.

Amy blew a strand of pink hair out of her eyes and hitched the heavy coil of insulated copper wire higher onto her shoulder. “If you locked the three of them in a carriage from here to Jinzhou, they’d probably communicate entirely by passing salted nuts and blinking.”

Mei didn’t laugh right away. She stood half a pace behind Chisa, her palms still gray with plaster dust from the courtyard barricades, looking at the heavy iron ring of the latch where Qingxiao’s hand had been a minute ago. The draft leaking under the sill smelled of cold tallow and wet mountain rock.

She turned her left wrist over and glanced at her Terminal. The signal bar at the top corner was already dropping from three lines to one as the moonwheel cleared the lower bevel of the shaft, and while she watched, the last white tick flickered and went out. *No Relay above Tier Four.*

Mei let her hand drop to her side, rubbed the plaster dust off her thumbs against the seam of her trousers, and followed Chisa down the passage into the branch hall.

Inside, the long timber room smelled of wet wool, boiled mugwort, and two hundred frightened people packed onto benches that had been built for forty clerks. Mothers from Xianling Village sat along the inner wainscot with children asleep across their knees; at the far end by the hearth, Ciaccona and Cartethyia, who had slipped back from the door ahead of the rest, were already tearing bolted linen into three-inch strips over an overturned ration crate while an old grandmother with a bandaged shin argued with a Ministry orderly about whether her copper kettle had been left on the mule cart at the gate.

“All clear?” Ciaccona asked, not looking up from the cloth as she ripped a yard of bleached hemp down the weave.

“Gone up,” Amy said, dropping her wire coil beside a crate of dry-cell lanterns with a thump. “Iuno didn’t even yell at me about the battery leads. Which means she’s saving it up for when she gets back, so I’m going to need everyone here to swear I was doing math the whole time.”

“You were stealing turnip cakes out of the pan,” Lupa said, stepping over a roll of straw matting to check the iron pot simmering on the brick hob. “I counted.”

Nobody asked how long it took a moonwheel to climb from the third gearwell ring to the Astrolabe Core. Everyone in the room knew how high the Core hung above the roof tiles; they had all looked up at the stalled bronze rings through the gap in the clouds while hauling the village wagons inside the portcullis. Counting the louver counterweights and the outer door, it was an eight-minute climb.

Ten minutes passed.

Mei sat on the corner of the bandage crate beside Cartethyia, rolling torn linen around her thumb until the cylinders were tight enough to pack into the leather field bags, and kept her eyes off the tall clerestory windows along the upper wall. Outside those panes, the sky stayed the color of dirty pewter. None of the brass balance-weights in the eaves gave the small, preliminary shudder that meant the great celestial calipers overhead were taking up their slack.

At fifteen minutes, Amy stopped stripping the rubber jacket off a lamp lead with her teeth, pulled her Terminal out of her pocket, and thumbed the local channel key twice.

The speaker gave a short, flat *bzz-t* of dead air.

Amy looked at the screen, flipped the casing over to check the antenna contact, and tucked it back into her vest without looking toward her mother.

Across the hearth, Lupa lifted the lid of the soup pot, stirred the simmering turnip greens once with a wooden ladle, and set the iron cover back down more quietly than she usually did anything. Cartethyia paused with a half-rolled bandage in her lap, her head tilting toward the high timber rafters where the watch gallery sat silent above the courtyard.

Still nobody said it. If you said *the sky hasn’t turned back on* in a room where sixty children and thirty wounded farmers were listening to every bootstep, you had to have a second sentence ready to put behind it, and none of them had one yet.

Then the courtyard doors at the south end of the hall banged open against their iron stops.

A young Warden—his leather breastplate still dark with sleet from the switchback road and his left knee bound in a tight hemp wrap—limped three steps over the threshold and stopped, looking for Liangyu among the benches.

Liangyu was already standing by the pillar nearest the door, her spear butt grounded beside her boot. She had been awake since before the valley road collapsed, and the skin under her eyes was the bruised grey of river slate, though her voice when she spoke was completely level.

“Report from the wall, Huo?” Liangyu asked.

Warden Huo swallowed once, keeping his voice down so it wouldn’t carry to the straw pallets by the stove. “The wind died on the lower switchback, Watcher. The Fog’s coming uphill without a draft behind it. It’s already over the third milestone, and—” He hesitated, glancing once toward Ciaccona’s lute leaning against the pillar. “You can hear them inside it. Marching in step. And talking.”

The murmur along the nearest benches went flat at once. The grandmother with the bandaged shin stopped arguing about her kettle and pulled her grandson’s elbow back against her skirt.

Liangyu did not look up toward the dark clerestory windows where the Core hung silent, and she did not ask if there had been word from the Paragon. She shifted her grip two inches up the ash shaft of her spear.

“How far from the outer ditch?” Liangyu asked.

“Four hundred paces, maybe less. Hard to tell with the Mingshen stones dead—the echoes come off the cliff face wrong.”

Liangyu nodded once. She turned half-around toward the twenty Wardens sitting along the wall racks with their helmets on their knees and their greaves still caked in valley mud.

“Ge, take twelve and the long pikes to the second terrace,” Liangyu said, her voice plain and unhurried, as if she were assigning the morning sweep of the granary steps. “Keep forty-two between the well-head and the grain stairs so the hall doors stay clear if the outer line bends. Huo, you and Old Chen stay on the inner capstan winch—your knees won’t take the wet ramp, and I need two people on the iron ratchet who won’t let go if the chain jumps. Everyone else, helmets on. Check the cloaks the Paragon touched before you go out; tie the throat-strings double so the wind doesn’t strip them.”

There was no speech about Xuanfang Hold, or two hundred years of unbroken walls, or Sentinel Hsin. Men and women who had been falling asleep over their spear shafts thirty seconds ago reached down, picked up their helmets, and tied their cloak strings in double knots.

Not all of them reached for pikes. Xuanfang’s garrison had never been only spear-carriers. Halfway down the wall racks, a lanky archer named Shen Lu was blowing on her fingers until a faint green draft curled up around her knuckles. Bai Tieshan, the quarry foreman from Xianling with forearms like gateposts, had one palm pressed flat to the hall’s foundation stones and was listening to them the way other men listened to horses. And a girl of perhaps fifteen from the relay office, Kou Xiaoman, was bouncing on her toes with violet static ticking off the ends of her braids, because with every Terminal in the Hold dead, someone had to carry the messages, and she was the fastest thing on two legs between the gate and the granary.

At the end of the rack, a small figure in a faded plum-colored coat hopped down off a water barrel.

She looked young, a girl of twelve or thirteen at most, and she was short enough that the barrel had put her eye to eye with the Wardens for the first time all evening. The archive clerks said she had looked exactly like that in the Hold’s muster rolls since the year Yuan Fortress fell, two hundred years ago, which nobody under sixty quite believed and nobody over sixty argued with. Nobody in the Hold called her anything to her face; people three times her height and a tenth of her age simply stopped talking when she spoke. She carried a closed oil-paper umbrella longer than she was, its ribs tipped in steel, and she wore the expression of someone who had been woken for a war she had specifically asked not to be woken for.

“Watcher,” she said, with vast, weary patience, hitching the umbrella’s strap over a shoulder that came up to Liangyu’s elbow. “I’ll take the west stair. Somebody find me something to stand on. The parapet was built for giants.”

“Yes, Elder,” said Liangyu, who outranked her on paper.

***

Out under the covered arcade fronting the inner courtyard, the cold hit them like a wet sack.

Sleet was beginning to slant down out of the low grey murk, rattling against the dark clay tiles overhead and turning the churned mud around the gatehouse capstans into black grease. High above the roofs, where the concentric bronze rings of the Astrolabe Core should have been turning their steady amber light across the mountain, there was only an immense, lightless smudge against the clouds. Without the Skywatch Rings, the Hold had no searchlights; the only illumination along the great main wall came from the string of bare work-bulbs Amy had wired onto the parapet rails an hour ago, swaying in the gusty draft and throwing hard, swinging shadows across the puddles.

In the center of the courtyard stood Nyx Ravenhollow. Even kneeling on one white-and-gold knee with her feather-blades folded back along her shoulders like a roosting hawk’s wings and the acid-green stenciling on her plate slick with sleet, the five-meter Startorch frame took up half the flagstones between the well-curb and the arcade steps.

“The fog’s already spilling over the outer barbican,” Ciaccona said, buttoning her coat to the chin as she stepped out onto the wet paving and slung her lute strap across her shoulder. She squinted toward the main gatehouse arch, where the first milky tendrils of the Disorienting Fog were curling under the iron teeth of the raised portcullis. “Look at the torch-bearers on the west stair—they’re facing the cistern wall. They already can’t tell which way the road comes up.”

“If the mist closes over the parapet, the archers will be shooting into our own pike-line within five minutes,” Cartethyia said, her crystal laurel catching the yellow glare of the work-bulbs as she checked the buckle at her wrist. “Cia, can you reach both towers from the gatehouse roof?”

“If I stand by the bell-frame and play loud enough to ruin my calluses, yes,” Ciaccona said dryly. “I’ll lay a four-four grid across the ramp so anyone with ears can tell uphill from down. Come on, cara, you’re keeping the things with teeth off my hem while I work.”

Mei stepped off the arcade curb behind them, her right hand already closed around her hilt and her coat tucked back behind her scabbard. “I’ll take the lower right capstan with Chisa. If they try to wedge the portcullis tracks—”

Four people stopped walking at the exact same instant.

Lupa didn’t even turn all the way around; she pivoted on her newly re-nailed heel, planted the butt of her spear on the flagstones directly in front of Mei’s toes, and pointed one gauntleted finger at the arcade steps.

“Sit. Down.”

Mei blinked at the iron spearhead blocking her boot, then looked up at Lupa. “I’m not climbing anything! The capstan’s right there on the ground, it’s completely flat—”

“Your gut’s sewed together with thread that isn’t a week old, and the princess told you to stay at rug height before she went through that door,” Lupa snapped, her wolf ears pinning flat back against her hair in the sleet. “You don’t touch the winch, you don’t go near the gate, and you take your hand off that hilt before I tie you to the pillar with Amy’s lamp wire.”

“There are twenty Wardens for the whole wall and three of us aren’t here!” Mei’s voice rose, sharp and sudden enough to cut across the clatter of pike-butts from the gatehouse. She didn’t put on her crooked grin, and she didn’t make a joke about her stitches. Her golden eyes went from Lupa’s face to the dark sky above the Ministry roof where the Core hung dead. “We don’t know what’s coming out of that fog, and we don’t know when Iuno and Yangyang and the Paragon are coming down. I’m not sitting in a doorway watching everyone else bleed while I’ve got two working hands.”

Ciaccona turned back from the courtyard steps, her hand resting flat on the neck of her lute. The easy, stage-bright mockery went clean out of her face.

“And who holds Amy while I’m on the roof?” Ciaccona asked.

Mei stopped. She looked at Amy standing by the arcade post, then back at Ciaccona. “One of the orderlies inside—or Grandmother Wen—”

“When five metres of machine takes a direct hit in the mud out there, the feedback comes straight back through the link into her nervous system and her body tries to bolt off the floor,” Ciaccona said, her voice quiet and surgical. “Wait until you’ve tried to keep her chin still while she’s dropping frames and coughing static before you hand her to an eighty-year-old woman with a bad leg. Or were you planning to let her sit against a grain sack by herself so you can feel useful at the gate?”

The sentence landed like cold iron. Mei’s fingers loosened a fraction on her pommel.

Beside Lupa, Chisa adjusted the heavy black strap of her scissor-blade across her shoulder, her eyes resting in the general direction of Mei’s chest.

“If you tear your middle open in the mud,” Chisa said in her flat, plain voice, “Lupa and I will have to stop fighting to carry you back inside. You’re heavy.”

Mei opened her mouth, shut it again, and looked at her daughter.

Amy was leaning one shoulder against the red-lacquered pillar of the arcade, her thumbs hooked into her pockets. The acid-green star clip in her hair caught the swinging light of the work-bulbs as she looked back at Mei—no theatrical eye-roll, no fast chatter.

“If you’re holding me,” Amy said plainly, “you can’t let go until I come back inside my head. Which means you can’t sneak out to the ditch when nobody’s looking. That’s the deal, Ma. Hold my chin, or Nyx stays parked in the yard and we lose the gate in ten minutes.”

For three breaths Mei stood in the sleet between the four of them. Her jaw worked once; she hated it—hated the dead gray sky above the gearwells, hated the pull across her lower ribs every time she drew a deep breath, hated being cornered by people who knew every trick she had before she took a step.

Slowly, Mei took her right hand off her sword hilt.

“Fine,” Mei said, the word coming out rough and short. “Get a rug.”

Lupa didn’t gloat. She reached through the open doorway of the hall, yanked a thick, doubled-over felt saddle-blanket off a stack of quartermaster stores, and threw it down across the dry flagstones of the arcade threshold—half inside the hall’s warmth, half outside under the eaves where the whole sweep of the courtyard and the back of the gatehouse arch lay in plain sight.

Mei walked back up the two steps, unbuckled her scabbard so the chape wouldn’t dig into the stone, and sat down cross-legged on the felt blanket, keeping her spine against the heavy cedar doorpost so her abdomen stayed straight. She laid the naked sword across her knees within reach of her right hand, then looked up and patted the wool in front of her shin.

“Come here,” Mei said, her voice dropping its edge.

Amy walked over, kicked the mud off her heels against the bottom step, and dropped down between Mei’s knees with her back resting against Mei’s good right side. She smelled of ozone, machine oil, and the cheap taro milk she’d bought in the market with Chisa two days ago.

“Don’t pinch my nose to see if I’m breathing,” Amy muttered, tipping her head back until the crown of her pink hair settled into the crook of Mei’s left collarbone. “I will be. It’s boring, but I will be.”

“I’m not going to pinch your nose,” Mei said, wrapping her left arm carefully around Amy’s ribs to anchor her shoulders and setting her right palm lightly under the curve of Amy’s chin. “Keep your feet out of the sleet puddles out there, okay?”

“I’m five meters of Startorch alloy, Ma, puddles are beneath me,” Amy said, and closed her eyes.

She drew one long breath in through her nose, and let it out.

On the exhale, Amy left.

Mei had seen her daughter do it half a dozen times from across a room or the back of a cart, and she had had no idea what it felt like in your hands. Between one heartbeat and the next, the small, restless tension that made Amy *Amy*—the slight brace of her neck muscles, the way her shoulders shifted when she was thinking of a comeback—simply went out of the flesh like a lamp turned down. Her head grew suddenly, startlingly heavy in Mei’s palm, her jaw slackening against Mei’s fingers while her chest dropped into a slow, mechanical four-count rhythm that didn’t change when the cold wind gusted under the eaves.

Thirty feet away in the sleet-swept courtyard, the twin blue optics beneath Nyx Ravenhollow’s crest flared to life, throwing two sharp cones of sapphire light across the wet flagstones.

The five-meter machine rose smoothly from its knee, the great white-and-gold shoulder plates locking into place with a deep, pneumatic *clack* that vibrated through the soles of Mei’s boots. Nyx turned her head toward the arcade, raised one massive articulated hand to tap two fingers against her brow, and spoke through her chest speakers at a volume that rattled every windowpane in the Ministry hall:

“TESTING, ONE TWO. IF YOU DROP ME IN A PUDDLE WHILE I’M GONE, MA, I’M TELLING IUNO YOU STOLE HER TEA FLASK.”

A dozen frightened children inside the hall doorway jumped and then stared out into the yard with round eyes as the giant white knight pivoted on her heel and strode toward the main gatehouse, her feather-blades fanning wide like wings of gold leaf.

Down on the felt blanket, the nineteen-year-old girl in Mei’s lap didn’t twitch an eyelash. Her cheek was warm and completely still against Mei’s thumb, a faint, steady prickle of golden static humming just under the skin of her jaw where the link ran out into the dark.

Mei looked down at the quiet face resting in her hand—at the small acid-green star clip pinned slightly crooked above Amy’s ear—and for a long moment the frustration went clean out of her chest, replaced by a fierce, unfamiliar ache that made her curl her left arm an inch tighter around her daughter’s shoulders.

***

From the top of the gatehouse roof, Ciaccona couldn’t see ten yards down the switchback road.

The Disorienting Fog didn’t drift like mountain mist; it climbed the stone ramp in a thick, tallow-colored wall that swallowed the glare of Amy’s wired work-bulbs and threw the sound of the sleet back at their ears from three directions at once. Down on the parapet walk to her left, a young archer kept turning his head toward the inner courtyard, convinced the scraping noise in the dark was coming from behind the granary roof.

Ciaccona planted her back against the timber upright of the bell-frame, wedged her boot heel against a drainage scupper so the wet tiles wouldn’t take her feet, and struck an open G chord across the belly of her lute.

Her Forte caught the note before the string stopped shaking. A pale emerald ripple expanded outward from the soundboard, thirty yards across, settling over the gatehouse arch and the wet stones of the upper ramp like an invisible dome of clear glass. Inside that ring, the false echoes died at once; the sleet sounded from the sky where it belonged, and the heavy, rhythmic *tramp-tramp-clink* of iron soles came straight up the center of the road out of the blind white dark.

“Front!” Liangyu called from the gateway below, her voice ringing crisp inside Ciaccona’s acoustic pocket. “Pikes down!”

Out of the fog wall stepped the first rank of the Censure Courts.

They were Autopuppets built on the heavy bronze chassis of the Hold’s own foundry, but someone had draped their oil-blackened shoulders in the rotted indigo robes and stiff-eared gauze caps of a two-hundred-year-old magistrate’s tribunal. Their optical lenses burned a flat, unblinking crimson through the sleet. In their left claws they carried long brass tally-rods stamped with execution marks; in their right, four-foot butcher’s cleavers of notched bronze that dragged sparks from the wet paving stones as they marched four abreast into the yellow light of the work-bulbs.

Behind them in the mist moved worse things—long-backed, multi-limbed Tacet Discords drawn up out of the burst valley cages like hounds on a leash, their gold-and-black cores pulsing in time with the puppets’ brass gears.

As the front four stepped across the outer ditch line, their jaw-plates clicked open in unison. The voices that came out of their chest-boxes were not mechanical buzzes; they were the flat, unhurried voices of old court clerks reading out a tax default at the end of the quarter.

*“Xuanfang Garrison. Found wanting.”*

*“Artisan Registry. Struck from the roll.”*

“Hold the arch!” Liangyu shouted, and drove her spear two-handed into the throat-joint of the lead magistrate as the iron ranks crashed into the gateway.

The shock of the first collision ran all the way up the stone buttresses into the soles of Ciaccona’s boots. Down in the twenty-foot throat of the gatehouse, beneath the raised teeth of the iron portcullis, the Wardens jammed their shield-bosses together in the mud while Nyx Ravenhollow’s feather-blades screamed down from above, shearing two of the robed machines in half in a shower of burning oil and indigo rags.

Ciaccona kept her fingers moving across the frets—one-two-three-four, steady as a metronome—holding the sound-pocket open against the fog that boiled around the buttresses and tried to turn the defenders’ ears inside out.

Inside that pocket of true sound, Shen Lu’s arrows finally went where they were aimed. The archer drew on the parapet below the bell-frame, breathed a thread of green wind along the shaft, and loosed blind into the white. The arrow bent in the air like a swallow, found the gap between a tribunal cap and its collar-plate, and the crimson lens behind it went out. Ciaccona heard the next one leave the string and land on the beat, and the one after that, and decided she liked her.

On the narrow stone step at the right side of the arch, a young Warden named Little Tan took the full weight of two tribunal puppets on his buckler. The wood split down the center with a wet crack, knocking him to one knee in the slush. Before he could bring his pike-shaft up to cover his head, the second puppet raised its four-foot bronze cleaver in both claws—

*“Struck from the roll.”*

—and brought the sixty-pound blade straight down toward the gap between his helmet and his collarbone.

Little Tan shut his eyes.

Three inches above his soaked wool cloak, the empty air snapped taut.

A single, hair-thin line of moon-white light flashed into existence between the falling cleaver and the boy’s shoulder, drawn as straight and rigid as a silk string pulled across a bridge. The notched bronze edge hit that thread at full force and skidded sideways with a pure, ringing *plang* that cut clean through the roar of the gateway, throwing a fan of frost-white sparks across the mud and burying the cleaver half an inch deep in the granite jamb instead.

For the space of a breath, the air around the gateway smelled sharply of high mountain snow. Where the pale string had touched the wet grey wool of Little Tan’s cloak, a neat white line of rime lay crystallized along the weave.

Little Tan stared down at his own intact shoulder, his mouth open, before Warden Ge dragged him backward by his belt and put a pike through the puppet’s crimson lens.

Up on the high, roofed watch gallery behind the courtyard—where the dark paulownia qin lay untouched beneath its silk cover—a single open string sounded once by itself in the sleet.

It wasn’t loud, and Ciaccona’s acoustic dome caught the vibration off the wet cedar beams and carried it down across the gateway, clear and cold as water dropping into a well.

Down in the mud at the center of the line, Liangyu heard it as she wrenched her spearhead free of a brass ribcage.

She knew that pitch. She had stood in the snow on a nameless peak until dusk listening to that exact fourth string ring whenever her spear-point drifted half an inch off the center line.

Liangyu didn’t look back at the gallery, and she didn’t shout anything to the men and women panting beside her in the slush. She simply set her back foot firmer in the rut, wiped the sleet from her brow with her forearm, and along the twenty-foot line of soaked cloaks, twenty exhausted shoulders squared up an inch higher behind their shields.

***

Up on the west parapet walk, Lupa was hating the stairs.

It was a forty-foot sheer drop from the inner lip of the wall down to the courtyard paving, and the wet stone steps had no rail on the inside—only slick grey mud, swinging electric bulbs that made the gulf beneath her heels pitch sideways, and a cold crosswind that smelled of nothing she liked. She kept her left hip brushed hard against the outer crenelations where she didn’t have to look at the drop, her spear gripped short in both hands and her teeth set together until her jaw ached.

Down below the outer wall, the switchback ramp was choked from curb to curb with indigo robes and red lenses, pushing up out of the fog like floodwater backing up a drain. Every thirty seconds another rank of brass tally-rods stepped out of the white murk, chanting their dead clerk-sentences over the screech of Tacet Discords clawing up the masonry.

They were crowding the gateway too tight for Nyx to swing her long blades without clipping the buttresses, and the archers on the parapet were running out of room to draw before the Discords reached the lower embrasures.

Lupa looked once at the unrailed stone stair going down into the dark courtyard behind her, and then looked over the outer battlement at the packed,solid ramp twenty feet below, where there was nowhere left to fall because the mud was already full of targets.

“Chisa,” Lupa said, her voice rough in her throat. “Watch the winch.”

Before Chisa could turn her head from the capstan housing, Lupa vaulted straight over the outer crenelation, caught a jutting iron banner-bracket halfway down the stone face to break her drop, and landed boots-first in the middle of the switchback ramp, ten paces out in front of the iron portcullis.

The red-hot flare of her spear went up in the sleet like a grease-fire catching in a chimney.

For the first three breaths the Wardens on the parapet thought she had slipped off the wet wall, and two archers leaned over the stone to throw a rope. Then the howling flame tore a twenty-foot circle out of the Disorienting Fog around the base of the ramp, and they saw her face in the red glare.

The archers pulled the rope back in and took half a step away from the parapet edge.

Lupa was smiling.

It wasn’t the wide, sharp grin she wore in the kitchen when she stole the biggest piece of meat out of the pan, and it wasn’t the showman’s grin she gave the carters in the public wash-house before signing their wrappers. It was wide, white-toothed, and completely uncanny in the freezing dark—the face of a creature that had been cooped up in small rooms tending soup pots and minding other people’s stitches for a week and had finally been let out into an arena where nothing had a name she needed to save.

*“Unregistered combatant,”* the nearest tribunal captain droned, swinging its brass tally-rod at her temple while two more drove their cleavers at her ribs. *“Found—”*

Lupa didn’t parry; she stepped inside the arc of the rod, caught the bronze wrist in her left gauntlet while a pale string of snow-light flashed once across her shoulder-plate with a sharp *ting*, and drove the butt of her spear up through the machine’s jaw-box until the clerk-voice choked on a mouthful of melted brass.

“Found *this*,” Lupa snarled, laughing out loud as she spun the heavy spear around her back and sheared the legs out from under all three of them in one wide, roaring sweep of crimson fire.

She didn’t fight back toward the gate. She took two steps *down* the switchback into the teeth of the column, where the sleet turned to steam before it could touch her shoulders.

In the thick of the fog, her fire burned higher and stranger than ordinary flame. As the red gusts whipped around her boots and licked up the indigo robes of the pressing ranks, the smoke inside the fire didn’t drift away on the wind—it held its shape at her elbows, tall and broad-shouldered in the glare, turning when she turned and striking when her spear-shaft swept left, as though two or three old friends from an older colosseum were keeping the blind side of her ribs the way they always had. For a second, to the Wardens staring down through the sleet from the wall, it looked as though there were three spears in that red ring on the road instead of one.

A long-jawed Discord lunged down from the cliffside rocks onto her back; a second white thread snapped taut at her nape with an icy chime from the gallery above, skidding the claws off her collar, and Lupa caught the beast by its throat-crest with one hand and slammed it straight into the chest of an advancing magistrate puppet until both of them went over the outer verge into the gorge.

She planted one boot on the stone curb of the ramp, her chest heaving, black oil smeared across her cheek and her wolf tail bushed out like a torch behind her, and tipped her head back toward the blind white wall of the valley fog.

The howl that tore out of her throat had nothing to do with the Septimont games.

It was long, flat, and bottomless, rising out of her ribs at a pitch that made the bronze capstan chains in the gatehouse vibrate and cut across Ciaccona’s steady four-four lute beat like a jagged knife through silk. Out in the milky dark of the switchbacks, the advancing ranks of the Censure Courts actually stopped marching for the space of a single breath, their red lenses flickering as their audio-pickups overloaded on a sound no two-hundred-year-old registry had a code for; behind them in the mist, the scraping claws of the Discords went dead quiet.

Inside the Ministry hall, sixty yards back, the howl went through the crowded benches like a bucket of river water. Every child in the room stopped crying at once.

Under the covered arcade at the doorway, Mei’s head came up sharply. She couldn’t see past the gatehouse arch from her rug, and she didn’t need to; she knew the exact sound of Lupa when the wolf got on top of the woman and started looking for a second mile of road to burn open.

Down at the gatehouse, Chisa heard it too.

She was standing by the left capstan with the two blades of her giant shears planted in the mud, her face smudged with black axle-grease from the iron ratchet. She couldn’t see faces twenty feet out in the sleet and the red smoke, and she didn’t need eyes to know the gait of Lupa’s boots on the stones—the weight shifting forward onto the toes instead of back toward the arch, getting ready to chase the next rank down into the fog where Ciaccona’s sound-pocket ended and nobody came back out.

Chisa walked three steps into the mouth of the gateway, right up to the line where the sleet met the heat of the fire, and didn’t shout.

“Lupa,” Chisa said.

Her voice was completely plain, cutting straight under the crackle of burning indigo cloth and the ring of Ciaccona’s lute because it was the exact voice she used at the breakfast table when she wanted the salt passed.

Out on the slick stones of the ramp, with her spear raised over a halved bronze torso and the red grin still fixed wide across her teeth, Lupa froze.

Her left ear twitched once toward the gatehouse arch. The tall shapes in the smoke around her elbows thinned back into ordinary sparks. For two heartbeats she stood there with her shoulders hunched and her breath coming in white, ragged gusts, looking down the dark switchback where more red lenses were already blinking back on in the mist—and then the uncanny grin dropped half an inch back into a scowl.

Lupa spat a mouthful of soot into the slush, dragged the back of her wrist across her mouth, and walked backward up the ramp under the iron teeth of the portcullis without taking her eyes off the fog.

She bumped her shoulder hard against Chisa’s upper arm as she came past the jamb, breathing like a bellows and smelling of scorched wool and hot bronze.

“There’s a lot of them out there,” Lupa muttered, her voice still half a tone too high and fast as her tail flicked once against Chisa’s shin.

“Yes,” Chisa said, not moving her arm away from the bump. “Drink some water.”

***

They didn’t stay paused in the fog for long.

Three minutes after Lupa came back inside the arch, the white wall on the switchback split open with the grinding roar of forty bronze gear-trains locked into a single cadence, and the Censure Courts came at the gatehouse like a battering ram.

Nyx Ravenhollow braced both white-and-gold boots in the mud at the western buttress, her feather-blades whirling in a blinding figure-eight that turned the lead rank into flying scrap—

“GET OFF THE PAINT, YOU RUSTY TAX AUDITORS!” Amy’s voice boomed from the courtyard speakers, echoing off the wet roofs. “I JUST BUFFED THAT SHOULDER!”

—while thirty more robed machines poured straight under her guard, jamming their brass tally-rods into the iron links of the portcullis chain and driving the Wardens back five paces from the outer sill.

“Drop the grate!” Liangyu shouted over the shriek of bronze on iron, taking a cleaver-stroke across her vambrace that bounced off a flashing white thread with a sharp *plink*. “Huo! Let the ratchet go!”

At the inner wall, Warden Huo and Chisa threw their weight against the iron release lever. The massive timber capstan groaned, spun half a turn, and jammed dead with an ear-splitting screech as three tribunal puppets wedged their own bronze shoulders straight into the vertical stone grooves of the portcullis track, holding six tons of spiked iron hung up four feet above the mud.

“The track’s fouled!” Huo yelled, blood running down his wrist where the kicking lever had barked his skin. “If the center gives before we clear the groove, they’re in the yard!”

In the center of the twenty-foot gateway, Cartethyia planted her small boots in the black slush between Liangyu and the wedged grate.

“Stand back two paces from the track, please!” Cartethyia called out, her voice bright and earnest against the din of clashing bronze.

She raised one hand toward the iron arch overhead, and the crystal thorns of her laurel flared like struck flint.

A column of pale, roaring wind and sea-blue light split the sleet under the gatehouse vault. When it cleared half a heartbeat later, the small knight in the mud was gone.

Fleurdelys stood beneath the iron portcullis, nine feet tall from her heeled boots to the tip of her horned crown, her long pale hair whipping across the wet stone vaulting and her two great swords—pure, translucent blue-white and longer than a Warden’s pike—crossed before her breastplate like the bars of a cathedral gate.

“None shall pass this threshold,” Fleurdelys intoned, her deep, archaic voice rolling down the stone throat of the passage like an organ chord, “whilst my blade yet holds the watch.”

She swept both blades outward in a single crescent that cleared the mouth of the arch from wall to wall, hurling six tribunal puppets bodily back into the press and buying Chisa the space to jam her giant scissor-blades into the right-hand portcullis groove and shear a wedged bronze torso clean out of the stonework.

Before they could clear the left groove, three heavy executioner-puppets linked their brass tally-rods together into a single twelve-foot beam, charged out of the fog with two crawling Discords riding their backs, and brought the combined iron-shod mass crashing down out of the dark straight onto Fleurdelys’s left shoulder as she braced the sagging grate with her right arm.

It was a blow meant to crack a stone bastion in half.

Even at nine feet tall, Fleurdelys’s knees buckled two inches into the mud as the timber beam and the Discord claws came down on her collarbone—and then a cord of white light as thick as a thumb snapped taut across her shoulder-plate.

The sound that came off it wasn’t a high chime; it was the deep, resonant *dong* of a bass string struck hard enough to shake the mortar out of the gatehouse arch. Up on the dark watch gallery behind them, the covered paulownia qin answered with a low note that shivered through every puddle in the courtyard.

The twelve-foot brass beam skidded six inches off the thread of snow-light, shattering its iron collars against the stone vault overhead instead of breaking her neck, while a sheet of white rime bloomed across Fleurdelys’s pale sleeve from shoulder to cuff.

She didn’t fall. She drove her heel down into the mud, shoved the broken beam back into the fog with one armored forearm, and held the arch.

Up on the wet tiles of the gatehouse roof, Ciaccona saw the flash of that bass string down through the sleet—saw how close the twelve-foot iron collar had come to the base of that tall, ridiculous chapel-saint neck—and for exactly one bar of four beats, her fingers stopped moving on the lute strings.

The emerald sound-pocket over the ramp flickered once, letting a wash of disorienting echo bleed in from the gorge, before Ciaccona bit the inside of her lip hard enough to taste salt, cursed under her breath in Ragunnese, and slammed her thumb back down on the downbeat louder than before.

Then the fog thirty yards down the switchback didn’t part—it bulged upward like a blister.

Out of the sleet lumbered what the Censure Courts had been clearing the road for.

It was thirty feet tall, stitched together out of six wrecked Cloud Shuttle hulls, three dozen fused Autopuppet torsos, and the entire black-lacquered timber bench of a Ministry high court. Six jointed bronze legs the size of pine trunks drove its bulk up the stone switchback, crushing the low parapet curb to powder under every step. Above its shoulders rose a rotting crimson magistrate’s canopy, and where a head should have sat hung a ten-foot slab of carved white verdict-jade scrawled with glowing red tallies. Its right arm was a siege-sized bronze gavel suspended on ship’s chains, swinging like a pendulum as twenty harmonizing clerk-voices boomed out of its ribcage at once:

*“XUANFANG HOLD. CASE CLOSED. SENTENCE: DEMOLITION.”*

The colossus planted its front legs on the outer ditch wall and raised the two-ton gavel above the gatehouse roof, high enough to bring Ciaccona’s bell-frame, the iron portcullis, and everyone standing beneath the arch down into a single pile of rubble.

“Nyx can’t reach the upper joint from the yard!” Amy’s voice cracked through the courtyard speakers as the white mech fired a volley of golden feathers that only scarred the colossus’s lower knee-plates. “Get out from under the arch! Now!”

Beneath the iron teeth of the portcullis, Fleurdelys looked up through the sleet at the descending bronze gavel.

She did not step back from the gateway.

“Then let the court,” Fleurdelys said, her deep voice dropping into the still, quiet center under the storm, “hear the answer of the knight.”

She drew both pure swords together above her crown until their hilts locked and their blades merged into a single, towering pillar of pale tempest-light that rose straight through the iron bars of the portcullis, piercing the sleet-clouds above the roof and turning every puddle in Xuanfang Hold as bright as noon.

The gavel started down.

Fleurdelys stepped forward one pace in the mud and swung once, diagonally, from the upper right to the lower left.

There was no sound of metal hitting metal. There was only a single, clean, whistling rush of wind, like a giant silk banner ripped in two from hem to hem.

The blade of condensed light passed straight through the descending bronze gavel, through the ten-foot slab of verdict-jade, through the black-lacquered magistrate’s bench and the central furnace-core of the colossus—and did not stop there.

The trailing edge of the stroke swept backward across the inner threshold and passed effortlessly through the four-foot-thick granite masonry of Xuanfang Hold’s great main wall, right where the eastern gatehouse buttress met the curtain rampart.

For the space of two long breaths, everything on the mountain froze.

Out on the switchback road, the two halves of the thirty-foot tribunal colossus slid apart along a mirror-smooth diagonal seam, glowing pale blue at the cut edges before collapsing into the mud on either side of the ramp with a deafening, wet crash of dead bronze.

And directly behind the gateway, a razor-thin diagonal black line ran forty feet from the top of the eastern battlements all the way down to the courtyard plinth—bisecting two hundred years of hand-chiseled frontier granite as cleanly as a knife through bean-curd.

In the dead silence that followed, the entire upper forty-ton triangle of the main wall gave a slow, subterranean *grrrrrk* of stone on stone.

It slid downward along the diagonal cut by one inch.

Then two inches, showering a fine line of grey granite flour onto Warden Huo’s helmet.

Then the lower angle of the cut jammed against the iron capstan housing, groaned once, and held fast.

At the foot of the cut, Bai Tieshan had both palms flat against the granite, his eyes shut and his jaw clenched, and along the seam where his hands lay, the pale stone flour had gone dark and damp as wet clay.

“She’ll stay,” the foreman said hoarsely, to nobody. “If nobody leans on her.”

Nobody on the parapet moved. Out on the ramp, the remaining tribunal puppets stood headless or sheared in half in the settling steam. Up on the gatehouse roof, Ciaccona looked slowly down over the edge of the tiles at the two-inch stone overhang running straight through the middle of the fortress wall.

Under the arch, a puff of pale light dissolved into the sleet, and Cartethyia stood in the mud in her own small boots again, blinking up at the forty-foot diagonal seam she had just put through the primary defense of the northern frontier.

“Oh,” Cartethyia said, in her normal voice.

Beside the capstan, Warden Huo slowly reached out with his right hand and slid all four of his fingers flat into the smooth, glassy gap between the upper and lower halves of the wall, up to the knuckle. He pulled his hand back out, stared at the stone dust on his palm, and looked at Liangyu.

Liangyu looked from the halved colossus smoking in the ditch to the two-inch granite lip jutting out above the winch. Her face was completely blank with exhaustion.

“That wall,” Liangyu said evenly, “has stood since the third year of the Founding Reign.”

Cartethyia’s hands came together in front of her breastplate, the crystal thorns of her laurel dipping an inch as her cheeks went bright pink in the yellow glare of the work-bulbs.

“I am very sorry!” Cartethyia called up toward the parapet, altogether too loud in the sudden quiet. “I pulled my left elbow in at the end so I wouldn’t hit the bell-frame, and the edge carried a little further than I meant it to! We still have the money Yangyang’s sister sent for the roof tarpaulins—I will pay the masons for the mortar!”

From the roof tiles twenty feet above her head, Ciaccona leaned her chin on her hand over the gutter-scupper.

“Five thousand credits doesn’t buy a new fortress wall, cara,” Ciaccona drawled down into the mud. “Next time, aim for the cheap rocks.”

***

Because every pair of eyes in the Hold was fixed on the smoking halves of the colossus and the diagonal crack in the main wall, nobody saw the things coming over the Ministry roof until they dropped below the eaves.

With the Skywatch Rings dark overhead, the upper air was completely blind. Half a dozen winged Tacet Discords—lean, long-beaked carrion shapes drawn by the flash of Fleurdelys’s sword through the cloud cover—slipped silently over the unlit ridge-tiles behind the watch gallery, tucked their ragged pinions, and dived straight down into the inner courtyard toward the open doors of the branch hall.

Out by the gatehouse, Nyx Ravenhollow had her back turned, using one massive white shoulder to brace the shifted granite buttress while Chisa and Lupa drove iron wedges under the slipped stone.

Between the diving shadows and the sixty children inside the hall doorway sat only the felt saddle-blanket under the arcade.

Mei heard the whistle of stiff feathers parting the sleet a split second before the first shadow crossed the work-bulbs.

She could not stand up, and she could not drop Amy’s head onto the flagstones; if she let go of the link while Nyx was bracing forty tons of sheared granite at the gate, the sudden disconnect would drop the mech to its knees under the wall.

Mei didn’t get up.

She stayed on her knees on the felt rug, locked her left arm like an iron hoop around Amy’s limp chest to haul her daughter’s weight tight against her own right hip—away from the fresh stitches across her left side—and snatched the naked sword off her lap with her right hand.

“Shut the door!” Mei barked over her shoulder into the hall.

The first two fliers hit the arcade steps together in a spray of black sleet, their hooked talons raking for the girl lying slack in her lap.

Mei pivoted on her bruised knees across the wool blanket, turning too fast on her right hip to put her own back between the claws and Amy’s face, and whipped her blade up in a single-handed rising cut that split the lead Discord from breastbone to beak in a shower of gold-and-black sparks.

The turn pulled hard across her lower abdomen—a hot, tearing bite under the linen wrap that made her breath catch in her teeth—and because she pulled her weight left to favor her middle while her left arm was pinned by Amy’s shoulders, she couldn’t bring her sword back across in time to meet the third flier dropping straight down under the arcade lintel.

Its hooked iron beak drove down at the gap between Mei’s neck and her left shoulder, right over the top of Amy’s head.

In Mei’s lap, even miles away inside a machine at the gate, Amy’s body felt the shadow fall across them: for the space of a single frame, the pink hair resting against Mei’s collarbone skipped violently into jagged white static, and two drops of raw golden light leaked from her slack fingertips onto the wool blanket like water from an open tap.

Mei hunched her shoulder to take the beak in the meat of her back rather than let it reach Amy’s skull—

And from inside the breast of Mei’s grey shirt, right where Shijie’s folded letter lay tucked against her ribs under her bandage, a thread of moon-white light snapped out through the linen.

It didn’t come from a cloak; she wasn’t wearing one. The pale string caught the diving beak half an inch from her shirt collar with a sharp, musical *twang* that smelled suddenly of clean winter snow, skidding the Discord’s strike sideways into the red-lacquered arcade pillar while the covered qin up on the dark watch gallery sounded a clear, solitary note above their heads.

Before the beast could wrench its beak out of the split cedar post, Mei shifted her grip on her hilt and drove her point straight up through its throat with her right arm alone, never lifting her knees an inch off the felt rug.

Then five tons of white-and-gold Startorch armor came across the courtyard in two ground-shaking bounds, landed with both boots in the middle of the remaining three fliers, and crushed two of them flat into the flagstones while a sweep of one golden feather-blade batted the last one over the granary roof like a shuttlecock.

Nyx Ravenhollow spun around in the sleet, her blue optics blazing down under the arcade eaves like twin furnace doors and her chest speakers cranked so loud the plaster dust snowed down from the hall ceiling:

“WHAT DID YOU JUST DO?! YOU DID NOT JUST SWING A SWORD WITH ONE HAND WHILE HOLDING MY HEAD, MA, I SAW MY OWN HAIR GLITCH ON THE TELEMETRY! ARE YOU OUT OF YOUR MIND?!”

Mei stayed on her knees on the wool blanket, breathing hard through her nose, her right hand dropping the bloodied sword onto the stones so she could bring her palm right back up under the slack, warm curve of Amy’s chin while the giant white machine thirty feet away waved both arms in furious circles.

“My knees stayed on the rug!” Mei called back at the fifteen-foot robot, her voice shaking slightly at the end as she smoothed her thumb over Amy’s cheek until the last flicker of white static settled back into solid pink hair. “Both of them! Look at my boots, I didn’t even touch the mud!”

“THAT IS A TECHNICALITY AND IUNO IS GOING TO SKIN US BOTH ALIVE!” Nyx roared across the courtyard, before pivoting back toward the gatehouse as a fresh horn sounded from the parapet. “DON’T YOU MOVE AN INCH, I’M WATCHING YOU ON THE REAR CAMERA!”

As the great white heels clanged back toward the wall, a small shadow slipped out from behind the half-shut doors of the Ministry hall.

It was Qingyan, the boy from Xianling Village, dragging his dead sister’s Warden broadsword in both hands with the heavy scabbard thumping against his shins. His face was white as chalk in the glare of the work-bulbs, and his jaw was set in the exact, terrible way Mei had seen on a hundred people right before they ran out into a road that was too big for them.

“There’s another hole by the well-steps,” the boy said, his voice cracking as he tried to edge past the corner of Mei’s blanket toward the courtyard. “Warden Huo’s arm is hurt. I know the first four forms—”

Mei didn’t let go of Amy’s chin with her right hand. She reached out with two fingers of her left, caught the back of Qingyan’s hemp coat before he could put a foot on the wet steps, and gave one firm tug backward.

“Sit,” Mei said.

Qingyan pulled against her grip, his eyes wide on the dark roofs. “My sister’s sword—”

“Your sister’s sword stays on the dry side of this doorway tonight,” Mei said. There was no smile on her face at all, and as she heard the flat, unmistakable cadence of her own voice under the arcade eaves, she realized she sounded exactly like Lupa five minutes ago. “Sit down on the left corner of the rug, put the scabbard across your knees, and if another bird comes off that roof while my hands are full, you yell so loud Nyx hears you at the gate. Understand?”

Qingyan looked at the blood drying on the naked blade beside Mei’s knee, then at the quiet girl sleeping in her lap, and slowly sat down on the dry corner of the felt blanket with the heavy scabbard across his shins.

Only when the boy was settled did Mei look down at the front of her own shirt.

Across the grey cotton over her left ribs, just above the tightness of her bandages, lay a six-inch line of powdery white rime. When she slipped two fingers inside her button-placket—right beside the crisp paper corner of the letter from Mingting—she touched a hair-fine filament of cold, solid light resting against her undershirt, still humming faintly from the blow it had turned aside.

Mei blinked down at it. She hadn’t gone near the watch gallery when the Paragon was playing the qin, and she hadn’t put on any of the garrison cloaks.

Above her in the sleet, the dark wooden rail of the watch gallery sat empty against the clouds.

Mei didn’t know when Qingxiao had put it there, or why the Sword Transcendent had spared a separate thread for someone who had been ordered to stay indoors tearing linen. She didn’t have time to puzzle it out. Gently, as if she were covering a cold hand that had reached across a table in the dark, Mei laid her palm flat over the front of her shirt for one breath, then returned her fingers to Amy’s chin.

***

Between the third hour after midnight and the fourth, the sleet turned to a thin, freezing drizzle that coated the bronze joints of the dead puppets on the ramp in black ice.

The Censure Courts did not stop coming. They came in smaller, uglier waves out of the fog—five or six robed frames lashed together with crawling Discords, throwing themselves against the timber shoring Chisa and Nyx had wedged under the bisected wall and forcing the Wardens in the gateway to fight over a waist-high barricade of sheared brass.

Nobody had time to talk anymore. On the gatehouse roof, Ciaccona’s fingertips were split and raw against the steel lute strings, keeping the four-four beat alive by sheer muscle memory while her breath came in white puffs. Down in the mud, Lupa and Cartethyia traded the center of the arch back and forth every ten minutes, and every few minutes a pale string of light would flash in the sleet—at Warden Ge’s thigh, at Little Tan’s neck, at Liangyu’s elbow—turning a notched cleaver aside with a clean, snowy chime and an answering note from the covered qin on the gallery above.

The Wardens didn’t even flinch when the white threads fired now; they leaned into them like men leaning against a familiar stone wall in the dark.

Then, in the middle of an ordinary scuffle by the right-hand capstan—just three water-logged tribunal puppets shoving their tally-rods across the barricade—the wall vanished.

Every single thread of Sword Intent across Xuanfang Hold went slack at the exact same heartbeat.

Up on the dark watch gallery, the paulownia qin cut off in the middle of a vibrating overtone, dropping into a silence so sudden and complete that even through the clash of iron in the gateway, twenty heads jerked halfway around toward the courtyard.

At the barricade, Warden Ge raised his buckler half a second late, expecting the pale string at his shoulder to catch a descending bronze cleaver the way it had caught the last three.

There was no string.

The notched bronze edge bit straight through his wet wool cloak and three inches into the flesh of his left forearm, knocking him backward into the mud with a hot spray of blood and a choked cry.

“Paragon—!” Little Tan shouted, his voice breaking high and terrified in the dark as he dragged Ge behind the capstan drum, and then stopped dead before he could say the rest of the sentence.

On the roof above them, Ciaccona’s hands froze for half a beat over her soundboard. Down under the arcade, Mei felt the cold filament inside her shirt go as limp and dead as a piece of wet cotton thread against her ribs.

For the length of a single heartbeat, everyone who had been listening to that covered qin all night understood at once what a dead string meant—and what it meant for the two people who had climbed into the dark beside its owner.

Nobody said a word.

Then, before Warden Ge had even hit the flagstones, the link snapped back.

It didn’t rise like a plucked note; it jerked taut across the courtyard with a silent, freezing shock that made the hair stand up on the backs of forty necks at once.

Up on the watch gallery, under its silk cover, the paulownia qin sounded again by itself—a single, heavy note, a full shade lower and colder than any pitch it had struck since dusk.

At the barricade, the tribunal puppet that had cut Warden Ge’s arm raised its bloodied cleaver for a second downward stroke at the fallen man.

A ribbon of light flashed across the gap above Ge’s chest.

It was not moon-white anymore; its edge was the bruised, dark blue-grey of winter twilight just before the pass closes. When the bronze cleaver struck it, the thread did not gently skid the blade aside into the mud.

The string held rigid as iron, and lashed *back*.

A hair-thin line of twilight light whipped outward from the point of impact and sheared straight through the puppet’s bronze wrist, through its indigo chest-robe, and out through its spine with the dry hiss of hot metal plunged into ice. Where the severed halves of the machine hit the gateway stones, a jagged circle of real, inch-thick hoarfrost exploded outward across the wet granite, freezing the black slush solid around Little Tan’s boots in the space of a breath.

Little Tan stared down at the halved puppet smoking in the ice, then up at the dark gallery rail. “She’s—she’s still there! The Paragon’s still holding!”

Along the parapet, a ragged, breathless sound of relief went up from the exhausted archers. Kou Xiaoman was already off the wall in a crackle of violet, a streak of light down the length of the courtyard to the hall doors, shouting it ahead of herself so the benches would hear before she got there: “Still holding! She’s still holding!”

Three paces away in the mud, Liangyu lowered the point of her spear an inch and looked down at the severed bronze torso lying in the frost.

The cut through the brass ribcage wasn’t clean and spare, the way Qingxiao’s needles had pinned the twenty Discords in Xianling Village without clipping a single persimmon branch. The frost along the sheared metal was jagged, bitter, and vicious—a blade that didn’t stop when the person behind it was safe, but kept cutting until the thing in front of it was in pieces.

Liangyu had taken three thousand wooden sword-strokes from Qingxiao’s hand on the snowpeak. She knew the exact temper of her teacher’s heart in a blade, and that cut was not quite her teacher’s.

Liangyu looked once up toward the shadowed eaves where the covered qin lay silent again, kept her mouth shut so the men beside her wouldn’t see her face, and stepped over the ice to bind Warden Ge’s arm with her sash.

***

The sky didn’t brighten before the fifth bell; the black murk above the roofs simply thinned into the color of wet ash, and the fog came one last time before the morning draft could rise off the gorge.

Everything left on the mountain switchbacks came with it.

Two dozen tribunal frames—their indigo robes shredded to rags and their brass gears shrieking from a night of sleet—surged up the ramp alongside a pack of burrowing Discords, splitting into two columns to hit both the jammed iron portcullis and the two-inch diagonal breach in the eastern curtain wall at the same time.

“Keep them off the shoring timbers!” Chisa called out, her blades opening and shutting like a weaver’s loom at the base of the cracked wall, stitching iron cable-ties across the gap with one edge and severing clawing limbs with the other.

Nyx Ravenhollow planted her back against the slipped granite triangle, holding forty tons of stone in place with her white-and-gold shoulders while her right hand swept her last unbroken feather-blade across the ditch. Under the gateway arch, Cartethyia and Liangyu held the center pike-line until their boots slid in frozen slush.

On the west stair, standing on an upturned bucket, the small woman in the plum coat opened her umbrella.

The oil-paper canopy snapped wide with a crack like a musket shot, every steel-tipped rib flaring out into a blade, and she spun it once, wearily, the way someone shakes the rain off at a door. Three Discords coming over the parapet lost their front legs. She closed it, used the point to tip the nearest one back off the wall, and opened it again over the head of the young archer beside her to keep the sleet off his bowstring.

“Draw,” she told him. “I’m not doing all of them.”

***

Not everything that came up out of the fog went to the wall.

Under the arcade, the first Mei knew of it was the felt rug shivering under her knees.

She looked down. At the foot of the arcade steps, where the Ministry’s cellar drains ran under the courtyard toward the cistern, the mortar lines between the flagstones were trembling like the skin of a drum. A thin trickle of black grit ran down into a seam that hadn’t been there a minute ago.

Across the courtyard, Nyx Ravenhollow had her back pressed to the slipped granite and forty tons of wall on her shoulders, her blue optics fixed on the ramp.

“Qingyan,” Mei said quietly. “Inside. Now. Tell Grandmother Wen to bar the door and keep the children up on the benches.”

He went this time without arguing. He had felt it through the rug too; she heard his breath catch before he scrambled backward through the gap in the hall doors, his sister’s scabbard banging against the jamb. The bar dropped into its brackets behind him with a solid wooden *thunk*.

Which left Mei on the rug, with Amy’s quiet weight in her lap and the sword beside her knee.

She had torn something in the first fight. Not all of it, not the way the spar had gone through, but enough that every breath since midnight had come in under a hot, wet tightness beneath the bandage. When she reached for the hilt now, her fingers closed on it a beat slower than she wanted them to.

The flagstones at the foot of the steps bulged, cracked, and burst upward.

The thing that came out of the drain was long and pale and blind: a segmented Discord as thick as a cart-axle, with a ring of shovel-claws around a mouth that was mostly mouth. Two more heads were already shouldering up through the broken paving behind it. It didn’t go for the barred hall door. It went for the warm thing on the rug that wasn’t moving.

Mei got the sword up. She got it across the first lunge, the claws screaming down the steel in a spray of grit, and the turn closed across her middle like a fist around her stitches. She knew, with the flat, clear arithmetic of someone who had done this too many times, that she would not have the second stroke in time for the second head.

Against her ribs, the thread under her shirt went suddenly, bitterly cold.

It never got to fire.

“*No*,” said a voice at enormous volume, directly beside her ear. “Absolutely *not*. Who *raised* you?”

Gold sparks fountained out of Mei like a kicked brazier, and Abby came out of her faster than she had ever seen him move for anything, dessert included.

He was exactly as large as he always was, a cat’s worth of dense white fur with his long ears flung wide, and he put every inch of it squarely between the second head and Amy’s face.

The shovel-claws went straight through him.

For half a breath he simply wasn’t there: a burst of white fur and gold light torn in two and scattered across the arcade in sparks that smelled of hot pennies. Mei made a sound she hadn’t made all night.

Then the sparks pulled back together in midair, the way her own body did when it decided she wasn’t finished. The white fur knitted over itself in a ripple, the floating gold links at his ears and throat snapped back into their orbits with a sound like a shaken purse, and Abby was hanging in front of the Discord’s mouth again, whole, upside down, and incandescently offended.

“That,” Abby announced, “was *rude*.”

The gold rings floating a finger’s breadth clear of his throat began to spin. Faster. They spread out around him into a whirling wheel of light as wide as a cart-wheel; his ears swept back, and the Tacet Mark on his forehead lit like a struck match.

“I am a *resident*,” Abby said, his voice climbing with every clause until the clerestory windows rattled. “I have *papers*. She is *mine*, she is *injured*, that is *her daughter*, and you are standing in the doorway of a hall full of *children* with your *mouth open*!”

The wheel came down.

It went through the first Discord’s head like a hot wire through lard and through the second like a millstone. The third, still halfway out of the drain, Abby hit personally, folding his ears and dropping on it from above with the full outraged weight of a well-fed cat moving at the speed of light. The flash lit the arcade gold to the rafters. When it cleared, three pale segmented bodies lay twitching in the broken paving, and a small white creature sat on top of the largest with his ears neatly folded, breathing hard.

Abby looked down into the drain-hole and spat a fleck of something grey into it.

“And stay down there,” he told it.

Mei’s sword arm dropped onto her knee. Her hand was shaking. Under her shirt the thread had gone quiet again, still cold, lying against her ribs unspent.

“Abby,” she said, and her voice came out wrong. “Abby, it went *through* you—”

“Yes, I noticed,” Abby said with dignity. He floated back up the steps, inspected Amy’s slack face from three inches away with great seriousness, appeared to approve of it, and then turned around and pressed his whole furry forehead against Mei’s breastbone, right over the bandages, hard enough that she felt his Tacet Mark through the cloth. “You weren’t going to make the second one.”

“I would have—”

“You weren’t,” Abby said, muffled. “I live in there. I can *hear* your stitches.”

Mei’s free arm came up and closed around him, and for a moment she simply held the two of them there on the rug, her daughter and her Echo, while out at the gate something enormous crashed and the clerk-voices chanted on.

From the courtyard speakers, very belatedly, came a crackle.

“WAS THAT ABBY? DID ABBY JUST—MA, WHAT’S HAPPENING BACK THERE, MY REAR CAMERA IS FULL OF WALL—”

“Nothing!” Mei called, her face still pressed into white fur. “Abby’s got it!”

“Abby,” Abby said into her collarbone, “would like breakfast. Several.”

Then he went back inside her in a puff of gold, quick as someone ducking back through a door before anybody can thank them.

***

Out at the gate, right in the V-shaped breach where the diagonal cut in the main wall met the eastern gatehouse buttress, Lupa planted both heels in the mud and refused to give an inch.

She was black with oil from brow to chin, her left sleeve burned away to the shoulder where a Discord’s acid spit had caught the wool, and her breath came in hoarse, whistling saw-strokes through her teeth. Three tribunal puppets went down under her spear in four heartbeats, her flame licking weaker and lower against the wet stone than it had at midnight, and behind them stepped the last of the Censure Court’s heavy captains.

It was a ten-foot bailiff frame built on an industrial hoist-chassis, dragging a four-hundred-pound bronze verdict-block chained to a two-handed timber shaft.

*“SENTENCE,”* its shattered chest-box rasped, half its gears stripped and smoking, *“FINAL.”*

Lupa drove her spearhead deep into the chest of a crawling Discord at her knee, tried to wrench the ash shaft free to parry, and felt the barbed bone of the beast’s ribs lock her point down in the mud for the half-second she didn’t have.

The ten-foot bailiff raised the four-hundred-pound bronze block with both hydraulic arms and brought it down straight onto the crown of Lupa’s head with enough force to drive her through the flagstones into the cellar drains below.

Lupa didn’t duck; she braced her soot-streaked forearms above her brow to meet the iron with her own bones—

And a bar of winter-twilight light as cold and solid as a glacier’s lip slammed across the air an inch above her gauntlets.

The impact shook the entire eastern half of Xuanfang Hold.

Up on the watch gallery, the paulownia qin gave a single, violent *clang* that snapped its silk dust-cover taut, while down under the arcade, three clay tiles slid off the eaves and shattered on the steps beside Mei’s boots.

Under the breach in the wall, a six-foot ring of solid white ice cracked outward from Lupa’s heels in every direction, splitting the two-hundred-year-old granite flagstones with the report of a musket shot and freezing the mud ankle-deep around her boots. The four-hundred-pound bronze verdict-block bounced off the dark-blue thread without bending it by the breadth of a hair—and in the same instant, a razor-thin line of black frost opened diagonally across the ten-foot bailiff’s chest, from left shoulder to right hip, shearing through four inches of cast bronze and internal spindles without a sound.

The top half of the giant machine slid off the bottom and hit the ice at Lupa’s toes with a dull thud.

Standing in the middle of the six-foot circle of cracked white frost, with the freezing vapor curling up around her scorched bare arm and her hair plastered to her forehead with sleet, Lupa stared down at the halved colossus—and let out a wild, breathless bark of laughter that showed every tooth in her head, spinning her freed spear up out of the ice to look for the next one.

There wasn’t a next one.

Ten paces down the switchback ramp, the four surviving tribunal puppets stopped in the slush. Their crimson lenses flickered twice; the clerk-voices in their chests clicked shut in the middle of a syllable; and as the first thin, grey draft of true morning wind rose out of the valley gorges to tug at the edge of the Disorienting Fog, the machines turned in unison on their heels and marched silently back down into the white mist.

In the circle of frost beneath the cracked wall, Lupa was still crouched with her spear cocked back at her hip, her chest heaving and that fierce, unblinking arena grin frozen on her soot-blackened face as she stared down the empty ramp.

“Lupa,” Chisa said from the capstan.

Lupa didn’t move. Her thumb stayed white-knuckled on the ash shaft, her ears pricked forward toward the retreating clink of bronze in the fog.

Chisa lowered the heavy points of her shears to the stone, walked four steps across the cracked ice until her boot touched the edge of the frost ring, and laid her left hand—the one with the red string wound loosely around the wrist—flat against the back of Lupa’s scorched shoulder.

“Lupa,” Chisa said again.

It took two full seconds this time. Then the breath went out of Lupa’s ribs in a long, shuddering sigh; her heels sank down flat on the ice, the point of her spear dipped to the stones, and she leaned five pounds of her weight back against Chisa’s palm without looking around.

“They’re going,” Lupa said hoarsely.

“For now,” Liangyu said quietly from the gateway, leaning both hands on the pommel of her spear as she watched the fog swallow the last red lens on the switchback. “When the wind drops at dusk, they’ll come back up the road.”

***

Nobody cheered, and nobody took off their armor.

As the grey light thickened into a sunless, dripping morning beneath the still-dead rings of the Astrolabe Core, the Wardens propped their pikes against the inner wall in reach of their hands and carried the wounded inside the Ministry hall first.

In the courtyard, Nyx Ravenhollow knelt down beside the well-curb with a long, pneumatic hiss of cooling vents, and the twin blue optics beneath her crest went dark.

Under the covered arcade, the girl in Mei’s lap drew a sudden, ragged breath through her teeth, coughed once, and flinched all over as five hours of borrowed shock and bruised knuckles came home to her nervous system at once.

“Ow,” Amy whispered, her voice tiny and human again as she curled both hands against her temples and burrowed her face straight into the front of Mei’s grey shirt. “Ow, my everything. Why do my shins hurt? Nyx doesn’t even have nerves in her shins.”

“Because you kicked a thirty-foot magistrate in the kneecap, my star,” Mei murmured, her own voice stiff with cold as she wrapped both arms around Amy’s shaking shoulders and rested her chin on top of the tangled pink hair, not caring who in the courtyard saw her do it. “Come on. Inside by the stove.”

It took Qingyan and Cartethyia together to help Mei get her legs uncrossed after five hours on the cold flagstones without pulling the stitches across her middle, and then they shuffled into the warm, mugwort-smelling gloom of the branch hall like a three-legged race.

Nobody sat at the long tables; people ate wherever their knees folded.

Old Mother Pu, the herbalist from Xianling, went down the line of pallets with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and both hands lit a soft lamp-yellow, closing the shallow cuts and leaving the deep ones neatly stitched with light for the orderlies to sew properly later. She did Warden Ge’s forearm twice. The small woman in the plum coat sat on the end of a bench with her furled umbrella across her knees and her feet a foot clear of the floor, drinking tea out of a bowl she held with both hands, and when Kou Xiaoman finally sat down beside her and fell asleep on the spot, the old woman in the small body moved the bowl to her other hand so the girl’s head had somewhere to land.

By the brick hearth at the back of the hall, Lupa sat on an upturned ration box with her scorched left arm propped across her thigh, scowling fiercely at the floor while Chisa knelt beside her knee and worked cooling salve into the blistered skin above her gauntlet with slow, unhurried strokes. Five feet away against the wainscot, Cartethyia had fallen half-asleep sitting bolt upright on a grain sack with a half-eaten millet cake in her hand; Ciaccona sat cross-legged in the straw at her feet, her own split fingertips wrapped in linen rags as she carefully unlaced Cartethyia’s mud-caked boots and eased them off her heels one at a time.

“Don’t snore on the flour sacks, cara,” Ciaccona muttered without looking up, tucking Cartethyia’s hem around her ankles once the boots were off. “The quartermaster’s already having kittens about the wall.”

At the brick hob, Mei stood with one hand braced against the warm chimney-breast and ladled hot turnip-and-millet porridge out of the big iron pot into cracked bowls for Qingyan to carry down the line of benches to Liangyu’s Wardens.

Beside the big soup pot sat a smaller, heavy-bottomed iron pan pushed to the edge of the coals, where the rice Lupa had put on before the climb had cooked down hours ago.

Warden Huo, his left arm bound in a fresh sling against his chest, limped up to the stove with his tin bowl in his right hand and nodded toward the smaller pan. “Soup’s nearly down to the water, ma’am. Want me to scrape that rice pan out for the boys at the gate?”

Mei’s left hand came off the chimney-breast and rested flat on the iron lid of the smaller pan.

She didn’t make a speech, and she didn’t look toward the iron service door at the back of the passage, which had stayed latched and silent through the whole five hours of the night. Inside the pan, under the warm steam, was the thick, golden-brown crust at the bottom of the rice.

“Not that one,” Mei said simply, sliding the small pan two inches further back behind the kettle. “The crust is spoken for. There’s still a sack of flatbread under the counter—give me a second, I’ll toast them on the flat iron.”

Warden Huo looked once at her hand on the lid, understood without having to be told whose supper was waiting by the stove, and nodded quietly before stepping back with the flatbread.

Before Mei could reach down for the iron tongs, a scarred tin mug full of clear boiled water came down with a solid *clink* on the brick ledge right beside her elbow.

Lupa stood there with her salved arm tucked against her ribs, glaring at Mei’s waist.

“Drink that,” Lupa ordered. “Before you touch the tongs.”

Mei looked at the tin mug, then up at Lupa’s soot-smudged face. For a second her mouth twitched—not a big grin, just the small, quiet warmth of remembering who had told her to drink the water Lupa gave her before walking through the gearwell door.

Mei picked up the mug with both hands and drank it down to the bottom.

When the last of the flatbread had gone out to the gatehouse and the hall settled into the heavy, breathing hush of two hundred people too tired to stay awake even with an army in the fog below, Mei walked slowly back to the doorway under the arcade.

Someone had swept the broken roof-tiles off the steps and laid the thick felt saddle-blanket back down across the threshold, just inside the open doors where the heat from the brick stove reached her boots.

Mei lowered herself carefully onto the rug with her back against the cedar doorpost, and thirty seconds later Amy came shuffling over from the benches with a wool blanket dragged behind her heels, dropped down sideways against Mei’s good right hip, and went to sleep with her cheek squashed against Mei’s thigh and one hand hooked into Mei’s pocket.

Above Mei’s Tacet Mark, the air shimmered once with gold sparks as Abby poked his nose out only far enough to rest his chin on Mei’s knee beside Amy’s elbow, letting out a single, muffled sigh—*wake me up when the loud robots come back*—before tucking his ears down and going still.

Mei rested her left hand in Amy’s pink hair and her right palm over the front of her grey shirt, where the paper corner of Shijie’s letter and the cold, thin thread of light lay together over her ribs.

Through the open arch of the doorway, across the frost-cracked flagstones of the inner courtyard and past the forty-foot diagonal cut in the ancient wall, the sky was a flat, sunless grey. High up in the mist above the Ministry roof, the colossal celadon shell of the Astrolabe Core hung motionless and dark, its bronze rings still two inches out of true. No one had come down the gearwell stairs. No Terminal had chimed.

Then, from the shadowed eaves of the high watch gallery above the courtyard, where the paulownia qin lay alone beneath its silk cover, a single string sounded once in the quiet morning air.

It was very faint, a shade lower and colder than the notes that had rung at dusk, vibrating once through the wet cedar beams before the drizzle swallowed it.

Down by the gatehouse, Warden Huo lifted his head from his pike-shaft at the sound, let his shoulders drop an inch in relief, and closed his eyes again. Under the arcade, Mei listened to the low note fade across the courtyard, knowing only that the Sword Transcendent was still holding the line somewhere in the dark above them.

Chapter 1: Porridge Day

By the time the bronze vibration of the sixth-bell muster died out of the storehouse rafters, Buling already had three empty blue-and-white duck-egg shells lined up like a row of little helmets beside her elbow.

She was perched on the eighteen-inch bench gap Chisa had cleared for her the night before, both knees pulled up under her royal-blue silk cheongsam and her heels hooked over the lower stretcher. Because the morning draft off the flagstones was still sharp enough to nip at the ears, she had taken her round black *nuanmao* off the fourth peg by the door and put it back on her head, with the yellow paper talisman flipped up onto the brim so it wouldn’t dip into her bowl.

In the center of the long pine table sat a two-gallon iron pot of millet-and-rice congee, bubbling softly on its braided straw trivet and sending a thick white column of ginger-scented steam straight up toward the main tie-beam. Around the pot, Lupa had laid out enough fixings to provision a siege: a stoneware platter of halved salted duck eggs with oily orange-red yolks, a mound of two-foot fried dough sticks stacked like split cordwood, a porcelain bowl of sweet roasted pork floss, pickled mustard tubers in sesame oil, and Wen’s wide-mouthed jar of charred-chili crisp.

Buling set her porcelain spoon down, braced both palms against the hot iron ears of the congee pot through her wide sleeves, and shoved the whole pot and its straw trivet four inches toward Ciaccona’s end of the board.

*SCREE-unch.*

Over at the brick hearth, Lupa spun around with a pair of long bamboo frying-tongs in one hand and a dripping golden dough-twist in the other.

“Oi!” Lupa barked. “Take your paws off the pot, magistrate! I put that trivet in the middle so everybody can reach the ladle without climbing over the pickles!”

“Middle is bad,” Buling said, pointing one chopstick straight up at the squared pine timber overhead before lifting the lid to inspect the rice. “Look up there. Big cross-beam right over the center of the table. You put a boiling pot directly under a heavy beam, the beam presses the steam back down into the bowls. Everybody who eats from the middle gets a stiff neck before noon and loses money at cards. Four inches west, the steam goes up into the rafters, nobody’s head hurts. I don’t even charge you for that one.”

“I’ll give you a stiff neck with this ladle if you scratch my table again,” Lupa growled, marching over to drop two scorching-hot fried dough sticks straight across the top of Buling’s bowl like timber rafters. “Eat the bread. You’ve already hollowed out three salted eggs and your cheeks still look like two spoons in a handkerchief.”

“Duck eggs very small on this mountain,” Buling said placidly. “Mostly shell.”

The instant Lupa turned her back to check the coffee kettle on the hob, Buling’s bamboo chopsticks shot across the pine plank with the silent, deadly grace of a kingfisher hitting a pond. With a single pinch of the tips, she lifted the bright orange yolk clean out of the half-egg sitting on Ciaccona’s saucer, leaving the empty white ring behind, and popped the yolk whole into her mouth.

Across the board, Ciaccona—who was sitting with her red hair in a sleep-tangled knot and her chin propped heavily on one palm while she waited for caffeine—watched the theft through one half-open green eye.

“I saw that, Consultant,” Ciaccona rasped. “That yolk was my reason for getting out of bed.”

“White part better for the voice,” Buling said around the yolk, not looking remotely sorry. “Yolk makes the throat sticky before lunch. I save your high notes for you.”

Beside Ciaccona, Cartethyia was already combed and wide awake, though her crystal laurel sat slightly crooked over her left brow where Old Man Zhao’s goose had bumped it by the grain-bin at dawn. She was eating her congee slowly, carefully spooning a heap of sweet brown pork floss and two cubes of sour pickled radish onto the surface so each bite had both crunch and sugar. When Buling’s elbow nudged the salt-jar, Cartethyia reached across the board with two fingers and rescued the tiny brown-paper jiangshi she had folded at supper, moving it three inches back toward the teapot so a drop of soy sauce on the pine wouldn’t soak into its paper boots.

“He stood guard all night by the salt,” Cartethyia told Buling brightly. “Not a single mouse came up the table leg.”

“Good worker,” Buling agreed, tapping the little paper hat brim with her pinky so it bobbed once. “Better than the wardens at the east gate, they snore until my ears ring.”

Down at the door-end of the bench, Chisa already had her dark wool coat buttoned to the throat and her pale blue silk tie tucked neatly inside her collar so the mountain wind wouldn’t catch it on the stairs. She ate her plain congee without fixings, and when Buling swung her black-bound shins under the table, Chisa quietly hooked her own boot-heel around the iron pivot of her giant shears and slid them six inches along the floorboard out of the way of Buling’s ankle.

Across from her, Yangyang lifted the tin coffee pot off the side trivet with her right hand, poured a mug of hot, tar-black coffee for Ciaccona before the bard had to un-prop her chin, and slid the saucer of pickled mustard tubers down the plank toward Chisa’s elbow.

“Bless your feathers, little bird,” Ciaccona muttered, wrapping both hands around the hot tin mug and breathing in the steam like church incense. “If I ever write a hymn, you’re getting the first verse.”

“The coffee is Lupa’s,” Yangyang said mildly, blowing on her spoon. “I only carried the pot.”

At the hearth-end of the table, Iuno sat with her heavy wool coat draped over her shoulders and her silver-blue hair pinned cleanly into its double coil. Her porcelain teacup sat to the right of her bowl; to the left, wedged against the warm straw rim of the congee trivet so the heat wouldn’t leave it, sat a thick stoneware mug of milk-coffee with a skin of nutmeg on top.

Lupa scraped the last fried dough stick out of the pan, dumped it onto the platter, and jerked her chin toward the closed pine door of the back bedroom.

“Thought you ordered the patient to stay flat until you brought her mug in,” Lupa said to Iuno.

Iuno picked up her spoon and stirred her congee once. “I did.”

Down by the door, Chisa set her empty bowl down on the wood and tilted her head a fraction toward the timber partition.

“She is putting her boots on,” Chisa said.

***

The wooden latch of the back bedroom flew up with a *clack* that rattled the hanging soup-ladles by the flue, and the door swung wide open against the wall.

Mei stepped out into the main room.

She had her dark jacket pulled on over her linen belly-wrap, her hair tied back in a crooked tail at her nape, and her heavy leather road-boots on her feet—except while the right boot was laced up to the top hook, the left boot wasn’t laced at all. Its leather tongue flopped out over her instep like a dog’s ear, and the two long rawhide strings trailed behind her heel across the floorboards because she hadn’t been able to bend past her knees to reach them.

She looked as though someone had lit a four-wick oil lantern inside her ribcage.

Before Lupa could open her mouth to bark about the stitches or Iuno could reach for the waiting mug, Mei planted both palms high on her chest, looked up at the smoke-darkened rafters, and announced to the entire storehouse:

“Good morning! Nobody tell me what day it is!”

Six spoons paused halfway between bowl and mouth.

“I’m retiring the question!” Mei declared, shuffling forward with a careful, flat-backed glide so she wouldn’t step on her own left laces. “New household policy! Checked my bandages myself in the mirror, totally dry, zero pulling, and I decided calendars are bad for the spleen! Could be Monday, could be Thursday, could be the middle of July! Don’t know, don’t care! Every day is porridge day!”

At the stove, Lupa lowered her iron tongs an inch and squinted at Iuno. “Did she roll off the pallet onto her head?”

“The wall is on her side of the pallet,” Iuno said evenly, sliding the stoneware mug of milk-coffee two inches across the pine plank. “And she is five minutes early.”

Mei came around the hearth-end of the table.

There were three full feet of empty pine bench between Iuno and Yangyang, as well as the wide oak stool by the ash-lip with the sheepskin cushion still sitting on it from the night before.

As Mei passed behind Iuno’s back, she slowed down, reached out with her right hand, and gave the left side of Iuno’s wool coat—directly over the deep inner breast pocket—two gentle, fond, unmistakable little pats, the exact way a farmer pats a prize squash before loading it onto the market cart.

Then Mei lowered herself carefully onto the bench two inches to Iuno’s right, scooted sideways until her hip was wedged solid against Iuno’s thigh, and hooked her chin briefly over Iuno’s good shoulder to beam straight down into Iuno’s teacup.

“Morning, Iuiu,” Mei said, her voice bright enough to melt frost off the shutters. “Did you get the salted eggs? Look at your hair, the back coil’s super straight today. Didn’t even miss a pin.”

Down at the foot of the table, Chisa turned her face toward the hearth-end of the board.

“Your left boot is unlaced,” Chisa said in her plain, level voice, “and you are speaking two notes higher than you did at supper.”

“That’s just my morning register!” Mei said happily, reaching for her mug of milk-coffee with both hands. “Very rested! Great air in that back room!”

Across the pine table, Buling’s chopsticks had come to a complete halt over her congee bowl.

Under the upturned yellow talisman on her hat brim, Buling’s bright red eyes flicked once to Mei’s chin resting on Iuno’s shoulder, down to the exact spot on Iuno’s left wool lapel that Mei’s palm had just patted, and back up to the tight, longsuffering line of Iuno’s jaw.

Without saying a word, Buling slid her left hand into her wide blue silk sleeve, drew out her Terminal, and propped it upright against the stoneware crock of pickled mustard tubers. Her thumb began to move across the dark glass in rapid, silent bursts while her right hand went back to her spoon.

Mei didn’t notice the Terminal at all. She took a long gulp of hot milk-coffee, got a faint white moustache of milk-foam across her upper lip, and picked up a foot-long strip of fried dough from the platter.

While she blew on the hot dough to cool it, she started to hum.

It wasn’t an absentminded hum under the breath. It was a bouncy, swinging, two-beat march that resonated right through the pine table, complete with a bright little rising turn at the end of the fourth bar.

Across the board, Ciaccona slowly lowered her tin coffee mug. Both of her eyebrows went up toward her hairline, and a slow, wicked grin spread from one corner of her mouth to the other.

“Well, well,” Ciaccona drawled, propping both elbows on the table and resting her chin on her interlaced fingers. “Correct me if my ears are full of mountain fog, cara, because I haven’t been home since the autumn floods—what tune is our fearless leader humming into her cruller?”

Cartethyia tilted her head, listening earnestly as Mei hummed the second bar. “Oh! That is *Two Sparrows in the Campanile*! Uncle Cosimo plays it on the button-accordion whenever the notary comes up from the harbor with the red wax!”

“The courtyard processional,” Ciaccona agreed with immense satisfaction. “Traditional Ragunnesi air. Played on two silver trumpets when both families sign the house-charter on the basilica steps. Usually in F, though Mei is currently taking it up to G-sharp out of sheer enthusiasm.”

Mei stopped blowing on the fried dough. Her golden eyes went wide.

“What? No it isn’t!” Mei protested at top volume. “That’s not a church song! That’s—that’s the Jinzhou canal boat song! About the guy with the flat-bottomed turnip barge!”

“The turnip barge song is in a minor key and ends with the mule falling in the lock,” Ciaccona said cheerfully. “You just did the bell-tower trill twice.”

“Turnip barges have bells!” Mei insisted, and immediately pivoted on the bench toward Iuno, tearing the crispest, golden-brown middle section out of her fried dough stick with both hands and holding it two inches from Iuno’s mouth while gazing at her with shining golden eyes. “Here, Iuiu, I got the crunchy center part before Buling saw it. Eat it while it’s hot, the ends are all chewy.”

Beside Mei, Yangyang’s porcelain spoon paused over her bowl. The small cerulean feathers along the left side of her collar lifted once against her jaw; she lowered her chin half an inch, took a very small, silent sip of porridge, and kept her gaze fixed diligently on a knot in the pine plank.

Iuno looked at the piece of fried dough hovering in front of her nose. Then she looked across the steam at Ciaccona’s delighted smirk, and at Buling’s right thumb tapping away behind the pickle crock at the speed of a woodpecker on an oak tree.

With impeccable calm, Iuno reached up with her right hand, took the piece of fried dough out of Mei’s fingers, and laid it neatly on the rim of her own saucer.

“Eat your porridge, Mei,” Iuno said. “Before it sets like mortar.”

The scullery door at the back of the room kicked open with a thump, letting in a swirl of freezing courtyard mist, and Amy stamped across the threshold in Lupa’s oversized sheepskin vest. Her cheeks were bright pink from the frost, an eight-inch iron spanner from Nyx’s ankle-strut was sticking out of her pocket, and she was carrying an armful of split cedar kindling against her chest.

“The ice on the cistern trough is thick enough to skate on,” Amy called out, dumping the kindling into the wood-box by the stove with a clatter and blowing on her cold knuckles as she walked toward the table. “And Old Man Zhao’s goose just chased a spear-corporal all the way down to the lime-kilns because he didn’t salute the—”

She didn’t even get one knee over the bench before Mei leaned back toward the brick hearth and called out in a loud, breezily casual voice:

“Hey, Lupa! Random kitchen question! Hypothetically—not for today, just for general culinary science—if somebody wanted to bake a cake in an iron camp-oven, how long does a really big one take? Like, say, three tiers with the white sugar paste on the outside, and maybe two little marzipan frogs on the top tier?”

Lupa turned around from the hob with the iron kettle in her hand, her wolf ears swiveling forward until their tips nearly touched.

“In that brick firebox?” Lupa squinted at her. “Half a day if the flour isn’t damp and somebody else beats the eggs. Why the hell do you want marzipan frogs? You’ve got two gallons of congee sitting right in front of your nose.”

“No reason!” Mei said brightly, waving her spoon in the air. “Just curious about oven capacity! You never know when a random weekday needs a pastry! Oh, and Carte—in Ragunna, when people do an important official thing with a witness and a stamp on a piece of paper, do they wear ties, or capes, or what? Just for comparative history!”

Cartethyia set her spoon down neatly beside her bowl and sat up straight, taking the inquiry with total knightly gravity.

“In Egla Town, a formal covenant requires the blue sash of the parish!” Cartethyia declared earnestly, placing her right hand over her breastbone to demonstrate the angle. “Both parties stand before the magistrate with their right boots upon a swept hearth-stone, and they hold a single loaf of salted barley bread between their palms while the clerk reads the boundaries of the olive grove! If either drops the loaf before the bell finishes striking, the goats remain with the mother’s brother!”

“Ooh, holding a loaf of bread,” Mei said, nodding enthusiastically and bumping Iuno’s knee under the table. “Hear that, Iuiu? Two feet flat on the floor! Way more dignified than hopping!”

Iuno’s hand came down on her porcelain teacup, setting it into its saucer with a single, sharp *clink*.

Mei didn’t even blink. She turned back to the center of the board, pointed her spoon straight at Buling’s round black hat, and announced at amphitheatre volume:

“And Buling! Full transparency! Iuno put me in charge of yard security after muster, so I’m sitting on the sunny step with my blanket and auditing your sleeves every time you walk past the woodpile! Up to four buns in the cuff is normal groceries, five buns means we tell Lupa to put her gauntlets on!”

***

On the far side of the pine table, Amy stood frozen with one boot on the floor and one knee on the bench, her cold hands still half-raised from the kindling box.

Slowly, Amy’s eyes moved from Mei—who was grinning into her congee bowl like a woman who had just swallowed the sun—to Iuno, whose eyes had closed for a long, silent count of three, and finally to the stoneware crock of pickled mustard tubers, where Buling’s thumb was flying across the glass of her Terminal so fast the little brass bell at her hip was jiggling on its cord.

Amy dropped onto the bench and swung her right boot straight out under the table.

*THWACK.*

“Ow!” Mei yelped, grabbing her shin under the board with one hand while her spoon clattered against her rim. “Amymy! That was my good shin! Why’d you kick me?!”

“Ma,” Amy said through her teeth, her voice pitched into a low, strangled hiss that vibrated across the pickled radishes. “Stop talking. Eat your rice. Put the whole piece of fried dough in your mouth at once and chew for ten minutes.”

“I’m just making breakfast conversation!” Mei protested innocently, rubbing her shin through her trousers and turning her sunny, wide-open gaze directly onto her daughter. “Hey, while you’re sitting down—if Lupa ever *did* bake a big three-tier sponge cake on a totally ordinary day of the week, do you like lemon curd in the middle or the mashed chestnut stuff? Because I know Auntie’s face goes numb if there’s ginger in the syrup, so we’d definitely want to keep the spice out of the top layer—”

“THE GOOSE!” Amy shouted across the board, loud enough to make the skin on the congee pot ripple. “Did everybody hear what I just said about the goose?! Old Man Zhao’s goose is out in the lane right now! It has a corporal’s canvas gaiter in its bill and it’s shaking it like a rat! Somebody should go look at it! Everybody should go look at it!”

Nobody at the table moved a muscle.

“Sir Honk only confiscates gaiters when the buttons are left undone on duty,” Cartethyia said reassuringly, picking up a cube of pickled radish with her chopsticks. “He is very strict about garrison turnout.”

“And about the barley loaf,” Mei continued happily to Cartethyia, completely immune to poultry emergencies, “does it have to be barley, or if you’re in a river city like Mengzhou, can you just use two sesame buns so nobody drops crumbs on the paper?”

Across the pine, Buling’s thumb hit the bottom corner of her Terminal screen with a sharp, satisfied *tink* of her fingernail against the glass.

Amy couldn’t stop herself.

The Terminal was propped at a neat slant against the stoneware pickle crock three feet from her nose, and reading upside-down writing was wired into Amy’s nervous system deeper than breathing. Her eyes locked onto the glowing lines of text. Her jaw dropped.

Before her brain could catch her tongue, Amy lunged halfway across the table to grab the Terminal—Buling smoothly lifted it four inches out of reach with one hand while scooping congee with the other—and Amy read the draft straight off the glass in a voice of pure, mortified horror:

“‘*Draft: Page four-thirteen, Boss Lady and Head Clerk Domestic Status Mega-Thread!*’” Amy’s voice went up half an octave as Buling tilted the screen away from her fingers. “‘*Update from BigThunder88: Roommate faction dead in a ditch. Boss lady just walked out to breakfast with one boot unlaced humming Two Sparrows in the Campanile, patted the clerk’s left coat pocket twice, tried to hand-feed her fried dough, and asked the Char Siew Dog how fast she can bake a three-tier sponge cake with two sugar frogs on top. Ceremony truthers eating good, hard launch before the thaw—*’”

Down the bench, Ciaccona dropped her forehead onto both arms and laughed so hard her elbow knocked her tin coffee mug spinning across the wood until Yangyang caught it neatly by the handle.

At the hearth, Lupa pointed her iron soup-ladle straight at Buling’s hat brim across the rising steam: “Stop calling me a dog on your magic slate, magistrate, or I’m boiling your shoelaces with the turnips!”

“Wait, what’s a roommate faction?” Mei asked, leaning forward over her mug with genuine interest. “Why are they in a ditch? Did the road wash out?”

Amy let out a long, hollow groan that seemed to come all the way up from the soles of her boots. She slid down off the bench until her chin hit the pine table between the vinegar cruet and her empty bowl, and dragged the fleece collar of Lupa’s sheepskin vest up over the top of her pink head.

“I’m going to go live in Nyx,” Amy told the underside of the bread-board. “I’m climbing into the chest cavity and welding the maintenance hatch shut from the inside. Nobody knock on my hull until we’re in another province.”

At the head of the bench, Iuno hadn’t moved an inch.

*Thirty years of statecraft, darling,* the High Priestess murmured inside her head, fanning herself languidly with a phantom wrist, *and we are undone before the second cup of tea by two marzipan amphibians.*

*Be quiet,* Iuno told her.

Out loud, Iuno lifted her porcelain teacup with her right hand, took a slow, measured sip of hot Earl Grey, and looked across the table at Buling’s upturned yellow talisman.

“First,” Iuno said in her coolest basilica voice, “if you type the phrase *hard launch* at this table again while I am eating, I am turning that water-butt forty degrees toward the stove myself. Second, nobody in this household has ordered a cake. And third, Mei, if you do not drink your coffee before the fat sets on the milk, I am giving your fried dough to Sir Honk.”

“Drinking it!” Mei chirped, lifting the stoneware mug with both hands and smiling into the nutmeg steam so hard her cheeks bunched up beneath her eyes.

Ten minutes later, when Lupa was scraping the bottom crust of the congee pot with an iron spoon and Cartethyia was earnestly asking Ciaccona whether olive-grove covenants applied to turnip patches, Buling hopped down off the bench gap and strolled over to the forty-gallon stoneware water-butt in the northeast corner to rinse her bamboo chopsticks in the dipper basin.

Amy materialized at her elbow like a pink-haired spectre in a sheepskin vest.

Buling glanced sideways under the brim of her *nuanmao*. “Wah. Walk very quiet, ah. Don’t stand on my left side, you block the light from the shutter.”

“Delete it,” Amy hissed, keeping her back square to the table so her voice wouldn’t carry past the clatter of Lupa stacking the iron grates.

Buling flicked the water droplets off her chopsticks and slid them into the silk loop at her waist. “Delete what? Field notes? That one is primary source material.”

“I have seven silver pieces in my left boot-lining,” Amy whispered fiercely, digging into the fleece pocket of her vest and pulling out the eight-inch spanner and a small spool of insulated copper magnet-wire. “And yesterday when you sat on the log I saw the hairline crack on your Terminal’s back receiver plate. Give me twenty minutes with a soldering iron and this copper coil and I’ll wind you a high-gain loop antenna that can catch a relay ping from the bottom of a well. Plus you can have my share of the fried dough every morning until Friday.”

Buling crossed both arms over the front of her royal-blue cheongsam, her red eyes narrowing in automatic market appraisal.

“Seven silvers,” Buling said. “Do you know how many views an exclusive like that gets when the board comes back online? My account gets gilded four times before lunch. Minimum fifteen silvers, the antenna job, the fried dough until Sunday, and when the glasses lady comes back at noon you tell her I already swept the east parapet.”

“I’m not lying to Ruoyun, she checks the dust with her fingernail!” Amy’s whisper cracked up half a note; she caught herself, dropped her chin into her collar, and glanced once over her shoulder toward the table—where Mei was currently sitting with her shoulder pressed warm against Iuno’s ribs, trying to coax Iuno into taking the last roasted chestnut off her spoon while Iuno pretended to inspect a chip in the salt-cellar.

When Amy turned back to the water-butt, the midnight-radio swagger had gone out of her shoulders. Both of her hands were twisted tight in the fleece hem of Lupa’s vest.

“Just—don’t put it on the board yet,” Amy said, very low and plain. “Okay? Auntie’s been awake since the water-cart went past worrying about the road, and Ma’s still got pack-thread in her stomach, and she’s been trying really hard all week not to ask for things. Just let them do it without four hundred pages of strangers yelling about it first.”

Buling looked at her for two beats.

She didn’t haggle over the fifteen silvers, and she didn’t make a face.

She reached into her wide blue sleeve, pulled out the Terminal, and woke the glass with her thumb. With two quick, flat taps, she opened the drafts folder, hit the small trash-basket icon beside *[Page 413]*, and tapped *Delete*.

She tilted the dark screen toward Amy for the space of a breath so Amy could see the empty queue, then slipped the Terminal back into her cuff.

“That thread is full of idiots anyway,” Buling said plainly. “Spoil the luck of the house if you shout about a thing before the ink is on the paper.”

Amy blinked at her, her fingers uncurling from the fleece vest. “Oh. Thanks, Buling. I—”

“Antenna job by Thursday,” Buling cut in briskly, reaching past Amy’s elbow to pluck the cold half-piece of fried dough straight out of the side pocket of Amy’s vest where Amy had stashed it before sitting down. “Otherwise I tell the whole forum you keep a wooden frog in your bed. Go wipe your chin, you got sesame oil on your collar.”

***

By the quarter-bell, the morning sun had climbed clear of the storehouse eaves outside, throwing a bright, cold square of yellow light across the threshold and the brass boar’s-head boot-scraper.

Chisa stood by the open timber door with her giant iron shears balanced across her shoulder, waiting calmly while Yangyang tucked a warm oilpaper packet of two salted eggs into Chisa’s coat pocket for the walk down to the gatehouse.

Iuno stepped away from the table, buttoning her heavy wool coat to the throat with her right hand. As she reached the threshold, her eyes met Chisa’s for a quiet beat; Iuno’s palm rested once against the outside of her left breast pocket—where the folded foolscap list sat flat beside the small velvet box—and Chisa gave a single, slight nod before stepping out onto the frost-rimmed flagstones.

*Do try not to trip on the stairs, darling,* the High Priestess murmured as the cold air hit the crystal at her shoulder. *It would ruin the suspense.*

Before Iuno could cross the sill behind Chisa, Mei shuffled rapidly down the length of the pine table—still in one laced boot and one flapping, unlaced boot, holding a grey wool blanket around her shoulders with one elbow and her refilled mug of milk-coffee in her hand.

“Wait, Iuiu!” Mei called out, catching her at the doorpost in front of the entire room.

At the table, Amy froze with her teacup halfway to her mouth, her shoulders hunching up toward her ears as she braced for impact.

Mei stopped right in front of Iuno on the mat. For half a second she looked as if she were about to announce something enormous—and then, instead, she simply reached up with her free hand and tucked a single wind-blown strand of silver-blue hair back behind Iuno’s ear, her voice dropping out of its amphitheatre bounce into the quiet, ordinary register she used when the lamp was out.

“Walk on the inside of the lane by the cooper’s shed,” Mei said softly. “The valley-gutter drips over the third step and the ice hides under the shadow.”

Iuno looked at her in the bright doorway, the dry line of her mouth softening by a hair.

“Lace your left boot before you sit down, sweetheart,” Iuno murmured, “or ask Amy to tie it so you don’t pull your middle.”

For two whole heartbeats, nobody in the room made a sound.

Then Mei rose half an inch onto the toes of her boots, tapped the side of her own nose twice with her forefinger like a stage conspirator in a penny farce, and whispered at a volume that echoed off the inside of the forty-gallon water-butt:

“And zero rush on the calendar! Take all the time you need down there! I’m not even thinking about boxes!”

At the pine table, Amy dropped her face squarely into her drawn-up knees with a muffled shriek.

Iuno closed her eyes for one full second, opened them, said, “Goodbye, Mei,” and stepped out onto the stone landing, pulling the heavy timber door shut behind her before Ciaccona could start whistling the bell-tower trill across the porridge pot.

Two minutes later, Mei was parked back out on the sunny stone step outside the scullery door, bundled from chin to ankle in two grey wool blankets with her left boot freshly laced by a deeply suffering Amy, who was sitting on the lower flagstone beside her mother’s shin with her arms wrapped around her knees and her face buried to the eyebrows in Lupa’s sheepskin vest.

Down in the woodyard, Buling strolled past the unsplit iron-elm butt toward the eastern breach where Ruoyun was waiting with the quicklime buckets. Her hands were tucked comfortably into the wide royal-blue cuffs of her cheongsam—both sleeves noticeably rounder and lumpier than they had been before breakfast, with the flaky golden tip of a fried dough stick poking half an inch out of the left wrist-hem and the little brass bell at her hip going *tink, tink* with every step.

On the stone step, Mei took a happy sip of hot milk-coffee and squinted over the rim of the mug at the passing blue sleeves.

“One,” Mei said out loud to the morning sun. “Two. Three. And the crust sticking out of the left cuff makes three and a half. Under the limit, Amymy! The sky’s staying up today!”

Beside her knee, Amy didn’t lift her nose out of the fleece. “Ma, if you say one more word before lunch, I’m telling Lupa you jumped off the step.”

Mei grinned into her mug, hooked her chin over the rough wool of her blanket, and watched the blue cheongsam turn the corner of the lane.

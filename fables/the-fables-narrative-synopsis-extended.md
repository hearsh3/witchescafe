# THE FABLES
### a narrative synopsis, in the order the story happens

---

## I. THE WORLD AS IT STANDS AT THE OPENING

A woman with golden eyes and no memory washed up half-drowned on the shore of Ragunna, in the country of Rinascita, and the woman who pulled her out of the surf had done it on purpose.

That is the debt the whole story is built on, and almost nobody in it knows the full shape of it yet.

Her name — the one she answers to — is Mei. She has no past she can reach. She has a sword called *Haultir*, the Sword of Virtue, that hums amber in her palm; she has a habit of putting her body between other people and whatever is coming; and she has a pack. There is Lupa, a gladiator out of the pits of Septimont who was never taught to read by a school and was taught by a fellow fighter, and who killed her own corrupted packmates, Mya and Avidius, to walk out of the arena alive. There is Cartethyia, the Blessed Maiden of Rinascita, who spent twenty years shut in a dark church because of the mark on her, and who made cloth dolls in that dark so she would not forget what people looked like. There is Ciaccona, a bard taught by her father until she was twelve and self-taught after that by theft and observation, who has been playing three notes of a four-note melody for as long as anyone has known her and has never played the fourth. There is Chisa, who is face-blind and reads people instead as frequencies, weights and invisible connections, and who spent twenty years locked in a single looping summer in Honami until her friend Sumika burned her own life away to let her out. And there is Iuno — the High Priestess of Septimont, retired, who governed a sovereign city-state for thirty years, who is missing her left forearm and wears an iridescent crystalline stump in its place, and who carries a second voice behind her sternum that she calls the High Priestess and argues with constantly.

The six of them broke a theocracy. They unsealed a drowned city and quieted sixty-three streets of grief inside a hole in the weather. They woke a silenced god called the Imperator, which spent the last of its strength healing a dying knight and then dissolved, telling its people: *From this day forth, you are free. Lead well.* They won a foreign nation's highest games under a team name that meant *weather*. And then they got on a ship called the *Gilded Swallow* and sailed north out of Ragunna's harbour at summer's end, and the ship went off every chart in the world for nineteen days.

What they found at the end of those nineteen days was a city inside a giant — Lahai-Roi, built underground on the world of Solaris beneath a sun its own engineers had agreed should rise, called Helios, with bioluminescent steppes and Solistrees that breathe on a schedule and weather that happens because ducts move air. They found a school, Startorch Academy, whose enrolment records showed all six of them already admitted three days before they arrived. And they found, eventually, what Mei had actually come north for without knowing it.

Mei had a daughter. Adopted, thirteen years of her, in a cabin with a door Mei painted blue and wisterias Mei planted in frozen ground because she believed they would grow. The girl's name in the Academy's sealed casualty file is Aemeath. The girl calls herself Amy. Fifteen years before the story opens, Amy was the synchronisation candidate a board chairman named Chase personally approved; she pushed past her limits because she wanted to be the hero; she burned out, and the Spacetrek Collective filed her dead — catastrophic Overclock during emergency Exostrider synchronisation — held a memorial, and forwarded the report to three nations. She was not dead. She was awake, and alone, inside the Nihility, the void beyond a buried gate, and she stayed there ten years holding a cosmic ending at bay, singing three notes to herself because they were the last thing she had that was her mother's.

By the time this synopsis begins, all of that has just finished happening. Mei's pack, plus Hiyuki — the last Miko of Flaming Sakura, who lost her whole city, Honami, to the Lament thirty years ago and has been collecting other people's ungrantable wishes ever since — woke a kneeling god-mech called Baldur, drove it through the Stridergate into the void, fought a Threnodian of ending called Aleph-1 that had been stitched together out of ten thousand consciousnesses lost in the dark, and pulled Amy out. Mei caught her. They said *I love you* to each other for the first time.

And two things went unresolved that night, and both of them are the engine of everything that follows.

The first: down in the collapsing centre of that fight, Mei was cornered by a version of herself who taunted her that her family was a story she was telling herself, and Mei swung at her and hit a cryo-unit door instead — and inside the cryo-unit was not a hero. Inside was a girl of twenty-three with dark hair, ID reading PENROSE-512: PILOT, NAME: LILITH, and a journal recording four and a half thousand ordinary days: school, tuition, soccer fields, exams, a friend comforting her crying in a field, *I rejected another girl today.... why?*, *I lost a friend today* followed by a blank page, watching everyone she knew get married and buy houses and become people, and then *Applied to the Penrose-512 program. Maybe travelling will help me,* and then *I started writing a story about a girl who wakes up with no memories… I think I'm jealous of her.* Beside the unit lay a pile of bodies wearing Mei's face, tagged Traveler, Ariane, Stellaron Hunter, Commander, Doctor, Hero. The voice that had commanded Mei across the Etheric Sea and refused a stranded woman's pleas to come home and told her coldly to finish the mission had never been a government. It had been the girl in the ice, who could not figure out how to grow up, and who had written herself an adventure rather than live an afternoon. Mei was invited to lie down among the dolls. Instead she opened the unit, woke her own author, and told her she was enough — and then wrote in the journal, in handwriting that matched the entries above it, *I'm still learning how to live.* Lilith went back into the frost with the journal in her hands and one instruction: *Just keep leaping.* Her hands, Mei noticed, were warm.

The second: far away, on an evacuation truck, a young woman named Nivora hummed three notes and smiled a borrowed smile and said, *Now show me what happens next, little Fool.* She had been at that school as a student, as the friend of a girl named Sigrika. She was the Grand Architect of the Fractsidus. And she was already several moves ahead.

## II. WHAT THE PEOPLE LEFT BEHIND DID WITH THE WAITING

Four seas south, none of this was visible. What Rinascita had was a ship that vanished for nineteen days.

Augusta, Ephor of Septimont, ran the search from the front. When the Senate proposed a marble memorial she refused it and ordered a plain bench on the harbour instead — *wide enough for nine* — and reserved Berth Nine for a ship that was not coming in, and paid the mooring fees out of her own purse with a note reading *Hold the berth.* When the Senate moved for a vigil she blocked it by making tea in front of them, four minutes, Earl Grey, the way Iuno had taught her at the gatehouse, and gave the city a doctrine it kept: someone is only gone when the people who love them stop setting the kettle. On the nineteenth day a relay came through signed only *I.* — *Detoured. All five intact, plus acquisitions. There is a city down here, and a school, and the school appears to have enrolled us.* Augusta answered publicly with *Leave extended* and privately with nothing at all.

Augusta's own field manifests unspoken feeling as curling copper wire, and for thirty years she had swept the results into drawers. Her lieutenant Galbrena — whose given name is Angel, which Augusta had never once said out loud in public — had been quietly collecting them, and soldered three decades of them into a copper trellis beside the rosebush. *You emptied my drawers.* *I filed your correspondence. You write a great deal, for a quiet woman.* Galbrena planted tulip bulbs and called it thirty years, for a tulip. Augusta fought a twenty-four-year-old challenger named Priya for her own seat under the old law, won in eleven minutes on a hip that has never healed right, and hired her on the spot.

In Ragunna, grief went to market. A grassroots chapel formed to venerate Cartethyia as ascended, and it kept forming even after she was confirmed alive; a black market opened in relics looted from her childhood home, including the cloth dolls she had made in the dark. Cantarella Fisalia, thirty-sixth Duchess of a house that had spent four centuries ritually poisoning its own matriarchs to keep its secrets, ordered her protégée Carlotta Montelli to buy back every doll — *They're hers. She'll want them counted when she gets back* — and Carlotta, following the money, found the auction house's silent backer: a syndicate called the Reliquary Combine, which had bought relics cheap during the silence on *enshrinement futures*, contracts that pay out only if the Council formally canonises Cartethyia, and which was funding the pro-enshrinement religious movement to force the vote. *They've securitized homesickness.*

The Petition of Enshrinement was championed sincerely by Sister Benedetta of the harbour chapel, who lost her brother at sea when she was nine and grieved him by setting his place at dinner for years, and who genuinely believed canonisation was mercy. Cantarella took tea with her beside a deliberate gap in her camellia hedge, kept open for a gardener she was expecting, and refused her: *I know how to end suffering. You're proposing to end waiting. Different pharmacology entirely.* Then she told her who was paying for her movement. At the Vigil banquet an attempt was made on the life of Sera, the anti-Petition swing vote, by poisoned wine; Cantarella caught it by scent, because that is her family's expertise, and saved her with the Fisalia Antidote Trust, which is what her house's poison knowledge has been converted into. And at the hearing, as her line's hereditary Devil's Advocate, Cantarella argued against sainthood by reading out an itemised ledger of everything Cartethyia still owed and was owed across the whole coastline — weeding debts, a Guild board seat, an unpaid coffee tab — and concluded that a saint owes nothing, and *this girl is in debt to half the coastline and telephones on Thursdays.* The Petition died 311 to 62.

What Ragunna built instead was the Office of the Expected, a registry where any citizen may file any awaited person as ATTESA — awaited — a file closable only by the person walking in and signing it themselves. Cantarella nominated Benedetta to run it, and Benedetta opened a twenty-one-year-cold file for her brother. Cantarella opened one naming Carlotta, with herself as the expecter and the observance listed as *tea, Thursdays.*

Meanwhile the Adventurers' Guild processed the whole season as case files. A talking fish named Gerald negotiated the Bay Accords. A sentient teddy bear named Palla applied for membership because she had decided to become a grown-up and wanted wages to buy her own stuffing, and was hired into child-recovery work, her notes reading *the bear stayed until she stopped crying.* A sixteen-year-old named Alice joined from a black-sand village that is now on official maps because a lieutenant kept a promise, and asked specifically for the Northern Desk, because a third big sister had once given her back her father for one afternoon. The Guild robbed House Ordalia's auction of a hundred and forty lots during a staged blackout and left a dried fish on the podium, and returned the dolls to the Fisalia Trust, and Laevatain went on tending the Sword of the Martyr Mya at a cliffside grave among the viburnum, monthly, and reported that she was getting there. Faster lately.

Then, at winter's end, the relay came again, and it was not asking to be found. *There is a girl on the far side of a gate, and a window of ten minutes, and a god that has agreed to stand up. Costs attached.* Iuno told Augusta plainly that she intended to spend everything she had ever saved. Augusta signed the writs at her kitchen table with jam still on the page, and told her rose bush that they had found a girl in the dark up there and were about to do something stupid about it, and the trellis put out a new leaf. The Guild emptied its Northern Desk reserve and opened co-signing to anyone who wanted in, and a harbourmaster and a café owner and a fish-market steward and an entire village bought passage for one additional passenger. *Don't you dare die running toward someone else,* Augusta told her. *You come back on your own feet, and you bring me the whole set.*

On the night the god stood, an aurora came over Rinascita that had no business being there, and everyone who mattered was awake for it and sent the same unspoken thing north. Cantarella watered the gap in her hedge and told it, *Come home, and let me tell you how proud I am.* And far to the north on a frost road, Hiyuki walked the aftermath gathering everyone's wishes where they had crystallised, and laid them as foundation stones for a shrine.

The relays confirmed it: *They walked out. All of them. Plus one.* The harbourmaster flew an arrival pennant over an empty berth. Benedetta cut a second stamp, TORNATA — *returned* — and it sat unused, because none of them came home to sign.

They still had not, years later, when Rinascita was three years old and busy proving it did not need them. A steward named Ottavio Manzato proposed adding the six liberators to the civic calendar with a feast day and a subscription-funded monument, and Carlotta — twenty-six now, running the largest trading house on the western continent — gutted it on procedure and arithmetic: the feast date fell in peak settlement season, and two-thirds of the monument money came from three donors, one of whom was Manzato's brother-in-law, who held the marble concession. She had watched this pattern before. *It also began with real gratitude for a real rescue, and it ended with a puppet in the reliquary and a fee schedule for miracles.* Her amendments passed: the feast became a working market holiday, harbour open, only the schools closed; the monument money went to rebuilding the Ninth District school; the honorary citizenship clause for the Priestess of Septimont stood untouched. That evening Carlotta ordered two coffees, drank one, and let the second go cold while she talked to it about handholds — the kettle rule, learned from Septimont by letter, in a bard's looping hand that calls her Lottie.

In Ragunna's Bureau of Records, a former oracle-transcriber named Anselmo Ferri registered fishing boats at the ninth window and admitted to a colleague that he missed being certain — *Certainty is warm, Vittoria. It's the warmest thing there is* — while keeping four hundred pages of a private daily journal in a drawer and beginning to suspect it was the first genuine oracle he had ever produced, being one man witnessing on his own authority. In Egla Town, Cartethyia's uncle Cosimo, who had carried lilies weekly to what he believed was his niece's grave for twenty years until a living girl with a laurel opened the door from inside, agreed to the pilgrim hostel and insisted only that the field where she had flown kites stay open and free, marked nothing but *she was a child here* — and it became the most-visited unticketed site in the town. At Porto Veno, Cantarella walked the year's fourth romantic applicant into the emptied Fisalia poison room, explained that the secrecy had been a tourniquet and you take a tourniquet off when there is finally a surgeon, and sent her instead into the Registry Commission to read old documents out loud in daylight, under a rewritten four-word house oath: *Read it. Say it.*

And on the first Feast of the Ninth, the Ninth District monument was unveiled: six lifesize bronzes built as a children's climbing frame, each with a functional handhold — a chakram wheel that turns, a greatsword built as a slide because a four-word instruction arrived from the north telling the sculptor to put a slide on it, and the Rover's outstretched catching hand already polished mirror-bright by use. Children climbed it and retold the story wrong in all the details and right in the ending: *And the god said you are free, and then everybody had to go to SCHOOL.* Anselmo went home and finally titled his four hundred pages, in the old sacred script — *THE DAYS. Witnessed by one clerk, on his own authority, unsure. Volume One* — and signed off the way the whole Republic now signs off: *Lead well. (You. Yes, you. Tuesdays.)*

The five ATTESA files stayed on the shelf. Nobody had come to sign them.

## III. SPRING BACK: THE WEEKS AFTER

Up north, nobody was thinking about berths. They were leaving a fried chicken place called Lucky Bird, which had somehow stayed open through the end of the world, and Amy stopped in the parking lot because Helios was going down gold over the underground steppes and she had spent ten years without a horizon.

Everything in the weeks that followed was small on purpose, and all of it was load-bearing.

Riding home through the caverns, the Observatory fired its beams into the crystal ceiling and made artificial shooting stars, and everyone made a wish except Amy, who discovered she did not have one. Ciaccona wished for more time. Chisa said her wish was the same as last time, she had just meant it differently. Amy said she had already got hers and did not know what came after that, and Mei told her that not knowing what to wish for means you're here.

At the dormitory Amy refused a bunk, refused the top bunk next door, and claimed the middle of Mei and Iuno's too-small bed — and then stalled in the bathroom, standing fully clothed in a dry tub, because she had just watched her mother kiss someone. Mei came in and washed her daughter's hair while she sat there in her clothes, humming three notes without noticing she was doing it. Iuno waited outside and reheated her tea four times, and understood exactly why the girl was taking so long, and let her take it.

When Amy bolted for the roof, Iuno pressed the key to her own private thinking-place into Mei's hand and did not follow. *She needs her mother. Not her mother's girlfriend. Not yet.*

On the roof under a magnolia and a false sky, Amy performed the savior for as long as she could hold it, and Mei pulled her into her lap the way she had when Amy was small and sang the old cabin song — *When it's cold, come back. Don't linger in the wind* — and the performance came apart. Amy told her what the void had actually been: she had tried to count the days, and the numbers failed, and then the marks failed, and then everything failed, and all that was left was singing three notes because they were the last thing she had that was her mother's. Mei said *I failed you,* and Amy refused it outright: *You taught me to sing. You painted the door blue. You planted wisterias in frozen ground because you believed they'd grow. I survived because of what you gave me. I came home.* And when Mei said she was proud of her, that undid Amy worse than the crying had, because nobody had ever said that — she had been called prodigy, pilot, star, savior, and never that.

Amy explained what she was made of now. Her body was a shell assembled out of borrowed traits and data from the researchers and explorers who had walked into the void seeking truth and never come back. She was not certain she grew hair. She was not certain she got hungry. She had decided that being real enough to eat terrible instant noodles and annoy her mother's girlfriend was enough. Then the two of them went back downstairs specifically to annoy Iuno, and Amy took the middle of the bed forever, and Iuno pulled the blanket over her shoulder and said *scoot over,* and the three of them slept.

The next morning the pack ate breakfast in a garden to dodge the student paparazzi, and Amy said she had forgotten food could taste like anything, and Cartethyia — who came back from her own dark and could taste nothing for weeks until Cantarella made her a lemon cake — crushed her in a hug: *I made dolls for twenty years so I'd remember what people looked like and you were alone in the dark for ten and you forgot how to taste.* Mei moved to intervene and Iuno stopped her. *Not this time. She needs someone who understands the shape of that particular dark.*

That was the shape of the whole season. Amy touched sage and Soliskin and got rained on and sheltered in a Royan camp where an elder told her that walking and seeing are different things. Mei admitted she had lived in Lahai-Roi for months and never once explored it, because there had always been a crisis. Amy made her promise Rinascita — a real sun, just once — and Mei swore it.

And in the common room, Amy asked how her mother had met these people, and got the truth: Mei washed up half-drowned on Ragunna's shore, and Iuno pulled her out in order to have her die in her place at the Appointed Hour, and manipulated her for months toward it, and then Mei jumped into the Dark Tide after her rather than let her die alone, and they both came out, and Iuno came out short one arm. Amy interrogated her mother's partner in front of everyone, established that Iuno had had every opportunity to exploit Mei's grief and had not, and then set her a trap — she had hacked Iuno's thirty-year Septimont records and asked her about an expense line for replacing velvet throne cushions. Iuno admitted she had done it out of spite. *I just wanted to see if she'd lie. She didn't. We're good.* The verdict was delivered anyway, for the record: *Okay, you're not completely terrible. But if she hurts you again, I'm destroying you.*

Professor Mornye — in a hoverchair now, stripped of the augmentations she used to have, with a Soliskin called Buddy in her collar — called them all in to warn them. Baldur was fully dormant; he had spent everything getting through the gate. The Spacetrek Collective board, chaired by a man named Chase, would want the pilots and the girl catalogued as proprietary assets, and Amy as a living battery. President Lucilla had already drafted the lie: the Exostrider woke on its own dormant directives, the pilots were passenger ballast, none of them is the true Resonator. The pack volunteered enthusiastically to play stupid. Mornye had the raw telemetry and refused to show them, because the waveform proved they had been consciously piloting, and that proof was an invitation to a laboratory.

Asked what the void had actually been like, Amy said: *Like holding your breath underwater. But the water is made of static, and you have to hold your breath for ten years.* It had been crowded. The dead researchers in there had forgotten why they were crying and then faded, and she had sung the same three notes for ten years as an anchor to keep them from going out entirely. Mornye stated flatly that the neural cost of that should have killed her.

Then the pack broke into the arcade, and Amy beat Iuno at *Space Fantasy Katya VI* while Iuno blamed the joystick, and they cashed seven thousand tickets for a giant stuffed finch which Amy installed in the middle of Mei and Iuno's bed.

Out on the plains that night, while the others invented a competitive ditch-jumping sport with judges, three quieter things happened. Mei thanked Iuno for staying, and Iuno said *I stayed because the alternative was unacceptable* — and it was the first moon she had looked at in thirty years without calculating what it cost. Amy led Mei to the glacier where Baldur kneels, and Iuno followed without being asked, and they found Mornye already there, alone, at a stone marker she had placed herself and visited for ten years under the pretext of data collection, with a dried wisteria stem laid on it. She had never reassigned Amy's seat in her lecture hall. And elsewhere on the plains, the rest of the pack worked out that their Academy enrolments had been processed before any of them had ever heard of Lahai-Roi — which required Startorch-level access — and arrived at the only possible answer: Amy, from inside the reactor, with ten years and nothing else to do, had identified her mother's frequency on arrival and built a support network for her out of forged admissions, so that Mei would not be alone. Ciaccona decided not to tell Mei. *She'll just cry again.* And Ciaccona, who had played three notes of a four-note phrase for as long as anyone had known her, played the fourth.

Mornye ended the night by examining Iuno's chakram and discovering it was not willpower at all but an inherited sympathetic frequency passed down the Septimont priesthood, oscillating at fourteen cycles a minute — a harmonic cousin to Baldur's own resting rhythm, which meant it was probably a fragment of something godlike and older than Septimont. Iuno agreed to let her study it, which was its own kind of surrender. And then Mornye mentioned, mildly, that she had logged three anomalous frequency signatures that matched nothing in her catalogue, and looked directly at Mei, and said *One of them is sitting here* — and then dropped it, promising to investigate only when Mei was ready.

The tribunal came the next morning. Iuno dressed in silver-blue with her crystal stump deliberately exposed, assigned everyone a demeanour, and walked them in to lie to the most powerful board in the city.

It did not survive contact. Chase recognised Amy on sight — *Aemeath* — because he was the man who had personally approved her synchronisation fifteen years earlier and had carried her death ever since. Dr. Kelan Voss pressed on the fact that pulling a specific consciousness out of the void required intent, not autonomous machinery, and Mei stopped lying: *I asked. I asked Baldur to help me find my daughter. And he did.* Baldur was never dormant. He had just been kneeling for a very long time. Hiyuki corroborated it out of her own unauthorised walk through the void, which she had made to grieve her city.

Then the Stridergate logs came out. It had been held open fourteen minutes past authorisation, on Lucilla's personal clearance codes, without board approval, to save one girl. Lucilla did not deny it. *I made the call I believed was correct. And I would make it again.* Chase moved to put her on administrative leave and hand interim authority to Vice-Director Hammond, and the pack responded by lying serially and badly — Lupa, Chisa, Cartethyia and Ciaccona each claiming personally to have held the gate — until Voss declared the testimony worthless. Amy stood and took the blame herself, confessing she had pushed past her limits fifteen years ago because she wanted to be the hero. Mei stood and lied outright, claiming she was the primary synchronist who had overridden an abort order.

And Iuno contradicted her in front of the entire board. *Mei is lying. To protect President Lucilla.* — and then, to Mei directly, in public: *You're falling on your sword. Again. And I love you for it. But I'm not letting you do it this time.* Behind her sternum, the reason: *Stopping her from destroying herself. Like I should have done thirty years ago.*

Then Iuno detonated the room on purpose. *We were never students to begin with. Conduct an investigation into our enrolments. You will find that they were falsified.* And Amy confessed that she had done it, from inside the Reactor Drive, with stolen administrator access, three days before any of them arrived, so her mother wouldn't be alone. Voss moved to press criminal fraud charges — and Lucilla played the card she had been holding, which was that Aemeath is legally dead. Sealed casualty records, a memorial, reports filed to three nations. Charging her meant admitting the Collective had covered up that a conscious girl was trapped alive in the dark for a decade. Amy, who has read her own casualty report, put it plainly: she was confessing to fraud committed by a legally dead person. Hiyuki added that she alone held the human-context data needed to read the telemetry, and would publish independently. Mornye offered to publish under her own academic licence.

The board folded. The inquiry into Lucilla was tabled and she kept her authority. The six forged enrolments were retroactively legitimised as an Emergency Synchronist Recruitment Program, which made them legal residents. Amy's death record was amended to a medical error and she was promised a real identity inside the week. *Welcome to Lahai-Roi,* Chase said. *Officially.* Amy cried, because she was finally allowed to exist on paper.

And then she asked whether she could take Mei's surname, since she had never had one — and Mei, flustered, started to say *well* while looking directly at Iuno, and the entire pack heard a marriage proposal happen by accident. Cartethyia guessed the name. Lupa howled. Lucilla appeared in the doorway and said *Congratulations* and offered to officiate, being legally certified in three nations. Mornye offered to calculate the venue acoustics. Hiyuki offered ceremonial frost arrangements, and noted it was traditional. Mei and Iuno fled.

Alone in a corridor, out of breath, Iuno admitted it had not been a joke: *I actually considered it. Amy Caecilia. That sounds… right.* And Mei said she thought she wouldn't hate it, and Iuno said neither would she, and they agreed to figure it out tomorrow.

Tomorrow took a while.

The pack teased them without mercy — Lynae opened a betting pool, Cartethyia witnessed the receipt in crayon — and Mei went quiet twice at dinner, and Amy eventually blocked the bathroom door and made her say why. Mei admitted she wanted to marry Iuno and could not, because she still did not know who she had been before. There was a promise she had made to someone from her erased past and then deleted along with the rest, and the wreckage of it had hurt people, and she could never repair it because she could not remember making it. She would not give Iuno a name until she knew what was attached to it. Amy named the flaw in that reasoning exactly: *That's the same math Lilith used. The same math that put you on a one-way ship. You're still finishing a mission nobody assigned you.*

Iuno had been standing in the bathroom with the water off for several minutes. *I heard. Some of it. Enough.* She was not troubled by Mei having a frightening past — she had spent thirty years curating which parts of herself people were permitted to see — but she refused Mei's right to decide unilaterally what Iuno could bear. Mei offered a compromise: no marriage, but she would walk with her until then. Iuno refused that too. *I didn't learn to stop settling for scraps so I could turn around and hand you one.* She would walk beside her, daily, by choice — the constancy was the hard part, not the paperwork. *I don't deserve you being this patient with me.* *Neither do I deserve you staying, after everything I did to you.*

Amy asked, for legal purposes, whether they were engaged, not engaged, or complicated. Iuno gave her *until then. Whatever then turns out to be,* and Amy climbed into the bed and announced she was not sitting this one out.

The next morning Mei admitted she was still doing it, and Iuno confirmed she was — that requiring the past first was jumping backward instead of forward, and that Mei was terrified of staying, and that Iuno knew because she had used the same tactic and it always makes sense. She asked whether Mei intended to keep running in place or let herself be caught. Mei asked her to keep calling it every time, and Iuno promised she would. Then Mei kissed her and said *I love you,* and Iuno froze and asked her to say it again, and admitted she had been ready since Mei smiled at her on a bridge. Amy, who had heard everything through the door, pressed for a date and got told: *We're doing it. Just not on your timeline.* She set a reminder for one year, after which she would intervene.

Then the two of them danced in the kitchen with no music and the kettle going cold, and Amy caught them and proposed adding *learns to dance* to the list of things her mother was relearning about herself.

Later, alone, Mei drove Iuno out past Atuja to a thermal spring heated by Baldur's reactor venting through bedrock — she had researched the venting patterns specifically to justify the trip — and in the water, holding to a vow of no more convenient omissions, Iuno said the worst of it out loud. She had not revived Mei to save her. She had revived her as a replacement corpse. She called herself Mei's architect and her executioner and refused the word savior, and said it had taken Mei throwing herself into the abyss for her to understand that a perfectly controlled board meant nothing without Mei on it. Mei did not argue. She redirected instead: she talked about the frostland cabin, and the weeks she had spent catatonic there, unable to feed herself, and the woman who had fed her and bathed her and held her together. She insisted that was also Iuno, and threatened, being a mother now, to punish her if she forgot it.

Afterward there were noodles at midnight with the whole pack crammed into the kitchenette, and Mei finally worked out what she had been afraid of dragging into a future with Iuno. *It's this. All of this.* And Iuno said it was a heavy burden, but she supposed she could help carry it.

The rest of that stretch was almost entirely nothing happening, which was the point. There was a football argument that became an actual match in which every rule about weapons, Echoes and Forte was broken inside a minute, and Chisa took a Lupa strike to the chest and said *I am the wall,* and Lupa immediately broke character afterward to ask about her ribs and offer to fetch Luuk, and got mocked for it by the entire pack, and Chisa finished her with flat sincerity — *Thank you, Senpai. It's comforting* — and Lupa fled the colour of a ripe tomato. There was a televised Arsenal–Riccioli match watched at three in the morning that went to penalties, in which Chisa provided statistical commentary because she had read the sports almanac three thousand times during her twenty-year loop.

And there was the money. Amy, Lynae and Chisa were broke, because forged students draw no hazard pay — and then it came out that Cartethyia's guardian Cantarella wires her a stipend for proper nutritional sustenance lest she wither into a peasant, and Lupa is paid in gold bars by Ephor Augusta, and Iuno kept her personal fortune when she stepped down and could buy the residential wing in cash, and Mei has an Arbiter's Trust established by Carlotta and the Council of Stewards after Carnevale, with weekly dividend reports from a Guild clerk named Zani, which she had never once opened. Amy was appalled to learn she had been eating cafeteria food while her mother held a sovereign wealth fund. Chisa, who reads people as frequencies, diagnosed Iuno's habit of paying for everything as a psychological need to establish financial and domestic dominance, and Iuno accepted the diagnosis approvingly and took everyone to dinner.

At the restaurant, Amy ate real food for the first time since the void and started crying into wagyu, and Mei reached for her, and Cartethyia got there first and silently slid her asparagus onto the girl's plate — one veteran of a dark room to another. Amy deflected. *I'm not crying. The truffle butter is just aggressive.*

Twice that day, though, Amy pushed her mother off. Once over a scraped elbow — *I can clean my own scraped elbow. Stop hovering* — and once when Mei tried to cut her steak: *I'm nineteen, not nine. I drove a god-mech through the void. I can cut my own wagyu.* Lupa backed her up. Iuno redirected Mei's attention to her own bleeding shin, which she had not noticed, and said the thing Mei needed: *She needs room. Let her be annoyed with you. It's a luxury she hasn't had in a decade.*

Walking home, Mei apologised and said she was trying to remember that Amy had grown up while she wasn't looking, and Amy told her the truth underneath the sharpness: *if you cut my meat for me, I'm never going to figure out how to be a person again. Otherwise I just feel like a ghost wearing a skin suit.* They struck a deal — no more cutting, but Mei retained the right to say *I told you so.* She earned it within the hour, because Amy had eaten a flaming dessert cake competitively against Lynae and won, and spent the night on the couch in genuine agony and completely delighted about it, because a stomach ache is proof of a functioning body. Iuno brewed peppermint tea for the sick teenagers and denied being a softie. *I am ensuring they do not vomit on my boots.* And she gave Mei the instruction that governs everything after: *You cannot pay off a decade of absence with truffle butter. You make it up to her by letting her have a stomach ache now, and being here when she wakes up tomorrow.*

## IV. THE PEOPLE WHO PUT THEIR BURDENS DOWN

Two people came out of that rescue carrying things nobody had asked them to carry, and both of them, in that season, set them down.

Hiyuki went first. Luuk examined her after two unshielded crossings of the Nihility and pronounced her perfectly stable, and for the first time in a hundred years the verdict felt like a description of her rather than of his instruments. She had been the last Miko of the Shrine of Flaming Sakura for a century, sole survivor of her line, and she had spent that century carrying ten thousand crystallised regrets from Honami behind her sternum, because a shrine's business is other people's ungrantable wishes and there had been nobody else to hold them.

Three small things gave her away that week. In Mornye's recovery room, asked how to keep the Collective's laboratories off the children, she supplied the alibi instantly — *Tell them I went to grieve* — and recognised that the most impenetrable mask she owned was a true sentence. In a corridor, Amy hugged her, and Hiyuki remembered kneeling inside the Threnodian's chest and asking the office's oldest question, *What is your wish?*, three times, until the girl stopped saying *I want her to be safe* and said *I want to go home* — and then building her a road out of every wish Hiyuki had never been able to grant. And in a boardroom, in the middle of an accidental engagement, Hiyuki made a joke. It was unprecedented.

The wishes came back out of the void as physical crystals, so she built somewhere to put them. Lucilla gave her a disused terrace above the Dimmr Plains road, facing the glacier where the god kneels, with a note reading *No incense. The ventilation committee will revolt.* Hiyuki burned incense. Her childhood shrine had been a fortress built around its keeper; this one she built as an open dry-stone lattice with no security and no treasury and the bell hung low enough for children. Mornye inspected it and called it inefficient, and Hiyuki agreed that this was the point — *A thing kept by one person is a hostage. A thing kept by everyone is a culture.* The plaque reads: *For everyone who walked into the dark and chose, anyway, to keep reaching. Someone remembers you. Ring the bell if you wish to be one of the someones.* One unrecognised woman came at dawn with the expression of someone practising being glad to see someone, and left no trace at all.

Chisa arrived on the third morning with a broom and swept without being asked. Over tea she asked whether the magnolia by Honami's printers' quarter had survived, and about a pudding stand by the east gate her mother used to take her to on festival days — and Hiyuki, who had spent a year cataloguing that city's dead and believed herself to be its last copy, learned that she was not. Chisa cut a length off her own red wrist-string and tied it to one of the crystals for someone she did not name. *Who tends it when you're gone?* *Whoever is standing nearest.* *Then it will be tended.* Hiyuki reread her line's ancient vow that night and decided it had never required solitude, only the office. Keep the vow. Lose the reason.

Amy turned up with a block of wood and demanded to be taught to carve penguins, on the grounds that penguins are just herons that gave up. She was cheerfully awful at it. It was the only art in Hiyuki's life in which imperfection had ever been permitted. Amy asked what ringing the bell costs, and got told that was between the bell and her. Amy asked why her, a stranger — and Hiyuki confessed she had recognised the whole pattern: orphan, prodigy, self-sacrificer. *I watched a theatre's worth of your story before I ever met you. I wrote it.* *It's a stupid manual.* *I'm writing a better one.*

Amy also asked what wish Hiyuki had spent on the puppet in the void, because she had felt something like summer go past. Hiyuki said only: *Popcorn.*

At sixteen, in the summer of the long drought, she had been an unvowed girl at the shrine under Miko Gyokuro, and there had been a boy of seventeen named Sōta who ran his family's popcorn stall and called her *yuki-jō* — snow miss. At the festival fireworks, crushed against him at the sea wall, she heard his heartbeat under the noise and wished, silently, *I wish this could last forever.* Within the year the Suzu rang unbidden in her hands and she took the vows, and the official record calls that devotion. She corrects it: it was fear, wearing devotion's coat. Sōta lived an entirely ordinary full life — married a girl from the fish market, ran a shop, had an awning fire, nothing serious — and came to the shrine once a year to make a wish at the box and never once told her what it was. He died at eighty-one and she performed his rites herself and did not falter. She kept that wish unspent for ninety years, the private stock. She spent it in the void, on a puppet with a stolen girl inside her, to freeze four seconds solid so a pair of scissors could reach the strings. Of ten thousand wishes granted, it was the only one she was ever glad to give.

Then she invited Mei to the finished shrine at dusk with two bowls of red bean pudding and told her the rest. Inside the void there had been a walled theatre with seven curated reels of Mei's life playing in it, and a proposition — a god's office, offered — and an Architect who could counterfeit any face but could not counterfeit *between*, the thing that happens in the space where two voices meet. Hiyuki froze the theatre to force honesty out of herself and found she was weeping, and understood that the catastrophe of her life had never been the rain. *It was the absence of hands.* Mei's bad mornings were the evidence that hands existed. *I refused a god's job offer on the strength of your bad mornings, Mei. I have come to return the light. It is yours.*

Mei told her, in exchange, about the girl behind the last door — the author who couldn't figure out how to grow up and wrote a braver woman into being, and about the horror of having been the mask a coward wore, and about where she had landed on it: that an author is only a reader who got so lonely she started writing letters to whoever she hoped would answer. Then she asked: *So whose story do you read next?*

Hiyuki went to Lucilla's office to ask about the atlas that kept appearing on her table, and Lucilla got there first. *I asked you to die.* She had ordered a friend of thirty years into the void because with the barriers failing she was the best weapon left, and she had been carrying that since. Hiyuki did not do what she has always done, which is absorb a confession into the reliquary. She invented something else on the spot: *Then it is heard. Not carried.* Lucilla laughed — twice, that year, which was a record — and then finally claimed an offer she had left standing for thirty years, *Let me hold some of them,* and Hiyuki gave her Sōta: the drought, the sea wall, the wish, the funeral, the four seconds. Lucilla said *Thank you for his name.*

The atlas had been placed there deliberately two weeks earlier, next to a leave-of-absence form already stamped. Hiyuki asked for open leave — not as a Miko, but as a woman with a bell and bad handicrafts who wanted to see things she was not responsible for. Lucilla left the duration blank and wrote *Until the weather turns,* and set one condition: *When it turns cold, come back.* She sent her off with sour candy, a demand for badly carved souvenir birds, and one half of a twinned mirror. She did not come to the platform, which was her own currency; the mirror in Hiyuki's sleeve warmed once as the lift went up.

The pack saw her off instead. Lupa inspected her boots and pronounced them acceptable, barely. Cartethyia drew her a map of Ragunna annotated GOAT (avoid)(or don't). Ciaccona gave a tuning fork, Chisa unlabelled magnolia tea, Mornye a frequency chart annotated *stable. — M.* Amy gave her a home-dubbed cassette of the whole song the three notes came from, with instructions to play it somewhere with a real sky, because it had never been under one. Mei hugged her and said *Order for two,* and *Same time next spring.* Iuno gave her a nod and one sentence: *The dark room. Tell me someday what you decided it contains.*

Hiyuki walked out onto the surface and felt unadministered weather on her face for the first time in a hundred years, and started the eleven-day walk east toward a Honami that was resettling, where the east-gate pudding stand would reopen at the summer festival with commemorative spoons, and where the great sakura her sister died under had put out one green branch. She intended to queue and order for two and give the second bowl to whoever was standing nearest. At a riverside inn she played Amy's cassette by an open window under a real sky and hummed along, and noted that she was, for the first time in a century, expected home.

The other burden came down on a laundry terrace, overnight, between two people who had been circling it for a week.

The pack had got drunk that evening because they had learned where they were going — east, and nobody said the word *Jinzhou* out loud all night. Eight of them passed out in the common room. Chisa made tea, washed up, and went to the roof, and Lupa — who had spent a week deliberately making noise on the stairs so as to be heard coming — found her there waiting.

Lupa had noticed a pattern: Chisa's hand goes to her throat when a room gets full. *Where's the collar.* *In a tin.* Which was true, prepared, and not an answer.

So Chisa told it. She was collared at sixteen in a storm at a Monitoring Center by a man whose face had gone — *That was the year they went* — and her mother signed the release despite the printed line saying the signature was a formality. The band chimed twice at six every morning, and every wearer learns the same posture. Then Honami's Sphere sealed, and the band reported to nothing for twenty years. Inside, there was an old woman named Sumika who collected stopped clocks, and for two months Chisa woke her every morning with the collar's chime — and the sealed city ate Chisa's memory nightly, so that what she has left of Sumika is her handwriting, her arithmetic, and the fact that something was said each morning about the noise, but not the words.

Chisa's stated reason for keeping the collar was the three-line wave punched by hand into the inside of it, proof that someone had marked it on purpose. Pressed, she gave the real one: the band still works, and its six a.m. hum is the last sound from that city she can still make happen. Lupa asked to hear it. Chisa refused flatly — *Someday, probably. When there's a reason that isn't curiosity* — and Lupa accepted it without argument.

Then Lupa paid her own toll. She was recruited at seven: one room, a curtain, her parents at a table, and twice a year for four years the same two questions — would she go, would she fight. Her family had been counted out in the second Tide. The sum her House paid for her is a matter of public record filed as Schedule B, and she has never read it, because she is afraid of knowing what she cost. Chisa reframed it for her: a padded number is itself a wave, evidence of some official's conscience, not a valuation of a child. Lupa had once had a clerk trace her birth parents and got a seventeen-year-old fund transfer and *no forwarding address* — and Chisa pointed out, without cruelty, that a person who wanted to stay lost would have used the same office to arrange it.

It also emerged that Lupa was the one who wrote the reform. Blanks disallowed on the correspondence line, the fifteen-year stipend instead of a lump sum so nobody sponsors a child on impulse, and the new third question every recruiter must now ask a seven-year-old: *Who will you write to?* And a mandatory free communal dinner at the eighth bell for every registered fighter in Septimont, attendance recorded, which the touts bet on and call *the wolf's soup* — authored, according to the trainer who taped her hands for ten years and watched her eat alone every night of it, precisely so that somebody would notice when a fighter stopped coming. She has never once attended a session in person. Her mail goes to an address listed as *north* and comes back unopened.

Chisa also revealed, that night, what she actually sees. Not faces — she has never seen anyone's face, including Lupa's, which is on soda cans and noodle packets and bathhouse walls across the city — but red threads between people, that move and slacken with intent. In Honami they had gone bad and pulled the Left Behind into loops. In the Nihility they had been sunk into a girl and going down, and Chisa cut those. Lupa has a thread, behind her, at spear height, which Chisa has deliberately never traced. And Chisa refuses to look at where her own ends, having already spent twenty years living in a city whose ending she knew.

Then she told Lupa about the barbershop. Six weeks earlier, in the Fourth Ward, she had recognised a grey-and-gold-haired scarred woman — the only true thing inside an illusion built once to make Lupa do something unforgivable, who had cut the lie apart in a single stroke. Chisa had asked her outright for the shears, the tool that ends things, and been refused: cutting fate means cutting through your own heart to reach it. The woman had left on one word. *Eventually.* Chisa went back twice: once to read a name in the appointment book that she forgot within nine steps of the door, and once to learn that the woman had left a tip unspent, wrapped in tissue in a drawer, which Chisa perceives as a gold thread. She has looked at where it goes. She is still deciding what she saw.

Lupa, who has been ready to die since she was seven and has never known the how or the when, admitted she envied that, and would take the foreknowledge off Chisa if she could, and called wanting it a disgusting thing to want. Chisa told her *You're the wall.* Lupa said she would move the wall anyway, and struck her tail on the concrete hard enough to be heard four floors down without waking anybody, because the sleeping pack long ago filed that sound as safe.

The last thing was the reason for the tin. Eleven weeks earlier a parcel had come up from Septimont, held back by a harbourmaster's note reading WAIT: six sealed jars of Huanglong tea and a card that said, in translation, *Whenever the road brings you east, the kettle is on.* Lupa read it aloud. Iuno said *ah,* quietly. Cartethyia proposed leaving immediately and then tomorrow. Ciaccona tuned approvingly. Amy pushed to go. Mei went silent and was already walking the road in her head.

And Lupa confessed that for three weeks and four days she had known — via her own information network — that Chisa's mother was in the city, and had been suppressing her instinct to formally count her into the pack. The woman is sixty-three. She kept a routine for twenty years inside a sealed city, waited eight months for an exit permit, sailed twenty days alone without telling her daughter, arrived, settled independently, and has been quietly waiting for Chisa to have time. She eats at a noodle stand in the Third Ward on Thursdays, orders two bowls, eats one and lets the other cool, and pays for both, and when asked once who the second was for said *My daughter.* On the Thursdays Chisa doesn't come, she copies out a list of seven names over and over in a notebook, practising the local alphabet. There is a resident capybara at that stand that sits exclusively at her feet. And one afternoon a well-dressed older man watched the stand for forty minutes and then boarded a tram in deliberately the wrong direction the instant Chisa arrived, and the mother — without looking up — put her hand flat on the cloth-wrapped box she carries everywhere.

Chisa wanted unmediated time with her before the pack's collective kindness absorbed the whole thing. She has had four Thursdays. Lupa agreed to hold, on her own pack law — *we hunt together* — and promised Chisa would hear any news from her first. Asked whether the father knew, Chisa answered only: *She came alone.* Then Lupa calculated aloud the distance to Kuresaki, two days, three with the ferry at Hitosaki, which was as close as she was going to come to saying anything, and Chisa mentioned that a letter sits in the tin beside the collar, drafted four times, three of them burned.

Meanwhile, in Kuresaki, a smith named Ojima Ren had spent the season taking regulators off children rather than putting them on, under a new law they call the unfastening. He keeps a box of the questions the children ask — a nine-year-old named Aki asked whether it says goodbye when it comes off — and he gave her the cut ribbon against reclamation rules. Since a newspaper photograph of a girl's throat went around eighteen months ago he has answered seven letters from former wearers asking whether the hand-punched wave inside the band means anything, and his answer is always the same: *Yes. On purpose.* There is an eighth letter in his shop, unsent, addressed to a girl in a city inside a giant where the sun is a machine. And a commission came through guild favours for a custom Kuresaki-pattern regulator, for a client who is asking — and Ren built it in nine days and took an unauthorised liberty, reversing the sealing mechanism so that the wearer herself can open it, and stamped the docket with a pattern name he invented on the spot: *Kuresaki, second.*

At dawn on the terrace, Lupa and Chisa took down the dry laundry together. Chisa cut a loose thread off Lupa's hem with her mother's floral-handled scissors — the same pair that once cut a tower into six hells and a god-worm down its length and a door in the middle of nothing. Then she counted the laundry by feel, the way she always does, and said *Ten.*

There were nine of them. Lupa opened her mouth to correct her, understood, and said *Ten* back. Neither of them said the tenth name out loud.

## V. FAR FROM THE ROAD

### До Свидания — until the meeting

There was a girl the Fractsidus made on purpose, and she had seven days left, and she had audited the figure twice.

Her name was Denia. She woke in a white room in a Spacetrek Collective medical ward under a ceiling of forty-eight tiles, and by the second day she had named all forty-eight of them after desserts, because a ceiling you can name is a ceiling you can live under. Mille-Feuille. Pavlova. Kasutera. Medovik. Snow. She woke smiling, and the machines had known for an hour, and the smile was for the machines.

What she had instead of a childhood was a fabrication date, a deployment date, and a projected obsolescence, and the projection had been optimistic. She had clearance to read the file herself, for being a payload. Inside her chest was a collar: an engineered countdown keyed to her own frequency, fastened at the moment of adoption — at the hour she accepted the gift, a hand had closed around her frequency and kept a copy of the key. The gift itself had gone home to its true vessel through a hole in the world. The hand remained.

The Collective wanted her data, because the collar was a mechanism their instruments had circled for years. She refused them everything, because refusal was the only property she owned. On the third day the supervisor with silver temples pressed the intercom and laid out, patiently, everything her cooperation could prevent. *There will be others. Others like you.* And Denia, addressing a ceiling tile rather than the glass, said: *Then be kinder to them than you are being to me.*

She had one possession she counted as real, and it was a dream she had been having her whole life: a lake, reeds, and a woman weeping on the far shore who called her name. She had decided it was a memory, because a girl needs collateral — proof that before the fabrication date there had been something worth weeping across a lake for.

Then the Collective sent her friend in as leverage.

Sigrika stood in the corridor rehearsing the document twice and skipped paragraph three both times. Denia knew before she sat down. *You skipped paragraph three* — the paragraph that promised it wouldn't hurt. *…It's the only part I couldn't say with a straight face.* *It's the only lie in the document.* Denia noted twenty-three missed calls; Sigrika corrected her to twenty-four, one that didn't connect; Denia said it had connected. *I was asleep. I heard it anyway. It sounded like you.*

Sigrika confessed her function — *I'm the — the leverage* — and offered to be refused, so she could go back and report the leverage defective. Denia made a counter-offer instead. *Thursday is my birthday.* One full ordinary day at the Academy: classes, the courtyard, cafeteria custard. And after that the Collective could have everything, the mapping and the marrow and whatever the needles wanted. *Deal.* At the door Sigrika asked why Thursday, and Denia said *It's my birthday. Keep up.*

She had picked Thursday because Sigrika's timetable was free.

For three days she was perfectly cooperative — needles, arrays, the resonance mapper — and she made jokes into the recording microphones on purpose, so that the archive would have something kind in it, for later, for whoever had to listen to it. A young technician had to leave the room, and she covered for him. On the second evening the supervisor stated the two load-bearing facts of her life: *It completes on frequency failure. Wherever you are.* And: *The discharge signature matches the residue at the Stridergate seam.* It wanted to go home. *Everything answers to something,* Denia said, and named the last four tiles, and slept honestly for the first time.

Sigrika was a Heliodic Six candidate who could read Royan runes, held a 98% attendance record, and had been told *We're so proud. Keep going* by her parents and *Remarkable aptitude. Generational talent* by Professor Halvard, and had built her entire life on working hard enough to be worth keeping. Her signature habit, when an exam question was impossible, was to write out a formula from an unrelated field and label it *attempted* — because an attempt entered in the wrong formula was worth infinitely more than an empty box. She carried a laboratory seal everywhere. She kept a bird journal: finches, a heron with a hairpin, and one entry reading *New species! White bird!* underlined twice. Denia never wrote in it. Denia drew caricatures in it — a finch with six legs, a professor with a beak — and said documentation was for people who didn't trust their memories.

Thursday, then. The Collective assigned an armed escort named Leva, who signed the form as *Leva* and struck through every other field, and kept a fixed forty metres. *Recovery outing.* *Routine.* *Nothing about you is routine.*

And Denia spent the day saying goodbye to everyone, engineered so that nobody would recognise it until afterward.

She took her old fourth-row aisle seat at Halvard's public lecture on Thermodynamic Ethics, fell asleep at minute six, and woke at minute nineteen with a crack against the seat-back that turned forty heads. *Miss Denia graces us.* *Present.* A boy called out *dream well!* and she answered *Wake me when it matters,* and filed the joke as delivered: a goodbye, wearing a bit.

She went up to the third-floor study room where Petra, Ilyos and Wen were failing Advanced Royan Syntax, and gave them her standard advice — *Drop it. Take Intro to Napping. Running away makes everything so much easier* — and then quizzed them until noon and fixed Ilyos's declensions, and while nobody was watching slid three semesters of her own annotated notes into the bottom of Petra's bag, where they would be found in a week and understood in a month. *Next year we're all taking Napping. I'll tutor.*

She stopped at the candy dish outside Dr. Luuk Herssen's door, which is refilled every Tuesday and lately every day, took two sour candies and put one back. *For the other thief.* *Tuesdays,* said Luuk, and then stood in the doorway a long time afterward with a gloved hand on the rim of the dish.

She stood for a photograph in the west courtyard under the flowering ash with the study crew and two dormmates and the aviary-club girl whose name was a bird call, with Sigrika at her right — and left a person-sized gap at her left shoulder. Sigrika saw the gap, understood it, said nothing, and stood closer to its edge so that the gap would read, later, as almost filled. *Everyone say credits.*

She went to the terminal booths at the east gate and performed a phone call to her mama, pitched to carry through the glass, promising *next year* — and Sigrika, standing outside, watched the call clock read 00:00, unconnected, the entire time. Sigrika looked away first, and held that looking-away all afternoon.

In the aviary courtyard there was a new volunteer groundskeeper with a metronome wrist, whom Denia had watched feed those same birds under three different faces across three semesters. They tilted their heads at each other in the same way — the mannerism meeting its manufacturer. *I built everything, dear. The collar, the coma, the week.* The birthday, though, had surprised her, and she asked whose idea it had been. *Mine. I'm told I take after my maker.* *The play closes Thursday night. It was always going to. The venue was booked before you were.* *I know. I read the contract from the inside.* Denia touched her wrist. *Thank you for the seating chart. Whatever you meant by it.* *I meant a tragedy.* *You'll get one.* And then: *Watch closely.* *I always do.* And the Grand Architect dissolved into an unmemorable stranger and walked off.

And on a bench by the reflecting pool she found Mei with a coffee going cold, and told her she'd seen the broadcast of Amy jumping — *Eyes shut, arms out. Best thing I've ever seen* — and delivered her thanks to the pack by proxy, because she would not be delivering it in person. *I owe your pack. The strings. Tell the one with the scissors: clean edges. A professional appreciates it.* And: *tell the wolf that it counted. Eleven days on my own hands. Best days on record.* Because Chisa had cut Denia's controlling strings once, and Denia had had eleven days of her own before the Architect put her into an engineered coma, and she had kept a ledger of them.

Mei offered to save her. *We've pulled people back from further.* And Denia asked the question that ends the day: *Who catches the ground?* Then she left before Mei could answer, and Mei's unspoken words stayed in her mouth, and on Thursday night she would take them out again and discover they had been the wrong ones all along.

The day's one genuine failure happened in the music annex, which was being packed up, the upright piano wearing a shipping tag that read DONATED: SHELTER SCHOOL 4. Nastasha, who had accompanied for three years, was wrapping metronomes with shaking hands, being transferred permanently to a surface clinic on the Federation side for nerve architecture — her mother had already sold the flat. She conducts now, because the tremor keeps excellent time. She described the barrier breach: a gate open twelve minutes, the worm coming through the gap, a city evacuating, and a pearled wall of light between her and it. And she named what had ruined her hands. *Your bubble.* Denia tried to take the blame and Nastasha refused to let her have it. *Hands are just hands. I'm alive to be furious about it. That's your fault too, and I'm keeping it.* *You should hate me.* *I should practice.* The hug lasted eleven seconds and destroyed a lifetime of engineering.

Denia went to the annex stairwell, second landing, took out the Dream Maker — the toy her Forte runs through, a wand and a ring and a reservoir of soap-bright frequency — and blew one bubble containing the pearl of what she was: the Fractsidus's chemistry, the void's inheritance, the campus's favourite slacker, the debt. Everyone here had paid for what she did. Nastasha's hands. The barrier crews' scars. The city's twelve minutes. Then she washed her face and reassembled the smile and went back up.

At dusk, on the terrace bench, she pre-empted Sigrika's prepared question and told the truth. *It isn't my birthday.* There was no record anywhere — no hospital, no register, no mother's handwriting. Only a fabrication date and a deployment date and a projection. Thursday had come out of Sigrika's timetable. The dream was the only thing she owned, and she had decided to treat it as a memory, for collateral. And then she said what the whole day had been for: *I wanted to die as a real person.* A name, a birthday, small clean endings — the kind real people get. She named Sigrika as the one difficulty in the plan. *You keep — witnessing. It's a terrible habit.*

Sigrika cried at examination pace, and then opened the bird journal past the finches and the white bird to a blank page, and wrote, and signed, and dated, and stamped it with her laboratory seal:

*This day, at sunset, on the terrace bench: DENIA was born. Present at the birth: Sigrika, candidate, Heliodic track. Witnessed and recorded. Witnesses constitute record.*

*You have a record now. In the registry I control. Partial credit is still credit.* Denia laughed — waterlogged, catastrophic — and said *You can really work wonders.* And Sigrika said *I'm a candidate for the Heliodic Six. Wonders are on the syllabus.*

Then Denia told the lie, deliberately, using her best materials. *I won't go anywhere. Today is my birthday. I'm not leaving.* And Sigrika — who had already seen the dead phone line and the gap in the photograph and knew the birthday was manufactured — made a choice, and said: *Okay. I believe you.* The coin stood on its edge, and both of them let it stand.

The party was five people, one cake of cafeteria custard in structural form, and one crooked candle. Petra asked how many candles. *One. I'm brand new. Ask Sigrika. She has the paperwork.* *Witnessed and stamped.* And Denia's one request, in place of a wish: *Next year, I want two.* Same table. Same custard. Deal.

At the vitrified scar of twelve bad minutes there was a lake with no name, because nobody had grieved there yet, and that is how places get their names. And Denia's lifelong dream assembled itself around her, arriving from the wrong direction and fitting perfectly.

### The second night

Sigrika woke at dawn to a heartbeat at her ear and a toy on her pillow and a room one occupant short and a journal open to *White bird. Migratory.* She ran to the Dimmr Plains fence line, where the morning shift confirmed that the seam was sealed, the residue gone, the plain quiet for the first time since the gate, and there was new water out there, reading clean.

Question six was impossible. She wrote out an unrelated formula and, beneath it, *attempted*, and took partial credit, and kept the marked script.

Lucilla, signing something, said: *She kept her side. Down to the minute.*

The Collective's archive kept the three days of mapping, the kind jokes on the recordings, and the collar's entire mechanism printed across their instruments in the act of completing. Then she was honest about the rest. *There is no point. Birth, pain, struggle, death. So Sigrika wouldn't study alone.* Cartethyia, weeping silently: *She made a birthday out of nothing. She had one day.* Chisa, scissors across her lap: *Clean edges. She cut the door exactly where it needed to be. No waste.* Lupa, dangerously flat, her hand white on her spear: *She died so you wouldn't have to watch the Architect use her death to break you* — and then, invoking pack law over a girl who was never theirs, *She kept her side. Down to the minute.* And Iuno, who recognised the shape from the inside — *I know that shape. I wore it once. The girl who dies to protect everyone from her own ending* — said out loud: *You're not allowed to hate yourself for this. I'm invoking veto.*

*No,* Iuno said. *You let her jump. There's a difference.*

Lupa put her forehead against Mei's and gave her back the message. *Eleven days, her own hands. Best days on record. For appearing at the end when it was too late to matter.* She recited the password from memory, and the promise of different cities and different classes and different cakes, and delivered her ruling: *She lied.*

At her door she told Mei: *Thank you. For trying to catch her anyway.* In the corridor Lynae said *I'm going to stay,* and laid out the spare futon, which had been Denia's, and refused to argue about it. Pack law.

Whether that was Denia, or Sigrika's own wishes wearing Denia's voice, is not established.

In the morning Sigrika remembered exactly one word of it. *Dasvidaniya.* Lynae, already awake, spoke to the ceiling to spare her being looked at. Sigrika translated it: *It means goodbye. But not, not really goodbye. It means "until the meeting."* And then raged at it, because all she had was a stupid toy and a word she couldn't pronounce. Lynae gave her permission to be angry — *She lied to you. About staying. You're allowed to be angry* — and Sigrika refused the permission. *She just, she couldn't say it. So she left me a word instead. A word that means she's coming back.* *A word you only remember from a dream.* *It was real.*

The pack laid siege at 07:47, which was the exact minute her first lecture started. Cartethyia opened the door uninvited with thermoses. Lupa brought soup at seven in the morning — *Grief doesn't check the clock.* Iuno arrived with a tea service and an announcement: *We're skipping. All of us. Lucilla has been notified. Your attendance record is being handled.* Sigrika said *I'm not part of your pack,* and Iuno answered: *You are grieving someone we failed to catch. And roads go both ways.* And Lupa, flat: *You're telling her to hope. That's cruel.* *I'm telling her it's allowed.* *Hope gets people killed—* and Iuno cut them both off, and gave Sigrika a binary instead: sit and let grief eat her, or get up and carry it, and either way the pack stays. *What if I can't pick?* *Then we wait until you can.* Sigrika said *I just want to find her,* and then, after a pause, *I just… need to be alone.* Cartethyia accepted that. Lupa added *After you eat.* The soup scalded her and she ate it anyway.

That morning she sat in the visitor's chair in Denia's empty ward and counted all forty-eight ceiling tiles, and found Mille-Feuille where it belonged, third row, fourth from the left — *Mille-Feuille. Very reliable. Never judges my life choices* — and improvised the names she couldn't remember, filling the gaps with desserts she thought Denia would have chosen. She finished at 09:30, two lectures missed, three pack messages unanswered. Luuk came in, said nothing, set a small paper bag on the counter and left. Two sour candies. Sigrika took one, pocketed it, and left the bag for the next thief. Outside, Lynae held the corridor and filed the absence report herself with one word in the notes — *Bereavement* — and Mornye approved it without comment.

At midday, in the east courtyard, she asked the question she had been building toward. *Do you think she will?* And Lynae answered honestly: Amy came back, but Amy had Mei; Denia had a fabrication date and a deployment schedule and a lake that reads clean. *The math doesn't look good.* *It's possible.* And Lynae, choosing between the hope that gets you killed and the hope that gets you through breakfast, said: *Yeah. It's possible.* Sigrika bargained anyway. Amy was in the Nihility for ten years; Denia had been gone three days. People come back from weekends. Then her terminal showed she had been added to a group chat called THE Bimbos go to skool — by Lupa, that morning, right after she went into the ward.

Because those might not be the same thing.* And then she sharpened it: if Denia comes back, what does she come back to — a world that built her to be bait, and a best friend waiting for someone who might not want to be waited for? *Are you waiting for Denia? Or are you waiting to not be sad anymore?*

Lupa shut her down. *You're testing the load. Seeing if it's grief or hope. And you don't get to do that. She gets to hold both. That's the rule.* Mei conceded that Amy wasn't wrong. Sigrika defended the word as a promise, and Iuno said *Or a kindness. Both of those things can be true.* And Sigrika asked why she had to decide, after three days, whether her hope was real or whether she was lying to herself.

Which is when Mei told the pack the thing she had never told anyone.

When Amy walked back into Aleph-1, Mei had brought her shell home. She had fed it. She had watched soup dribble out of its mouth and wiped it. The pack filled in the details, because Sigrika needed the corroboration and Amy needed to hear it. Lupa: *You fed her. Every morning. Soup. Same kind I'm making now.* And then, cracking: *I didn't know if you were hoping or breaking. And I decided it didn't matter.* Cartethyia: *You tucked her in. At night. Smoothed the blanket.* Ciaccona: *She did it for three weeks. Every day. Same routine.* And Iuno: *Because there was no why. Or there were ten thousand whys and none of them mattered.*

Same as we did for me. That's pack law.* *Okay. One day at a time.* Lynae sat on her other side — *I got you. Perimeter's covered* — and Chisa poured tea. *It's hot. Don't burn yourself. Behind her sternum the High Priestess remarked that you cannot catch every fall before it happens, and that this is called smothering. *I'm not good at this,* Iuno admitted, and Chisa said *None of us are. That's why it counts.* Cartethyia said *You did good.* It didn't feel good. *It's not supposed to,* said Lupa. *That's how you know it's real.*

### The pilgrimage

Hiyuki was drinking Earl Grey that Lucilla had made — a ritual she had finally learned to accept without deflecting, after a century of practice — when the request reached her, and she read it twice, because the shape of a wish deserves proper attention and this one had passed through several hands. *A student wishes to visit the Dimmr Plains. The girl whose friend sealed the lake.* Lucilla said the Collective would never permit it, not through official channels, not this soon; she had signed the lockdown order herself and was now watching it meet a cost she had anticipated and could not prevent. *They won't need to,* Hiyuki said. She was building a shrine at the lake. The Collective monitors the seam; she tends the ground; there is no conflict. And Lucilla smiled like a woman watching a lock open that she had assumed was welded shut. *You're doing it again. The thing where you answer a wish by pretending it's an errand.* *It is an errand. I was going there anyway.*

Her message said the walk was not long but the ground was uneven, and to bring water and comfortable shoes. The walk is two days each way.

Lynae walked Sigrika to the east gate on her left, close enough that their shoulders bumped when her stride wobbled, having grabbed her gear on reflex and felt stupid about it, because this was not a smash job and the girl beside her was not cargo. Chisa walked on the right with the folded scissor-blade across her back; when Sigrika had announced her decision Chisa had said nothing, nodded once, rinsed her own cup and said *I'll walk you.* Lynae came because Lynae always follows Chisa, and because somebody needed to stand perimeter, and because the girl in the middle had broken herself open in a way Lynae recognised from the inside. Chisa, who has never seen a face, read Hiyuki at the gate and found more threads than she expected.

*You're ready.* *Yes* — her voice steady, her hands not. Then Hiyuki produced a bike, old frame, well maintained, nothing to do with the Collective or the Academy. *We ride.* Sigrika did not know why the Miko was doing this for her, and found that she did not need to ask.

They went down out of managed daylight into a real grey overcast sky, and then into the Riseway, whose stonework is older than the Academy and older than the Collective, carved by people who understood what it means to build a door into a deep place and prop it open with your life. The air cooled and dried, and then carried the sense of pressure equalising across a membrane. Her ears popped. The engine's note fractured into harmonics that never resolved.

And then the Dimmr Plains, which are not a plain. They are a world, fractured and suspended over an abyss with no bottom: islands of dark slate in lazy patient orbits around nothing, and lavender mist moving on its own slow tides rather than on any wind, glowing faintly, painting the undersides of the plateaus violet and rose. At the centre stands the Solvein Heartwood, vast and gnarled, its crown heavy with leaves the colour of bruises, and embedded in the wood — not carved into it, embedded — a vortex of iridescent light that pulses slowly. Like an eye, or a heart, or the iris of something looking back. There is no wind at all. The static lifts the hair on your arms. The Heartwood's only light source is the eye inside it, so its shadow should fall away in every direction at once, a wheel of dark spokes. Here. It's blooming here.* *Every night.* *That's obscene.* *Or it is the only decent thing in the entire province. An hour on, the plain tilted up into broken slate climbing in sharp-angled shelves toward the base of the Heartwood, thirty metres or more — the kind of terrain that looks like nothing from a distance and becomes a problem the moment you are standing at the bottom of it looking for handholds. Hiyuki was still calculating a route when Sigrika walked past her and started climbing. Hands to the rock, boot to the ledge, weight forward, with the practised economy of somebody who has done it a thousand times in a wood where the hills were taller and the rocks were wetter and you learn young or you learn the hard way.

Hiyuki followed, not slowly but deliberately, testing every hold and trusting her weight to the rock only after confirming it would take her. A hundred years of careful living, and it shows in the hands.

Sigrika was ten metres up at the first shelf and moving like water — no wasted motion, no hesitation, reading the rock the way she reads everything else, with her whole body instead of her head — and her face was not happy and not proud. It was present. The face of somebody who has arrived in the one place she is fluent. *There's a chimney twenty meters right. Easier than it looks. You can brace your back.* At the second shelf she put out her hand and hauled the Miko up the last metre, her palm scraped raw from the widow's ladder and still functioning, with the unhesitating strength of a girl who has pulled younger cousins out of creek beds. And standing together up there, for the first time since the Riseway, Hiyuki saw Sigrika's shoulders come all the way down.

*You're good at this.* *I'm from Bjartr,* she said, as though that explained it. It did.

At the base of the Heartwood, Hiyuki called a halt. *We rest.* *I'm fine.* *Mm. Nevertheless.* There was a canteen of water that was cold and tasted faintly of metal and was the best thing Sigrika had had in three days, and a small waxed pouch with two onigiri in it, plain, no filling, wrapped in dried seaweed, packed with the care somebody might use packing a letter. *Did you make these?* *Yes.* The seaweed was uneven and the rice packed too tight on one side, the whole thing constructed with the earnest imperfection of somebody who learned to do it at seven and has been doing it the same way ever since. Sigrika ate it in four bites. It was fine. Just rice, just seaweed. And she found she was crying quietly into the second bite, because somebody had got up at five in the morning to make her lunch. Hiyuki did not comment on the crying.

The Heartwood loomed directly overhead now, its trunk so vast it bent perspective around itself, its roots breaking up through the slate in massive twisted cables, some wide enough to walk on, and the eleven-beat pulse was no longer something you noticed but something you were inside of, and the eye stared down and kept time with it. *How much further?* *Past the tree. Another hour. Maybe two.* *And then the lake.* *Yes.*

Sigrika folded the wax paper, creased it once, and put it in her pocket. She would keep it. She did not know why yet. She would work that out later.

Past the tree the path ended at a cliff, and across a gap of fifteen or twenty metres the next island hung in the mist, and it was fading. Not transparent, quite — thinning, the way a photograph thins if you leave it in the sun too long, the colour bleeding out, the edges gone soft and uncertain, the outline of a thing no longer sure it was a thing. *Things don't fade here. You said — the jasmine, the classroom, the tree — things are kept here. That's the whole—* *Yes.* The single word confirmed the rule, acknowledged the exception, and did not explain it, because Hiyuki did not have an explanation and had stopped offering empty ones sometime around her fortieth year. The mist moved through the island rather than around it, and the Heartwood's eleven beats came up through the stone and met nothing on the other side.

The Kronaclaw arrived like a landslide with wings — no cry, no warning, no displacement of air, a wingspan wider than the shelf they were standing on and talons the length of Sigrika's forearm — and Hiyuki moved, not fast but *first*, which is a different thing and is what a century of knowing exactly when something will arrive buys you. She pulled Sigrika down and sideways and the talons went past close enough to change the pressure on the back of her neck. On the second pass Sigrika saw the eyes, and they were not predatory. They were frantic. Hiyuki rang the Suzu, and the note went out into the mist and did not echo, because there was nothing for it to bounce off, and so it simply stayed, hanging there like a held breath. The bird pulled up, wheeled twice, and did not dive again.

*It's not hunting. The dive angle was wrong. It wasn't closing, it was — it was herding. It wanted us to move.* *Mm,* said Hiyuki, and did not put the bell away. It circled three more times, banked east, and was gone. Sigrika sat up with her palms scraped fresh and her finger bandage torn loose and realised, with the disorienting shame of somebody caught in an act they had not planned, that she was grinning. She killed it immediately. But it had been there.

Four minutes later a Soliskin came up over the cliff edge on six articulated legs, rabbit-sized, amber carapace, its bioluminescent underbelly pulsing in a rhythm her body recognised before her mind did: eleven beats, synced to the Heartwood. It looked at Hiyuki, looked at Sigrika, walked directly to Sigrika, sat down in front of her boots and put one forelimb on her knee. *…Hi.* It tapped her knee twice, walked three metres toward the descending edge, and looked back. *It wants you to follow.* *I can see that.* *Can you also see the three-hundred-meter drop it is walking toward.* *I'm from Bjartr,* she said, and stood up, and followed.

It was not a drop. It was a scramble — steep and ugly, fatal from above and merely miserable once you were on it — and the Soliskin's glow was the only reliable light, pulsing its eleven beats like a lantern in a mine, and Sigrika followed it the way she had followed game trails at home, not with her eyes but with her hands, reading the rock, trusting the rock, in the one language she had never had to earn.

On a ledge the Soliskin stopped and tapped the wall, where a vein of ice ran through the slate like a frozen artery, clear enough to see into. Suspended inside it at three depths: a magazine — a real one, glossy, faded but legible, a nature periodical with a broad-winged something from the northern territories on the cover, dated thirteen years ago. A highlighter, yellow, cap still on, the plastic clouded with age, the kind you buy in bulk for exam season, the kind Sigrika had gone through by the box. And deeper, near the limit of visibility, a long slate-grey flight feather banded with copper. *That's from the Kronaclaw.*

Hiyuki drew the blade — not the bell — and cut, and the ice split clean rather than shattering, along a seam that had always been there waiting for somebody to find it. Sigrika caught all three the way you hold a bird that has hit a window: carefully, understanding that the thing in your hands is not yours and is also not okay. *Someone was here. Thirteen years ago. They were — studying? Marking things. Reading about birds.* Hiyuki sheathed the blade. She did not ask why they were retrieving a dried-out highlighter and an old magazine from a vein of ice at the bottom of a cliff. She had simply done it, the way she had made the onigiri, the way she had packed the second pudding.

Further down, past a chimney, there was a small frozen lake tucked into a hollow like a puddle in a cupped palm — not *the* lake — and on a ledge jutting over it like a diving board, a nest. Massive. Built out of shattered slate and dried vine and things with no business being construction material: canvas strips, lengths of wire, the corner of a tarpaulin. Big enough for the Kronaclaw. Big enough for more than one. Empty.

The bird came back, this time with a cry, enormous and resonant and doubling off the walls, and Hiyuki had the blade out before the echo died. *Wait—* *Behind me.* *Wait—* It dived, and she stepped forward, and at the apex of the swing, in the sympathetic flare of the Suzu at her hip, she saw it. The bird was fading. Mist visible through the primary feathers, rock visible through the breast, the great hooked beak losing its edge and its definition, becoming a suggestion of a beak on a suggestion of a bird that was no longer entirely certain it existed.

Her blade stopped.

*It's leaving,* she said, very quietly. *It's fading. Like the island. It's the same thing, it's going the same way—* It landed on the rim thirty metres up, folded its translucent wings, and screamed again, and the scream came down to them thin and desperate and not at all like hunting.

So Sigrika climbed the nest. It was not graceful; the slate cut her palms and the wire caught her sleeve and the whole structure creaked, and she climbed it the way she climbs everything, stubbornly, with her chin set, because trying was the only armour she had ever owned. And in the hollow at the centre she put the magazine, open to the page with the bird on it. The highlighter, cap still on. And the feather laid across them both, the way you lay a flower on a grave, or a pen beside a journal.

The Kronaclaw went silent. It looked at the nest with its fading eyes and something in its posture changed — not relaxed. Recognised. The way a person recognises a thing they lost and had stopped hoping to find. Then it dropped, controlled and heavy, wings barely spread, landing with a sound like a sail collapsing, and the ice did not crack under its weight, because there was not enough of it left to crack anything. It folded its wings and sat.

Somebody had been there thirteen years ago. Somebody who read about birds and used a highlighter and sat near a nest, in the comfortable silence of a person who was not afraid. And then they had left, and nobody had come back, and the bird had waited and the island had waited and the magazine had frozen into the wall, and thirteen years of not being witnessed had worn them both down to suggestions.

Hiyuki walked forward and put her hand on the throat plumage where the feathers were still dense enough to touch. It was warm — body-warm, the same as the jasmine, the warmth of a thing keeping itself alive through effort. She smoothed the feathers in long slow strokes and the breathing slowed and the tremor in the great body eased, and in the hollow of the nest three objects sat where a human hand had placed them, and the word for what they were was *proof.*

Then she asked her question. *What is your wish?*

It was the voice of her office, the voice that had asked ten thousand times and carried what it was given. And the difference — the whole difference — was that she had not knelt. She was standing. She was looking up. Her hand was on the creature's throat and the question was not *what do you want me to bear.* It was *what do you need.*

The Kronaclaw turned its head toward the fading island, lowered its body to the ice, spread one wing flat across the frozen surface, and waited.

*…It's asking us to get on?* *Yes,* said Hiyuki, and climbed onto the wing without hesitating, because a century of hearing wishes had taught her exactly one thing about what to do when somebody answers, which is that you go.

They flew. The feathers were warm and thinning and Sigrika could feel the bird's own pulse through them — not the Heartwood's eleven but its own, fast and shallow and fading and still going — and it carried them toward its island with the desperate directness of a thing going home. And Sigrika did not reach for the journal. Not the pen, not the green ink or the blue or the red. She did not compose a taxonomy or a field note. She held onto the back of a bird that was almost not there any more and she *looked,* and the looking was the record, and the record was her, and for the first time in her life the act of witnessing was not a performance for an audience that would read about it later. She was the audience. She and Hiyuki, and the only proof it had ever existed would be that they had seen it.

At the island's centre was a roost — old, built into the rock, generations of it, layers of slate and vine compacted into something more like a house than a nest. With more nests inside it. Smaller ones. Some broken. Some empty for a long, long time. *It had a family.* *Yes.* *And they left. Or they faded. And it's the last one.* Hiyuki said nothing, because she had read the plaque on the cerulean tree and knew what *last known sample* means, and knew what it means to be the only one left in a place that is going away.

Sigrika looked at the broken nests and the worn grooves in the rock where talons had landed ten thousand times, and she did not write any of it down, and it was all true anyway. And the bird settled into its roost and curled around the space where the smaller nests had been — and the fading slowed. Not stopped. Slowed, the way a candle slows when you cup your hand round it. Witnessed. That was all. Not preserved, not frozen, not stamped. And for as long as they were there, the island did not go away.

They stayed. Then the bird stirred, looked toward the three objects in the nest, and spread its wing again, and flew them low, and banked once over its home — its family's home, the rock where ten thousand landings had worn grooves into slate that was barely slate any more — and cried once, thin and long, and the cry went out into the lavender mist and did not come back. Sigrika watched it go and did not look away. That was the job now. Looking was the job. It set them down on the last shelf of real ground before the Heartwood's roots plunged into the abyss, waited while they slid off, and climbed back into the mist toward its fading world without ceremony and without a backward glance. Sigrika watched until she could not see it, and then watched the space where it had been.

Underneath the Heartwood her shoulder touched Hiyuki's — she did not decide it; her body decided — and then her weight followed, and just settled, the way a head settles against a seatback at minute six of every lecture. Hiyuki did not move and did not stiffen and did not put an arm around her, because that was not the shape of this. She simply stayed exactly where she was and bore the weight, and the weight was not heavy. *Your hands are warm,* Sigrika said, very quietly. *Mm.*

Then there was a bench, which was wrong: not damaged, not displaced, so thoroughly correct that it read as impossible, the way a word looks wrong if you stare at it. And on it an old man, fading more subtly than the bird — the kind of fading you might take for distance until you were close enough to see the bench showing through the fabric of his coat in long pale stripes. He sat with the careful posture of somebody whose back had been talking to him for decades, head tilted as though listening for something.

*Oh, hello, stranger! This rain just came out of nowhere, didn't it?* He couldn't make the walk back in a downpour, not at his age. Hiyuki looked at the mist and the pulsing eye and said, *I don't see any rain.* *Oh. Is that right? I see. Guess you and I aren't in the same place. Or the same time, for that matter.* And then he patted the bench, an old familiar thing done a thousand times. *It's late. And with this miserable rain — if you don't mind… would you sit with me for a while?*

She sat. The bench held her. Sigrika stood three metres off and did not sit, and watched.

He looked at his hands, translucent now, the bones visible, the wedding ring on his left there and not-there at once. *Forgive me for asking, but. Do you have any regrets?*

A year earlier she would have said *tell me yours.* She would have opened her sternum like a ledger and said *I will carry it,* and swallowed it behind the bone and added it to the ten thousand — without ever noticing that she had arranged never to answer the question herself.

She said: *Yes.*

There was a city, taken by a storm thirty years ago, and she had spent thirty years telling herself she had buried it properly, and she had not. She had preserved it. Every face, every voice, every unanswered question, all of the footage kept, and she had called it duty. And there was a girl, three weeks ago, in a place like this one, who had been falling for years, and Hiyuki had knelt beside her and asked her what she wanted and taken her home. *I did not kneel beside another girl who was three meters from me and full of strings and running out of time, and she died alone in the dark on the other side of this lake.* Her hands lay in her lap, warm. *I carry the cold of thirty years. I did not carry her.*

*Well,* he said, very quietly, his edges gone softer. *I think we all have the ones we didn't kneel for.*

Then he laughed the laugh of somebody who had stopped being embarrassed about the size of his regrets, and offered his own inventory, counted off on fingers that were mostly suggestion: never beat the highest difficulty of Lahai-Roi Blocks; never worked out the name of a dessert he ate forty years ago, yellow, sweet, some kind of custard, and the chef wouldn't tell him; spent his whole life chasing truth and never got close to the door. Things like that would keep him tossing even in his grave.

And then the real one. An afternoon, a long time ago. The New Federation had invited him onto a major academic project — a once-in-a-lifetime shot — and it meant leaving home, and leaving the people he loved. *It was like rational and irrational numbers. One seemingly insignificant decision, and our paths diverged forever.* They sat face to face that afternoon and neither of them said a word, and the whole time he was thinking: *if she asks me to stay, I will. To hell with the project and the prestige. I would have been fine as a nobody teacher. I would have stayed… if only she'd asked.* But nobody said anything. They just sat there staring at each other. *And after that day, I never saw her again.*

Sigrika had not moved. She stood three metres off with her hands at her sides, and she was not looking at the old man. She was looking at Hiyuki.

*If you were in my shoes, stranger… what would you have done?*

Hiyuki thought about a city, and thirty years of keeping its dead in ice and calling that carrying, and a girl she had not knelt for. She thought about her sister, and a second bowl of red bean pudding, and a standing appointment for tea with a woman whose *yes* had not yet caught up. And she thought about all the things she had not said across all the years she had not said them, and the frost that had built up in the absence, and the decade-long business of defrosting a word at a time that she was still, at a hundred and some years old, in the middle of.

*I don't know.* And then: *I spent thirty years not saying things across distances I called professional, and calling it duty. I know exactly how that afternoon works from the inside. The waiting for someone else to speak first. The certainty that the other person's silence means the same as yours.* She turned the Suzu once on her palm, a habit so old it had worn grooves. *I cannot tell you I would have been braver. I have evidence to the contrary.* But: *I know what I would tell you now, with thirty years of having been wrong about silence: she was waiting too. They always are. The waiting is not permission. It is just two people trusting the afternoon to do what neither of them will.*

*…Yeah. Yeah, I think you might be right.* And then: *No one swims upstream against time. Let alone changes it. But sometimes I wonder — would everything be different?* *Yes. And neither of you would have been wrong. And neither of you was. The afternoon was what it was, and both of you were in it, and that does not stop being true.*

She stood. What was left of him was very gentle and very tired and very close to done. And then she answered one of his regrets outright. *The dessert was probably madeleine. If it was forty years ago and you were in the western territories. The recipe was lost in a Void Storm. Almost nobody remembers the name.*

He stared, and then laughed — real and full, the most solid sound he had made, and it went up into the mist and bounced off nothing and came back changed. *Well. There it is. A whole cabinet full of regrets, and a stranger on a bench knows the answer to one of them.* *It was a very good dessert. I have the recipe. In ice. I have been meaning to give it to someone who would use it.*

She inclined her head, the bow of a shrine properly left. He raised one translucent hand — not a wave, a salute, the kind that means *safe travels* in a language older than the Collective. And walking away, Sigrika glanced back once, and the bench was empty. Not gone; the Plains keep things. Just empty, the way a bench is empty after somebody has got up and gone somewhere they were waiting to go, with the imprint of him still on the seat, the particular compression of a person who had been sitting in one place a long time. She did not write it down.

And then, from the shelf, they could see the lake — a dark mirror at the base of a long slope, reflecting nothing and giving back everything the way black glass does, exactly the right size for what had happened there, which is to say immense. Sigrika's shoulder found Hiyuki's arm again and her weight followed, and what she handed over was itemised: the part of her that had been carrying the search grid and the attendance record and the speech she had not said and the pen she had not uncapped and the journal she had not opened and the three colours of ink and the second vacancy in the photograph and four years of double fries and two in the morning and the paragraph read four times, and the word she had woken up with every day for a week that she could feel the shape of in her mouth and could not yet make herself say out loud. Hiyuki bore it the way the bench had borne the old man: completely, without comment, for as long as it was needed. And as they walked she began to hum without appearing to notice — three notes, no tune, the sound of somebody who has finally decided the silence does not need to be kept.

Then they reached the shore, and the lake was gone.

Not frozen. Not black glass. Dry. A basin of cracked slate descending in a shallow bowl to a centre that held nothing — no water, no glass, no residue, no stain. The seam had closed and the lake that formed in its wake had drained into somewhere below the world, and what was left was a hole in the ground the exact size and shape of a girl's last act, and it was empty.

Sigrika's knees went first. She caught herself on the bandaged, thorn-scarred hand and the slate bit through the gauze and she barely felt it. She had imagined putting her feet on the ground Denia had walked on and feeling *something* — the temperature of a goodbye, the shape of a frequency still humming in the rock. There was nothing. *She's not here. There's nothing to stand in. There's nothing to touch. She walked into water and the water left and I came two days too late.*

And then she saw it: at the lowest point of the basin, where the cracks converged and the slate had split wide enough to swallow a boot, a darkness. Not the mist-dark of the Plains, not the silver-dark of the false moon. A cave darkness. Dense. Real. Going down.

She did not know what it was and did not care what it was. It was a direction that was not *collapse,* and she had spent her entire life substituting forward motion for the luxury of falling apart, and her body knew the trade before her mind approved it. She got up. Everything was wrong and the lake was gone and Denia was gone and she was walking toward a hole in the ground because the alternative was lying on dry slate for the rest of her life. Hiyuki did not catch her and did not call out. She followed two steps behind, close enough to reach, and watched a girl from Bjartr Woods do what a girl from Bjartr Woods does when the ground disappears, which is find the next handhold.

The crack swallowed them the way a throat swallows — downward, muscular, inevitable — and opened into a cavern vaulted and immense, every surface alive with the slow cold fire of things that had been growing in the dark for longer than either of them could usefully imagine. And sitting inside it was an escalator. Metal, grooved treads, rubber handrails, the institutional geometry of a thing built to move people efficiently between floors, its motor seized, its handrail cracked and peeling. It had not run in years. Beyond it a corridor ran back into the rock with dead fluorescent fixtures and floor tiles laid in a pattern that was unmistakably deliberate.

*This is not Startorch.* *No.* *Nor Spacetrek.* *No.*

Sigrika climbed the frozen steps one at a time, and at the top was a complex, scientific, the air tasting of recycled nothing — the particular deadness of a ventilation system that has gone on breathing for itself long after the last person left. Beside the first door was a placard with a logo on it that was neither the Collective's compass nor the Academy's crest, and Sigrika read it and her lips moved and no sound came out, and her hand went to the journal in her bag on pure reflex, and she left it there.

Hiyuki knew the sigil. Not from a file or a briefing — from the inside of a Threnodian's chest, where the architecture of a girl's puppet-strings had been stamped with the same angular mark, burned into every anchor point like a maker's mark on pottery. Fractsidus. And Sigrika did not know it, because the pack had made sure of that; the pack had made sure of a great many things, and every one of them had been done with love, and every one of them had been a door closed in a girl's face with a smile on the other side of it.

Hiyuki weighed it. She could say *we should go back.* She could say *this is not relevant to the lake.* She could become every adult who had ever stood between this girl and an unbearable fact and called it protection.

She did not. *Sigrika. That sigil is Fractsidus.*

Nothing moved in Sigrika's face for three full seconds. Then everything moved at once, inward — a whole architecture of understanding rearranging itself behind her eyes, the same intake procedure she had used on the thread and the loop and the fading island, except that this time the object she was disassembling was the ground under her feet. *This is where they made her.* *I believe so.* *The lake was empty.* *Yes.* *But this isn't.*

They walked it. Past fluorescents, some dead, some buzzing, a few still burning with the pale institutional patience of things nobody ever told to stop. Past vines splitting the grout in slow green lines. Past computing stations with the chairs still pushed back at the angle of people who had stood up and not come back. Past a mug on a desk and a jacket on a hook and a pen left uncapped long enough that the ink had dried into a black teardrop on the blotter. Past a coffee machine whose residue ring had fossilised into something geological. Past a break room with a table for six and a schedule nobody would ever follow and a cartoon somebody had clipped from a newspaper and pinned up because it was funny — and it was still funny, and that was the worst thing in the room.

*Sigrika.* Her boots squeaked on the tile and the sound was enormous and she flinched at it and was angry at herself for flinching. *You are walking the way you study. Intake. Processing. Forward motion as substitute. You have been accelerating for four minutes.* And: *This is the place where your friend was made. It is not an exam. You do not have to finish it.* Her hands were shaking; she had not noticed, having been busy walking. *I don't know how to be here slowly.* *Mm. Then we stand.* They stood.

She went back to the break room because her eye had caught something. A cracked pleather couch. A dartboard with three darts still in it, two in the outer ring and one in the seventeen — somebody had been bad at darts and had kept playing anyway. Dead potted plants leaning toward a window that looked into rock. And on the notice board, two notes. One jagged with the specific fury of a person wronged before lunch: *WHOEVER LEFT FISH IN THE FRIDGE — IT SMELLS. LABEL YOUR FOOD OR IT GETS TOSSED.* And one pinned with a blue thumbtack in neat shift-start handwriting:

*— Audit 12/5*
*— Submit outline to Overseer*
*— Clear trash!!*
*— Subject D rations*

Four items. Same weight, same hand, same blue ink. Clear the trash, feed the subject. Between the exclamation points and the girl, not even a line break — just a dash, the way you dash anything that needs doing before you clock out and go home and have dinner and complain about the fish.

Sigrika read the fish note twice and her face did nothing, and Hiyuki watched her face do nothing and understood that the nothing was load-bearing. She touched the fourth line with her bandaged finger. *D.* And then: *They didn't even write her name.* She unpinned it — a small rectangle of paper, lighter than a feather, lighter than a word written in a journal — folded it once with the crease aligned, because she was still who she was and still aligned things, and put it in her pocket next to the wax paper from the onigiri.

*We can go.* *Not yet. I want to find her room.*

Deeper: another dead escalator furred with dust. A storage bay of crates labelled only with serial numbers and dates. A long corridor aquarium of the kind installed in waiting rooms to calm people who are about to hear things they don't want to hear, its glass clouded and its gravel grey, with a single plastic castle listing on the bottom and its turret broken off and lying in the silt like a fallen tooth. A med-bay with a steel examination table bolted to the floor and a drain underneath it. A monitoring station with six dark screens, all angled at the same room. A door marked CALIBRATION in letters that had not faded because they were etched rather than painted, into metal built to outlast whatever was being calibrated.

And the pods. Six of them recessed into an alcove, each the size and rough shape of a person lying down, glass-fronted, open, the padding gone stiff and yellow and still conforming to the impression of bodies that had lain in them long enough to leave a dent. Five were identical and standard, their connectors uniform, interchangeable. The sixth was smaller — not by much; by the span of a girl's shoulders against a woman's — and its padding had been replaced with newer material in a different colour, fitted more carefully, the way you fit a thing you intend to use more than once. Its connectors were different too. More of them. Denser. Arranged in a pattern that climbed from the base of the pad to the place where a neck would rest.

Sigrika's entire response was: *Let's keep going.*

The corridor ended not in a wall but in air. The fluorescents gave way to something organic and warmer, and the floor went from tile to stone to soil — actual soil, dark and damp and giving under her boots in a way that made her whole body remember Bjartr so hard her knees nearly buckled. There was wind, real wind, moving through real grass that grew in low tufts across the cavern floor in patterns she could read, because grass was the first language she ever spoke. And at the far wall, thick as a hedgerow, waving in a current from nowhere: forget-me-nots. Blue. Hundreds of them, growing wild and dense and entirely without permission in a facility built for calibration and ration schedules and serial numbers, and they did not care, and they were not sorry, and they were blooming.

Sigrika pressed her bare hand flat against her lips and held it there hard, the way you hold a door shut against weather, standing with grass under her boots and wind in her hair and her best friend's favourite flower growing in the place where her best friend had been kept.

Most of the doors off that cavern were shut. One was open. She looked at Hiyuki, and Hiyuki did not nod and did not gesture; she simply stood where she was with her hands loose, and the standing was the whole of the permission, and it was enough. The grass sprang back behind Sigrika as she crossed it, erasing her steps, and the forget-me-nots turned their small blue faces toward the movement as she passed.

Taped to the door at eye level, handwritten in black marker, in letters big and uneven — the handwriting of somebody who had learned penmanship from observation rather than instruction:

*Denia's Room. Keep Out!*

The exclamation point was emphatic. The tape was peeling at three corners and holding at one, which meant somebody had re-taped it at least once, which meant somebody had wanted it to stay.

Sigrika stood in the doorway and did not go in — not because she was afraid, but because the sign asked her not to, and she was a girl who follows signs, and the girl who wrote it was the girl who had never once asked her for anything in four years, and this was the first request she had ever put in writing, and Sigrika needed a moment to stand in front of it. Then she went in.

It was not a room. It was two things at once that had been living on top of each other so long that neither could be separated from the other without tearing.

There was a medicine cabinet, glass-fronted, institutional, the kind you see in clinic hallways and school nurse offices, full of bottles and blister packs and ampoules with New Federation labelling — nutritional agents, antibiotics, suppressants, the clinical vocabulary of a body being managed by people who thought of it as equipment. And some of the labels had been replaced, in the same black marker as the door, the same uneven hand, the same peeling tape.

*Painkillers: Gross but work*
*Sedatives: Somewhat sweet*
*For fevers.*

Sigrika knew that handwriting from margins and borrowed notebooks and caricatures of professors drawn with beaks during lectures Denia was supposedly sleeping through. She had seen it once in her own journal: two words and a period, the last thing written before a girl walked into the dark. Here it was labelling poison. Except that it was medicine, and it was also poison, because it was keeping a body running that had been built to stop, and the girl inside the body had looked at the bottles and decided that if she was going to swallow them she was at least going to name them in her own language — and the language she chose was the one she used for everything, which was lazy and gentle and precise enough to cut. *Gross but work.* Three words. Four years of that voice, and Sigrika had thought it was personality. It *was* personality. It had started here, in a cabinet, with a girl teaching herself how to describe pain in terms that sounded like an inconvenience.

There was a refrigerator, ordinary, white, still humming off whatever power kept the fluorescents alive. Inside it: compressed ration packs, tightly wrapped, identical, rows and rows of them, the same packaging and the same shape and the same weight, stacked with the efficiency of somebody who had been eating the same meal for years. No fries. There had never been fries. Not here, not once. The fries had come later, at the Academy, and the doubling had started before Sigrika noticed it was happening. Denia's first fry had been at Startorch. Her first meal that wasn't this. Sigrika closed the refrigerator the way you close something you are not going to open again.

There was a study table with one leg snapped clean, the fracture old, the raw wood grey with age, the whole surface listing at fifteen degrees and held roughly level by a stack of ration boxes wedged underneath. It had been broken a long time. It had been used anyway.

And there were books, and Sigrika went still in the way that means she is reading. *A Professional Guide to Emotional Performance* — cover worn soft, dog-eared, spine cracked in four places from being opened flat, the way you open a textbook you are studying from rather than reading through, with sections marked by torn strips of ration packaging. She did not pick it up. She understood that her best friend had learned how to be a person from a manual. The sleeping. The nonchalance. The slow blink, the half-smile, the specific tilt of the head that made everything sound like she couldn't be bothered. Studied. Practised. Rehearsed in a room with a broken table and a cabinet full of things labelled *gross but work,* until the rehearsal became the role and the role became the girl — and it was a performance, and it was real, and those were the same thing, and Sigrika was never going to be able to separate them and was not going to try.

*A Seal-Proof Guide to Enrollment* — the student handbook they give you at orientation, which Sigrika had read cover to cover on the train from Bjartr. Denia had got a copy before she ever set foot on campus, because she had been planning this, because the performance of being a student had started here in the dark with a broken table and a guide that told her where to sit and what to wear and how to enrol. A Voidmatters Science textbook, standard issue, the same edition everybody had — with its spine cracked in six places and margin notes in black marker, belonging to a girl who had never once opened hers in class. And *Lahai-Roi Meteorology,* slim and specialist, the kind of thing you borrow from a friend, with a borrower's slip inside the front cover that said *Nastasha* — read, with the pages on storm prediction marked. Nastasha, whose hands would shake for the rest of her life, who had forgiven Denia and meant it, who was not fine about the breaking and had said so. Denia had borrowed her book.

Beside it, casual as a pen, a syringe labelled in the same marker: *Painkiller.* Held up to the light the liquid was clear and colourless and far too thin for any analgesic in the Academy's med bay. Saline. Which meant she had access to the real thing, which meant this was not a substitution but a choice. She had made herself a placebo — because having a thing that says *painkiller* is different from having a painkiller, and the difference is that this one she had made herself, and it worked because she said it did, and nobody could take it away because it was not medicine, it was water with a name she had given it, and the name was the point. Sigrika put it back down gently, exactly where it had been.

And underneath the textbooks, half hidden by the listing surface, a copy of the previous year's entrance examination, its margins full of working — not Denia's lazy scrawl but something careful and effortful, the handwriting of somebody trying very hard to get the right answer, some questions attempted twice, some three times. One of them circled, with a note beside it.

*Ask Sigrika.*

Two words, written in advance. Written before they had met. Written by a girl in a dark room who had already decided who she was going to sit next to and borrow notes from and pretend to sleep through lectures with. And the asking had never happened, because by the time they were in the same room she had got the answer on her own, or decided it didn't matter, or decided that the asking would cost more than the not-knowing.

She had never asked.

And there was the operating table. Steel, bolted to the floor, with restraining cuffs at the wrists and ankles — padded, adjustable, the leather darkened and softened by use. A rolling cart beside it with the implements laid out in a tray: scalpels, clamps, retractors, a cautery pen, sterilisation swabs still in their packaging. An IV stand, tall, the hook at the top empty.

There was a sticker on the IV stand. Fleet Snowfluff, round and cheerful, the cartoon mascot grinning with its arms spread wide, the kind you get from a vending machine or peel off a snack wrapper. It had been placed at exactly the height a girl lying on that table would be able to see it. It was the only thing in the room with colour in it. And it was smiling.

And there was a pillow tied to the head of the table. Ordinary. Flat, institutional, the kind issued in dormitories and barracks. Secured with strips of the same packaging tape that held the door sign, wrapped twice around the frame, knotted with the graceless determination of somebody who did not know how to tie knots and had tied this one anyway.

She had tied a pillow to the place where they strapped her head.

Sigrika's knees did not go. Her hands did not shake. Her face did not come apart. She stood in front of the table where her best friend had been cut open and calibrated and maintained like a machine on a schedule filed between *clear trash* and *audit 12/5,* and she looked at the pillow, and she was very quiet, and the quiet was not composure. It was the sound of a girl who had run out of ways to take things apart into pieces small enough to hold. Hiyuki stood in the doorway, not entering, not asked, with the face of a woman who had carried ten thousand regrets behind her sternum and was now looking at something that could not be carried, because it was not a regret. It was a fact — a small, flat, institutional fact, tied to a steel table with packaging tape by a girl who had wanted her head to hurt less when they opened her up. Hiyuki's hands were warm. She checked.

*You were studying,* Sigrika said, in a very small voice. *You were — the whole time, you were studying, you were getting ready, you had the handbook and the exam and the — you were going to ask me, you wrote my name before you knew me, and you came in and you sat down and you slept through everything and you'd already done the reading—* And: *You learned how to be a person from a book. You practiced. You labeled your own medicine and you made a fake painkiller and you tied a pillow to the — and then you came to school and you were so—*

Her voice broke on the unfinished word. The room had already said it, in every object and every label and every strip of tape: *so human.*

She crossed the room and knelt beside the table and put her hand on the pillow. She did not untie it. She just held it, the way you hold a thing somebody made with their hands, badly, with love, with tape, because it was all they had. *I would have helped you study,* she whispered. *I would have — you could have asked. Any of it. All of it. I would have come here and sat on the floor and read the textbook out loud and you could have slept through the whole thing and I would have just — been here.* She pressed her forehead to the edge of the steel, and the steel was cold, and the pillow was not, because a girl had tied it there so that something in that room would be soft, and it was still soft, and it had been waiting, and it did not know she was gone.

*Take your time,* said Hiyuki from the doorway, very quietly. Sigrika did.

She found the drawer when she lifted her head — the bottom drawer of the study table, the one that had taken the worst of the listing, its face scraped raw from being forced open against the tilt for what must have been years, the handle gone and replaced with a loop of the same packaging tape, knotted twice.

Everything else in that room had been managed. Labelled, taped, propped. The journal was different. It sat alone, with no ration packs on top of it and nothing beside it, wrapped carefully and precisely in a clean cloth of the kind you find in an art supply closet, folded to keep dust out and pressure off — and the fold was perfect, and Denia could not fold things perfectly, which meant she had practised that fold specifically, for this one object.

It was the same make as Sigrika's own: standard Academy issue, hardcover, navy, the crest stamped in silver. The one they hand out at orientation. And it had been decorated — Fleet Snowfluff in five different poses across the front, a cheap holographic bird from a capsule machine in the upper right corner, a strip of puffy star stickers down the spine with one peeling and most of them crooked, and on the back, in silver marker, a cat with too many legs or possibly a spider with ears, with a speech bubble reading *nap time* in letters so uneven they tilted off the edge of the cover. Childish. Wonderful. The decoration of somebody who had never been given a thing to decorate and had gone at it with the full unguarded enthusiasm of a first attempt, and had not shown it to anyone, and had not performed it, and had not done it to be seen.

Inside there were no words. Not one — not a sentence, not a label, not a date. The pages were thick because every single one had something glued or taped or pressed into it with the same care that had wrapped the cloth around the cover.

Photographs.

The cafeteria, shot from across the table, blurry, clearly taken in secret: Sigrika mid-sentence with her mouth open and one hand gesturing off-frame, a tray of fries between them, the angle low, held under the table or from a lap, the kind of photo you take when you don't want anyone to know you're taking it. The study room, late: Sigrika bent over her notes with three pens lined up beside the textbook and her hair across her face, asleep — she had fallen asleep at the desk, and Denia, who was supposedly always the one sleeping, had been awake, and had taken the photo, and had kept it. The courtyard, and the flowering ash, with Sigrika and Nivora under it and the sunlight catching the blossoms and a space between them where somebody had been standing and had stepped out of frame to take the picture; the pigeons were there, and one of them was on Nivora's shoulder, and Nivora looked delighted.

And then four years of it. The library, the quad, the fake sky with its too-warm blues. Somebody else's birthday party, with Sigrika in a paper crown looking embarrassed. A field trip. A sunset through greenhouse glass. Sigrika asleep on a bench, Sigrika reading, Sigrika laughing, Sigrika eating and talking and studying and existing, being alive in the way that alive people are alive — and Denia had been watching, the whole time, for four years, collecting the evidence.

And in every single photograph Denia was somewhere in it. A reflection in a window behind Sigrika's head. A shadow across a cafeteria table. The edge of a sleeve at the border of a frame. Never centred. Present anyway. She was in her own photographs the way she had been in her own life: off to the side, barely visible, there.

And in the shots taken by other people, she was smiling. Not the half-smile, not the rehearsed tilt out of the performance manual. Smiling — the full, unguarded, unsorted thing, the face of a girl who had not yet had time to arrange her expression into something that looked like not-caring, caught between one second and the next in the act of being happy and not knowing what to do about it.

Sigrika turned the pages. Her hands were steady. Her face was not. Four years she had sat across from this girl, and Denia had never mentioned a journal, had never pulled it out, had never once said *look at this* or *remember when* — because keeping was asking, and asking was the one thing she would not do, because asking meant admitting that the thing you have is something you are afraid to lose. *Ask Sigrika.* She had written it on an exam sheet and never followed through, because if you ask, you admit you care, and if you admit you care, the caring becomes a thing that can be taken, and Denia had been made in a facility where everything was taken on a schedule filed between *clear trash* and *audit 12/5.* So she kept it here. In a drawer with no handle. Wrapped in cloth. In the dark. Where nobody could schedule it for removal.

And then Sigrika's hands stopped, because she knew the last photograph. Three girls under a flowering ash, sunlight, blossoms falling — the same image that sat framed on her own desk with *Best friends forever!* across the bottom in her own permanent marker, with three underlines, because three felt correct and two wasn't enough.

But in this one Nivora had been cut out. Not torn. Cut — carefully, with something sharp, a scalpel from the cart beside the operating table, along a line that followed the exact contour of a shoulder and an arm and the edge of her hair. Precise. Deliberate. The work of somebody who had sat down and decided with surgical patience exactly how much of the photograph to keep and how much to remove.

What was left was two girls under a flowering ash, shoulders almost touching, the space between them closed by the absence of the girl who had been in the middle, with the blossoms falling over both of them equally and the light catching them both. And underneath, in the same black marker that had labelled the medicine and the door and the painkiller that was salt water:

*Best friend forever.*

Not friends. Friend. Singular. Not an exclamation point. A period. Not a declaration to a group but a statement of fact, written by a girl who had learned how to describe the world from a manual and had chosen, at the end, to describe it like this. One person. One word. Final.

Sigrika closed it and held it against her chest with both hands, and she did not cry, because she was past crying — in the place past crying, where the body has used up every available response and what is left is the fact of being alive in a room where somebody loved you and never said so and never planned to and kept the proof in a drawer with no handle, and you were three days too late to find it while she was still there to be embarrassed about it. The journal was warm in her hands. Not body-warm, not sun-warm. Just the warmth of a thing kept in the dark by somebody who intended to come back for it, or not, but who had wrapped it carefully either way.

In the doorway, Hiyuki's breathing changed. Sigrika did not notice, being unavailable for anything that was not the weight of a navy-covered book against her sternum. Hiyuki noticed because that is what she does — a century of cataloguing what belongs and what doesn't, and finding the one object that sits wrong against the grain of everything else.

There was a piece of paper on the floor, half under the edge of the operating table, as though it had been slipped beneath the door, or dropped from above, or simply *placed,* with the quiet confidence of somebody who knew exactly where it would land and exactly who would find it. And it was white. Not the grey-white of old paper left in a dead facility for years. White. The white of something printed this morning. Too smooth, too clean, too new.

The handwriting was not Denia's jagged marker. It was the same cadence, the same slightly-too-deliberate spacing, the same careful construction of a person writing in a hand they had studied rather than grown up with — the same, and not. The way a perfect copy is the same: every stroke in the right place, every curve at the right angle, and the wrongness not in any individual letter but in the total, in the fact that no living hand writes this consistently, this cleanly, this *well,* unless it has been doing it for centuries and learned everything, a student's penmanship included, as a costume it enjoyed wearing.

Hiyuki crouched and did not pick it up. She read it from where she was, the way you read the inscription on unexploded ordnance: carefully, from a distance, with your weight on the balls of your feet. Two words, centred, unsigned.

*Welcome home!*

The exclamation point was cheerful. The kind of punctuation a friend would use. The kind a girl named Nivora would put at the end of a note left on a study desk between a borrowed pen and a bag of cafeteria chips — the small bright debris of a friendship that had lasted two years and been a lie and been kind and been both at once without cancelling.

*Sigrika.* The girl did not look up; she was holding the journal. *Sigrika. We need to leave.*

Then the floor moved.

Not a tremor — a lurch, the whole room pitching sideways as though something beneath it had turned over in its sleep. Sigrika hit the tile shoulder-first and the journal left her hands and skidded across the floor, spinning once on its sticker-covered spine, and came to rest face-down beneath the operating table.

And the not-water came in. From the corridor beyond the cavern, from the deeper dark, from below the facility's lowest level — not water, but something that behaved like water, finding every crack and seam, the way the mist on the Plains was not wind. The black of it came first, spilling over the threshold in a thin sheet, and where it touched the tile it did not pool.

It ate. The tile went translucent, then the grout, then the steel leg of the operating table with the bolts dissolving upward and the restraining cuffs going soft at the edges like wax near a flame. The IV stand listed as its base lost coherence. The Fleet Snowfluff sticker smiled, and smiled, and went. The cabinet — *gross but work, somewhat sweet, for fevers* — flickered, its glass going to smoke, the bottles inside hanging for one impossible second in the shape of a shelf that no longer existed, before they went too.

Hiyuki caught Sigrika's arm and hauled her toward the door, toward the cavern, toward up, toward out.

*No!* — and it came out of her like something torn. *Sigri—* *NO!*

Her whole body pivoted against the grip with her eyes locked on the journal face-down under the dissolving table, and she wrenched her arm free with a strength that had nothing to do with strength and everything to do with a girl who had just held four years of secret love in her hands and was not going to leave it in a drowning room. She lunged. Hiyuki's hand closed on empty air.

Hiyuki's frost left her palms in a sheet — instinct, muscle memory, the reflex of a century. It hit the water and the water took it. Swallowed it whole. Not resisted, not overwhelmed: absorbed, the way a larger silence swallows a smaller one. Her frost was gone and the water kept rising and she could not freeze this. She could not keep this.

And the not-water was not cold and was not hot. It was *nothing* — an absence so total that the body could not process it as sensation and reported it instead as pain, the kind with no location, because it was not damage. It was subtraction: the nervous system screaming about things it used to have and suddenly didn't. Sigrika's Royan tatau began to disappear, the spiralling ink that ran from her wrists to her shoulders — written on her skin by her grandmother's needle in a kitchen in Bjartr while her mother held her hand and hummed — dissolving at the extremities first. The oldest marks. The ones that meant *family* and *home* and *the valley remembers you,* going translucent, going absent, as though they had never been inscribed. The ends of her braids eroded — not cut, reduced, fibre by fibre, the careful plaits her mother had taught her coming undone from the tips upward. One step, two, and her shin went translucent to the knee and she could see the tile through her own leg. Three steps, and her fingers went, the bones showing through like the Kronaclaw's feathers through its breast.

Ahead of her the journal floated face-down with its stickers catching the last of the bioluminescence, its pages fanning open, the photographs going one by one — Sigrika's face dissolving out of image after image, four years of secret watching un-watched in reverse.

And Hiyuki stood at the threshold with her hands at her sides. Warm. Useless. This was the exact intersection she had named on the cliff — *whether you keep walking with it or set up house* — except that it was not an abstraction now, and not a lesson, and not something she was administering to a student with the competence of a shrine. It was a girl dissolving in front of her, with the frost swallowed and the century nothing and the office nothing. She had said *I will not carry you out.* She had meant it then and she meant it now, and the meaning cost her something she had not expected, which was the discovery that a hundred years of letting go had not actually prepared her for standing still while somebody she had walked two days beside walked into the dark on purpose.

She stood. She waited. It was all she had. It was all she had ever had.

Sigrika's hands closed on the journal, both of them translucent at the fingers, and she pulled it to her chest and the weight of it was almost nothing, because it was leaving too. The navy cover softened. The crest bled into the not-water one silver molecule at a time. She held it open and the pages turned themselves — not wind, not current, just the slow dissolution of a thing letting go of itself. The cafeteria, gone. The study room at midnight, gone. The courtyard with the pigeons, going. Every image going the way Denia had gone: not violently, just *away.*

The last page held longer than the others, as though the paper were heavier, or the truth were, or the period at the end of the sentence were an anchor the void needed an extra second to dissolve. Two girls in a cut photograph, shoulders almost touching. Denia's face caught in the act of the real smile. Sigrika's face mid-laugh, unaware she was being loved. The ink bled. The faces softened. The period at the end was the last mark on the page.

And her fingers tightened, because every instinct she owned said *keep it.* Every reflex, every trained response, every muscle that had spent twenty years gripping pens and ropes and runework and attendance records and candidacy forms and the straps of bags full of journals she filled because filling was the only proof she knew. *Keep it. Carry it out. It's proof. Without it you have nothing.*

And then: she had the fries.

The thought came sideways, the way the white bird had come through the kitchen window. Uninvited. Unscheduled. She had the double order of fries. She had the paragraph read four times. She had the specific seat, empty at minute one and occupied by minute six. She had two in the morning. She had the reading aloud. She had four years of somebody sitting across from her who never asked for anything, because asking meant admitting that the thing you have could be lost.

The fries were not in the journal. The fries were in her hands — in the reflex, in the automatic *double the order,* in the part of her that would walk into a cafeteria for the rest of her life and hesitate for one second before ordering for one. That hesitation could not dissolve. It was not kept in a drawer or wrapped in cloth or stamped with a seal. It lived in the muscle, in the marrow, in the place below memory where the body keeps what the hands have learned. Denia was in her hands. Not in the photographs — in the *hands.* And they did not need the paper, because the paper had never been the point.

*Best friend forever.* The period dissolved. The sentence held — not on the page, but in the place where Denia had always lived: the breath between speaking and silence, the seat that was warm before you noticed it was occupied.

Sigrika let go. Her hands opened, and the journal settled into the not-water and went down slowly, with the dignity of a thing returning to where it had been kept. The stickers caught the last light. The silver-marker cat with too many legs turned once in the current that was not a current. Then it was gone, and the dark closed over it, and the dark was where Denia had put it, and Denia's choices were hers.

Standing in the water to her waist, tattoos half-written, braids at her jaw, able to see through her own hands, she said: *Dasvidaniya.*

Her voice was nothing, a whisper into nothing. The word sat on the surface for exactly one second, the way a petal sits on ice, and then it was gone too, and it meant what it meant, and she had finally said it out loud.

She turned around. Three steps. Two. One. And each one cost her something she could feel and could not name — not pain, not subtraction, but *choice,* the weight of a body moving away from a thing it wants and toward a thing it needs, and she had spent her whole life avoiding the difference and was now walking through it with her ink gone and nothing in her hands.

She came out of the water and Hiyuki caught her. Not with frost. Not with the bell. With her hands, both of them, warm.

Her legs went and Hiyuki took all the weight and lowered her at the cavern's edge, where the forget-me-nots were going under one by one, blue after blue after blue swallowed into the dark. The facility drowned behind them: the fluorescents popping down the corridor in small bright deaths, each one taking a section of hallway into darkness, the refrigerator's hum cutting out, the break room going, the post-it about the fish going. The to-do list was already in Sigrika's pocket, because it had been outside the water when she took it, and it was ugly and institutional and it said *Subject D rations* in the handwriting of a person who had gone home and had dinner afterward, and it was proof, and it was the kind that mattered, because it was evidence of wrong, and wrong needs a witness.

The forget-me-nots were the last to go. They stood in the rising dark with their blue faces turned upward, blooming — the way the widow's ladder bloomed and the jasmine bloomed and the cerulean tree bloomed — because the bloom was not for anyone, and was not a record, and did not require a witness, and happened anyway, and was true anyway. Then the water closed over them and the dark went quiet.

*I saw it. Every page.* *Yes.* *It's gone.* *Yes.* *I let it go.* *Yes.*

Then the tears came — not the crying from the onigiri or the bent laughter from the pudding or the silent grief at the pillow, but something with no name in any language she spoke, because all her languages were built for keeping and this was the sound of somebody who had stopped. *She had photos of me. Four years. I was eating, and studying, and sleeping, and she was — every day, Hiyuki, every single—* *I know.* *And I can't prove it. There's no record. I can't show anyone, I can't—* *No.* And then, with a hand on the back of her head, warm and steady: *You do not need to prove it. You saw it. I was there. That is enough.* And: *It will not feel like enough. Not today. Not for a long time. And then one morning you will order fries and your hand will stop over the number, and you will know what you know, and no record in any archive will be more true than your hand.*

She pressed her face into Hiyuki's shoulder and breathed, and the breathing was ugly and broken and real, and Hiyuki held her, and the holding was not a century of practice and not an office and not a skill. It was a woman with warm hands holding a girl who had let go. And after a while — neither of them counted — Sigrika's fingers found her pocket, and found the wax paper, still creased, and the note, still real. Small. Ugly. Hers. Enough.

Then the water came up through the crack like a tide that had found its schedule — not fast, but with the patient inevitability of something that had been waiting below the world a long time and had finally been given permission to fill the space it was always meant to fill. Where it touched the bioluminescence the blue-green light went out, gently, the way a hand closes over a candle. *Climb.*

They climbed, and Sigrika went first because Sigrika always goes first on rock, and because her body had already decided, and because climbing was the one language she had never had to earn. Her fingers were translucent and she could see the rock through them when she gripped, and the spiral on her left forearm that meant *the valley remembers you* — her grandmother's first mark, the one that had hurt the most and meant the most — was gone, and where it had been there was bare unmarked skin, as though the needle had never touched her. She climbed anyway. The holds did not care about her ink. The rock did not care about her record. The crack was a chimney and she braced her back and walked her feet up the opposite wall the way she had done in Bjartr since she was nine.

She came out into the dry basin and rolled onto her back and breathed, with real dust under her shoulders and the false silver non-sky above her with its eye and its pulse, and it was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, because it was *up.* Hiyuki came out after her and brushed the dust off her sleeves with the economy of somebody who had been dishevelled before and saw no reason to make an event of it.

And the water followed. It came up through the crack the way it had come into the room, not rushing and not spilling but rising — a dark mirror climbing the walls of the fissure and cresting the edge and spreading across the cracked slate in a thin perfect sheet, and where it went the cracks sealed and the dust settled and the basin remembered what it was supposed to be.

A lake. Not the nothing-water from the facility. Something else. The void had eaten everything below — the tile, the fluorescents, the cabinet, the books, the operating table, the pillow, the sticker, the journal — and what it gave back was black glass. Still. Perfect. The surface so flat and so dark that it reflected the silver mist above it like a second sky, and between the two skies there was a girl sitting on the shore with her half-written arms around her knees.

The lake was made of her. It had always been made of her, and now it was made of everything that had been hers — the medicine, the manual, the fake painkiller, the exam with *Ask Sigrika* in the margin, the journal with four years of secret photographs, the forget-me-nots, the pillow tied with tape — all of it dissolved and reconstituted into dark water in a dark place. And it was not a grave and it was not a library and it was not a record. It was a lake, the size and shape of what had happened there, and it held everything and showed nothing, which is what Denia would have wanted, because Denia never once showed anyone what she was holding.

The translucence faded slowly, the way frostbite fades, her fingers regaining their opacity one at a time as the distance from the water grew. But the tattoos did not come back. The oldest spirals — *family, home, the valley remembers you* — stayed gone. What remained were the newer marks, the ones her grandmother had added the summer before the Academy: *the road ahead,* and *carry what you can,* and *come back different.* Half a story. The half that pointed forward. She looked at her arms a long time and then put them back round her knees.

Hiyuki sat down beside her, close, not touching. The frost around her boots had melted and she had not re-formed it.

The prepared speech was gone. The journal was gone. The photographs were gone. The search grid was gone. The three colours of ink were in a bag on the other side of two days of walking and might as well have been on the other side of the world. She had the wax paper. The note. Her hands. And a voice.

It started so quietly that Hiyuki almost missed it — not words, a hum, threadbare and toneless, the sound a person makes when they are not trying to make a sound but their body has decided to make one anyway, the way a body decides to breathe, or blink, or reach for a thing that isn't there. Then the hum found a shape, and the shape was four notes, and the four notes were the most ordinary melody in the world.

*Happy birthday to you.* Her voice cracked on *birthday.* She kept going — not because she was brave, but because the song is four lines long and she had started it and she is a girl who finishes things, even badly, even broken, even sitting on the shore of a black lake with half her ink gone and nothing to show for two days of walking except a crumpled piece of wax paper and a to-do list proving her best friend had been somebody's Tuesday errand.

*Happy birthday to you.* The second line was worse. Thinner. The tears were in it now, not falling, just *in* it, the way water is in a sponge.

*Happy birthday to—*

And she stopped. Not because she couldn't finish, but because the name was there in her mouth, on the edge of her tongue, and she had to choose how to say it. She could say it the way the to-do list said it: *Subject D,* clinical, filed, scheduled between trash and audit. She could say it the way the Academy records said it — a name on an enrolment form filled out from a manual by a girl who had never been enrolled in anything. She could say it the way her own journal said it: *DENIA was born,* stamped and sealed and made official by the authority of a candidacy that had given her the right to make things real.

She said it the way you say the name of somebody you love. Which is to say she said it the way she had always said it, across a cafeteria table, over a stack of borrowed notes, into the silence of two in the morning when neither of them was sleeping and both of them were pretending.

*Happy birthday, Denia.*

The name went out across the lake. It did not echo. The black glass took it the way it took everything — completely, without fuss, the way a girl takes a fry from a doubled order, without comment and without ceremony, because the taking is the whole of the acknowledgement and no further acknowledgement is required.

*Happy birthday to you.*

The last line. The worst line, because it was the end, and endings were the thing Denia had tried to make clean, and this one was not clean. It was scraped and broken and too quiet and it went flat on *you,* and it was the most honest sound Sigrika had ever made in her life, because it was not for an audience and not for a record and not for the pack and not for her parents and not for the professors who used her first name as though they had earned it. It was for a girl who never had a birthday until a friend gave her one, and who had put that friend to sleep before anyone could sing.

The song ended, and the lake held it, silently and completely, showing nothing on the surface and keeping everything below. And Hiyuki said nothing at all — not *that was enough,* not *she heard you,* nothing — because the silence after a birthday song is not empty. It is the space where the candle smoke goes, and the wish lives, and the breath that blew it out is still warm in the air, and you do not fill that space. You sit in it. You let it be the size it needs to be.

The first gift had been a birthday, stamped into existence by a girl who carried a seal everywhere because of course she did. The last gift was the song that goes with it, sung by the same girl three days late, with nothing in her hands, to a lake that was listening.

And far below — past the drowned facility, past the dissolved forget-me-nots, past the place where a journal full of secret love had settled into the dark it had been kept in and kept from — something that was not the Heartwood's eleven beats moved through the black water. One beat. Patient. Alone. The kind of rhythm that does not belong to a tree or a machine or a frequency calibrated by people who went home afterward. The kind that belongs to a heart.

Sigrika did not hear it. Hiyuki did not hear it. The lake kept it the way it kept everything, below the surface, in the dark, where the things that are not ready to be found wait until the route comes back around.

*Migratory.*

Then she stood up — not dramatically; the way you stand when your legs have finished resting and the ground beneath you has stopped being the destination — and brushed her knees with the inked hand, because the bare one was still strange to her and would be strange for a while, and looked at the lake once more, and the lake looked back with its black glass face, holding everything and giving nothing. She did not say goodbye. She had already said the only word she had for that, and it meant something else, and the something else was the point.

*Ready?* *No. Let's go.*

The return was faster, because the body knew the ground now and the hands knew the holds, and the route that had taken two days to find had been carved into her legs by the walking of it, the way a river is faster once it has found its channel and stopped looking for another one. The Heartwood's eye pulsed overhead and she counted one cycle and stopped counting. She went first up the scramble with the sure grammar of Bjartr, and they did not speak, and the not-speaking was comfortable now in a way it had not been on the first day — it had lost its shape as a lesson and become what it actually was, which is two people who have walked far enough together that the silence between them has been furnished. She walked past the cerulean tree without slowing down and without speeding up, and looked at it the way you look at a thing you have understood and do not need to solve. She passed the jasmine classroom and saw the lantern and the crane and twenty-three flowers in attendance, every one marked present, and did not go in, and inclined her head as she passed — a gesture that was new in her body and fitted anyway, like a coat borrowed from somebody taller that turns out to keep the rain off just the same. She passed the old man's bench, empty, still there, the imprint still in the seat, and thought about yellow custard and a salute and a man who had chased truth his whole life and never reached the door, and thought: *some doors you don't reach. Some doors you are.* And she did not write that down, and it was true anyway.

They came to the loop from the far side, which meant the seven walked toward them and the salute came first, and the small straight-backed woman said *May you return victorious,* and the words landed differently from that angle, which is the whole point of angles. Denia's figure walked at the far edge. Thin. A stain on the air. Chin level. Sigrika watched for one full fold. Then she turned and kept walking, and the keeping was a choice, and the choice was hers, and it cost exactly what it cost and not one gram more.

The widow's ladder had closed its flowers; thorns only, now. But she knew what they did at night. She had the scar on her finger to prove it, and the knowing was in her body, and she did not need to see them bloom again to believe they bloomed.

The Riseway swallowed them and gave them back, and the air turned warmer and wetter and carried the first real smells she had tasted in days — damp rock, mineral water, the faint vegetable sweetness of moss growing on a surface that receives actual rain. Her ears popped, and the sound of their boots came back to them doubled, and the doubling was company. She breathed real air and it tasted like nothing and it was the sweetest thing she had had since the pudding.

And then, quietly, before the gate: *Lucilla.* *What?* *She called them.*

Sigrika looked up. The gate was full of people.

Mei was leaning against the left gatepost with her arms crossed and her golden eyes fixed on the Riseway mouth, and she had been leaning there for three hours, which Iuno had told her was excessive and Lupa had told her was a fire hazard and which she had done anyway, because standing at gates is what she does and she was not going to stop doing it because a one-armed ex-prophetess had opinions about load-bearing architecture. Lynae sat on the ground beside her with her jacket over her knees. Chisa stood at the right gatepost with the scissor-blade folded against her back, in the particular stillness of somebody listening to the acoustics for the sound of a returning engine. Amy sat cross-legged in the grass six metres back with an unopened thermos in her lap. Iuno stood behind Mei with a hand on her shoulder, her blind eye toward the Riseway and her sighted one on Lupa.

And Lupa was holding a pot. Of course it was soup.

The bike came out — one rider white-haired and composed, with the frost absent from her boots; one smaller, leaning slightly forward, braids at her jaw. Mei pushed off the gatepost, took three steps, and stopped. Because the girl climbing off the bike was climbing off it under her own power, and Mei had learned — was learning, kept learning — the difference between catching somebody and standing where they can reach you.

They saw. Lynae saw first, because Lynae was close to the ground and looks from the feet up. The feet were fine. The arms were not: the ink half gone, the oldest marks erased, the remaining tattoos starting mid-forearm in the middle of words. *Road ahead. Carry what you can. Come back different.* The braids at her jaw. The bandage grey with rock dust. The bag lighter than when she left.

*…Your ink.* *I know.* *Your* hair. *I know.* *Hiyuki, what happened down—* *She took me to the lake.* And Lynae's mouth opened on a question and closed on the answer, because the answer was standing in front of her and did not require explanation.

Chisa, who has never seen a face, read it in threads. Fewer lines. Not none. But the ones that remained ran taut in a way the old ones hadn't — the particular tension of a string somebody has chosen to hold rather than one they have been hung from. *Lighter,* she said, to nobody, the way you note a change in weather.

Mei looked at Sigrika, and Sigrika looked at Mei, and Mei did the thing she is learning to do, the thing that costs her more than any leap or any sword: she stayed where she was. She did not cross the distance. She waited.

Sigrika walked to her. Three steps. Under her own power. Chin at the angle.

*She had a journal.* Mei's face did something complicated and honest and she held it together with the musculature of ten years of motherhood and the knowledge that some sentences require the listener to be a wall rather than a window. *Yeah?* *Photos. Four years of photos. All of us.* Her voice was even. Scraped raw, but even. *It's gone. The water took it.* *Okay.* *I let it go.* *Okay* — and the second one was different from the first, the way a hand on a shoulder is different from a hand held out, and Sigrika heard the difference, and leaned forward, and Mei held her.

Not caught. Held. The distinction mattered. They both knew it. Neither said so.

*Inside. Eat. Then talk,* said Lupa, setting the pot down with the authority of a woman who has built entire civilisations on the principle that the feeding comes first and everything else is architecture. *Your classes have been cancelled through Monday,* said Iuno, with the quiet authority of somebody who had cancelled them two days ago and would cancel more if needed and was not requesting permission for either.

Amy opened the thermos and poured a cup and put it into Sigrika's hands without ceremony and without eye contact, because Amy knows what it is like to come back with pieces missing, and the best thing anybody ever did for her was put something warm in her hands and not look at her while she drank it. Sigrika drank. Earl Grey, lukewarm. It tasted like a kitchen at 7:47 in the morning and an open window and a bird she had never managed to identify.

*I sang her happy birthday,* she said, to the tea, to the gate, to nobody in particular.

Lynae made the sound a person makes when they have been sitting on a stone floor for three hours and the girl they were waiting for comes back drinking tea and delivers the most devastating sentence of the week in the tone you would use to report a weather change. Then she stood and brushed her knees and put a hand on Sigrika's shoulder. *Good.* And: *Was it on key?* *No.* *Good* — and she meant it differently the second time.

Hiyuki parked the bike and stood slightly apart, the way she stands at the edge of everything. Not outside. At the threshold, because thresholds are where she works and always have been. *Thank you,* Mei said over Sigrika's head. *It was an errand. I was going there anyway.* *Uh-huh.* *Mm.* Mei smiled, small, the one that reaches the eyes. Hiyuki did not smile back, because Hiyuki does not smile — but the frost that had been absent from her boots stayed absent, and she did not check her hands, and she did not need to.

They went inside. Lupa led. The pot went first, because the pot always goes first. Through the east gate and past the greenhouse and up to the dormitory where a kitchen was waiting with a table big enough for all of them, and Sigrika walked in the middle of them the way water walks in a channel — not carried, not pushed, just finding the path that was already there.

At the door she stopped and looked at her arms: the half-written ink, the tattoos that start mid-word and point forward. *Road ahead. Carry what you can. Come back different.* Half a story — the half her grandmother had given her the summer before the Academy, for exactly this doorway, exactly this afternoon, exactly these people, holding soup and lukewarm tea and the specific silence of a perimeter that does not need to be named to be held.

*I need to call my mother.* *After soup.* *After soup.*

She went inside, and the door closed behind her. And somewhere past the greenhouse, at the exact angle where the light caught its wingspan, a white bird crossed the fake sky — small, quick, gone before the eye could name it — banking once, twice, and then behind the east building and out of sight.

Nobody was watching. It flew anyway.

### The marigolds

They came back on a Thursday, and Iuno had already cancelled classes through Monday, and there was soup. Chisa put her hands on Sigrika's shoulders and read her and gave the whole finding in one word. *Lighter.* Sigrika said she was going to eat, and then call her mother — the family whose marks the void had just taken off her arms.

And up on a terrace above them, in the artificial evening, Lucilla sat down across from a volunteer groundskeeper who was watering marigolds that were already drowning.

Lucilla had worked out who it was, and did not run, and did not call security, and poured two cups of institutional Academy ceramic instead. What she got in exchange was the closest thing to an accounting that the Grand Architect of the Fractsidus has ever given anyone.

*I built her to resonate with a Threnodian. I calibrated her frequency. I designed the collar. I gave her the handbook and the ration packs and the operating table.* And then, precisely: *I did not give her the inclination to put stickers on things. That was hers. That was — entirely hers.* *And that fascinates you.* *Of course it fascinates me.*

She has been alive longer than the institution she was sitting in. She has watched civilisations build themselves and tear themselves down and build again, and she has steered the ones that mattered, and steered the ones that didn't anyway, because the steering is the point. *I have worn — I have been — everyone. I have been the friend and the enemy and the stranger on the road and the dead thing in the river and the god at the end of the hallway. I have been every face that has ever been needed.* The groundskeeper's eyes were brown. The thing behind them was not.

And then the wound, dropped onto the bench between them like a coin landing on its edge.

*She cut me out of the photograph.*

Three girls under a flowering ash, and *best friends forever* written by a student with a marker and underlines and the heartbreaking conviction that documenting a thing makes it true. Denia had her own copy in the journal. And she took a scalpel — a proper one, from the surgical tray, *I taught her precision, at least* — and cut the Architect out of it, following the line of her shoulder, her arm, her hair. Very careful. Very deliberate.

*And kept Sigrika.* *And kept Sigrika.* And then: *Best friend forever. Singular. With a period.*

*That hurt.* *Don't be ridiculous.* *That's not a denial.* *It's a redirection. I assumed you'd recognize the technique; your faculty meetings are full of them.*

So Lucilla did the scoring out loud. *You won.* *I'm aware.* Denia died on schedule; the seam is sealed; the frequency discharged exactly as calibrated; the weapon performed its function; and the Collective has a new lake and a classified file and a committee that will never produce a finding.

*And you lost.* *You lost because the weapon had preferences.* Because she built a vessel and it developed taste. It liked fries. It didn't like mornings. It kept a journal full of a girl it loved and hid it in a drawer with no handle, and when it chose who to keep and who to cut out, it chose the one the Architect considered furniture.

*I never considered her furniture* — and the speed of the correction told Lucilla everything the words didn't. *I considered her — backdrop. Set dressing.* A competent, earnest, hardworking girl who would provide a plausible social context and could be relied on to follow the script, because following scripts is the only thing anyone had ever taught her to do.

*She didn't follow the script.*

She walked onto the Dimmr Plains and found the facility and stood in the room where a girl was strapped to a table and had a collar put in her chest. *Yes.* *And she came back.* *Yes.* And then, with the delight coming back into it and something underneath the delight showing through like bedrock through a field: *She came back lighter. Isn't that — isn't that remarkable. She went into the worst room I've ever built, and she held the proof of what I did to her friend, and she let it drown, and she came back lighter.* *She sang happy birthday. Off-key, apparently.* *Of course off-key. She's from Bjartr.*

Then Lucilla offered her the one thing she had. *You could go back.* *Don't.* *Sit in the study room. Borrow her pens. Be ordinary.*

*Don't.*

And for the first time the charm was gone and the wit was gone and the quicksilver deflection was gone, and what was left was a single flat syllable from a woman who had been alive for centuries and had just been offered simplicity.

Because that was the part she could not get past, and she said it plainly: *I sat in study sessions with that girl for two years. I borrowed her pens. I ate lunch with her. I talked about whether the artificial sky was getting its blues right. I called her little bird.* And: *in two years of sitting beside that girl, she never once asked me what I wanted.* She asked about birds. She asked about cafeteria food. She asked if she'd finished the reading. Seven hundred small questions and not a single important one, *because she is exactly the kind of person who will let you be ordinary, and that is…* — and she did not finish it.

Then she watered the drowning marigolds anyway, because the disguise required it, or because old habits did, or because a woman who had called a girl *little bird* for two years had learned what a watering can was for and had not yet unlearned it. And she left a standing order behind her. *Keep an eye on her.* *On Sigrika.* *On the little bird. She is becoming — very interesting.* *She was always interesting. You just weren't looking.*

*I'm looking now.*

She walked away past the greenhouse, and by the time she reached the corner she was nobody again, and nobody turned the corner, and nobody was gone.

Lucilla picked up the empty cup — standard Academy ceramic, chip on the rim, a residue ring; the only proof it had happened — and took out the communicator she keeps disguised as a makeup mirror, because some traditions are worth keeping, and sent one line to Hiyuki and to Mei.

*She's still here. She's watching the girl.*

Then she carried both cups inside, because the machine needed refilling and the flock needed tending, and somewhere on her campus a woman with no face was watering flowers that were already drowning — and the drowning was a kindness, and the kindness was real, and that was the most frightening thing Lucilla had learned all week.

Nobody has told Sigrika.

The terrace emptied and the artificial evening went on pretending the sky was real, and the marigolds grew in it anyway, because marigolds don't check whether the sun is genuine before they bloom. They just bloom.

And somewhere in the space between one face and the next, a woman who had been everyone stopped in a corridor she had no reason to be in, beside a classroom she had no reason to remember, and pressed two fingers to the glass of a window that looked out onto a courtyard where a flowering ash still stood. The gesture lasted exactly one second. She did not look at the bench beneath the tree where three girls had once sat together in the sun.

She didn't need to. She had cut herself out of that photograph a long time ago.

She just hadn't expected someone else to finish the cut.

## VI. FORCE MAJEURE: WHAT THE REST OF THE WORLD DID ABOUT IT

Nobody in Lahai-Roi was tracking the consequences. Six women had toppled a theocracy, woken and buried a god, won a foreign nation's highest games, unsealed a drowned city and enrolled in school, and the world's institutions were each, separately, quietly reorganising themselves around the news.

In Mingting, in the Secretariat of the Seven Cities, a junior clerk named Wen brought the Western File to Minister Shen Qiu, seventy-one, veteran of three Magistrates. The ballad of the six had become a Mengzhou teahouse opera in three acts — *The Maiden of Two Names*, in which the god dies at the end of the second act and the heroine goes home in the third — and the Ministry of Rites wanted it suppressed as theological contagion. Shen, who had spent a career watching this exact mistake, ordered the Secretariat to commission it instead: imperial stage, unedited text, the Rites Ministry invited and seated well. *A banned song grows a second verse in the dark,* he said. *A story you host is a guest. A story you bar is a landlord.* Then he ran into the second problem, which was that protocol had no address for the six — they had crowned nobody and held no office — and solved it by precedent, citing a harbourside bench in Septimont that was wide enough for nine. He sent sealed Huanglong tea to the Septimont harbourmaster with a single line attached: *Whenever the road brings you east, the kettle is on.* Wen filed it as *Precedent, new.* The parcel spent eleven weeks under a harbourmaster's WAIT note before it reached a dormitory table underground and started a pack drinking.

In Jinzhou — the youngest gate of Huanglong, sitting in its bowl of water and mountain with its lamp-grid going gold below the dam — the young Magistrate Jinhsi stood on the crest wall at night with General Hou and reread a Federation annex. At a foreign gate, a director had given the textbook order, *Seal it. Save the city,* and her officer had frozen and said *Don't make me be the one who locks the door,* and a technician had said *wait, look,* and in the minutes that bought, the impossible arrived; the gate held eleven extra minutes and the sortie came home, and the director then reversed her own order in front of her whole command: *Hold.* Hou read it as reckless luck. Jinhsi read it as a command culture built on purpose, in which subordinates could be heard and a commander could be seen changing her mind, and amended her own doctrine on the spot. Contingency Nine — the order that drowns the lower city to save the upper — would from now on be given from the north wall, in the lower city's sight, never from the Heights: *If I drown those streets I will do it looking at them.* Two hundred breaths, fixed, between order and release, drilled into every child. The hand on the sluice wheel to be lower-city born, so that it is heavy. And then the lake-god Jue spoke into her chest — *COUNT THE RETURNING* — and she added a fourth clause: after any release, and after any hold, the first census is of who came back. She sealed it at dawn under two calendars, the second reading *third year of the open gate.*

In Xuanfang, at the Mengzhou frontier court, the Bulwark Commission petitioned to scrap the Generation-Three automata over memory anomalies. The case study was unit XF-114, forty years on the line, which boils a kettle every night and sets out one cup for a garrison cook named Sergeant Ma Delun, who shared dusk tea with it for twenty-two years and died in spring, retired, at home. The garrison has rotated. The unit still boils the kettle. Hsin, the nine-tailed Sentinel who taught Mengzhou its doll-craft, offered the court her oldest teaching-proverb and told them it had never been a proverb: *Everything that keeps faith long enough begins to resemble the faithful.* Magistrate Dou Lian ruled that Generation Three retires rather than scraps, to rear posts with honours; that failed cores are interred and named by what soldiers actually call them instead of by serial number; and that harmless anomalies are to be left alone. Hsin went down at dusk to inspect and found that XF-114 had built itself an iron tea-stand out of shell fragments and was pouring one cup, facing west. She drank it. *Tomorrow, set out two.*

In Ashinohara, Minister Katagiri Sōma sat with the childlike thing that has been bound to his family's alcove for a hundred and forty years, which his grandfather introduced him to when he was six with the words *While she is pleased to stay, the house stands,* and whose payment has been three generations of Katagiri luck. Unprompted, she confessed the arrangement's real mechanism. The family's flagship achievement — his grandfather's Frequency Safety act, the law that collars marked children — had been designed with her help, and she had shaped the collar herself: bare alloy, no felt lining, because comfort dulls the lesson. *The collar was the press. Someone of mine is always at the dock.* Three generations of frightened, exiled young people had been her harvest. And for the first time in her existence the orchard was failing, out-competed by a story with no army and no agents at all. *Someone told a better story, Minister.* She offered the old terms: the luck continues if he stalls four collar-reform bills in the Diet. His unmarked granddaughter had recently begun humming the ballad and asking pointed questions. He refused, opened the storm shutters that had been closed nightly for a hundred and forty years at her request, and said: *The bills proceed on schedule. The house will take its weather as it comes.* She did not leave. She was delighted — genuinely surprised, a first in fourteen decades — and stayed to see how it ends, and he poured her tea freely, as a host, for the first time in his life.

In the New Federation, ninety floors above the lawless zones, Director Anselm Vey of the Vey Directorate asked the philanthropist Ilona Vess why her foundation was secretly funding an underground shrine to the six while declining the naming rights. Context: Baldur had just been reclassified from asset to sovereign and every acquisition scenario withdrawn — *One doesn't acquire something that has demonstrated preferences* — and forty-one unauthorised shrines had appeared in the zones below, with the removal crews quietly giving up. Vess confessed. Her fortune, like most sound institutions, had compounded for generations on despair; then she watched footage of a girl fall from a god and be caught, ran the numbers, and concluded that the story is the asset class and hope has begun compounding. She was hedging. Vey filed the official line — *The parasite is diversifying* — and a private one he circulated to nobody: *Watch the hedge. Hedges grow.* He had also just reopened a file on Lucilla, Bureau Nine's lost star, a vanished spymaster now writing to him on a school's letterhead from a city inside a giant, whose cipher read: *The rot goes higher than you've dared write down. Follow the philanthropies. — L.*

And on the lake at night, a fox and a lake-god met on the water and talked about the opera. Jue, who has guarded the youngest gate since the first wall and believed that guarding was the whole of his purpose, described watching Jinhsi's amendment go into force, and the lower city laughing through its two hundred breaths, and the children learning a sluice-song that a foreign garnish-singer had left them. *I FIND I AM PROUD,* he said. *THE FEELING IS INEFFICIENT. IT PERSISTS.* Hsin matched it with her automata teaching themselves grief and hospitality, confirmed that the six were, in fact, in school, and delivered her verdict on the true third act: all of this had been caused by six women doing homework. Then she left, and Jue went back to his long arithmetic with one permanent new line in it. *Count the returning.*

## VII. THE WIND THAT CAME LOOKING

In a Jinzhou that no longer exists, the library burned for three days. The fire took the stacks first and the reading rooms after, and on the third morning the wind changed and carried the ash out over the lake, and the lake accepted it page by unwritten page. The sluices failed and the lower city stood under water to the second-floor windows, and boats went down streets where a girl had once been taught the names of vegetables. She pulled a patroller's cap out of that water, red as fruit, carried it a year, and gave it at last to a shrine that accepted such things. Afterward people said the city *held*, the way you say a rope held, meaning: look at your hands.

She left when the counting was done, and walked out the way she had been taught to walk — alone, forward, into whatever was loudest — and a wrongness travelled with her the whole way: a compass needle under the sternum, swinging steadily toward a person she had never met. She knew the feeling's name the way you know a word in a language you have lost. *Someone important.* Someone she had been meant to find on a shore, at a certain hour, in a story that went another way.

There is a post in a courtyard in Mingting, iron-hard wood, black-grey all the way through, and a guest of her family once cut it three inches deep with a wooden sword and set that mark as the measure of *ready.* She cut at that post for two years and left home with the measure unmet, and at the checkpoint her teacher patted her head and gave her the sentence she has kept longer than her own name: there are things in this world you will never do — *walk on, all the way.* So at the edge of the world she finished the arithmetic her teacher had started in that courtyard. You give the stroke everything, and what the wood refuses, you *become.* She set the person down, and unbound herself, gladly, with no road back, into the one traveller every border admits. Wind is the difference between two pressures, given somewhere to go. She had been a difference all her life. The rest was letting go of the shape.

What lies between stories is a dark that reads you. It turned her over like a bookseller checking a spine and let her pass. She would say afterward — where *say* means shape steam, tilt a page — that she passed fourteen shelves, spines out, each one a whole telling, and that on one shelf a light had been left on. She carried exactly two things across: a sentence, which was an address — *the Gorges of Spirits. Let her wake there* — and her mother's seashell with a tide sealed inside, which survived the road because a tide is already a kind of wind. The crossing keeps what is made of sound. It took the rest. It took, among other things, most of her name.

Then the lamp above the shore, and here the record splits, and the frontier court has declined to rule on it. The Woodblock Edition says she came late, and found the shore already lit and a woman kneeling in the surf with the sleeper in her arms, silver-blue hair stuck to her cheek, saying a borrowed name into wet hair as if trying it for size; the wind circled the beach twice, and on the second pass the address dissolved in her grip like sugar in rain. The Movable-Type Edition says she came early, and the sea itself was strung — one string run out across the whole dark of the water, tuned low and singing — and the sleeper turned toward the sound the way sleepers turn toward a window, and the wind set her shoulder against the drifting woman and pushed east all night and lost, because you can lean on wind and wind moves, while a note stays exactly where it is sung. The Burned Edition is missing its crossing chapter entirely: the pages stop mid-line at the lamp and resume with the wind already grieving, and the founders of Bookbinders' Row report that the type for the missing passage was bought out of every case in the city across a single spring, by a customer who paid in very old coin and wore, they all agree, a different face each visit.

What all three agree on is that the going was accepted and the errand was refused.

She tried once more, later — followed the story's scent south to a moon that turned out to be a machine above a warrior city, slipped in through its seams, and reached at last for a golden-eyed woman's hand — and came apart at the touch, cold air gone the instant skin met it, and spent a season in the hills reassembling. She took the lesson the hard way, which was the only way she had ever taken anything. She cannot be delivered by hand. She will have to be read.

So she went to Mengzhou, the city where things are made to stand for other things, because wind frays and Xu — 虛, the hollow art, the printing of what is absent — is the one cloth a wind can wear. The gate wards there are cut against 心傀, heart-puppets, wants that stand up and walk, and a want with one desire left is exactly what they were built to stop; they read her all the way down and parted, like a crowd making way for something it recognised. The city lent her a shape at interest, paper-pale, off-register at the edges whenever she forgot herself, good for an evening at a time.

In a teahouse off the Row she stood at the back through nine nights of *The Maiden of Two Names.* She watched a painted saint drop a grey cloak in a square. She watched a priestess in blue silk step off a high perch like falling water. And in the third act, when the woman with the painted gold eyes walked past her kneeling prophet without turning, the wind went so still that three lanterns by the door guttered and the stage-master looked up and found only his audience, weeping happily, exactly as designed. She knew that walk. She had followed it across a ruined country on a compass needle alone. She lifted a programme sheet off an empty stool with a nudge of air and read the character list twice, and there it stood, set in honest type — the name the needle had been swinging toward since before she had a sternum for it to live under.

**Mei.** Awake. Loved — the opera was insistent on the point, the entire second act ran on it. And far: past four seas, under a mountain, in a city lit by a sun its people had built themselves.

The story had gone on without her. Stories do. It is their best quality and their worst. At a table near the stage, a girl in a student's uniform hummed the entr'acte four bars before the musicians reached it, perfectly, the way you hum a thing you have heard fourteen times. The wind gave her a wide berth on the way out. The girl smiled at the door anyway. At the weather.

Up on the shrine terrace above the frontier court, Hsin was already lying on the warm stones with a stick of candied hawthorn, reading the evening the way other people read dockets. *There you are. The gate wards filed a report on you in the passive voice. I flag everything written in the passive voice. Sit. Have a hawthorn.* A beat. *Ah. Forgive me. Habit.* Then the only question that mattered: *Which are you, then. A visitor out of another book — or our own last chapter, blown loose, and come back around to the front?* The court dislikes both filings. Hsin gave her verdict anyway: *You kept faith past the body, little gust. What you resemble now is a woman. The court can work with that.*

And then she offered the mercy Mengzhou keeps for the bereaved and rations like one. They could print it. Yesterday's moon. A Jinzhou that stands. A table at a certain restaurant with the lamps on and two particular idiots mid-argument. A room over the garden and an evening that reprints itself at dawn, forever. People do it. The house asks one thing only, which is that you know what it is.

She took one evening. The house set it faithfully: Panhua's back room, the good table by the window, oil and vinegar and the crispy thing done right, Chixia mid-story with her hands wide and the story growing as it went, Baizhi's dry correction landing on the exact pause, and a third seat kept between them. She sat in it. The tea steamed, and the steam had the correct shape and an absent smell. When the story came round again, Chixia's best line arrived a second time with the same fresh delight, word for word, like a block re-inked; and on the shelf by the window, in the printed version of the hat, the little stitched bird sat a hair off-register, its red just outside its outline, forever.

She stayed until the lamps were trimmed. She laughed in the right places. Both of them.

Then she rose and thanked the house and paid, because Xu is set in type and type is paid in type, and the house takes characters. She opened her hand — which is the Row's figure of speech; what she opened was herself — and the founder lifted each sort out with bamboo tongs and read it aloud as it went into the tray, because that is the courtesy there. You hear what you spend. Then the tray clicked shut.

*The plate,* she said. Her voice cost the room its candle-flames. *Break it.*

The founder bowed and asked whether that was for everyone, since the Jinzhou plate is popular. Wind moved in the rafters, considering, with one whole street of Chixia's laugh still warm in the block.

*…Keep the moon for those who stayed. Break my table.*

What she bought with what was left was a kite: red paper, light bamboo, a long tail against crosswinds, and a string she spun herself out of what a wind has for thread, which is to say out of staying. To the spine she bound the shell. The letter was the last thing, and it was already written, and it belonged to another woman.

Because some weeks before — the Row's calendar and the wind's disagree — a window in Jinzhou had stood open on a gusty night, and on the desk inside, weighted with an inkstone, a letter lay finished at last after a season of salutations: *To the one the wind is looking for.* The girl who wrote it had gone to bed with her hat on the bedpost and the lamp turned low, the way you leave a door unlocked while declining to say for whom. By morning the page lay exactly as she had left it, ink dry, stone in place. And the letter was also, somehow, gone. The paper stayed; the letter left. The Row makes that distinction without blinking — a text can be lifted off its page the way a tune is lifted off a throat — and the wind had carried the reading of it east in her ribs for weeks, word-perfect, and now the founders set it back into type, begging her pardon at each blank where her own spent characters should have gone, and setting the girl's whole, and printed one fair copy on paper light enough to fly. An edition of one, bound beneath a shell, beneath a red kite, addressed to a woman under a mountain, in a city where kites have nothing to ride.

Hsin came down to the Row to see it off, which the founders will be retelling for a generation. *When the war god comes to the youngest gate,* she said, conversationally, to the weather, *my line walks. They're old, the third generation, and they'll go anyway. Faith is what they're made of; I did that on purpose. But dolls hold walls. Finishing wars wants a different craft. Bring back many hands, little gust. Bring back the kind that catch.* The kite went up red against the first stars and the whole street came out to watch, because in Mengzhou everyone knows what it means when a kite climbs against the wind's own grain: the wind is carrying it personally. By then the register noted that her remaining sorts numbered fewer than a sentence.

### The house that learned it was watching

An unsigned letter with a year's run of new coin in it reached the stage door of Master Ping's teahouse after the two-hundredth night of the *Maiden.* *You staged the Maiden well. The god dies on schedule and the house cries on cue. Craft. Now write me the wind. You have a year. Spend what you like. — a friend of the house.* And a postscript: *The second act is where it will break. Break it beautifully.* Master Qu of the Plum Rain wrote underneath it that every subject yields to structure — a city had yielded, a saint had yielded, a god yields nightly at the interval to music, wind is air with a habit, and he had broken habits for a living for thirty years. Much later, ink almost dry in the brush, he wrote beneath that: *I was thirty years wrong about what I do for a living.*

He collected testimony for a year. The sixteen-year-old door attendant had seen her nine nights, standing at the back by the door hinge, where the fire warden's stool stayed empty — eight nights it stayed empty, and on the ninth it was warm. She watched act two, the tea act, the cold-cup scene, the window scene, and leaned in for those, both shows, the same lean, like checking a stitch. She left at the interval every night, before the war drums. On the ninth night she stayed, and nothing had been changed, and when the lanterns came up the door stood open and the stool was warm and there was a coin in the box that had no business being there — a Jinzhou half-copper worn smooth, like it had crossed water in somebody's shoe. Master Ping nailed it over the door for luck and dusts it himself. And once, on that ninth night, she made a sound: at the line where the priestess says *I'm selfish*, the third time it comes round, where the house always sighs — a breath from the back, a laugh the size of a moth. Ping thought a bat had got in. *It wasn't the sigh line to her. It was the funny one.* Qu's margin: *She laughed where the widows sigh. File under: knows something the house does not.*

He went west to the Xuanfang line, to the post the garrison calls the parting-tree, where a pear grows through the wall with its roots on the fighting side and its crown on the home side, and pear and parting share a sound in the old tongue, so the soldiers hang nothing on it and sweep beneath it daily. The unit there is third-generation, forty-one years on the wall, and the rule is that you may ask it anything and it answers with what it keeps. It banked its core when he named his subject, and petals came down off the pear out of season, and then it spoke in her voice. *My teacher set a mark I never reached. I reached everything else by aiming at it.* A county called Qixi, where the flood took the bridge and she carried the children over in eleven trips, and when the water went down the village asked her to leave before the funerals, because an outsider's luck shames the drowned — and she understood it, and dug the graves that night from the far bank, and left before the ground was light enough to read. A siege where a general broke the line a day after she broke it, and they made an opera of him within the year, and it was very good, and she saw it twice and sat where she could watch the audience. Then the sergeant said, quietly, that there was one it saved, and the unit's tone changed to a night-time register, a voice with its armour off: *There is a woman I was made to find. In another —* a gap, held to the length of a breath, because the unit reproduces her pauses — *she is loved the way harvests are loved. Some nights I hate her. Then the wind changes.* It plays that one at the third watch, some nights, to itself. The first time it did, the whole post stood to. Now the watch just lets it finish. The sergeant took Qu round to the shield-side and showed him a vent kept open a hand's width, against a standing order that shields seal at dusk, entered in the court's register as *modification — vent, one; combat function — void; filed under — faith.* She sheltered there through a bad winter and talked all night, some nights. Old Seven has held the door for her since. You would have to scrap it to close it. And when Qu asked the question the whole country asks its walls — why the Drum sits over the youngest gate, why war of all countries keeps house in this one — the unit answered in her voice, evenly: *It came for the same reason I did.* Then, unprompted, to his back at the gate, the line he set down entire because it was the whole opera: *Every wind is met once. She will meet me once. Let it be a good wind.*

He hired a fight-master out of Septimont, twenty years of arena floors, a man who could make a duel out of two chairs and a grudge. *Where's the bout?* *There's a war god.* *And she draws on it when?* *She endures it.* *For how long?* *That's the play.* He quit by dinner and kept the advance, which was fair, and turned round at the door to say the quiet part at full volume: *In my country we'd have built her a statue by now.* *In hers,* Qu said, *they trimmed her a lamp.*

A seven-year-old on the shrine street told him about the lantern-store fire, in festival season, with paper everywhere. And the fire stood up, tall as the shrine gate, because fire loves wind the way bad boys love an audience. And the wind went away — fast — *Like when Ma burns the rice and says nothing and cooks it again.* Three hours later it rained, out of nothing, all night, on that one street only; Auntie Two-Doors was furious, her street got smoke and theirs got clean roofs. And the rain-wind came back after and dried all their shoes, in rows. The Almanac Bureau files single-street rain from a clear sky as 賠禮雨, the apology rain: recorded five times in two hundred years, each instance following a fire, and a wind that fled it. Qu's margin: *A being whose element feeds the one disaster she cannot stand before. She has an enemy she strengthens by approaching. Then the labour: three valleys of sky hauled over one street, all night, for shoes. The apology outweighing the fault five to one. That is her arithmetic. That is the whole ledger of her.*

Master Ping put a notice by the door. THE HINGE CUP — one copper. A cup of clear tea, poured hot and left by the door hinge, for the guest; paid cups chalked on the frame; strokes wiped at dawn; the house makes nothing on it. Ask for wind tea. It started with a bargeman who had heard the coin story, then his whole crew, then everyone, and after a *Maiden* night they sometimes run out of frame. Asked whether the cup ever empties, the attendant said it cools, and that cooling is drinking, for some guests, and that Master Ping says a house should know what it's pouring for. Qu's margin: *A whole city buying tea for an absence. Somewhere across the water, I am told, a nation once did its believing in cathedrals. We do ours by the door hinge, one copper at a time, and wipe the ledger every dawn so it stays a gift.*

His first draft was called *The Sword of the East Wind* and had a thunder machine and the Drum descending on the fly-lines, credibly enormous. He read it to the attendant, who listened all the way through out of politeness, which should have warned him, and then said: *She'd leave at the interval.* Struck. Burn the thunder machine. His second draft was called *The Ghost of Ten Thousand Doors*, all willows and unswept thresholds and a weeping part in every scene and a moon, and it went to the commissioner by courier and came back the same week, rewrapped, one line on the wrapper in that swift small hand: *She laughed once. Where is the laugh?* His margin: *How does she know that. I have checked. The testimony went into the trunk the day it was taken. I told nobody. I told the trunk.*

The third draft has no title and is one page. ACT TWO. The stage stands empty, house lamps banked to coals, doors unbarred. For the length of a meal, the audience hears wind. Whatever enters, enters. When a lamp relights of itself, act three may begin. If every lamp stays dark, the play is over: the house refunds the coppers at the door and apologises for the weather, and the audience goes home having seen the true version, which is the one where you wait. The impresario wrote back that he had read act two and it was a draft of his bankruptcy, that the *Maiden* runs because a god dies in it on stage to music at the same hour nightly, and that his patrons can get weather outside where it is free and there is food. *Come back when you have a duel. Or a death. Or, gods forgive us both, a wedding. P.S. The hinge cup stays on the menu.*

So Qu went east, told Ping it was for the mountain air, and stood on the public stair below the dam before dawn with the fishermen while the beacons went out one by one. There is a girl who stands at the northeast shoulder at first light, Outrider's badge, a hat with a little bird stitched at the brim, checking the horizon the way you check a platform — for someone particular, on no schedule she could name, and she would blush if you asked, and buy you breakfast to change the subject. He knows because he asked, and she did, and the fried dough was excellent. He went back the next dawn and kept his distance and his trade's oldest skill, which is watching. The wind came off the water at the grey hour, crossed, climbed the cliff face, and stopped on her cheek. Like a hand. She closed her eyes and stood for it the way you hold still for a shy animal, and it stayed a moment past its own momentum, and then she said one word to it, too low for the stair, meant for one listener in all the world, and the wind took the word and went up the sky with it like a spark going home to its fire. *That scene is hers. I leave it where I found it.*

His accounting for the year: thirty years of making things happen on schedule, and he had taken a rich girl's coin to do it to the weather — but her second act was already running, nightly, at the youngest gate, admission free, and the audience slept through it, and the fault was the house's, never the play's. *I set out to stage a wind. The wind is staged. What it lacks is a house that knows it is watching. Very well. I have spent my life building houses that watch. I will go home and open the doors.*

The Plum Rain now throws its doors wide before every performance, house lamps dipped, one breath, then the play. Qu called it the guest bar. Ping bills it as ventilation. The audiences have learned to go quiet for it, and some of the old aunties bow to the doorway, and nobody has explained anything to anybody. On top of the drafts in the trunk sits a card in the commissioner's hand: *Keep everything. She'll want them someday. — the friend of the house. P.S. You broke it beautifully.* The last page in the trunk is a single sheet titled ACT TWO, otherwise blank, with one word beneath the title in the master's smallest hand: **running.** The night porter reports that the trunk keeps its peace on windy nights; it is the still nights it rattles on. Asked about this on his way out the door, hat going on, Qu answered: *Stillness is how a wind sits down.*

### The year kept in a second ink

Meanwhile the girl on the wall was keeping her own record, ruled between the lines of an Outrider's patrol log, in a second ink, because an Outrider files what repeats and she trusted the trade: name the pattern, and the pattern must eventually introduce itself.

Yangyang of the Mingting registry, Jinzhou posting, works patrols with Chixia, who arrives mid-sentence with a bag already going translucent with oil, and Baizhi, who slides a vial of Huaxu's new formulation to Chixia's elbow and informs her that colds resolve in ten days and she has run out of excuses. Yangyang eats her lunch and lets the two temperatures argue across her, warm and cool, the way weather argues over a valley and the valley grows things anyway.

The season's incident ledger held oddities. A Tacet Discord's claw turned aside mid-strike by a gust, indoors. A rope bridge over the gorge that held through a squall because — the surviving porter swore on his mother — the wind leaned on the knot. An Outrider shoved clear of a falling beam by air that arrived with intent. The patrols wrote *weather* in the cause column, and Baizhi had begun, in her private notes, to underline the word.

At the first thunder Yangyang killed her third echo of the day out of a seed bin with Chixia's back in front of it, in the way the world had taught her in Yuezhou, where everything slows and simplifies down to a line and a length and a wrist. Afterward she untied her hair ribbon at the trough and washed it. The ribbon was clean. She washed it anyway, and Baizhi watched the whole procedure from the cart with the expression she saves for symptoms and wrote something small in the corner of the incident form. Then they walked home along the willows in the first warm hour of the year with the catkins out and the river loud with snowmelt, and the wind found her at the second bridge, and arrived at her cheek and stayed past the excuse of physics. *You're early,* she said, under Chixia's argument about lunch.

On the sweeping-day the trio split three ways as they always do — Chixia south to her family plot hauling enough food for a garrison because the dead eat first and she didn't make the rule; Baizhi to the Huaxu memorial wall with white chrysanthemums and a list of teachers, standing there exactly as long as the list required, which was long; and Yangyang, whose dead sleep in Mingting where her sister sweeps for both of them, up to the wall's unowned altar, the one for soldiers whose families the wars misplaced. She swept it and laid out oranges and lit incense in the wet grey air, and then sat back on her heels and asked the question nobody on that wall had ever asked. *Everyone tells you theirs. The whole wall. Keeper Pei talked your ear off for three watches, he brags about it. Whose do you carry?* The smoke stood straight for a breath, then bent — smoothly, the way a finger points when the pointing is meant kindly — and it bent at her. She held very still. Then she bowed to the smoke, the full mourner's bow, forehead to the wet stone. *Then they're mine too. Today.* She walked down in the rain, and behind her, in a wind that could have taken it a hundred times, the incense burned to the end of its stick with every thread of smoke rising straight, as if the weather had cupped its hands around a small flame and was holding, and holding, and holding.

By early summer she had the price list. Scaffolding above the cooper's lane, a rotten lashing, a load of roof tile going over with two apprentices under it — and the gust arrived late and ragged at the edges and shoved the load a body's width sideways so it came down on cabbages, and in the same instant every banner in the market dropped dead on its pole. A whole street of stillness, sudden as a held breath. The fruit-seller fanned herself and complained the day had broken. Yangyang stood in the stopped air and did the arithmetic.

That night she sat on her windowsill with her arms crossed. *I saw the banners.* The curtain moved a very small amount. *I'm an Outrider. Danger is my wage. I signed for it. You're out there spending yourself on my portion, and I—* the report voice ran out — *I left home to carry my own weight. It's the whole of what I am. You of all weather should know that.* The lamp flame leaned toward the window and forgot to wander. *…Fine. New terms. If you're going to haunt me, do it like a person.* And she stood up, decided, the way she decides everything, all at once and permanently. *Come to dinner.*

At Panhua's the next evening she set a third bowl at the end of the table and filled it and said nothing, and the table conducted its own investigation. *Are we feeding your someone now?* said Chixia. *Bold. I respect it. Does your someone like the crispy thing? Everyone likes the crispy thing* — and she put a piece in the bowl so it wouldn't go to waste, and then left it there. *Caloric intent noted,* said Baizhi, and moved the bowl three finger-widths out of the draught from the door, which put it directly into a different draught, the one that had no business existing, and said nothing further, and underlined something in her private book. The steam off the third bowl leaned all through dinner, one way, like a flame with company.

That same week, on the wall at first light on the fortieth stroke of the cutting forms, her guard drifted the old half-inch — the flaw her teacher used to fix with two fingers and a sigh — and a pressure arrived at her right elbow. Exact. Patient. The correction. She froze mid-form. Then she ran the form again and drifted the guard on purpose, watching the air, and the pressure came back and stayed a fraction longer, the way you hold a correction for a student you suspect of being clever. She lowered the sword and looked out at the lake for a long time, and that night she wrote three words in the second ink and ruled a box around them. *Same school. Senior.*

Then the great heat came in and the stillness stayed for twenty-one days. Cicadas owned the city, the lake lay flat as a poured mirror, and up at the yamen the Magistrate slept badly, because Jinhsi dreams things and in the still weeks she dreamed nothing at all, and it turned out nothing was worse. Yangyang wrote *Still. Still. Still.* in the log, and caught herself on the ninth day saving things — Chixia's war with the tax office goose, the melon vendor's opera voice, a cloud shaped exactly like Sergeant Mo — filing the day's absurdities under *to tell*, and coming home each evening to a window where the telling had nowhere to go. She told the room anyway, twice, out loud, heard how it sounded, and after that wrote the absurdities in the second ink instead, which was quieter but kept better.

On the twenty-first night the stillness broke: one night of wind, westbound and businesslike, in and out of the city like a courier who knows the address. In the morning her desk stood exactly as she had left it, inkstone on the letter, the letter under the inkstone, a season of salutations and one page finally finished. She picked it up and read it and her stomach dropped through the floor, gently, because every stroke was where she had left it — and the page felt *performed.* Like sheet music after the concert. Like a room where your name has just been said. She sat down on the bed and let the panic arrive and introduce itself, private words gone somewhere, gone *west* — and then did what she does with everything, which is turn it over and look at the workmanship. The wind had carried her letter to its someone. She thought about that someone, whom she had assembled over the years out of evidence: worth crossing a country for; the reason the spring detoured to reach her. She thought about her own someone, the person the letters were addressed to, the pull she had stopped explaining to herself around the same time she stopped explaining the wind — the certainty, ridiculous and load-bearing, that somewhere a stranger was owed a seat at her table. Then she thought about the shape of a wind that would carry the first person's letter to the second person, and how those two errands would fold together if — if —

She got up and opened the window all the way, into the wake of the going. *Tell her the seat's saved,* she called after it, into the ordinary morning. Then, much smaller, inside, where it could be tidied away before inspection: *and come back.*

Her sister's parcel came the week of the Double Seventh: their mother's xiao, wrapped in oiled cloth the way you dress a wound. *Ma says the damp is bad for it. She says nothing else. You know how she says nothing. — S.* And a postscript: *Solstice. Come home when it's cold. I already told the kitchen.* Yangyang had chosen the sword at eight, in that courtyard, with the whole family watching, and the flute had watched from the shelf for twelve years. She put it to her lips on the bunk and blew one note to see, and the note came out clean and centred and in tune — and she lowered it and looked at it for a long moment, because it had crossed a country in a box and spent twelve years in a storeroom, and everyone knows instruments forget. The flute arrived in tune. She wrapped it back up carefully and thought about her mother's hands and said nothing to anyone, which was, she supposed, the family craft.

On festival night Chixia shoved two skewers into her hand at the lantern rafts and pointed her at the wall stairs with her chin — *Go stand on your wall, weirdo. Bring your someone a skewer* — and Baizhi appeared at her other shoulder to say, deadpan, that it was for the fire warden, and incidentally that the Huaxu archive holds a petition to found a county named Qixi, refused twice, the Board of Rites having ruled it inauspicious to name land after a parting. *I have always thought the Board lacked romance.*

The wind came at moonrise, and it came home thin. She felt the difference the way you feel a friend's bad month in their handshake: the spring hand still in it, the palm that had decided to be gentle, and it was paper now, it was onionskin, it was a warmth arriving on the last of itself. It stopped on her cheek. It stayed. *Long trip,* she said. She laid the skewers on the parapet, one and one, unwrapped the flute, and stood it against her lip. *Ma's craft. I'm out of practice. Complaints go to the Board of Rites.*

The first notes came out crooked — the old lullaby, the one for summer fevers — and her fingers remembered more than her breath did, and at the phrase where the tune goes up and stays up she felt her lungs reach the bottom, and the note continued. Her breath had ended. The note went on, round and full, pouring up out of the flute like water finding its level, and she stood there with her fingers on the holes and her eyes very wide while something breathed through her mother's instrument from the other side, and the sound they made together was a third thing wearing both of them, and below the wall the whole festival paused in the middle of its sugar — she heard it pause — and a child's voice said *look,* to the sky. They finished the lullaby. Her fingering, its breath. Where her lungs gave out, it poured; where it thinned, she carried. The last note they split without discussing it, half and half, and it faded off the wall and out over the black water like a lantern let go.

She lowered the flute. *The post in my courtyard. Three inches. One stroke.* The night listened. *That's her stroke. My teacher's.* Her thumb moved on the mouthpiece, a small maintenance of courage. *The elbow correction. The arithmetic. You give the stroke everything, and what the wood refuses—* and her voice did something and she let it. *You trained under her. Ahead of me. Years ahead, by the cut.*

The wind stood in front of her, whatever that means for wind, and she felt it the way you feel a held breath in a dark room: the whole night, waiting on a word.

*師姐,* she said. Elder sister of my school.

And the wind sat down. The wall's air, the banners' whisper, the river's static — everything settled at once, all around her, into a stillness with a centre, and the centre was warm, and it was the deepest quiet she had ever stood inside, and it lasted three heartbeats, and then the flute in her hands, untouched at the mouth, sounded one note, low and even. *Yes.*

Yangyang laughed once, and it came out wet, and she wiped her face with her sleeve in the practical way and picked up the first skewer and ate it, standing guard over the second one, under the river of stars where the magpies were building their annual bridge out of their own bodies, which was, she thought, the going rate for bridges, apparently, everywhere. Later she asked the sky: *When you find her. Will spring still come by here?* The wind lingered on her face a long time. Longer than an answer. *…Forget I asked.* She adjusted her hat. The little bird held its stitch. *Eat your skewer.* She left the second skewer on the parapet and walked down at the change of watch, and in the morning it was still there, cold in a warm dawn, and it looked, to her, finished. She ate it on the walk to muster and it lasted the whole stair.

By autumn the dawn drills had become lessons. The correction at her elbow arrived earlier now — before the fault, where her teacher's had come after — a pressure that knew her errors by heart and met them at the door, and she drilled the cutting forms against an opponent every stroke passed through, and improved the way you improve under family: fast, and complaining. *Show-off,* she said, one morning, when a falling willow leaf came apart midair in front of her, halved clean along the spine by nothing, both pieces spiralling down in formation. The air had a smugness to it. She would have testified to the smugness before the Board of Rites. She cut the next leaf herself, a quarter-second late, one edge ragged, and the wind ruffled her hat brim in the precise cadence of applause performed slowly. Chixia had started saluting gusts — *Covering my bases. If the weather's promoted, I want it remembered I was polite early.* And Baizhi handed Yangyang a folder in the clinic doorway on the equinox without preamble: a year of the ledger's oddities, plotted, saves against shifts, weather against watch rosters, two lines on one chart walking together like old women. *Correlation study. Unpublished. Read the last page.* The last page held one note in Baizhi's smallest hand: *Underweight, your weather. Whatever changed at the festival — continue.* The two of them stood in that doorway having the entire conversation without spending a word of it. *Lunch?* *Lunch.*

Then the tremors started past the north face in the frost month. The garrison felt the first one through their boot soles at supper, a pressure in the ground more than a sound, one slow beat, far off, patient, and the beacons went to double watch. *Eight days since the last one,* said Keeper Pei, forty-one years of steady hands, setting a glass. *The one before came ten apart. The one before that, twelve.* Somebody down the wall said maybe it was settling. *It's counting,* Pei said, and trimmed the next lamp.

The bad night came at month's end: an outwork on the north road, a sortie caught by a rockfall with something underneath the rockfall, and the wall watched a whole cliff face let go above Third Squad and watched Third Squad walk out of it — every one of them — stumbling out of a boil of dust through a corridor of air that stood open in the collapse like a held door. And the door held, and held, and held, until the last man cleared it, and then the mountain finished falling. The cause column got its entry. The whole wall saw what the word cost.

Yangyang came off shift at the grey hour and found her window as she'd left it, the flute on the sill where it lived now, and the wind came to greet her the way it had every evening that season — through the instrument, one low note, their private *home again.*

The note cracked. It came out in a thread, wavered, guttered like a lamp at the end of its oil, and stopped a quarter of the way through itself, and the silence after it was the loudest thing she had heard all year.

She stood in the middle of her room with her sword still on her back. Then she went to the lake — down through the sleeping streets and out along the bank below the beacon, where the grass grows in the long soft rows the keepers leave alone — and knelt at the waterline in the frost, and gave the black water the Outrider's full bow, because you address the office, her teacher had said, whatever you think of the god. *Sentinel. Outrider Yangyang, Mingting registry, Jinzhou posting. Filing a request outside my rank.* The lake lay still, holding the last stars. *It comes here. Fourth watch, when the wall's asleep. It tells you—* her hands closed on her knees — *everyone gives it their weight to carry, the whole country, we've been doing it for years, it's the custom, and it carries all of it, and it comes here and gives the weight to you, because you're the only thing in Huanglong bigger than what it's holding.* The water breathed against the bank, in, out. *You keep what it tells you.* She bowed again, forehead to the frost. *Keep* it.

Far down, under the held stars, the lake turned over. Slowly. With great courtesy. The lamps along the crest wall guttered once, all together, and steadied. She knelt there until the cold came through her knees, and got an answer the size of a god's silence, and walked back up in the dark.

At her window the wind was waiting. It went to the flute and tried again — their note, the low one — and managed half of it, a true warm half, and frayed. Yangyang picked up the flute, set her lips where the breath was failing, and finished the note. Her breath, its beginning. One sound wearing both of them, the way it had on festival night with the directions reversed, and she held their note to its full length and let it fade properly, the way notes deserve, out over the frost and the black water and the counting dark beyond the mountains. *New terms,* she said softly, to the air by her cheek. *I carry too.*

The summons came on Bureau paper with the Magistrate's private seal, which happens to Outriders about once a dynasty, and the veterans of the muster yard watched her go up through the falling snow with the particular silence of men revising their memories of everything they had ever said in front of her. Jinhsi received her standing at the window with the lake grey below and the file open on the desk. *Outrider. Your registry says Mingting.* *Yes, Excellency.* *The lake dreams. You will have heard. On wind nights it dreams a library burning that has never burned. Floodwater on dry streets. A red cap.* Another page. *The runners map the dreams. The dreams walk your patrol lines.* The snow ticked at the window and Yangyang stood at attention and said nothing, which was, she was discovering, the family craft. *On still nights, it dreams a girl. A courtyard, a black post, a wooden sword. The girl cuts at the post. The lake wakes me, every time, before the stroke lands.* She closed the file, and her eyes came up, gold and level. *Last night it dreamed you, Outrider. Standing where you are standing now. I have summoned you to reduce the lake's workload.* *Excellency. I'm finding out. I'll report to you when I have it whole.* *To me alone.* *To you alone.*

Jinhsi studied her for the length of a held breath — the Magistrate of the youngest gate, who is being remembered by something, and has begun to suspect the something is fond — and then looked back to the lake, and the audience was over. Yangyang was at the door when the flat voice came again, quieter, half addressed to the window. *The girl at the post. A year of dreams, and she never lands the cut. The lake always wakes me first.* A pause the exact width of a magistrate deciding to be a person. *I find I want very much to see her land it.*

Yangyang stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame. *She will, Excellency. She's had good teachers.*

Leave was granted for the solstice, as promised, as threatened. She rode the post road west with the flute in her pack and her breath standing white ahead of her, and Mingting received her the way home receives anyone: as if she had stepped out an hour ago on an errand, and where had she put the good ladle. At dusk, with the kitchen lit and loud behind her, she went out alone to the courtyard.

The post stood where it had stood all her life, black-grey and iron through, holding twelve years of her — the shallow years, cut above cut above cut, a childhood of mornings written in tenths of inches — and across them all, higher, this year's guest-stroke: one cut, three inches deep, the grain parted like water. The wooden sword stood leaned at its foot, edge to the east, where a guest had left it. She picked it up. It knew her hands.

She took her stance in the snow-lit dark, and the year arranged itself in her arms — the ribbon in the trough, the incense that bent at her, the third bowl's leaning steam, the letter gone west, the lullaby with its impossible note, *shijie*, the door of air that held while a squad walked out, the half-note finished, the lake turning over, a magistrate at a window wanting a stranger's stroke to land — all of it, the whole almanac, gathering into her shoulders the way her teacher had promised things gather, if you carry them honestly, all the way.

She breathed out. And the solstice wind came over the courtyard wall — down the roof tiles and along her arms like a sleeve being rolled, the year's first north-born warmth inside the year's deepest cold — and she felt it settle into the stroke with her, her wrists and its weight, her line and its length, and there was a moment mid-swing where she could have said whose arm this was and chose to lose track instead.

The sound the post made was one she had never got out of it in twelve years.

She stood in the follow-through, breathing. Snow came off the roof in a soft applause. The cut was there under her palm when she covered it, flat hand over parted grain, warm wood in the cold — and she covered it without looking. *Together doesn't count,* she told the courtyard, and heard how happy she sounded, and let that stand too. *When I can do it alone, I'll look.*

Then the kitchen door opened behind her, spilling gold, and Suisui called her in for dinner in the tone that had fetched her out of that courtyard ten thousand times. Inside, the table was crowded and steaming; her father was losing an argument with the stove; Suisui was winning an argument with the seating; and their mother moved down the table setting places with the unhurried exactness she brings to everything — one, two, three, four — and a fifth pair of chopsticks, laid neat at the empty end, at the seat nearest the door.

The kitchen went on around it. Suisui's eyes came up, found Yangyang's, and asked. Yangyang's answered, and asked back. Their mother set down the last bowl and said nothing, in the fluent way she says it. And out in the courtyard the wind moved once around the post and settled at the eaves, and the lamps leaned, all together, toward the table, and steadied.

*Solstice. Wind rises. Inside the deepest cold, the first warmth is born.*

### The balcony

The wind came the long way round to Lahai-Roi, and the city logged her as a ticket. *Air movement, source unknown. Non-hazard.* Somebody wrote *investigate?* in the margin, and somebody else wrote *it's air* underneath it, and the city — young and busy and recently saved — moved on.

Iuno was at the rail of the east stairwell balcony past midnight with the pot and one cup, doing the thing she calls thinking and the High Priestess calls patrol. The steam off her cup bent east. The vent draws west. She watched it do that for the length of one slow breath, and then set out a second cup — the way she does everything now, in full view so that it costs her: kettle, leaves, four minutes, hot, placed on the rail across from her own with the handle turned outward, guest-side. *It's Earl Grey. There's a history. Drink it anyway.* The air on the balcony gathered itself, composed itself, and something at the rim of the guest cup shaped the steam for a moment and released it, a bow the size of a breath.

*You're a long way from your weather.* The wind spent a word. *Jinzhou.* *Mm. Your kite's ahead of you. It cleared the passage at dusk; the maintenance union is writing poetry about it.* A beat. *And you came the long way round regardless. To see me first.* Motion in the steam: yes. *Then ask.*

The balcony went very quiet, the way rooms do when something gathers itself to pay, and when the voice came it was thin as onion-skin paper, and it asked the question another woman had once asked at a dock and been answered with a railing gripped bloodless and a word that died in the asking. *Why did you wake her?*

Iuno looked at her tea. *To kill her,* she said. *I loved her instead.* Level, worn smooth with handling; she has said it to a Senate, to a Council of Elders, and to the woman herself, and it costs the same every time. *The order of those two sentences is my entire defense.* The steam moved, waiting. *Your turn. Why did you die?* A long stillness. Down in the courtyard one magnolia petal left the branch out of season and crossed the flagstones the wrong way. *…Same answer,* the wind said.

Then Iuno said the thing she had climbed those stairs every night that week half-knowing she would eventually have to say to somebody. Years ago, when the hour of the waking first showed itself in her basin, she had audited it, because you audit anything you intend to build a life on. And there was wind on the water. Clear sky in every direction of the reading, the sea like glass, and wind, over that one hour, leaning east. *I logged it. One line.* Weather. *Telling anyone would have handed fate an ending, and I was the kind of priestess who kept endings in a drawer.* A breath. *I saw you coming. I filed you under weather. If it's an apology you want, I keep a large stock, and they're all secondhand.*

The wind made a sound in the rail's hollow that might have been the ghost of a laugh and might have been a draught finding a bolt-hole. Iuno elected to hear the first. Then three words came back, evenly, at full price. *Your shore. My toll.*

*Yes. That's the account as I'd draw it up too. I'd contest a single line — I never learned who countersigned.* And then, for what it was worth, which was little: she had been delivered to that shore herself. Summoned, by a grief that hadn't happened yet, through a door she didn't build. She has stood on that sand a hundred times since, in dreams and worse, hunting the hand that steered her, and every time she finds it, it is wearing her own face — and she has never once been sure it was hers. *There's a string in one telling, and a hand in another, and a hole where the third should be, and I'm in two of them. Believe whichever lets you sleep. If you do that.* Then: *I spent my life reading forward and dreading it. You've spent your death reading backward and grieving it. Between the two of us we own the whole clock — and look. The minute in hand still surprises us both.* She tipped her head at the guest cup. *Now say the part you flew here to say.*

The wind gathered, and each word drew on principal now, and the balcony heard it happen. *Ovathrax.* A pause like a held breath. *Comes. To the youngest gate.*

*I know the name.* Iuno's voice cooled by degrees; behind her sternum the High Priestess stopped lounging. *The Threnodian of War.*

*I watched it — win.* The steam shivered. *The city — held. The word — cost—* The voice frayed to threads and re-spun. *Everything.*

*And you want her.*

*I want—* The longest pause of the night. And then the correction, gentler than anything on that balcony had a right to be. *Her to* choose.

Iuno was silent a moment. *Three weeks,* she said. *She has had her daughter back for three weeks. She sleeps through the night as of Tuesday. You are asking me to hand her a war.*

The temperature dropped. Between one heartbeat and the next the guest cup went cold, tea skinned over with a whorl of frost at the rim, thin as a held grudge — a whole drowned winter surfacing in a teacup — and the magnolia below stood suddenly, absolutely still. Then the frost withdrew. Slowly. Deliberately. The way a sword goes back in its sheath by the hand of someone who has decided, again, tonight, what she is for. The steam returned to the cup, contrite.

*…I'm asking you,* the wind said, *to hand her a letter.*

Iuno looked at the cup. The steam had begun, very faintly, to spiral — the way it does over a cup someone is actually drinking from. *She'll go. You know that.* *…Yes.* *Then you already know her.* She exhaled through her nose, the sound she makes in place of surrender. *Fine. It goes through me standing beside her, and through half a dozen people with opinions and weaponry. And your plan, as I understand it, is one letter.*

*My plan,* said the wind, spending nearly the last of the night's purse on it, *is her —* reading *it.*

Iuno reached over and settled the guest cup a quarter-turn. *One more thing. What do I call you?*

The wind moved across the tea's surface. The liquid dimpled; lines drew themselves in the sheen — a stroke, another, the start of a character, careful, like a student's first brushwork — and stopped. Half a name. The surface held its half-stroke like a held breath, and then even that smoothed away, and the air on the balcony went thin and apologetic.

Iuno sat with it. She, of all women living, knows the exact weight of a sentence sealed mid-syllable; she carried three words in a crystallising throat down the whole side of a falling world. *Later, then. We'll find the rest of it. There are people in this house who are extremely good at names.* She stood, collected the pot, and left the guest cup where it was — full, hot again, handle out. *Finish your tea. I'm told, on excellent authority, that the temperature matters.*

At the door she paused. *Mingting,* she said, testing the word like a coin. *There's a Marshal there who has kept a berth warm for us all season. Your kite has good timing.* A beat. *Or good handlers.*

Then she went in, and at the landing she stopped with her hand on the rail and rewrote her morning. Breakfast. All of it. First. She was getting good at that. It cost the same every time.

Helios came up on schedule, because the engineers had agreed it should, and on a dormitory roof two people watched it happen while their cups went cold in their hands. *Ma,* said Amy. *Kite.*

Red, high, coming down the long air under a false sky with a tail like a brushstroke, descending through a city where kites have nothing to ride, steady as a decision. Mei was up before the thought finished, one foot on the parapet, hand out — the Fool catches things; it is the first fact about her — and the string crossed her palm and her fingers closed, and the kite came around overhead once, twice, like something making sure of her, and settled.

Bound beneath the red paper, along the spine: a seashell, pale, sea-worn, warm from a sun it had never stood under. And a letter, one page, folded small.

*It's humming,* Amy said. Mei turned the shell over and put it to her ear, because that is what you do; some knowledge lives in the arm. A tide — long, patient, shore after shore of it, the sound of a sea she had never stood beside arriving anyway, familiar as a room out of a dream. Under the tide, wind. And under the wind, at the very bottom of the shell, a breath: the sound a far shore makes when someone standing on it has just, after a very long time, decided to speak.

The roof door opened. Lupa, hair wrecked from sleep, nose already working. *Rain,* she said. Amy looked at the sealed sky. *We're underground.* *I know what I smell.*

Mei lowered the shell. The letter sat in her other hand, and she read the salutation twice, once with her eyes and once with her thumb, tracing it. Honest type, pressed deep. *To the one the wind is looking for —*

And behind them all, in the doorway, tea in hand, stood Iuno: awake the whole night by the look of her, wearing the face of a woman arriving on schedule at a conversation she has already rehearsed losing.

*Mei,* she said. *There's something I need to tell you. All of it.*

The shell's inscription, as legible after the crossing, reads: *"Like moths to a ■■, all who hear the ■■ walk the road of ■■■■. To the last ■■■■ of the ■■■ left in ■■ world: may a journey unhoped-for, and fated to ■■, find its ■■■■ in the presence of ■■■."* The founders of Bookbinders' Row note that the missing sorts share a property: cut fresh, each one cracks in the mould. The Row has stopped cutting. The court keeps the blanks in the register against the day the type comes home. And a later hand has added, underneath, that the sort for *fail* cracked first.

## VIII. DON'T FEAR THE REAPER

The next thing that came out of Mei's past did not arrive as a letter. It arrived as a wound in a glacier.

Days after Amy came home, Mornye sent Mei an encrypted priority message: a new interdimensional tear had opened on the Roya Frostlands glacier, near Baldur's knees, close to the buried Stridergate, and its residual frequency matched the far side. It was bleeding through. Mornye had locked Chase out of the grid to buy time and left the decision to Mei.

Lupa refused outright — they had got Amy back days ago and were not walking straight back into the meat grinder. Ciaccona called an open door to the Nihility a trap. Chisa predicted that if Chase's people went in uncalibrated they would die, and the anomaly would use their grief to widen the aperture. Amy flinched at the word *anomaly*, and then insisted on coming, because being left behind was worse — *Like hell I'm staying behind! I lived in that static for ten years.* Iuno made it a pack decision rather than a panic, Lupa extracted a promise of no heroic sacrifices, and Mei gave it: *We go in together, we look at the door, and we come out together.*

At the site the tear was wrong in a new way. Not the light-eating static of the Nihility but a glitching vertical seam bleeding neon yellow and cyan and chrome. Amy nearly touched it to patch the frequency and Iuno hauled her back by the collar. And Mei, closest, caught the smell coming off it: sterile antiseptic and freon — the smell of her own cryo-pod — layered over hot asphalt, blood, burning rubber, neon gas. The smell of a city built out of chrome and violence. Another doll. Another life Lilith had lived to avoid living her own.

Then the tear did not pull them in. It unpacked, and vomited a cyberpunk megacity outward over the Roya Frostlands: a skyscraper clipping through Baldur's armoured knee, the glacier becoming rain-slick asphalt. Iuno lunged for Mei's hand and the geometry warped and tore them apart, and the pack was scattered across fragments of a world that Lilith had written and abandoned.

Mei landed with Lynae, whose gyaru manner dropped instantly into Lawless Zone mercenary instinct, and they fought featureless grey clay dolls wearing torn Startorch uniforms with the faces of Chisa, Lynae and Cartethyia — mannequins that did not bleed and did not stop. *She told me I was just a doll. Someone's trying to prove it.* Iuno landed with Amy in a transit hub and grabbed her hoping she was Mei, and Amy, claustrophobic and panicking, lashed out — *You are not my mother!* — and Iuno, who had just lost Mei to a glitch in the asphalt, snapped back with the truest thing she had: *I am the woman who keeps your mother from falling apart.* Lupa landed with Mornye pinned under displaced glacier ice, hauled it off her, offered to carry her, and was refused — *I am not a casualty* — and so walked beside her while she stood up on a metal pipe instead. Ciaccona, Cartethyia and Chisa landed on an overpass, where Ciaccona was overwhelmed by the city's dissonance, Cartethyia was overwhelmed with delight after twenty years in a silent dark room, and Chisa observed that the architecture was seamed together out of their own memories — a Ragunnan cathedral, the Phosphor District — fused into Lilith's city, and was deliberately corralling them toward a centre.

And a pastel-haired netrunner named Lucy woke alone in a neon plaza, unable to reach the Net, with an unfamiliar tattoo on her arm and her hacking suddenly frictionless, and saw a figure in a yellow jacket. *David…? H-how?!*

Everything converged. Mei saw Lucy on the ground with a nine-foot cyborg bearing down on her and moved to shield a stranger on reflex. Rebecca — a girl Lucy had watched Adam Smasher crush to death — opened suppressive fire that nearly hit Mei and grazed Lupa, and Lupa, enraged, threw her burning spear into Rebecca's chest, which produced not gore but a catastrophic system failure that dumped every one of them into the Startorch Academy medical wing.

Luuk greeted the arrival of two extra students with clinical calm and offered Rebecca a candy for dimensional nausea. She swatted it away and attacked him, and Lupa tackled her through the cabinets. Then Lucy asked the only question she had. *…Where's David?* And Chisa said that when the anomaly's geometry sealed itself, whatever was left inside was gone.

What followed, locked in a disused archive while Mornye warned them that Chase would vivisect unregistered interdimensional refugees for telemetry, was two damaged worlds explaining themselves to each other. Lucy insisted it was a braindance, because a simulation can rip memories from a cortex and reality does not. Iuno told her flatly: *You are breathing our air. You bled on our floors.* Chisa identified that the anomaly had read Lucy's resonance and was using her grief as bait to keep her anchored, and Amy confirmed it from experience: *It shows you the people you lost because it wants you to stop moving.* Rebecca called the pack's trust a liability — *You trust too easy* — and Chisa answered: *We have all died, or been trapped in variations of death, at least once. Trust did not kill us. The absence of it did.* Lynae and Rebecca bonded over recoil mods and custom paint. Cartethyia was delighted by mantis blades and accidentally kicked up an archive-dust cyclone that coated everybody, which was the moment Lucy laughed for the first time. *You guys… are funny.*

It went wrong when Iuno, refusing to authorise a second suicide mission for strangers, called David *a hologram*. Rebecca nearly put a fist through her; the room armed itself; Mei ordered everyone down and held both sides accountable. Then Lucy collapsed — not attacked, just a cumulative neurological crash — and Lupa extinguished her own weapon and stepped unarmed into the line of Rebecca's gun to say so. *Nobody touched her. Her brain's just crashing.* That was the moment the two worlds started trusting each other, and it was immediately interrupted by Adam Smasher coming through a Category-4 security door.

He could not be hurt. Rebecca's rounds ricocheted; Iuno's hex-shield absorbed a missile and blew her off her feet; Mei's strike to his knee healed over with chromatic static; he snapped Lupa's spear and choked her until cartilage cracked. And Chisa, who sees weights and connections rather than faces, found the reason: he was not really there. He was tethered by a pulsating umbilical cord of static and red neon, anchored to Lucy's grief and feeding on it — *Just like Sumika had been.* She called the anchor point above his right collarbone, and Cartethyia went into the air channelling Fleurdelys and cut the tether with a blade of compressed divine wind, and Smasher crystallised and shattered into pixels and ash. Not killed. *It stopped. For now.*

Out on the glacier the tear had become a fractal infection overwriting Lahai-Roi's cavern architecture, threatening the seal over millions of people. Iuno did the cold arithmetic and ordered Lucy and Rebecca back through it alone, because they were the anchors of the paradox. And Mei said the cruellest true thing available to her: *And you will sacrifice the lucky few for a city again?* — the docks at Septimont, the Appointed Hour, the day Iuno decided Mei's death was an acceptable price. It landed. Internally: *I promised I would never do that again. I am doing it again.*

Amy defended Iuno's motive while telling her mother the comparison was a low blow. Lucy agreed to go back anyway, because their incompatible data could not be made to fit Lahai-Roi's geometry, and because without their world's immunosuppressants she had three days before her nervous system fried and Rebecca had two before her cybernetics seized her lungs. And Mei refused to let them go alone — because she had recognised the source. This was Smasher, out of one of Lilith's unfinished worlds, and there was a mercenary in that world who could kill him. A merc called V.

The entire pack physically formed a wall to stop her, and she went anyway. *If we go with them, we change the ending.* Iuno relented — *…You selfish, reckless idiot* — on condition the mission stayed narrow: find V, kill Smasher, extract, and if the window closed she would burn that city down herself rather than risk Lahai-Roi. Amy, terrified of anything void-adjacent, came because waiting not knowing was worse. *I hate you so much right now.* Lucy set the rally point at Arasaka Tower, the one landmark visible from every district, on a twenty-four-hour window — which Rebecca thought was insane and Chisa endorsed as logistically sound.

Then Mei squeezed her daughter's hand, levelled her blade, and led her pack into the bleeding neon dark.

The crossing decompressed and scattered them across four districts with their comms dead. Mei landed alone in a trash heap near Kabuki and immediately learned the rules of a world written to punish hope and reward cowardice: Scavengers who wanted to have fun with her before selling her for parts, bartenders who mocked an un-chromed organic for asking after V, a bouncer at Afterlife who turned her away. Walking Corpo Plaza she was hit by the realisation that every extra in the crowd was the protagonist of a sprawling desperate life. Then she broke down against a pillar in the rain, which was the only time her composure failed.

A fixer kid offered to take her to a ripperdoc who had seen V, for the price of her gold Septimont coin, and led her into a locked basement with four Maelstrom gangoons in it. A shotgun went off point-blank and the pellets failed to register — the world's code could not index her frequency; she and the other outsiders were glitches in Lilith's simulation. She killed all four in under three seconds.

V caught her on the way out at gunpoint, and Mei stated her business plainly: *I'm not from this city. I'm looking for you because I want to kill Adam Smasher.* V shot her in the leg on reflex and the wound grazed, which unnerved her more than the request did. A spectral Johnny Silverhand, visible only to V, told her to recruit the stray.

In the car V laid out her own countdown: the Relic in her skull was overwriting her neural pathways with Johnny's engram, and she had about a day before her body stopped being hers. Smasher was Arasaka's guard dog and any cure was in their basement, and her plan had been to go in alone precisely so nobody she loved would be in the crossfire. When Mei asked her to wait a few hours for her family to rally, V turned on her and invoked Jackie Welles, who bled out in a Delamain cab during the Relic heist and is now a jar of ashes. *You drag them in, they die.*

And Mei answered with her own version of the mistake. She had once left her family behind to face the Nihility alone, believing she was protecting them, and *they hated me for it. I hurt them.* And then her daughter had mirrored it straight back, leaping in front of danger to protect her — *I'd rather die than watch her do that again.* Her argument: *If you leave them behind, I am making the choice for them. They just become ghosts who happen to still be breathing.* V ordered her out of the car and drove off alone, calling her a dead woman walking, and Mei said she'd see her at the front doors.

Alone on her balcony in Megabuilding H10, V punched the concrete railing until the synthetic skin came off her knuckles, feeling nothing, with Mei's words looping. And she worked out that she had been living Jackie's dream of dying a legend and Johnny's unfinished vendetta rather than anything of her own. *I want to live!* She called Judy Alvarez and promised she still had a tower to burn but was going to bring an army to do it, and then she was coming home. She called Panam Palmer and called in her favour: *We're going to war.* Viktor Vektor told her her vitals were collapsing and sent her off with *Give 'em hell, kid.* Misty dealt her the Fool — a stranger's leap of faith — the Magician, the Moon, and the Sun. *You're going to survive this. All of you.*

Elsewhere in the city the pack was assembling under its own power. Iuno shielded Amy through the crash and injured her arm, and then coached her through the sensory hell of an unshielded network by anchoring her against the crystalline stump, which reads as nothing in that world and functions as a wall. In a laundromat Amy called her *Auntie,* and Iuno swore Mei *will not die before she finds us. I forbid it* — and privately, to the High Priestess needling her for going soft: *She is Mei's. Which makes her ours.* Amy pushed through nosebleeds to trace the gold Septimont coin's transaction to a subterranean clinic in Watson, which proved Mei was alive. Then five Tyger Claws jumped them for Iuno's exotic chrome, blinded her with an EMP dazzler, and aimed a smart-pistol at Amy — and Iuno threw herself into the bullet's path, took it in the back, and did not penetrate. Then the Moonwheel came out and two of them stopped existing. *I am exceptionally difficult to kill.* They stole two Yaiba Kusanagis, Amy declared herself the Speed Queen, and en route Alt Cunningham forced a message into her head: *Wait for V.*

Lupa, Chisa and Lucy came down in Kabuki, where Lupa grabbed Lucy by the throat for saying Mei was probably already dead, and Chisa said *Senpai. Drop her.* On the monorail Lupa caught Mei's scent — cedar and rain — through the window and could not confirm it, because Mei was directly below at that moment. At Lucy's old apartment in Megabuilding H4 a yellow EMT jacket was waiting, and Lucy admitted the thing she had been carrying: at the foot of Arasaka Tower, Smasher had killed Rebecca and killed David. Which meant the Rebecca walking around this city was some kind of echo the anomaly had dredged up.

Lucy tried a net-dive to find V's signal and was overwhelmed by David until Lupa physically pulled her out of the ice bath and Chisa cut the cable. And then the three of them traded their dead. ## IX. WHAT THEY DID WITH THE PEACE

## X. A LEDGER OF LIGHT

In the week Amy first slept through the night, Iuno started copying out a dead girl's diary by hand, one hour every morning, from memory.

She was keeping a promise. Kneeling over a pile of finished stories at the bottom of the void, Mei had asked her author *You remember them, don't you? All of them,* and been told yes, and had said: *then I will.* Iuno had heard that. So she began transcribing the journal of the pilot of Penrose-512 — the ordinary four and a half thousand days of the girl in the cryo-unit — in a first ink, and annotating it in a second, her own, signed each time with a single initial.

The entries came up in folios and the household lived alongside them. Day 612, a child crying in a field and a friend called Dingy coming to sit with her — and that same week, on the Startorch training ground in the thaw, Iuno watched a first-year cry and another first-year go over. Day 1839, *I rejected another girl today.... why?* — and in the kitchen that morning a girl who had spent a decade in the dark ate breakfast normally while the whole household treated it as sacred, and the dorm's false sky ran a rain program, and Amy played in it with an IV cap taped down while Mornye took photographs. Day 2182, *I lost a friend today,* and the rest of the page blank — and Iuno disclosed that she keeps a blank page of her own, involving a door, a barrier of glass, and a spoon, and the pack marked the day quietly with tea and splitting wood. Day 3345, *Been writing a lot lately. I finally feel.* Day 3502, doubt, at other people's weddings.

Somewhere in the middle of it Iuno admitted what she had done, which was read all fourteen of the earlier stories, plus the fifteenth, which is this one. She had found the pattern: every one of them opens with a girl coming out of water under a new name, and somebody always arrives to meet her early — a wolf, a bard, a doll-maker — and there is weather all through them, and not one ending is a closed wall. And she had read the story she is herself a character in, including the thirty winters the author wrote for her, and she had sat with the anger about that, and then found that she had also been written surviving, and staying, and loved. *Love, or apprenticeship. This house has stopped distinguishing.* Her verdict on the whole shelf was that the author had made fourteen girls and been one of them, and that this fifteenth telling is the one where the practice took.

Day 4301: *I started writing a story about a girl who wakes up with no memories.* Day 4302: the admission of being jealous of her. Iuno read three buried wants in that — to start over, to be someone a story happens to, and simply to be wanted — and noted that all three are currently employed in her household. That week a record player arrived, honouring a promise Iuno had made to Mei on a bench once, and they played an impossible record, and Amy sang live harmony over her own decade-old recorded voice for one chorus.

Then the Penrose folios: twelve selected out of eight thousand applicants; the strange discovery, in space, of feeling entirely ordinary; rereading Dingy's message telling her to come back; and something passing the ship, turning pages on their own, which she found comforting rather than frightening. Iuno matched that against a frontier court's record of a crossing between printings. And the household went up to the frostlands to look at real stars, and Ciaccona reported that the wind had begun making requests, and Amy broadcast on a dead frequency — *Broadcast resumes when it resumes. Keep the radios on* — while Mornye, unasked, recorded it.

Day 4501 stops mid-sentence. She is falling toward something the instruments call the Etheric Sea, and she calls it—

The household speculated. Lupa said *home.* Ciaccona said a downbeat. Cartethyia, who was born in the dark water herself, implied she knew the word and would not say it. Chisa said a taut string. Amy said the deep end. And the ledger ruled that the blank stays blank until the author fills it in herself.

That night Iuno sat up alone with tea, watching east, and the steam off her cup leaned against a vent that draws west.

She put boots by the door.

Then the book started writing itself forward. Day 4502 arrived in the first ink, in the present tense of the household: *I read a great story today. I think I can walk a little further tomorrow.* So they answered the standing question — what does she need — with a list. Amy wanted her to have someone to be bored with, snacks, and to finish their co-op game. Others offered breakfast, sunlight, an unwise crush, a well-timed joke, weather, and to be caught looking. Iuno's own answer was that what she needs is somebody to believe it for her until hers catches up, a post Iuno has held before and is available for. A scented bookmark card signed *H.* turned up, reserving the east-gate stand order for two. Luuk read the folio, approved of it, and left his usual Tuesday candy dish. Buddy slept on the closed book. And an unsigned third hand, architecturally neat, silently corrected a dating error, as it had been doing for some time, and nobody knows whose it is.

So Iuno started drafting entries forward, on approval — hot coffee; finding the childhood fields still standing; finally answering Dingy; writing one new page and forgiving it for being short. And she named the mechanism out loud: *her line lengthens when mine does. The current runs both directions.* Which is why the boots were by the door, and why she had been watching east while a lamplit country west of four seas counted tremors. Then the book wrote itself again, unprompted, at Day 4511: something is coming in the story, and she has sharpened her pencil for the first time in years.

Her cherished item is a pencil. Standard Penrose issue, twelve to a tin, stamped AD ASTRA PER ASPERA. Her tin holds ten unsharpened and one worn down and freshly sharpened. That accounts for eleven, against a roster of twelve, and the unsigned third hand has flagged the discrepancy in the margin and left it open.

At the season's end Amy spotted a red kite coming down through a sealed sky, and Iuno caught it, and there was a humming seashell bound to its spine and a letter that began *To the one the wind is looking for —*, and Lupa smelled rain underground. And Iuno stood in the doorway with her tea and told Mei there was something she needed to tell her. All of it. The ledger declines to record what it was; it says the confession will keep its own pages in its own hour.

What the ledger does record is the season's outstanding accounts. Fireworks owed to Amy: pending. A frozen co-op save: to be finished. A conversation Luuk has been owed for years about what Iuno is meant to do with gods and gates: filed under *soon.* An unclaimed berth in a far country called Mingting, kept warm by an interested Marshal. Standing tea behind a door thirty years on oiled hinges. Pudding at the east gate, reserved for two. And a nightly appointment: same time tomorrow.

Then the pilot wrote back — and the copyist noted that the reply was already in the book before it was written, in a hand she now admits she recognises. She said the answer to the blank could have been any three words. She said she had met others, birds gliding across the dark sky, and that she roosts with them now. She said she still does not know where she is heading, or how to answer Dingy. She said her blue door is always open, and a trip back doesn't sound too bad, and that she is, in fact, doing alright. She called Iuno her hero. And she signed off *until we meet again,* and closed her journal humming a tune Ciaccona wrote, about flight, and being free of the ground.

In the common room, at that moment, Ciaccona was humming the harmony to it as though somebody unseen had the lead, and asked Iuno whether the vents were doing something. Iuno said it was weather. Amy's crayon note in the margin records that the household now knows this is their song. Iuno was crying, happily.

*Same time tomorrow.*

## XI. WHERE EVERYTHING STANDS

At the beginning of this there was a woman with no past, pulled out of the sea by someone who intended to spend her, in a country that had just retired its god and did not yet know how to govern itself; and a girl holding a door shut in a void that nobody had come for in ten years; and a school full of forged enrolments; and a fabricated girl with a countdown in her chest who had never been issued a birthday; and a dead city's last priestess carrying ten thousand ungrantable wishes alone; and a wind on the far side of the world with most of its name gone, holding an address that had already been superseded.

At the end of it, Rinascita governs itself so thoroughly that its founders' monument had to be redesigned as playground equipment, and the five ATTESA files sit unclaimed on a shelf beside an unused TORNATA stamp, because the Office of the Expected only closes a file when the person walks in and signs it themselves, and none of them has come home yet. Septimont's Ephor has a plan to lose a fight in public on purpose so her city will learn to stand without her. Ragunna's Devil's Advocate has emptied her family's poison room into a university and rewritten a four-hundred-year oath down to four words. Huanglong hosts the opera it wanted to ban. Jinzhou has a doctrine that counts the returning first. A frontier court retires its automata with honours and lets them keep their kettles. A hundred-and-forty-year arrangement in Ashinohara has been refused, with the shutters open, by a minister who now pours tea as a host. And a philanthropist ninety floors above the lawless zones has begun hedging her fortune on hope, on the grounds that it has started compounding.

Amy is nineteen and legally alive under a name she chose, with a body she has stopped auditing, a mech she named after her own teenage alter ego, a pirate radio station she is broadcasting on again, and permission to be worried about instead of needed. Mei has stopped running, has said *I wish I never left you* out loud and been answered, has been forgiven by her own author and forgiven her own architect, and is engaged to her in every way except the paperwork. Iuno, who once filed a woman's arrival under *weather* and kept endings in a drawer, spends an hour every morning copying a stranger's diary by hand and has boots by the door. Lupa has told somebody about Schedule B. Chisa has a mother in the city who orders two bowls and pays for both, and a letter in a tin beside a collar whose six a.m. chime she has still never let anyone hear. Cartethyia carries a cane with a finch on it. Ciaccona plays the fourth note now, and has written a song that someone in another world is humming. Rebecca is stuck here, permanently, and has drawn her dead on a cabin wall in marker. Hiyuki has an open leave of absence dated *until the weather turns,* a shrine with no lock on it, and a standing order for two bowls of pudding at a gate in a city that is being resettled.

Sigrika has a journal with three stamped entries in it, one of which is a birthday, one of which is a species, and one of which is a promise to wait.

Denia is dead, and the seam she died to close is sealed, and something warm was lifted out of that lake by a woman standing in the shallows and carried home, and nobody in Lahai-Roi knows it. Under the refilled water there is one heartbeat, patient, alone, keeping its own time.

The Grand Architect is alive, unpunished, on campus, wearing whatever face is required, having been offered ordinariness and been unable to accept it — and she is no longer looking at the weapon she built. She is looking at the girl she called *little bird.*

And four seas away, at the youngest gate of Huanglong, the tremors past the north face are eight days apart and closing, and a keeper who has trimmed lamps for forty-one years says the ground is not settling, it is counting; and a Magistrate dreams a city that never burned; and a wind with fewer than a sentence's worth of characters left has spent all of them on a kite, a shell, and one page of somebody else's handwriting.

The letter has been caught. It has not yet been answered.
